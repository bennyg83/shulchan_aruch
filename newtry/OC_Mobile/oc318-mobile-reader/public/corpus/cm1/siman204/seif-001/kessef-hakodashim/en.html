It is proper for him to stand by his word. If one first gave his word and is subject to the rule that the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with one who retracts, and afterward sold that same property to another for money so that he is now subject to Mi Shepara toward the second buyer, at first glance Mi Shepara is more severe and overrides the concern for his first promise. Yet perhaps the severity of Mi Shepara exists chiefly in the public and interpersonal realm, because the formal curse of Mi Shepara creates a particularly strong social consequence. As to the obligation before Heaven, retracting from one’s word may be comparable to retracting after payment. One might therefore say that a second verbal commitment cannot nullify the first, and that the second commitment does not take hold at all. Nevertheless, where the beit din does not know that there was a prior commitment, it administers Mi Shepara regarding the payment. The verse requiring one’s “yes” to be righteous is applied by the Sages to one’s word. Even if that teaching is of Torah force, the verse may prohibit speaking deceitfully, where one’s mouth says one thing while his heart intends another, rather than a later change of mind. A later retraction may instead be condemned because it disturbs peace among people. If so, the payment to the second buyer is more severe, because the Sages attached Mi Shepara to it. At the moment he sold to the second buyer, the first verbal commitment was displaced, and the prior disapproval for changing his word has already attached; the more serious sanction of Mi Shepara now governs. This is comparable to the Sages’ teaching that greater attention is given to one who cries out in distress. Accordingly, the payment itself may constitute the retraction from the first statement. Even if the beit din and second buyer know of the first promise only after the second transaction, the seller may still be subject to Mi Shepara and cannot excuse himself by saying that he must fulfill the first promise, because that promise was already displaced by the second act. This requires careful study. If there were two verbal promises one after another and witnesses establish both without interruption, the second buyer may perhaps tell the seller, “You must appease the first buyer in whatever manner he will accept; your word to me is not thereby nullified.” Likewise, if the first buyer refuses to waive his claim, the second statement may not have uprooted the first because the conflict could potentially be resolved by monetary appeasement. Where the merchandise is in a third party’s hands and the seller undertook no personal obligation, however, perhaps the second verbal commitment has no effect at all. This requires further study.
