With the debt I have against you. Rambam holds that a prior debt can function as consideration for a sale, while other authorities disagree. Maggid Mishneh explains that Rambam understands the sugya of "the value of an ox is like barter" as having displaced the statement in Kiddushin that an ordinary loan does not acquire. Ramban and Rashba instead explain the sugya as one where the buyer says explicitly that the acquisition is made through the benefit of forgiving the loan. Bach objected: if benefit of forgiving a loan works, it should work even for a debt unrelated to a prior sale. That is not a difficulty. The benefit of extending or forgiving a debt is no stronger than actual money. Since actual money does not acquire movables rabbinically, neither does this benefit. A debt arising from a prior sale is exceptional because such a case is uncommon and the Sages left the Torah law in place. For land, which is acquired by money, the benefit of forgiving a debt works because that benefit is treated like money. Without the language of benefit, the debt itself is not money for acquisition. Bach and Shach in Yoreh Deah write that for sale of a firstborn animal to a gentile, one needs both money and meshichah, and if the Jew was already owed money by the gentile and simply applies that debt as the purchase price, this does not count as payment. According to the analysis above, if the Jew says, "Your cow is acquired to me through the benefit of my forgiving the debt," that benefit should serve as money. Indeed, I would go further. Where there is meshichah and the seller agrees to accept the existing debt as full consideration, the sale should work according to all opinions even without expressly saying "benefit of forgiveness." Tosafot explains that even according to the view that money ordinarily acquires, in a gift or found object where there is no money component, meshichah certainly acquires. Similarly, where the seller accepts the outstanding debt as the complete price, nothing further remains to be paid. The debt is being discharged and the seller is treated as having received his consideration. The transaction is therefore no worse than a gift with respect to the sufficiency of meshichah. The requirement of separate money applies when money remains outstanding, not where the seller has accepted the existing debt in satisfaction of the whole price.
Because he extended the repayment period. Rosh says that if a matured debt is extended and the debtor says, "Acquire my field through the benefit of the additional time you have granted me," the field is acquired. In my view this means only that the field itself is acquired. The value of the field must nevertheless be restored, because this arrangement constitutes fixed interest. A prohibited sale can still be legally effective, just as the garment received as interest is acquired even though its value must be returned. Kiddushin says that if a woman is betrothed through an extension of time, the kiddushin can be effective while the arrangement is treated as an evasion of interest. Rashi says there is no fixed interest there because no specific monetary amount was stipulated, only the marriage itself. Tosafot understands that even the marriage case can involve fixed interest under the appropriate reading. Here, where land is acquired in exchange for extending a loan term, there is certainly a fixed interest problem, including where the extension is made after the original loan. Nevertheless, the acquisition of the land itself can stand because a transaction performed through a prohibition is legally effective. It follows that this rule is confined to land or other property actually acquired through the prohibited consideration. For movables, where the parties have only given money and the sale is not yet complete, there should be no "Who exacted payment" curse in such a case. Rama rules that where a price was fixed through a prohibited transaction, such as before the market price was established, one does not impose "Who exacted payment." All the more here, where the consideration itself constitutes a Torah prohibition of interest. I therefore question Rama's application of the curse in this case.
