Reuven gave Shimon one hundred gold coins as earnest money toward ten measures of wool at fifty gold coins per measure. When the wool became due, Shimon delivered ten measures for five hundred gold coins and Reuven paid the full five hundred without deducting the original hundred, saying that the first hundred should remain as earnest money for potash to be produced the following summer. When the potash season came, Shimon sold it to another at a higher price. Reuven claimed the profit, arguing that Shimon had sold merchandise already belonging to him. Chatam Sofer rules that Reuven had no complete acquisition. If the original hundred was applied to the wool, then one hundred of the later payment became the cash advance for potash, and acquisition by money creates only Mi Shepara, not a full acquisition. If the original hundred became a debt and was then applied as the advance on the potash, most poskim hold that a debt effects no acquisition at all, while Rambam would impose only Mi Shepara because this debt did not arise from a prior sale. Chatam Sofer then considers whether, if Shimon nevertheless wishes to honor his commitment and treat the potash as Reuven's, payment of the profit would involve ribbit. He concludes that even if the hundred became a debt and according to most poskim did not effect even Mi Shepara, Shimon may give Reuven the profit corresponding to five hundred gold coins. One may rely on the authorities who hold that having available money is equivalent, for these purposes, to having the commodity itself. Since Shimon had at least five hundred in cash when the price was fixed, and the market price of potash had presumably already been established, there is room to rely on the Nimukei Yosef in the name of Rashba, Rokeach, and Rav Hai Gaon that where the seller has the means to obtain the commodity, a purchase against an existing debt can be valid without concern for even rabbinic ribbit. Profit beyond the amount corresponding to five hundred depends upon whether Shimon then actually had additional funds with which he could have bought and set aside more potash.
