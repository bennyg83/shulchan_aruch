He is believed to say. The same applies to an arbitrator, who is not believed only while the parties still stand before him. The Sema cites a case in which a person gave his fellow a promissory note to collect. A dispute arose between them, they compromised, and mutual releases were written, stating that the release did not include the promissory note, which was to "remain in force as it is today." It was later discovered that before the releases were issued, the transferor had already collected part of the debts. He then claimed that the subsequent release exempted him from returning what he had collected. Although the release excluded the promissory note, it said that it would remain "as it is today," and on that day some money had already been collected. The arbitrator stated that this had indeed been his intent in the compromise. The ruling was that the transferor must return what he had collected, because at the time of the releases it was not known that he had collected anything, and indeed he was presumed not to have collected. The arbitrator's later statement does not help, since he did not state this while the litigants were still before him, just as with a judge. A judge is believed while the parties remain before him even where the opposing litigant has a migo that he could have denied ever accepting him as judge. The Rosh, in Responsa, rule 56, section 6, explains that the judges pay greater attention to the matter than ordinary listeners, because judges scrutinize the case more carefully than witnesses. Therefore, the judges are believed more than outside witnesses. If one judge and one other witness support one version while two witnesses contradict them, however, the judge and the additional witness have no superior credibility. Although their combination prevents a litigant from simply contradicting them, there is no reason to regard them as more reliable than other witnesses, because the witness who joins the judge merely heard the matter as an observer.
