They have withdrawn. The Sema cites the Rashba concerning a person who gave another promissory notes and the recipient swore that he would not collect any of those debts for himself. A dispute later arose between them, and they accepted a single arbitrator whose compromise until a specified date would be binding. The arbitrator directed them to grant mutual releases, except that the promissory notes were to remain in force "as they are today." It was later discovered that before the releases were given, the original giver had collected part of those debts. He argued that the releases protected him from returning what he collected, and that although they excepted the promissory notes, the phrase "as they are today" referred to the status after he had already collected the money. The arbitrator also testified that this had been his intent. The Rashba ruled that the giver must return what he collected, because at the time the releases were issued no one knew that he had collected anything. On the contrary, he was presumed not to have done so because of his oath. The arbitrator's testimony cannot help once the litigants are no longer standing before him.
Shuda. For the nature and meaning of shuda d'dayanei, see the Sema and Shach.
Credibility. Even while the parties are still before the judge, he is not believed merely to state a conclusion. He must clarify and say, "You argued such-and-such and you argued such-and-such; according to your claims, you are entitled and you are liable." Sema.
Contradicts. This implies that if the judge's account contradicts the parties' original claims, he is not believed to say that this is what they argued, even while they remain before him. The judge is believed only to state whom he ruled entitled or liable, namely matters dependent upon legal judgment. Shach.
Two. Two judges are believed because they speak as witnesses. Thus the Sema. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, siman 116, rules that arbitrators who were selected to judge and compromise according to their discretion are likewise believed to explain their own words. He further writes that if a compromise document contains language capable of two interpretations, the arbitrators may explain which interpretation they intended even if the alternative reading is linguistically more natural, and the party in possession cannot say, "I rely on the other interpretation." If, however, the interpretation they offer is strained, the matter requires study. See also there, siman 119. Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 120, agrees with these rulings. Thus the Shach.
Who contradict. Meaning witnesses who say that they heard these judges issue a different ruling. It is possible that the witnesses misunderstood what they heard, so the judges themselves are believed over them. For this reason, it appears that specifically two judges are more credible than two ordinary witnesses. But one witness together with one judge is not more credible than two ordinary witnesses. Although Darkei Moshe writes that the judges are believed, he means against the litigant, even in a matter of shuda after they have withdrawn, not that they are superior to ordinary witnesses. Sema.
