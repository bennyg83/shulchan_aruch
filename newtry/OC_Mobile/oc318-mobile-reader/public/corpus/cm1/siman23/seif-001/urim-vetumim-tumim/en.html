He has the written judgment in his possession. See Urim for what I cited from Shach. The case is a written ruling that does not name either litigant but merely states, for example, "such-and-such field belongs to you." One party possesses the document, while the other says: "The ruling was issued for me. I lost it, and you found it." Shach rules that if the possessor of the written judgment does not independently hold the disputed property, the judge's later identification of the document's intended recipient has no greater force than a single witness. I am surprised. Why should the person holding the written judgment not prevail? Everyone agrees the document was written while the judges still had authority over the case. The other litigant's only explanation is that it originally belonged to him, was lost, and was found by the present possessor. But we generally do not suspect accidental loss, even where many people could have lost a document, as explained at the end of Perek Get Pashut. The special concern where two people bear the same name has other reasons. A promissory note can be written for a borrower even when the lender is absent, or, as Rashbam explains, perhaps the debtor repaid and the lender returned the note. See below, siman 49, where I discuss this at length. But mere loss is not ordinarily presumed. Therefore, where the written judgment is found in a person's possession, the possession itself identifies him as the intended party. Why else is the document in his hands? Since we do not normally fear that it fell from someone else, he should prevail by virtue of holding the written judgment. This appears clear.
Through shuda d'dayanei. Rashi explains that the judge decides according to his best assessment of which party the underlying person intended to favor. For example, where two gift documents were issued on the same day, the judge evaluates toward which recipient the donor's intent was more likely directed and awards the property accordingly. Tosafot ask: if so, why does the Gemara say that once one judge exercised shuda he cannot later return and judge the matter again? The same assessment of the donor's intent that he had previously should still be available to him. Tosafot therefore explain that shuda d'dayanei gives the judge discretionary power to award the property to whichever party he chooses, without attempting to reconstruct the donor's intent. Because it is pure discretion rather than a reproducible legal inference, once he exercised it he cannot later rejudge the same matter. Shach uses this against Bach and Shiltei Gibborim, who say that the same no-reconsideration rule applies where the parties accepted a judge to decide according to his own assessment and judgment. According to Tosafot, Shach argues, the opposite is explicit. Where the decision rests on reasoned assessment, the judge can return to it because the reasoning itself remains available. Only pure shuda, which is not based on such reasoning, cannot be redone. I do not understand the objection. That is Tosafot's explanation, but Rashi necessarily disagrees. According to Rashi, even shuda itself is based on the judge's assessment of intent, yet the ruling still cannot simply be revisited. Below, siman 240, seif 3, Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule without qualification in accordance with Rashi's understanding of shuda. See what I write there. Once we accept Rashi's model, a judge's reasoned discretionary assessment can indeed acquire finality in a way that prevents him from reopening the matter merely because he might reassess the same circumstances. Therefore the ruling of Shiltei Gibborim and Bach is sound. There is no meaningful distinction between that accepted discretionary judgment and Rashi's shuda d'dayanei.
Provided that he does not contradict his original claims. Shach, seif katan 5, writes that even while the litigants are still before the judge, if they contradict the judge and say, "That is not what we argued," the judge is not believed to say that these were in fact their arguments. This does not appear consistent with Ritva. Ritva asks why the Gemara answers by establishing the case as one of shuda d'dayanei rather than simply saying that the parties are disputing what claims were made. He answers that ordinarily there is a written record of the litigants' claims. According to Shach, however, the Gemara could not have established the case as a dispute about the claims, because the Mishnah says that while the litigants are still standing before the judge, the judge is believed to say, "I ruled in favor of this one." If the litigants' denial of the claims always defeats the judge's statement, then the Mishnah's distinction between their still being before him and no longer being before him would not hold in such a case. Rather, it is clear that while the parties are still before the judge, the judge has full credibility both regarding what claims were presented and regarding the judgment he issued. This is unlike Shach's ruling.
But two judges are believed, and even where there is no shuda, he cannot say that they must return and adjudicate again, since two are believed that this is how they ruled. Mahari ben Lev writes this. Shach questions it in seif katan 8. He argues that wherever the matter can still be clarified, why should we rely on their own statement rather than require them to adjudicate again? But according to the textual reading Shach himself cites in Raavyah, there is support for Mahari ben Lev. Raavyah explains that when two judges adjudicated, they nevertheless write a formal judgment, even though the two themselves are believed as to what they ruled, because where a single judge adjudicated a written judgment is needed. If Mahari ben Lev were wrong, Raavyah could have given a much simpler reason. Most civil disputes are not matters of shuda d'dayanei. Without a written judgment, the judges would be required to return and adjudicate them again, creating endless repetition of litigation. The need for writing would then be obvious. The fact that Raavyah does not say this suggests that two judges' statement that "this is what we ruled" is itself sufficient, without requiring a renewed adjudication. The same appears from Rosh, responsum klal 6, siman 15. That case did not involve shuda, yet Rosh rules that two judges are always believed to say, "This is how we ruled," and he does not require them to retry the matter. The reason is clear. Two witnesses are believed by Torah law. Once two judges say what judgment they rendered, why burden them with a new adjudication? What greater clarification would be produced by repeating the case? Two are like one hundred. A single judge is different. His credibility about his own prior ruling is not ordinary Torah-law testimony. It is a special procedural credibility because the parties accepted him. Where the matter can still be clarified by having him adjudicate again, we therefore prefer renewed adjudication rather than relying on that special credibility. With two judges, however, their testimony-level credibility is itself sufficient. Mahari ben Lev's ruling is therefore correct.
