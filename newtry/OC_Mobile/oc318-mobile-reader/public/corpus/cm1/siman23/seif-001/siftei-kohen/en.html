They have withdrawn from before him, etc. The qualification, "if he does not have the written ruling in his possession," implies that if he does have the written ruling, the judge is believed even after the litigants have left him. The Mechaber therefore understands the Gemara's question, "Let us see who holds the written decision," as referring to the latter clause. Ritva, however, writes in chapter Asarah Yuchasin that the question cannot refer to the latter clause. Since the judge himself is no longer believed, why should the fact that one party holds the written ruling make the judge more credible? What proof does it provide unless the document itself is independently relied upon? Rather, the Gemara asks regarding the first clause: Why do we need the judge's personal credibility at all? Let us see who holds the written decision and rely upon it. Nevertheless, it appears that Ritva does not disagree with the Tur and Mechaber as to the law. The Tur and Mechaber discuss a case where both parties agree that the written ruling was prepared immediately, before they withdrew from the judge, but dispute what was actually ruled. Ritva discusses a case where one litigant claims that the written ruling was given only after they had already withdrawn. One should not ask why Ritva chose that interpretation. He was troubled by the Gemara's need to answer that the document had been torn. Why not answer simply in this manner?
If he does not have a written ruling in his possession. If he does have the written ruling, the decision is stated in it and the judge's personal credibility is unnecessary. The Bach explains this way. He further suggests another interpretation. Suppose the written ruling merely states, "Such and such land is yours," without identifying the successful party by name. One litigant holds the ruling, while the other claims, "I was the one who prevailed. The ruling fell from me and you found it, or you took it from me," or a similar claim. The judge is believed to say that the party holding the ruling was the one who prevailed, and the holder need not even take a heset oath, like the incident involving two men named Tuviah. If, however, no ruling is in his possession, or the judge says that the party who does not hold the ruling was the successful party, the judge is not believed. His credibility extends only to assisting the party who holds the ruling, thereby exempting him from an oath. These are the Bach's words. We must understand him as discussing a case in which neither party is presently in possession of the disputed property. If the party holding the ruling were also in possession of the property, then even without the written ruling the judge would be believed at least to exempt him from an oath, for the judge is no worse than an ordinary single witness. Conversely, if the opposing party were in possession, the judge would not be believed to extract from him merely because the other party holds the written ruling, since the ruling itself does not prove which party prevailed and the possessor claims that it fell from him. This resembles the rule below, siman 49, concerning two people with the identical name Yosef ben Shimon, where neither can collect from the other with a document bearing that name. Rather, the Bach must be discussing a case like the property awarded to Tuviah, where neither party was in possession. Even so, the Bach's ruling still requires study. Since the written ruling itself proves nothing, why should the judge be more credible than an ordinary single witness so as to cause a loss to the other party, when the property is presumptively held for both? Examine this carefully.
And specifically in a case of shuda d'dayanei. The Bach writes in the name of Shiltei Giborim, chapter Asarah Yuchasin, that the rule is not limited literally to shuda d'dayanei. The same applies where the parties accepted the judge to decide according to his assessment. He may not adjudicate the matter a second time, lest his assessment change after the successful litigant has already acquired his right under the first judgment. The Bach adds that this is also clear from Responsa Rivash, siman 404, which shows that the rule applies not only to shuda d'dayanei but to other comparable judgments. The Bach's inference from Rivash is incorrect. Although Rivash there discusses other comparable judgments, the case involved two judges appointed for a fixed period, and Rivash ruled that two judges are always believed because they are no worse than witnesses. If he did not say that they should redetermine the case, that is because their term had expired. All the more so according to what I write below in the name of Responsa Mahari ben Lev, that with two judges the concept of returning to redetermine the case may not apply at all. Shiltei Giborim there does appear to say what the Bach attributes to him. That is apparently because he follows Rashi and those who agree with him, discussed below in siman 240, that shuda d'dayanei means awarding the property to the party whom the judge's reasoning favors. Tosafot and Mordechai in chapter Asarah Yuchasin, Tosafot and the Rosh in chapter Chezkas HaBatim, and Tosafot, Ritva, and Ran in chapter HaKotev make clear that this is unique to shuda d'dayanei. They challenge Rashi's interpretation by asking: If the decision rests on the judge's reasoned preference, why can he not simply redetermine the case? Presumably his original reasoning will not change. See there. Thus, the Bach's categorical ruling that in comparable ordinary judgments the judge also cannot redetermine the case has many authorities against it, and the practical law therefore requires study.
Credibility does not apply to him. This is true even while the litigants are still standing before him. So writes the Sema, and this is also implied by the authorities. Ritva there and Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, siman 116, state it explicitly. The matter is straightforward.
Provided that he does not contradict their original claims. This implies that if the judge's account contradicts the litigants' original claims, he is not believed to say, "This is what they claimed," even while they are still before him. A single judge is given special credibility only to say, "I ruled in favor of this one and against that one," a matter that depends upon the judgment itself, but not to establish what claims the litigants originally made.
With one judge. For example, where an expert judge sat alone, or where the parties accepted him upon themselves. So write the Tur, Ritva, and the other authorities. Even according to what I wrote above in siman 22, seif katan 2, in the name of the Rosh and Tur, that accepting one person as though he were three is ineffective, one can say that here the parties made a kinyan accepting him, as explained there. Responsa Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition, siman 533, records a case where Reuven said, "I never litigated before that judge and never submitted anything to him," while an administrator answered, "You did appear before him and the matter was submitted to him." Maharam responded that the Gemara in chapter Asarah Yuchasin states that a judge is believed to say, "I ruled in favor of this one and against that one," when the litigants stand before him. In that case, the rabbi was still in the city and both litigants were likewise present, so, figuratively, "here is the camel, here is the measure, and here is the load," and they can ask the rabbi. Maharam treated this like litigants still standing before him. Even if one says that many days may have passed since the ruling and forgetfulness is therefore possible, the rabbi can redetermine the case because they had accepted him. If Reuven says, "I never accepted him," Maharam says the judge is believed about that as well, because the Gemara asks why he cannot simply redetermine the case and answers that it was shuda d'dayanei. Why did the Gemara not answer that one litigant now says, "I do not wish to be judged by him and never accepted him"? This shows, Maharam argues, that the judge is believed regarding the acceptance itself. These words are astonishing in my eyes. If so, no one could live securely, for any individual could extract money by claiming, "He accepted me as judge and this is what I ruled." The Gemara did not give that answer because the plain meaning of the baraita is that both litigants agree they accepted the judge, as it says that the judge is believed to say, "I ruled in favor of this one and against that one." Their dispute concerns the ruling, not his authority. Moreover, if they disputed whether they ever accepted him, it is obvious that even while they stand before him the judge is not believed, since his special credibility relates only to the judgment itself. The Gemara therefore could not have answered in the manner Maharam proposes, because then the rule that the judge is believed while they stand before him would itself be false. See what I wrote above, siman 22, seif katan 13, where you will find proofs for this. Nor does it appear correct to me that because the rabbi and litigants remain in the same city this is like their standing before him. Once they have left the proceeding and withdrawn from him, he is no longer believed merely because all are in the same city. Maharam himself writes this only conditionally, which indicates that he was not certain of it. In my view, there is no room for that possibility at all. The rule is as I have stated.
But two judges are always believed. The Bach writes below, in siman 39, that Raavyah, cited by Mordechai in chapter 1 of Bava Metzia, gives as the reason that a debtor cannot say "I paid" against a written ruling that the ruling was written only because the judges are not otherwise believed to say which litigant prevailed. From this the Bach infers that even two judges are not believed. He also argues from Rashi, who explains that while the litigants stand before the judge he remembers because the matter rests upon him, whereas after they leave he is not believed because it is no longer incumbent upon him to remember. According to this reasoning, the same problem should apply to two judges. Despite all this, the Bach's conclusion is incorrect. It is inconceivable that two judges should not be believed. Are they worse than two witnesses? Rashi's explanation concerns one judge, whom the Sages specially believed as long as the litigants stand before him because he is attentive and remembers. Once they withdraw, he is no longer expected to remember, and the Sages did not treat him like two witnesses, so he remains only a single witness. But if even one additional witness joins him, they are certainly believed, all the more so two judges. Indeed, Ritva follows Rashi's explanation and nevertheless writes that two judges are always believed. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, siman 119, likewise says so. Ir Shushan also gives the reason that the matter is incumbent upon the judge and he therefore remembers, yet still rules that two judges are always believed. As for Raavyah's statement that a written ruling serves as proof because the judges are otherwise not believed, Mahari Lev ben Chaviv already addressed this in Responsa, siman 109. He writes that the manuscript copies of Mordechai have the statement in the singular: Raavyah writes that the same applies if one judge wrote a ruling and obligated the party, because "the judge" is not believed after the litigants are no longer before him. The plural in printed copies is a scribal error, or refers generically to judges. Alternatively, even when there are two or three judges, the written ruling itself does not become independent proof for the claimant merely because of their number, just as when there is one judge the document was not written because it independently proves the right. One can add that the written ruling may be useful so that the judges need not redetermine the case, according to what I write in the following seif katan that where redetermination is possible even two judges are not specially believed. This also resolves the difficulty that Raavyah speaks of an ordinary ruling and not shuda d'dayanei. Subsequently, Sefer Raavyah came into my possession, and I saw that he writes explicitly that two judges are believed, yet the written ruling itself still does not serve as proof, because just as it is not independent proof when there is one judge, so too when there are two. Raavyah, siman 1020, writes: When the litigants no longer stand before him, a single judge is not believed. But two or three judges are believed like ordinary witnesses. The Tosefta in Kiddushin states that when the litigants do not stand before him, "he is like any other person," meaning like an ordinary witness. At times an expert judge sits alone and is not believed afterward, so he immediately writes a ruling. This directly contradicts the Bach. Rivash, simanim 300 and 404, also rules plainly like the Rosh. The same appears in Responsa Mahari Lev ben Chaviv there; Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, simanim 116 and 119; Responsa Rashdam, siman 9; Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, part 2, siman 83; Responsa Maharam Alshich? no, Responsa Mahari Sasson, simanim 71 and 120; and many other later authorities. Two judges are always believed, and no authority disputes this. This is the settled law in Israel, contrary to the Bach. See Responsa Maharit, siman 141.
They are always believed. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, siman 116, was asked about arbitrators who had issued a compromise. He writes that one must consider whether the rule that two judges are always believed applies only to shuda d'dayanei, whereas in an ordinary judgment they should redetermine the case, or whether only a single judge must redetermine it, while two need not. He concludes that the latter is more reasonable. This also appears from the Tur, who writes, "All this applies specifically to one judge," apparently referring to the entire preceding discussion, including the qualification that the case was shuda d'dayanei. Therefore, Mahari ben Lev concludes, arbitrators appointed to decide and compromise according to what appeared proper and equitable in their eyes are believed to explain what they intended. His proof is not fully compelling. One can say that two are believed only where the matter cannot be redetermined. Where it can be redetermined, there is no need for special credibility, because the truth of the matter can be established directly. The Tur's phrase, "All this, etc.," may refer to the rules that apply after the litigants withdrew and even where the later account contradicts the original claims. Nevertheless, Mahari ben Lev's practical ruling about arbitrators is correct. Since they were appointed to decide and compromise according to their own assessment, the matter cannot simply be reconstructed objectively, and I do not know why he made this depend on the broader question. He further writes that if a settlement document contains language capable of two interpretations, the arbitrators may explain, "This is what we intended," even when the other interpretation is linguistically more natural, and the possessor cannot say, "I maintain the law follows the other interpretation." If the interpretation they now offer is linguistically strained, however, the matter requires study. See further there, siman 119. Mahari Sasson, siman 120, agrees with all these rulings.
Even if there are two witnesses who contradict them, etc. The witnesses testify that they heard these judges render the ruling differently. It is possible that the witnesses misunderstood what they heard, and therefore the judges themselves are believed more. It appears that for this reason specifically two judges are believed over two outside witnesses. One outside witness together with one judge is not more credible than two ordinary witnesses. Although the Rama writes in Darkei Moshe, based on Responsa of the Rosh, rule 6, siman 14, cited by Beit Yosef, that they are believed, this means only that they are believed against the litigant even in shuda d'dayanei after the litigants have withdrawn. It does not mean that they have greater credibility than two witnesses. So writes the Sema.
Even during the appointed period. The period merely gives the arbitrator time to issue whatever ruling he will issue during that period. Once he has ruled, written, and delivered the decision, he no longer has authority to add to or subtract from it. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, siman 119, writes that even a single arbitrator may perhaps be believed to say, "This is what I intended by the language written in the settlement document." Since the point was not expressly stated, it is as though he never decided that point, and because he has not yet decided it, the authority to do so remains in his hands. According to this, the Rama and Rashba must be discussing a written settlement whose language cannot bear the proposed addition or subtraction. For example, Responsa Rashba discusses a settlement stating that all claims were waived except for gift documents, which would remain in force. The arbitrator later said that he intended that what one party had already received under those gift documents should also be waived. See there. The same applies to comparable cases. Mahari ben Lev's case differs because the arbitrator's later statement does not add to or subtract from the settlement, but merely explains its existing language. Examine this carefully.
