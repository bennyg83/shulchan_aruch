He is believed to say, etc. Darkei Moshe cites from Beit Yosef in siman 12 a Responsa Rashba involving Reuven and Shimon, where one transferred promissory notes to the other and swore not to collect any portion of those debts for himself. Later disputes arose and they appointed an arbitrator whose settlement would bind them through a set date. The arbitrator directed mutual releases except that the stated debt documents should remain in force 'as they are today.' It was later discovered that the transferor had already collected part of the debts before the releases. He argued that the later releases exempted him, since 'as they are today' referred to a day on which part had already been collected. The arbitrator said this indeed had been his intention. Rashba ruled that he must return what he collected, because at the time of the releases no one knew of the collection and, after his oath, the presumption was that he had not collected. The arbitrator's later explanation was ineffective because he did not state it while the parties were still before him, like the law of a judge who recalls his ruling only while the litigants remain before him.
In a matter of shuda d'dayanei. Some cases cannot be resolved from the parties' claims by fixed law, and the decision is entrusted to the judge's evaluation of which recipient was likely intended by the giver. Others explain that the judge may award it to either side at his discretion. See the end of siman 138 and siman 253. If after such a decision the parties disagree, each saying the judge awarded it to him, and they do not want the matter reheard because the judge's discretionary inclination might change, the judge's own recollection controls while they are still before him, since the matter remains fresh in his mind. Once they have left, the parties must compromise or divide, unless one is in possession, in which case the burden of proof rests on the other.
Credibility does not apply to him. In a case governed by ordinary law, the judge is not simply 'believed' to announce an unexplained result after the parties leave. Rather, he reconstructs the claims and shows why one was entitled and the other liable, or the parties plead again consistently with their original claims. Even while they are before him, he should clarify the basis of the ruling rather than rely on personal credibility.
Provided he does not contradict the original claims. Meaning the parties must not now disagree as to what they originally pleaded. If they do, the judge cannot simply reconstruct and restate the claims. See the Perishah.
With one judge. Such as a single expert, or where they accepted one judge. So writes the Tur.
But two judges are believed forever. Two speak as witnesses and are therefore believed as testimony.
Even if there are two witnesses who contradict them. Those witnesses merely testify to what they heard the judges say, and may have misunderstood. The judges themselves are therefore believed more concerning their own ruling. It appears that this preference applies specifically to two judges against two outside witnesses. One judge together with one witness would not necessarily be stronger than two ordinary witnesses.
Two witnesses. The same applies to more than two, because two witnesses carry the same testimonial force as one hundred.
