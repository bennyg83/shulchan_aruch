After they left his presence. There is a difficulty from Ketubot, where a woman loses her ketubah for violating Torah law, for example by feeding her husband untithed food or having relations while a niddah. The Gemara establishes the case where she says, "So and so, the sage, ruled this blood pure," and the husband asks the sage and discovers that she lied. Ran cites Rashba that the sage alone cannot cause her to lose the ketubah, because he is only one witness. She loses it only if she gave a particular date and witnesses show that the sage could not have issued the ruling then. Ramban, however, writes that if one person reports information in another's name and the original source comes and denies it, the reporter is not believed. He derives this very rule from the Ketubot passage, and states that the original source is believed over the one quoting him, even in monetary matters. Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel indicate that Rambam and Rosh follow Ramban. If so, why here, once the litigants have left, is the judge not believed? One party says, "So and so, the judge, awarded me the land," and the judge himself contradicts him. Why is that different from, "So and so, the sage, ruled this blood pure"? According to Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah siman 186, who distinguishes between prohibitions and money, the matter is resolved. But according to Rambam and Semag, who appear to apply the original source's superiority even to money, difficulty remains. It appears that the judge is believed only to deny the statement attributed to him. If a litigant says, "The judge awarded me the land," the judge may say, "I did not award it to you." But the judge is not thereby believed affirmatively to say, "I awarded it to your opponent." On that latter point the opposing litigant can deny him. We divide the judge's statement: he is believed to deny the words attributed to him, but not to establish a new award in favor of the other party. Accordingly, once they left the judge, if the matter was one of shuda deDayanei, the first party cannot say, "He already awarded me the field," because the judge can deny the statement made in his name. He can only say, "I know that he did not award it to my opponent." If the other litigant now wishes the judge to exercise shuda again, the law returns to shuda. The Sema's statement that they compromise or divide therefore applies where the other litigant does not want a renewed shuda because he fears the judge's discretion may change, as Tosafot writes. This also resolves Ritva's question on the Gemara's answer, "Let us see who holds the written ruling." If, after they left, the judge is no longer believed, what proof is there from a written ruling, since perhaps he wrote it only now? Ritva answers that the Gemara refers to the first clause while they are still before him. The Shulchan Aruch, however, implies that a written ruling helps even after they left. According to what I wrote, this is clear. If one party holds the written ruling, the other cannot say, "The judge already awarded it to me," because the judge is believed to deny that attribution. If he says only, "The judge did not award it to my fellow," that does not help because the fellow now has the written ruling and, if he wishes, can demand renewed shuda. Thus the written ruling remains decisive even after they left. This also fits Rambam and Rif, who imply that whenever a written ruling exists it is effective.
If it is not in his possession. The Shach, discussing Bach, writes that where there is a written ruling whose wording is not explicit, such as, "the field of so and so is yours," Bach holds that if the judge supports the person holding the ruling, the judge is believed at least to exempt him from an oath. The Shach disagrees. Since neither party is in physical possession of the land, the judge cannot add anything not evident from the ruling, and the other party can say, "It fell from me," like the case of two men with the same name in siman 49. This is difficult because we generally do not suspect a document fell, as shown in Bava Batra by the episode of Rav Huna's household. The concern in the case of two men with the same name applies specifically to an acquisition document because a deed may be written for the borrower without the lender present. Perhaps the Shach means what he wrote in the preceding seif katan: the written ruling helps after the parties left only where both admit that it had been written before they left. Since its effect then depends upon the opponent's admission that it predated their departure, when the opponent says, "Yes, it was written then, but it is mine and fell from me," he is believed through a migo. This resembles siman 50. According to what I wrote in the preceding seif katan, however, the written ruling can help even without such an admission, and then no such migo exists.
Two are believed. The Shach concludes that in an ordinary case, not involving shuda, where the law is simply retried, there is no special credibility even for two judges. This resolves Maharsha's question in Sanhedrin. Tosafot writes that when Shmuel says two who judged have a valid judgment, the same would apply to one, and he mentions two only to teach that even two are called an improperly bold court. Maharsha asks why Tosafot did not say that two are mentioned so that there will be witnesses to what occurred, just as the Gemara says concerning compromise by two. According to Tosafot in Perek HaKotev, shuda is effective only before an expert judge. Thus there is no practical evidentiary role for the two. If they testify that the parties accepted them, the case is not one where they judged by compulsion. If they testify to the ruling in an ordinary non-shuda case, there is no need for their special credibility because the law is simply retried. If they testify to the parties' original claims so that the claims cannot later be changed, that too does not help. Shmuel says only that two judges can issue a judgment after the fact, but he agrees that formal admissions require three. Just as an admission before two does not become a binding admission, claims made before two can likewise be changed so long as there were not three, as explained in siman 3. Tosafot therefore had to explain that Shmuel mentions two merely to teach that even a two judge court is called bold.
