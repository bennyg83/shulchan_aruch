Seif 1. “When does this apply? With a single judge, for example an expert or one accepted by the litigants.” Note: The Shach cites this from the Tur and the authorities. The implication is that only where there was initially one judge is he believed, so long as the litigants have not left his presence. But if there were originally three judges, or two, and one or more later departed, leaving only one judge while the litigants are still before him, that remaining judge is not believed to say which litigant prevailed. Otherwise the authorities could simply have explained the case as one in which the other two judges left and one remained. Why did they specifically give the examples of an expert judge or one accepted by the litigants? The reason appears as follows. If the remaining judge’s credibility while the parties are still before him rests on the principle that they entrusted the matter to him, then even where he is an expert judge without express acceptance, one can say that since the Torah itself granted him authority to decide the law, his judicial credibility is complete and not partial. So long as they remain before him he retains the status of judge and is believed concerning the ruling he issued. But where there were other judges together with him, he never possessed sole authority over the substance of the judgment, and therefore he is not believed alone afterward to say which party prevailed. Even according to the explanation that this credibility is only a rabbinic enactment, the Sages may have established it only for the common situation of a matter heard initially by one judge. Where a panel of three had already sat, the case in which two immediately depart while the litigants are still present is uncommon, and the Sages did not create such a special credibility rule. According to Rashi’s explanation that the judge is believed while the litigants are still before him because he has the matter in mind and remembers it, there is also a distinction. If he alone decided the case, he naturally keeps the ruling in mind while the parties remain before him. If he sat with two others, he need not personally retain the details in that same way. Moreover, what difference is there between the litigants departing and the other judges departing? Once two judges leave, the original judicial setting has been dissolved. That should be treated like the departure of the litigants, after which the remaining judge is no longer specially believed. This appears correct and clear.
