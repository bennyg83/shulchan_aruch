If he does not have the written ruling. This implies that where he holds a written ruling he is believed. Shach, citing Ritva to Kiddushin, limits this to a ruling written while the parties had not yet withdrawn from the judge. If it was written only later, why should the judge's written statement be stronger than his oral statement after the proceeding, when he is no longer believed? If the parties accepted the judge without limiting the time, and both the judge and the litigant holding the ruling say that it was delivered before the parties withdrew, while the opponent denies this, the judge should be believed. He has the power even now to say, "I had not yet ruled; I now rule in this manner," because his authority continues until he finishes the case. This is not a migo against two witnesses, especially where the matter concerns the defendant's property. See Rashba to Kiddushin and my rules of migo. If the ruling is written anonymously, such as "the specified land belongs to you," and Reuven holds it while Shimon claims it was written for him and was lost, Bach and Shach conclude that if the judge supports the holder and the holder is already in possession, the judge can function at least as a supporting single witness. If the holder is not in possession, or the opponent possesses the property, the judge is no better than one witness and cannot extract. In Tumim I conclude that if the ruling was written before the parties withdrew, the person who possesses the written ruling is believed. We do not ordinarily suspect that a judicial ruling fell and was found by the wrong person.
Only in shuda, etc. The same applies where the parties accepted the judge to rule according to his assessment and discretion. He cannot later reconsider after the parties have left, because his assessment may change. Bach, citing Shiltei Gibborim. See Tumim.
A matter dependent on the claims does not involve special credibility. When the parties are no longer before him, it is as though they stood before him and the judge restated their claims and then ruled. The parties have no power to remove him from the case. Likewise, while they are still before him, the judge's personal credibility adds nothing where the law is determined objectively from the claims and demands. Torah law does not change from one time to another. There is therefore no need for a special doctrine of credibility. Sema and Shach.

With one judge. This means either an expert judge or a nonexpert whom the parties accepted. According to Sema no kinyan is needed. According to Shach, because this may involve two defects, a kinyan can be required. Knesset HaGedolah asks how Rosh can speak of a single nonexpert accepted by the parties in a case that necessarily concerns *shuda deDayanei*, because otherwise the Gemara's question, "Let him judge again," would apply. Rosh follows Rabbeinu Chananel that *shuda* is entrusted only to a single expert, so how can a nonexpert exercise it? There is no difficulty. The case can be one where the parties accepted him specifically to rule according to his own assessment, as Shiltei Gibborim stated. Alternatively, they explicitly accepted him to decide through *shuda*. Once they accepted him for that purpose, even a nonexpert can do so. If the litigant denies having accepted the judge at all while the judge says he did, Shach correctly rules that the litigant is believed. This is already explicit above in siman 22:1 from a responsum of Rosh. The judge is not believed merely because he is the judge.

Even if there are witnesses, etc. Rosh explains that the judges are more reliable concerning what occurred in the proceeding because the matter is their direct judicial responsibility. They carefully attend to the precise claims and rulings, whereas bystanders who merely heard the discussion may err. Sema cites this explanation. If one judge and one ordinary witness say one thing while two other witnesses contradict them, the judge and supporting witness have no superior credibility. Although together they prevent a litigant from contradicting them as a single judge, the second person heard the matter only as an ordinary listener. There is therefore no reason to prefer them over the two opposing witnesses. The special credibility lies in the judges' concentrated attention, not in the bare fact of having one judge among the witnesses. Sema.
