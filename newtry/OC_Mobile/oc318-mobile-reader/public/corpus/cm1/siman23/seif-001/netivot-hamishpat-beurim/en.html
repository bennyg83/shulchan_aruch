After they have departed from before him. Ketzot HaChoshen asks that since one litigant says, "So and so, the sage, awarded me the land," while the sage denies it, the litigant should not be believed, just as where one says, "So and so, the sage, declared my stain pure," and the matter is found false. He answers that, nevertheless, the judge is believed at least to say that he did not award it to the other party, and from this he derives a novel ruling that the judge may again exercise judicial discretion and make a new discretionary award. This is incorrect. In the case of "So and so, the sage, declared the stain pure" or "corrected the produce heap," the woman's credibility is only by virtue of testimony, and the sage too is believed by virtue of testimony, because one witness is believed in matters of prohibition. The authorities therefore correctly state the general rule that whenever a person's credibility is based upon testimony, and he reports testimony in another person's name, if that other person contradicts him his testimony is nullified. The sage's own testimony, however, is not thereby nullified, and his credibility remains, because one witness is believed in matters of prohibition. Even though the issue may later affect the ketubah, which is monetary, the principal testimony concerns a matter of prohibition. Here, by contrast, both parties agree that judgment has already been rendered and that the land belongs to one of them. The possessor who says, "He awarded it to me and the land is mine," is not believed as a witness, but because he is in possession. The sage cannot remove property from the possessor by his statement. Consequently, the possessor is believed. Likewise, where neither party is in possession and the rule would be that whoever is stronger prevails, the sage is not believed so as to disturb that legal posture, because a single witness has no credibility at all in monetary matters, while the litigant's claim is a valid claim sufficient for the rule of whoever is stronger to apply. Even in matters of prohibition, where the owner is believed because the matter is within his control, the Rama writes in a responsum that the sage is not believed against him. The judge also cannot conduct a new discretionary award, since both parties agree that the judge no longer has authority, having already completed his function, and the land already belongs exclusively to one of them. In any event, his comparison to the case of "So and so, the sage, declared it pure" is mistaken, as I wrote in my work Chavat Da'at, siman 185, see there. Even according to his own approach, his resolution of the Ritva's difficulty is unsatisfactory. The reason the judge is not believed is that he may be speaking from an uncertain recollection. If so, he is not believed when he says that he already awarded it to one party, and he would have to make a new discretionary award. What difference is there between his saying orally that he awarded it to this party, in which case, according to Ketzot HaChoshen himself, he must make a new discretionary award, and his having written the same thing in the written judgment?
He is not believed. If the judge and one litigant say that judgment has not yet been rendered and the judge now wishes to rule, while the other litigant says that the judge already ruled and awarded the property to him, it is evident from Rashba's novellae to Kiddushin 74 that the judge is believed. Rashba asks why the judge should not be believed to say that he awarded it to this party through a migo that he could have said, "I have not yet rendered judgment, and I now rule in this manner." He answers that in a case of judicial discretion, the second litigant also would not consent to have the judge reconsider the matter, because that litigant maintains that the judge already awarded it to him. In other cases as well, the judge is not believed through a migo that he had not yet ruled, when both litigants agree that judgment was already rendered. It follows expressly from Rashba that where only one litigant says the judge already awarded it to him, while the judge and the other litigant say judgment has not yet been rendered, the judge is believed, even after they have departed from before him, provided there are witnesses that they accepted him as judge. If there are no witnesses that they accepted him as judge and they have already departed, the litigant is believed through a migo that he could have said that they never accepted him as judge. The judge does not then have the status of two witnesses, because once the parties have departed he no longer has that judicial status.
If he does not have the written judgment in his possession. See Shach, who discusses a written ruling that merely states, "This land is yours," in which case the other party can say, "It fell from me." Tumim asks that we do not ordinarily suspect that a document was lost, just as with a document stating, "I borrowed from you." His intent plainly concerns a case where there is no migo, such as where witnesses testify that the judge gave this written ruling to one of the parties before they departed from him, but they do not remember to whom he gave it. Otherwise the possessor would be believed through a migo that he could have said the judge gave him the ruling only afterward, when the judge would no longer be believed, as Shach writes in seif katan 1. Where there is a migo, the claim that the document fell from the other party is believed, as explained in siman 50. Even where there is no migo, however, Shach's ruling appears correct. We do not suspect loss specifically with an evidentiary document, because Yevamot 117 says that if loss were a concern, the holder is especially careful with it. Since such a document itself constitutes proof, and when it is in his hand the other party cannot contradict it, he guards it carefully. A written judgment, however, is not itself proof when held by one party, because the opposing party can still contradict him and say that the judge gave it to him only after they had departed. If the opposing party admits the matter, he admits everything and no documentary proof is needed, so the holder is not especially careful with it. Likewise, if witnesses know that it was given before the parties departed from the judge, presumably they will know the entire ruling. The holder therefore does not guard it especially carefully, because we do not ordinarily suspect that witnesses know only part of the matter, as Rashba writes in his novellae to Kiddushin 74. For this reason we do suspect that such a written ruling may have fallen. What Urim writes, that the judge is believed to say that the written ruling reached the litigant before they departed through a migo that he could say, "I have not yet rendered judgment, and I now rule this way," is incorrect. If that were so, he should likewise be believed to say, "I awarded it to this party," through the same migo. Rather, this is not a migo when both litigants acknowledge that judgment has already been rendered, as Rashba writes there. In Shach there, seif katan 3, there is a scribal error. It should read: "or where he says, regarding the one who does not have the written judgment, I awarded it to him," and it must be speaking where neither party is in possession. In Shach, seif katan 7, there is another scribal error. Twice it reads "cannot," but it should read "can." In Shach, seif katan 9, there is a scribal error as well, and it should read that this is from Responsa Rosh, rule 56, responsum 6.
