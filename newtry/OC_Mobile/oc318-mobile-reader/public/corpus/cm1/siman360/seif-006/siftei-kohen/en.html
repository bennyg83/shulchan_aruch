Wool that he dyed. This means with a dye that cannot be removed, as the Sema writes based on the end of siman 398. He is referring to the gloss there that a reversible change does not effect acquisition. He could have derived this more directly from Rambam and the Tur and Mechaber here in seif 5, and in seif 7 they also write this explicitly concerning dyeing. There is not much proof from the end of siman 358, because Rabbeinu Yerucham holds that a reversible change does not acquire, yet he rules that even where the dye can be removed, it is considered a change. As a matter of law, however, Rabbeinu Yerucham's ruling does not appear correct. This is not because of the Bach's question, how he could distinguish between dyeing and whitening, because that is no difficulty. The Sages who disagree with Rabbi Shimon do hold that whitening is not a change while dyeing is a change even where it can be removed, as stated by the Gemara at the beginning of Perek HaGozel Kamma. This apparently is Rabbeinu Yerucham's reason for ruling like the Sages. In truth, however, that applies only according to the opinion that a reversible change effects acquisition. According to our ruling that a reversible change does not acquire, if the dye can be removed it is not considered a change. This is explicit in Tosafos at the end of Perek Merubah, Bava Kamma 65, and likewise in Raavan, page 89b.
Or he whitened it, etc. This is difficult, because at the beginning of Perek HaGozel the Gemara says that whitening is not considered a change, just as regarding the first fleece. The Mechaber himself rules this way in Yoreh Deah, siman 333, seif 3. Rambam is not difficult, because he writes, "he beat it and whitened it," while in chapter 10 of Hilchot Bikkurim regarding the first fleece he writes only "he whitened it." It appears that he holds that according to Rabbi Chiya, who says that beating is like whitening with sulfur, the meaning is that after beating it he whitens it. The Rosh, Tur, and Maharshal likewise rule that specifically whitening it with sulfur constitutes a change. But the Mechaber's wording, "or he whitened it," is difficult, and one must say that there is a scribal error in editions of the Mechaber, and likewise in the Ir Shushan.
Unless the name of the stolen object changed, etc. This is the Tur's language. The Torat Chattat disagrees without a compelling argument. Its proof from Rambam and the Mechaber, who write without qualification that smoothing constitutes a change and do not distinguish, is no proof. They later write in seif 9, "until their name changes." Thus when they earlier write "and he smoothed them," they mean that their name changed because of the smoothing. The Ir Shushan writes likewise. We therefore have only the position of Tosafos, Rosh, Tur, and Rama, and Maharshal likewise rules this way.
