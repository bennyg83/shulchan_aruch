The stolen property changed. Rabbah derives that change acquires from the verse requiring return of "the stolen thing that he stole": if it remains as stolen, return it; if not, return is no longer required. Tosafot asks why a second verse is needed when a changed prohibited payment is already permitted, and how this accords with authorities who deny acquisition through reversible change. The matter can be explained through the dispute whether an act done in violation of the Torah is legally effective. If unlawful acts were generally ineffective, we might think even a physical change in stolen property cannot create acquisition unless Scripture specifically teaches otherwise. The verse concerning prohibited payment cannot alone teach theft because sacred law can differ. According to Abaye, who holds that prohibited acts can still be legally effective, the teaching concerning changed prohibited payment can establish the general acquisitive power of change, while the theft verse serves other derivations such as liability for an added fifth on one's own theft. In the final analysis, the dispute can concern reversible change: Abaye can derive basic change from the other source and use the theft verse to include reversible change, whereas Rava needs the theft verse for ordinary irreversible change and has no source for reversible change. This also resolves the difficult printed Rashi suggesting that Abaye and Rava dispute whether change acquires. Rava elsewhere certainly accepts ordinary change. The better reading is that the final dispute concerns reversible change.
How so? One stole wood. Rashi holds that stones fixed into a building undergo an acquisitive change; Tosafot disagrees, viewing the attachment as reversible. The famous beam built into a palace does not prove otherwise because the beam retains its name. The Torah itself calls structural beams by that name. Change of act requires at least some change in the object's identity or designation. Stones incorporated into a building can acquire a new designation as part of the structure, whereas a beam may remain a beam even when built in. Thus Rashi's statement that attachment to a building is a change can be understood primarily as change of name produced by the building context, not mere physical attachment. That also explains why the beam enactment remained necessary.
And he made it into coinage. If one stole a bar of metal, minted it into coins, and then melted the coins back into a bar, I was uncertain whether the final bar counts as changed from the original theft. It appears that it does. Tosafot says that a cow stolen while empty, later becoming pregnant and then giving birth, can undergo a legally significant nonreversible change even though it was empty both at the beginning and the end. The intermediate state matters. So too here, although the object was a bar both when stolen and at the end, it passed through a minted-coin state and was later remade into a bar. That sequence can constitute acquisitive change.
