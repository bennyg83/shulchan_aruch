Two houses beside one another, etc. To explain this seif, the next, Rama's gloss there, and the beginning of siman 160, I will set out the Gemara. Bava Batra 6b states: Abaye said, two houses on opposite sides of the public domain, each builds a parapet for half his roof, not directly opposite the other's, and extends it slightly. The Gemara asks why specify the public domain, since the same should apply in a private domain, and answers that the public domain is the novelty: one might have thought that in any event each must conceal himself from passersby, so no additional wall is required. The Gemara later states in Rav Nachman's name that a roof adjacent to another roof does not require a four-amah parapet, but does require a ten-tefach partition. No one understands Abaye as disagreeing with Rav Nachman. Rambam distinguishes that Abaye deals with roofs used for dwelling, which require a four-amah parapet because of visual harm, whereas Rav Nachman deals with roofs not used for dwelling, where ten tefachim suffice so that an intruder will be caught as a thief. This is the Mechaber's meaning in seif 2 by 'between one roof and another from other roofs,' namely roofs not all used for dwelling. Rashbam and Tosafos instead distinguish by proximity. Abaye deals with roofs separated from one another, as in his example on opposite sides of a public domain. Because they are not adjacent, each person may use his roof without noticing when the other arrives, creating visual harm; therefore four amot are required. Rav Nachman deals with adjacent roofs, where each sees when the other comes and can guard his privacy, so ten tefachim suffice. The Tur follows this. This is Rama's gloss in seif 2 that some say ten tefachim suffice specifically when the roofs are adjacent, but if air separates them a parapet is required as explained above, meaning four amot as in seif 1. According to this view, even roofs used for dwelling need only ten tefachim when adjacent. With this framework the details are clear.
Beside one another. Meaning, neither is higher than the other. Otherwise, the lower roof may not require a four-amah wall if it is not wide enough for a person to stand at a distance and look over, as the Tur explains. If the Mechaber meant merely proximity, he would not have needed this phrase because he later applies the law even to roofs on opposite sides of the public domain.
Used for dwelling. Meaning, covered and level, excluding a sloped roof.
Even if they are on opposite sides of the public domain. Although each must already conceal himself from passersby below, as the Gemara says. All the more so when they are near one another or face one another across a private way.
Not directly opposite, and he extends it. One builds on the eastern half of his roof and the other on the western half, and each extends his wall slightly past the other's endpoint so they cannot see around the edges. It appears that even according to Rambam this extra extension is needed only when the roofs face one another, because then each can look sideways onto the other roof. If the houses and roofs are directly side by side, once one builds the eastern half and the other the western half right up to one another, no additional overlap is necessary.
He sees me constantly. Meaning, even at night, even while I am sitting, and even without intending to look, merely in the course of walking. Passersby in the public domain do not see in this constant manner. See the Derishah and Perishah.
