A sale effected through a prohibition. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 75.
And likewise, if he swore, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 3, who distinguishes a case in which the sale can be maintained lawfully from one involving an oath or vow that cannot be fulfilled without violating the prohibition. This also resolves the apparent difficulty from the Mishna and Gemara in the chapter Merubah regarding one who slaughtered or sold on Shabbos, where the sale is effective even when the transaction involved a Torah prohibition, such as carrying between domains, or when one said, 'Pluck my fig and your stolen article shall become mine.' Rav himself states there that the sale is valid, and all the poskim, as well as the Tur below in siman 350, rule accordingly. Likewise, in the chapter Mashilin the Rif and Rosh write, and the Maggid Mishneh at the end of Hilchot Mechirah states, that the Yerushalmi proves that one who unlawfully transfers land or movable property on Shabbos nevertheless effects the transfer. The Rambam rules this in chapter 28 of Hilchot Shabbos and chapter 5 of Hilchot Mechirah, as do the Tur and Mechaber at the ends of simanim 195 and 235. Similarly, Rava holds that a festival loan may be claimed in court, as the poskim state in Orach Chaim 525. The distinction is that all these acts could have been performed permissibly, whereas the cases of temurah or an oath cannot be carried out without transgression. This is unlike the Shiltei Giborim in the chapter Mashilin, who leaves the contradiction unresolved. The law of a festival loan presents no difficulty independently, for if an unlawful act were ineffective, it would be as though no loan had occurred and the money came to his hand as stolen property, which must be returned. Nor is the Shiltei Giborim's further question sound, namely why the Rif, Rosh, and Maggid Mishneh needed the Yerushalmi when the Bavli in Merubah already proves the same. The Bavli might establish only that the sale is effective by Torah law but not rabbinically. He would still owe fourfold or fivefold payment by Torah law, since a rabbinic enactment plainly would not exempt him, as is evident throughout that sugya. Exemption for invalid slaughter applies only where the slaughter is invalid by Torah law, not where it is merely rabbinically unfit, such as rabbinic Shabbos work or slaughtering unconsecrated animals in the Temple courtyard. The Yerushalmi, however, comments on the Mishna's list of rabbinic prohibitions and thus proves that the sale is valid even rabbinically. This appears correct. See Responsa Maharit, siman 69, and Responsa Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 380. Maharashdam concludes that the Mordechai's ruling remains fully authoritative. The Bach treats the matter as a monetary doubt and applies the rule that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. I ruled similarly in Shach, Yoreh Deah 230, seif katan 4, and this also appears from Responsa Rabbi Avraham ibn Chaim, siman 25, page 47, column 3. The Shiltei Giborim, chapter Mashilin, page 299a, further writes that where the person himself created the prohibition through his oath, no one says the act is ineffective. Thus, one who swore not to gamble and subsequently gambled does not undo the result of the game because of his oath. He neither returns his winnings nor recovers his losses; what was done remains done, because he himself created the prohibition. According to Tosafot in the first chapter of Temurah and Rabbi Peretz in Hagahot Mordechai to Shevuot, this is clear according to all opinions.
