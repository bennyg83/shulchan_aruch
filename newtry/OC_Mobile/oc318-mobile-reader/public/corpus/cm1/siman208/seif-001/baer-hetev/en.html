Swore. The Sema discusses the distinctions in this law at length. The Shach likewise agrees with him that the Mordechai's ruling remains valid. He concludes that the same applies to one who swore not to gamble and afterward gambled: we do not say that the transaction is ineffective because of his oath. Therefore he does not return money he won, nor is money he lost returned to him. What was done remains done, because he himself created the prohibition by his oath. This is straightforward according to all opinions in light of Tosafot at the beginning of Temurah and Hagahot Mordechai to Shevuot in the name of Rabbeinu Peretz. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 75; Responsa Maharit, vol. 1, siman 69; Rashdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 380; Responsa Ra'anach, siman 25, p. 47c; Yoreh Deah 228:35 in the gloss and siman 230, with the Taz and Shach there. The Taz writes: according to the Sema's conclusion, the rulings at the end of siman 195 and end of siman 235 are difficult, for one who effects an acquisition on Shabbat has a valid acquisition, and we do not say, "if he acted in violation, his act is ineffective." It appears to me that whenever the prohibition inheres in the act itself, we say that if he acted the act is ineffective, as in the chapter HaIshah Shenaflu, where the Sages said not to sell a deceased brother's property, and if he transgressed and sold, the sale is void because whatever the Sages said not to do is legally ineffective if done. By contrast, when one sells on Shabbat the prohibition results from the day, not from the sale itself. So too in Bava Kamma 70, "Throw me your stolen goods on Shabbat and my courtyard will acquire them," the acquisition is effective even though the person incurs capital liability. Therefore, where one swore not to sell his property, the prohibition stems from the oath and not from the sale itself, so the sale is valid after the fact. Likewise, in a sale made with a prohibited price, the prohibition does not lie in the essence of the sale but in the added money. Consequently, if he transgressed, the sale is effective. This is a sound distinction: only where there is a defect in the essential act do we treat it as though it was not done. The Rama's ruling here is therefore primary, and this is the proper practical ruling, particularly because the Beit Yosef also agrees with the Rama here. Moreover, here we are dealing with the primary laws of sale, whereas in Yoreh Deah the matter is cited only incidentally in the laws of oaths. Taz.
