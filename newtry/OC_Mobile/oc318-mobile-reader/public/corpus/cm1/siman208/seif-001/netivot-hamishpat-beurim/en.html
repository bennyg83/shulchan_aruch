A transaction performed through a prohibition. Ritva on Kiddushin 6 writes that fixed ribbis, although recoverable through beit din, becomes the lender’s property immediately upon receipt, and if he uses it for kiddushin the kiddushin takes effect. The same appears in Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia. At first glance this is difficult, because the borrower’s delivery to the lender was itself prohibited. It resembles a gift made through a prohibited act, which one might think should be void. If the parties merely stipulated ribbis in money and the lender later bought an object with the debt owed to him, one could say that the sale itself was not the prohibited act. But where they stipulated from the outset that a particular object or use of land would be given as compensation for waiting, the very transfer is the Torah prohibition of taking ribbis. There is an additional difficulty from Tosafot in Bava Kamma 112, who say that so long as the ribbis itself remains intact it resembles an existing stolen object and has not been acquired. This seems inconsistent with Ritva and Rashba that the lender can use it for kiddushin. The answer is that Tosafot wrote that only according to the Gemara’s initial assumption that heirs are exempt solely because of change of domain. The conclusion derives their exemption from the verse “your brother shall live with you”: the Torah obligated the lender himself to return the ribbis so that the borrower may live, but did not impose that mitzvah upon the lender’s heirs. This proves that the Torah allows the lender to acquire the ribbis, while imposing a positive commandment upon him personally to return it. For that reason the heirs are exempt, and for the same reason return of the monetary value can suffice even where the lender received a particular object as ribbis. Were the object never acquired at all, the heirs would have to return it as property never belonging to their father. This also explains why delivery of fixed or rabbinic ribbis is not treated as mistaken waiver merely because the borrower would not have paid had he known he was not obligated. Ribbis is different: when given knowingly, the Torah recognizes the lender’s acquisition and imposes a duty of return. Rabbinic ribbis was not made stricter in this respect than Torah ribbis. Accordingly, if one stipulated a particular object or use of land as ribbis, the transfer itself can take effect, while the lender remains obligated to return the value because of the mitzvah of “your brother shall live with you.” Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 28 cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that kiddushin with fixed ribbis is ineffective. Perhaps that case concerns ribbis still owed by the woman herself, which she has not yet paid, or even ribbis she already paid where she can say, “I took back what was mine,” because the husband was obligated to return it. The issue is discussed there.
He swore or vowed. See Shach, seif katan 2, who distinguishes cases where the act could have been done permissibly. This is difficult from Tosafot in Temurah concerning slaughtering the Pesach offering while possessing chametz, even though slaughter can ordinarily be done permissibly, and from the Gemara’s question about separating terumah before bikkurim. There is no satisfactory basis for that distinction by itself. The correct principle is that “if he acted, it does not take effect” applies where invalidating the act repairs the very violation. Thus, if one unlawfully substitutes a korban, divorces a woman contrary to a Torah command, or performs another act where recognizing the act would defeat the Torah’s directive, the law can refuse effect to the act and thereby preserve the command. By contrast, slaughtering on Shabbat remains an act of taking life even if the slaughter is declared halachically invalid; invalidating the shechitah does not undo the Shabbat violation. Therefore the meat can nevertheless be valid. This is also why Tosafot properly ask from slaughtering the Pesach offering while owning chametz. That prohibition specifically requires a valid act of slaughter. If we say the slaughter has no halachic effect, the person has not violated “you shall not slaughter...with chametz.” By contrast, with “it and its offspring,” where the Torah separately teaches that the second animal remains permitted for ordinary use, Scripture itself overrides any general reasoning that the slaughter should be ineffective. The same explains rabbinic cases. Where the Sages forbade a sale because they wanted a lien or other legal status to remain intact, validating the sale would directly nullify their enactment, so they can make the sale ineffective. But where they prohibited buying and selling on Shabbat only lest one come to write, the forbidden act has already been done and invalidating the sale would not repair that violation. The continued legal existence of the sale is itself permissible. This is Shach’s intent in saying the matter “could have been done permissibly”: the legal result can exist in a permitted state without defeating the rabbinic command. Shach’s suggestion that a certain sale might be effective d'oraisa but ineffective d'rabbanan is difficult where the underlying act itself was a Torah prohibition.
And it was confirmed by a kinyan. Sema, seif katan 4, explains this regarding fixing a price before the market rate was established. At first glance this is difficult, because before the market rate is established the subject resembles a davar shelo ba la'olam, as Sema writes in siman 209, and Shach there says even a kinyan creates only mi shepara. A prohibited transaction does not create mi shepara. Perishah already asks this and gives two answers. His first answer is that the person framed it as a personal obligation. His second is that the object required only three acts of processing, so the item itself was sufficiently in existence. In my view the first answer is primary. The second confuses acquisition of the actual unfinished object, which can be valid and is itself permitted, with fixing a future obligation based on having such an unfinished object. In the latter case the buyer does not acquire that particular object; the seller may consume or sell it and remains obligated to provide equivalent goods. It is therefore still a future obligation. This is clear from Tosafot in Bava Metzia and from the laws of permitted forward pricing. Although a personal obligation does not ordinarily help regarding something impossible or not reasonably available to acquire, here the commodity is ordinarily available, and the only problem is that the market rate has not yet been established. A personal obligation can therefore be effective.
However, the buyer. See Sema, seif katan 7. His meaning is that where the seller added to the price after a market rate had already been established, it is known that immediate payment would have entitled the buyer to the market quantity and the excess is compensation for waiting. The prohibited ribbis component is therefore void, while the underlying transaction at the market rate remains. By contrast, where no market rate yet existed and the price later rose, the original sale lacked a fixed lawful price. According to what I wrote above, the case is in any event one of a personal obligation, and that explains why the seller cannot retract from the obligation while the buyer retains the rights given by the applicable law.
