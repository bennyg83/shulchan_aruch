Seif 1. Note: See the Sema and Ketzot HaChoshen, who disagrees with the Sema and offers another explanation of Tosafot. See my responsum in Even HaEzer, siman 134, addressed to the province of Volhynia, concerning one who swore not to divorce, where I discussed the words of Ketzot HaChoshen. The learned Rabbi Simchah Bick of Dubno asked me about the Rambam, who rules regarding a yevamah awaiting levirate marriage like Rav Yosef, that once the Torah said not to sell, if one sold the transaction accomplishes nothing. This appears to follow the principle that an act performed in violation of law is ineffective, yet the Rambam elsewhere rules that one may make an eruv by placing it in the corners, which seems to follow the opposite view. Know that identifying Rav Yosef's ruling with the principle that an unlawful act is ineffective is the Sema's explanation here, seif katan 28. Your question is an old one, cited by Yeshuot Yaakov in Even HaEzer, volume 1, and still earlier in Responsa Shaar Ephraim from other rulings of the Rambam. From a rabbinic prohibition there is no difficulty. Even if the reason is that an unlawful act is ineffective, rabbinic enactments may be stronger than Torah law because the Sages strengthened their decrees. See Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer simanim 92 and 66. The question in siman 92 can be answered as follows. Where applying the rabbinic rule would remove money from its present possessor, the Sages did not strengthen their enactment beyond Torah law, because possession of money is especially strong. Thus regarding the husband's inheritance, where enforcement would transfer property from the heirs to the husband, they did not strengthen the enactment beyond Torah law. But where the Torah says that one should not sell, invalidating the sale merely leaves the property with its present owner. In such a case the Sages did strengthen their enactment beyond Torah law and voided the transaction. A ketubah is different. There the Sages did not directly confiscate money through their enactment. Rather, they declared that intercourse without a ketubah has the status of licentious intercourse. That is within their authority because anyone who marries does so subject to rabbinic law, enabling the Sages to affect the marital framework. Generally, however, they did not strengthen their words so as to extract money from one possessor, although they did so to preserve money with its possessor. One may further distinguish between a rabbinic enactment whose very purpose is to prohibit the act and one that merely establishes a legal consequence. In the husband's inheritance, the Sages never prohibited a person from stipulating that the husband should not inherit; their enactment simply established that the husband ordinarily inherits. Since the act itself is not forbidden, they did not strengthen the rule beyond Torah law. By contrast, where the Sages expressly prohibited the act itself, they did strengthen it, and according to all opinions a prohibited act is ineffective. This explains Rav Yosef's precise wording: "Now that the Sages said he may not sell, if he sold, it is nothing." Since their enactment itself was that he may not sell, the act is rabbinically forbidden and is therefore ineffective. This appears correct upon initial analysis.
Seif 1. It appears to me as a new principle that even according to those who rule that an act performed in violation of law is ineffective, that applies only to an optional act. When a mitzvah is involved, all would agree that if it was performed it is effective. The challenges raised in Temurah from various mitzvot involve cases where the mitzvah itself was not to perform that act, and he violated the command by doing it. Since the act constituting the mitzvah framework was itself improper, it resembles an optional prohibited act. But where the mitzvah itself was performed properly and the only restraint came from an external matter, such as a handshake undertaking not to perform it, then the mitzvah act itself was validly performed, and according to all opinions it is effective. It is preferable to minimize disputes, and this appears correct and clear.
Seif 1. See the Sema, who proves that even regarding a rabbinic prohibition we say that the prohibited act is ineffective. This is difficult from explicit mishnayot in the first and second chapters of Bechorot: one who sells an animal to a non-Jew or takes a non-Jew as a partner in it, although he is not permitted to do so, exempts the animal from the firstborn laws. Thus, although the sale was prohibited, if he sold it the transaction is valid. This requires study. See also my Even HaEzer writings, fifth edition, responsum 118 on Kiddushin addressed to Lviv, where I raised a similar difficulty from the rule that if one sold a prohibited item and betrothed a woman with the proceeds, the betrothal is valid. I concluded there that even if one says that an unlawful monetary acquisition is ineffective, an acquisition of land is certainly effective, as derived from Kiddushin. See there.
