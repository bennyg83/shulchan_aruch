He increased the purchase price because of the delay in payment, etc. Meaning, a market price had already been established, for example one measure of wheat for one sela, and he gives five dinarim for it because the seller waits for payment. This is compensation for waiting. It is not Torah-level interest because it does not arise through a loan but through a sale, yet it is rabbinic interest imposed upon the seller. If the parties set a price before a market price had been established, there is concern for rabbinic interest on the purchaser's side, because perhaps when the market price is later established it will be higher than the price he paid, so he will have obtained the produce cheaply in return for advancing the money. These laws are explained in Yoreh Deah simanim 173, 174, and 175.
And the seller did not have the merchandise. If he had it, there is no concern of interest, because a person may sell what he presently owns at a reduced price. However, he must have enough for the entire transaction. It is not sufficient that he have one measure and accept money for ten measures. That leniency was stated only in a loan of one se'ah for one se'ah, where the borrower later repays the same kind. Here, however, he receives money and in return gives produce. This is clear from Yoreh Deah siman 175, as I wrote there in the Perishah.
Likewise, if he swore or vowed not to sell and then violated the oath and sold, etc. See Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, who cite Hagahot Mordechai at the end of tractate Shevuot. It rules that if one swore, or accepted a ban upon himself, not to give an item, not to sell, not to waive, or the like, and then violated his oath by selling or giving, the act is legally ineffective. They write that this opinion was already rejected by Rav Hai and the Rosh in the case of one who increases the price because payment is delayed, or one who fixed a price before the market price was established, where the sale remains valid. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 8 of Hilchot Malveh, likewise writes in the names of Ramban and Rashba that a transaction performed through a prohibited act remains valid. These are the words of Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. It is surprising that here Rama rejects Hagahot Mordechai, while in Yoreh Deah siman 230 he rules in the gloss in accordance with it. More fundamentally, it is difficult to understand how Beit Yosef and Rama rejected Mordechai by comparing the case to a sale conducted through a prohibition. According to their argument, Rava would contradict himself. In Temurah Rava holds that when the Torah says not to perform an act, if one performs it the act is ineffective and void. Yet Rava himself says in chapter Eizehu Neshech, Bava Metzia 65, that one extracts the garment from the borrower only so people should not say, "That garment he is wearing is an interest-garment." This implies that absent that concern the sale itself would be fully valid, as the Rosh, Rav Hai, and the other authorities infer. Why so, if it is a transaction conducted through a prohibition and according to Rava an act done contrary to the Torah is ineffective? Rather, the cases are not comparable. There the sale itself remains valid because we do not penalize the permitted component on account of the prohibited component. Once he returns the prohibited interest, the actual sale of the garment can stand, and only public suspicion requires further action. The same applies where the price was increased because payment was deferred. If the prohibited interest is not taken, the prohibition is removed and the underlying sale remains valid. By contrast, where he swore or vowed not to sell, the sale cannot exist at all without the very act constituting violation of his oath. This distinction is straightforward, and therefore no difficulty against Mordechai arises from the law of a transaction conducted with prohibited interest. The real difficulty, however, is from Temurah 6a itself. The Gemara there shows that Rava's principle, that an act done against the Torah is ineffective, was stated regarding a preexisting prohibition imposed by the Torah, not regarding a prohibition that the person created upon himself. Tosafos there ask why the Gemara does not say that Abaye and Rava disagree in a case where a person swore not to divorce his wife and then divorced her, so that according to Abaye the divorce would be effective and according to Rava ineffective. Tosafos answer that where the act itself is not inherently prohibited and the person created the prohibition through his own oath, Rava does not say the act is ineffective. Thus in the very case discussed by Mordechai, where one swore not to sell and then violated the oath, even Rava would hold the sale effective. After writing this I found that Rabbi Shmuel de Medina also challenged Mordechai on this basis in Responsa, Yoreh Deah section, responsum 151. In my humble opinion one can defend Mordechai by saying that Tosafos' distinction there is merely an answer to a local question, and we do not necessarily rely upon it for halacha. In Ketubot, the halacha is stated generally like Rav Yosef, who says that an act the Sages said not to perform is ineffective if done. Were Tosafos' distinction a fixed rule, the Gemara should have mentioned somewhere a difference between a preexisting prohibition and one a person created upon himself. Moreover, Tosafos' answer does not fully resolve the Gemara's search for a practical difference between Abaye and Rava, since, as Rabbeinu Baruch notes in the same Tosafos, other distinctions could still have been proposed, and Tosafos leave the matter unresolved. Mordechai may therefore hold that Tosafos introduced the distinction only to explain why the Gemara did not choose this as the central dispute between Abaye and Rava: first, the phrase "anything the Torah says not to do" naturally refers to an inherent Torah prohibition, and second, it remained possible that even as a matter of law Rava would distinguish where the person created the prohibition himself. The Gemara therefore sought another case of disagreement. But once we conclude in Ketubot that even with rabbinic prohibitions an act done contrary to the prohibition may be ineffective, Mordechai finds it reasonable that a self-imposed Torah prohibition should be no weaker than a preexisting rabbinic prohibition. This appears to me a proper defense of Mordechai. Rabbi Shmuel de Medina discusses the objection at length, and his words require study. I have also written at length elsewhere in defense of Mordechai. The conclusion is that Mordechai's ruling appears to me sound and binding in practice. Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 186, also implies this. Rama himself in Yoreh Deah siman 230 cites Mordechai's ruling without any dissent, and that is the primary rule. Examine this carefully. Maharam Yaffe follows Rama in both locations without explaining the contradiction.
And it was completed through an act of acquisition, etc. This refers to fixing a price before the market price was established, the immediately preceding case. Where the seller increased the purchase price because of delayed payment, meaning the purchaser immediately took the goods into his possession while the seller agreed to wait for the money until the fixed date, no further kinyan is required. The purchaser already acquired through meshichah into his domain.
Through one of the modes of acquisition. If there was no valid mode of acquisition, then even if he gave money he does not need to accept mi shepara, because the transaction was conducted through a prohibition. Beit Yosef writes this, and Rama likewise in the gloss to siman 209, seif 7. See there.
And he gives according to the permitted price, etc. In its plain meaning this refers to the first case. If he promised twelve dinarim for an item worth only ten because the seller waited for payment, he pays only ten dinarim and takes the merchandise. Neither party may cancel the sale merely because the original arrangement involved a prohibition. Regarding the second case, however, where they fixed a price before a market rate existed and the prohibited aspect arises if the market later increases, it is difficult to say that he must pay according to the later higher permitted price and neither party may retract, because the Mechaber concludes that the purchaser may retract. Therefore I wrote in the Perishah that the principal application of this phrase is to the first case. It applies incidentally to the second only where the market price did not rise above the agreed price. If it did rise, the Mechaber's concluding rule applies and the purchaser may retract.
Nevertheless, the purchaser may retract, etc. In the Perishah I explained that this differs from a case where the price was increased because payment was delayed. There the ordinary market price was already known when the sale occurred; the purchaser merely added above it. We therefore remove the unlawful increase from their agreement and the sale remains at the ordinary market price that already existed. In the latter case, however, the parties fixed a price when there was no established market price at all. If we remove their agreed price, there is no remaining price framework, and therefore the purchaser may retract. The seller may not retract if the purchaser wishes to pay the later higher market price. Certainly, if the market did not rise, neither party may retract because the sale was completed by a valid kinyan. See the Derishah, where I wrote that there is an obvious omission in the responsum of Rav Hai copied by Beit Yosef in this siman. Once that missing portion is recognized, it is clear that the statement "the purchaser may retract" refers only to the second case. See there.
