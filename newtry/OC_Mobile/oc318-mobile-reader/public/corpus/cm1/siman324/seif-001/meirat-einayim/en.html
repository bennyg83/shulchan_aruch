In Babylonia and similar places he should not change to legumes, etc. This is difficult. According to the textual version and view cited here, grain weakens the soil more than legumes. Why then may he not change from grain to legumes, which would benefit the landowner? It is farfetched to say that the nature of the soil is the reverse in Eretz Yisrael and Babylonia. One can explain that although Babylonian soil is moister than that of Eretz Yisrael, it is not as rich. Even legumes weaken it there to some degree. The tenant therefore may not change from the crop specified by the owner. Perhaps the owner specifically needs grain and willingly accepts somewhat greater soil depletion in order to receive grain. He does not need legumes and objects to weakening his field for no useful reason. Even though a tenant who changes the crop must buy the agreed produce in the market for the owner's share, the owner may prefer produce grown in his own field. According to this, all the more so if the owner instructed him to plant legumes, he may not plant grain in Babylonia. Eretz Yisrael differs because the land is richer and stronger. Even if he plants something the owner does not need, where the change does not harm the soil at all the owner is not considered particular. Nevertheless, from legumes to grain he may not change because grain requires greater strength from the soil and causes some additional weakening even there. This explanation remains somewhat strained. It is also difficult to say that Rashi and the Rosh disagree with the Rif and Rambam over an agricultural fact known to farmers, namely which crop exhausts the soil more. In the Derishah I offered another reconciliation. According to that approach, in Babylonia the prohibition is specifically against changing from grain to legumes, while changing from barley to wheat can be permitted. That approach fits the Rif and Rambam well, though it is harder to fit the Mechaber and Rama. See there.
