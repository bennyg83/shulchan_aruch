One who rents a house for a year at a stated total, etc. The language of the Rashba, as cited by the Tur, Maggid Mishneh, Ran, and Sema, indicates that the year for house rentals begins on Rosh Hashanah, the first of Tishrei. Although the Mishna lists the first of Nisan as the new year for months, leap years, and shekalim, and one opinion adds house rentals, the first Tanna plainly treats the first of Tishrei as the new year for rentals. The Gemara's discussion confirms this, and the law follows the first Tanna. The Rif, Rosh, and other authorities reproduce the Mishna without adopting the dissenting view. Accordingly, one who rents a house “for a year” counts twelve months from day to day. If he says “for this year,” even if the lease begins on the first of Elul, the arrival of the first of Tishrei completes the year. If it begins on the second of Elul or later, that cannot be the intended meaning, because a person does not rent a house for fewer than thirty days; he therefore receives a full year. This follows from the Gemara and Rashi. See Responsa Rabbi David ibn Lev, part 2, p. 51; Mahari ibn Lev, part 2, siman 21; the Sema; and my comments in Shach, Yoreh Deah 220:15.
The intercalated month belongs to the landlord, etc. The Rosh's discussions of seizure and conflicting witnesses indicate that the landlord prevails here only because the tenant has no definite claim. If the tenant says with certainty, “You expressly rented it to me for twelve dinars for the year even if the year is intercalated,” money is not extracted from him. This does not conflict with the law that a mortgagee who definitely claims a three-year term must surrender produce because land remains in its owner's possession. There the amount of produce already consumed is known, and the mortgagee seeks to keep it through his definite claim. Since the produce comes from the owner's land, it is treated as though still in the borrower's possession. Here, by contrast, if the tenant's definite claim is true, the property never produced a thirteenth dinar at all. The landlord owns the land, but the original rental value may have been only twelve dinars. The tenant who makes a definite claim is therefore the possessor. The Rosh accordingly proves from Rav Nachman that uncertain seizure does not help. In the mortgage case, however, the produce is treated as though in the landowner's possession because its amount is known, even against the mortgagee's definite claim. The Sema's distinction between land and movables is correct, but with movables a further distinction applies. Seizure without permission does not help in a doubtful case unless the possessor has a definite claim. Possession received with permission can help even where the possessor is uncertain, as the Ramban, Rosh, and Baal HaTerumot explain. This also accounts for the ruling concerning a borrowed hoe used to dig orchards: the borrower may continue using it for all his orchards because he received possession lawfully, even though he cannot make a definite claim about the intended number.
Because land remains in its owner's possession. Hagahot Ashri cites Or Zarua that if a house was rented “until Adar,” and one party says Adar I while the other says Adar II, or if it was rented “until the end of Adar” and they dispute which Adar, they divide the disputed period. This appears to derive from the Tosefta. In my view we do not rule like that Tosefta. It follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and Rabbi Yose, who divide the intercalated month where the rent was stated both annually and monthly and who do not apply the presumption that land remains in its owner's possession. The accepted law follows Rav Nachman: where a lease is stated as twelve gold pieces per year and one gold piece per month, the entire intercalated month belongs to the landlord. Thus, in every doubt the landlord prevails because the land remains in his possession. The same should apply where the parties dispute whether “Adar” meant Adar I or Adar II. Or Zarua may follow his broader view that when the law itself remained unresolved, Rabbeinu Chananel treats the matter as a doubt to be divided. He could therefore distinguish between an internally ambiguous rental formula, where Rav Nachman's presumption applies, and a dispute among authorities over whether “Adar” legally denotes the first or second Adar, where the uncertainty lies in the law and is divided. According to our accepted rule, however, a dispute among authorities is governed by the burden of proof. Here the landlord is the possessor because the land remains in his ownership, and the tenant must prove his claim. Even if the dispute arises at the end of the month, the entire amount belongs to the landlord.
