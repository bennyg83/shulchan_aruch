Until he completes his term. See Shach, who writes that the renter may sublet it during the remaining term. See siman 212, where I discussed this at length. I will state the point briefly here. Something lacking only the passage of time is not considered something not yet in existence, because time arrives automatically. This is certainly true according to Rabbeinu Tam in siman 211, who holds that "this field that I will inherit from my father" is not considered nonexistent because inheritance comes automatically, and time is an even stronger case. Even those who disagree regarding inheritance can agree regarding time, because inheritance still depends on an external event. A proof appears in Kesubos from the case of "this field that I mortgaged for ten years," where it is not treated as nonexistent because of the passage of time, but only because it remains mortgaged. Nevertheless the leased object must be specifically identified. Thus, one may lease "this field" for a future period after another tenant's lease ends, just as one may speak of "this field that I will inherit from my father." But if he leases an unspecified field and presently owns no available field because all his fields are leased to others, the transaction is ineffective, just as Rabbeinu Tam does not validate "an unspecified field that I will inherit from my father." This is evident from the Tur in siman 312 concerning one who leases an unspecified house and that house collapses, while his only other house is currently leased to others. The future lease is ineffective because the subject is not presently available. I also wrote there that an unspecified future lease is treated as taking effect from now. Only where the acquisition cannot take effect now, or where the parties expressly suspend it by saying, "Do not acquire until after thirty days," or "Acquire after thirty days," is it delayed. If he simply leases the property, even if the owner says that he still needs to live there for some time or the property is presently leased, ordinary language is treated as "from now," because a transaction is not presumed to have been done for no effect, as explained in siman 195. Even where money could effect an acquisition after thirty days, we still apply this presumption, since without an immediate effect both parties could retract and the present act would otherwise accomplish nothing.
A place to live. See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that the same rule applies if the tenant becomes ill. See the Rama's responsum there, which indicates that even a contagious illness is included. It appears to me, however, that if the tenant already had a contagious illness and the landlord did not know, the lease is a mistaken transaction.
If it was leased in such a manner that he could never redeem it. This ruling is initially difficult to understand. Mordechai in HaShoel, which is the source, describes a case in which the landlord's house had been mortgaged to a non Jew. If the landlord failed to obtain money to redeem it, the house would become permanently the creditor's. Mordechai therefore ruled that the landlord could sell the house currently leased to the tenant, requiring the tenant to leave immediately, in order to redeem his mortgaged house and prevent its permanent loss. The Rosh and Rama must be understood similarly, and the Rama's text should read "mortgaged." Yet Mordechai compares this to the case where the landlord's own house collapsed, allowing him to say that the tenant should not be in a better position than he is. We, however, rule that where the lease is for a fixed term, even if the landlord's house collapses he cannot evict the tenant, contrary to Rashi. Perhaps Mordechai follows Rashi. If so, what basis have we to rule here that the landlord may evict the tenant, when the very analogy upon which Mordechai relied is rejected in practice? Moreover, why should this differ from a case where the leased house itself becomes damaged, where the landlord cannot compel the tenant to leave even if continued occupancy will cause major additional damage? Perhaps the Rosh and Mordechai distinguish between collapse of the landlord's residence, which deprives him of housing only during the remaining lease term, and inability to redeem the mortgaged house, which can cause a permanent and very great loss together with permanent loss of his dwelling. This distinction nevertheless requires further study.
Even if he did not specify a term. See Shach, seif katan 4, who rules that the amount of rent does not prove the intended duration. Ketzos HaChoshen writes that where the language does not contradict the amount paid, the amount certainly clarifies the intent. In my view, if one says, "Rent me your house for one hundred," without stating a term, ordinary rental is for thirty days. The ordinary meaning of the language therefore conflicts with using the price to infer a longer term, and the amount paid does not establish duration. The exception is a city where the custom is that houses are never rented for less than a year, in which case the unstated term is one year.
