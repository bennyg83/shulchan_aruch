Seif 1. One who rents a house to another, etc. Note: In my second edition on Choshen Mishpat, in this siman, I ruled that if one rents to another property that is presently rented to a third party and whose current rental term has not yet expired, the later rental does not take effect. I explained the reason there and wrote that even though the only missing element is time, which will pass automatically, this still counts as something not yet in existence for purposes of acquisition, contrary to the reasoning of Tosafot in Arakhin 21. I have now, thank God, found support in Ran to Nedarim 17, on the mishna concerning whether one vow can take effect upon another. Ran indicates that if the later vow does not take effect now, it will not automatically take effect after time passes merely because the obstacle will disappear on its own. This proves my position that, for the rule of something not yet in existence, the automatic passage of time does not suffice. Consequently, such a rental does not take effect by ordinary law. The common commercial practice that does treat it as effective may support what I wrote there, that situmta, a binding commercial custom, can validate even a transaction concerning something not yet in existence. But this applies only where the customary formalities are performed, namely the customary handshake or agreement together with earnest money. Ordinary rental acquisition by money alone works where the property is presently transferable, but not where it is not yet in the lessor's possession in the relevant sense. In that setting effectiveness rests solely on commercial custom, and custom requires all of its usual components, not merely one of them. There: Even if the landlord's own house collapsed and he has nowhere to live. An incident occurred here in Brody in the year 5619, on the second day of Rosh Chodesh Iyar after midday, when a great fire, Heaven protect us, destroyed most of the city. Reuven lived in a house belonging to Shimon under a yearly rental arrangement that had begun years earlier on the twenty-first of Iyar. They had initially stipulated that a leap month would not change the anniversary date, and every year the landlord accepted the rent without the parties discussing a fresh term, relying on the original arrangement. In the fire the upper dwelling in which the landlord lived was destroyed, while the lower dwelling occupied by the tenant survived. The landlord then prevented the tenant from reentering the rooms he had rented and took them for himself. In my view he acted unlawfully. Approximately three weeks remained until the twenty-first of Iyar, and Shulchan Aruch 312:1 explicitly states that where a house is rented for a fixed term, even if the landlord's own house collapses he cannot evict the tenant before the term ends. Since the original rental was for fixed yearly terms and every subsequent year proceeded on the original conditions, the tenant retained possession until that anniversary. Moreover, I believe that even after the anniversary the landlord could not immediately evict him. The Shulchan Aruch requires thirty days' prior notice to remove an ordinary tenant. It is true that this notice rule is generally for an open-ended rental rather than one with a fixed term, because a fixed-term tenant should anticipate the end and ask whether he may remain. But that rationale does not apply where the tenant has remained for many years without either party speaking anew each year. He had no reason to expect that the landlord would suddenly require the premises. In such a longstanding relationship, notice again becomes the landlord's responsibility. If he gave no thirty-day notice, the continued occupancy is treated as though the property were rented onward, and he cannot evict the tenant even because he himself lacks another dwelling. Especially where the landlord admits that, absent the fire, he had intended to let the tenant continue, the arrangement is effectively renewed. As I wrote in my second edition on this siman, a tenant's continued possession before expiration can support the continuation of the tenancy beyond the term, whether by operation of the Sages' enactment requiring notice or because the parties are presumed to have entered the original arrangement subject to that legal condition. Understand this well.
