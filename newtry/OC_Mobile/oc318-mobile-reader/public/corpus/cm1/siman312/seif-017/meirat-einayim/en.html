He is not obligated to rebuild it. The Tur writes that Ramah says that if the wood and stones are sufficient to buy or rent another house, the owner must buy or rent one. My master the Rosh writes that he need not do so. The Rosh's reason is that only where one rents "this donkey" and it dies do we say that its value is used to provide another, as the Tur wrote in siman 310, because a dead donkey cannot be restored and its only practical disposition is to sell it. A collapsed house is different. It is not ordinarily intended for sale but rather for reconstruction by adding materials and rebuilding it. When he said "this house," he meant this house as it is, not that he would add to it or provide another house. See the Rosh and the Perishah. I explained there why one should not derive the opposite from an animal whose legs became worm-infested that was rented for riding, where even when he said "this donkey" its value is used to rent or purchase another. See there.
Rather, he calculates according to the period he used it, etc. The converse is likewise true: if he has not yet paid anything, he pays according to the period during which he lived there. So writes the Tur.
But if it is still standing and merely became damaged, etc. The Tur writes that in such a case we cannot say, "it is gone," because a standing house is ordinarily intended for repair. It is still called a house, though dangerous to inhabit, and therefore the owner must repair it. End of his words. The Mechaber's phrase, "if he has prepaid rent in his possession," is not written in the Tur, and I did not find it in Rambam at the end of chapter 5 of Hilchot Sechirut. It appears that "if he has prepaid rent in his possession" does not necessarily mean that the very money is still intact, but rather that the owner already received more rent than corresponds to the time the renter has lived there. He must then repair the house from that rent. If he did not receive excess rent, he could perhaps retract and tell the renter, "Leave and rent another dwelling." Still, this reasoning is not entirely satisfactory, because once he obligated himself to rent this particular house and it retains the name of a house, apparently he is bound to repair it.
Rather, he must provide him a house. It must be of the same length and width, but he need not provide one similarly situated near a river, bathhouse, or market. So writes Nimukei Yosef. He further writes that even where he rented an unspecified house, if he had already entered and begun living in a large house, the owner must provide another large house like it.
If the house burned, etc. At the beginning of this seif it was explained that if the house collapsed, the owner calculates rent for the period of use. If so, what is Rama adding by concluding that when the entire city burned it is a regional calamity and the renter deducts from the rent? Even if the entire city did not burn, he would deduct from the rent. One could answer with difficulty that initially the case concerned renting an unspecified house and one house burned. Its law is like a collapsed house, and where an unspecified house collapses the owner must provide another house. Rama then says that if the entire city burned, he need not provide another because it is a regional calamity. But if so, Rama should not have stated the law without explanation. It therefore appears preferable to emend the gloss to read: "If the house burned, its law is like one that collapsed; and some say that he must pay the entire rent. However, if the entire city burned, etc." This follows Darkei Moshe, seif 17, which cites Mordechai in chapter HaOmanin that if a house burned its law is like a collapsed house, and Responsa Maharam in Maimoniyot, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 47, says the same. But siman 27 there cites Riva that the renter must pay the entire rent, unless the entire city burned, which is a regional calamity. See what I wrote in siman 321 regarding these laws. According to Rama one must say that he deliberately omitted this second view, as I explain there.
