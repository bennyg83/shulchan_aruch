As long as the first house has not fallen. See Shach, seif katan 19, who implies that with "this house" the landlord cannot move the tenant even from an inferior house to a superior one, but with a generic house he can. In my view it is unreasonable to say that under a generic-house rental the landlord may move the tenant repeatedly from one dwelling to another. The tenant can say, "I did not rent on the understanding that I would be burdened with moving every day. I rented to live in one place for the term." Nimukei Yosef's language indicates a different point. Rambam implies that even if the tenant first occupied a larger house and it later fell, the landlord may then provide a smaller house, because the original house no longer exists. Ritva disagrees and holds that once the tenant had actually been placed in the larger house, the landlord may not downgrade him even after it falls. That, in my view, is the correct subject of the dispute. Rama and Shach appear to read otherwise, but even on their approach one should not permit repeated arbitrary relocation. At most the landlord may make one substitution where he can plausibly say that the first placement was mistaken, temporary, or compelled by circumstances, and that from the outset he intended the second house. Repeated changes are not reasonable.
The house burned. See Sema, seif katan 34, who is greatly troubled by Rama. The matter can be reconciled through the dispute in siman 334. There Rama cites two opinions where one rents a house to live in and then dies. One opinion says the tenant need pay only for the period used, because the landlord resembles a worker who should have stipulated. The other says the entire rent remains due because rental for a day is treated as a sale for that day. The same dispute explains the opinions here. The authority requiring full payment even after the house burns treats rental as a time-limited sale. Once the tenant acquired the use-right for the term, accidental loss falls on him like an accident befalling a purchaser. Nimukei Yosef indicates that a house can differ from a donkey or hired worker for this reason. The special structure of "this donkey" is different because the carcass itself remains pledged toward replacement and the arrangement resembles an apoteiki. With "this house," once it falls there is no remaining collateral and the rental can be treated as a completed time-sale. Machaneh Efrayim further writes that where prepayment was an express term of the rental, everyone can agree that the arrangement is more strongly treated as a sale for the period. In that case even if an accident befalls the house the landlord need not return the prepaid rent. Mechaber's statement that the landlord calculates the period of actual use and returns the rest can therefore refer to a case where prepayment was not a contractual condition, but the landlord merely happened to receive or seize the rent early. Rama's gloss in siman 334, saying that if the landlord received the entire rent he need not return it, can be understood as Rama's own ruling based on this distinction: express advance payment gives the rental a completed-sale character. On this approach, Rama's statement here that a burned house is like a fallen house and his different treatment of a province-wide calamity can be harmonized with the broader dispute. In an ordinary isolated accident, the rental may retain the sale-like allocation of risk. In a regional calamity, the special rules of makkat medinah can reduce the rent.
