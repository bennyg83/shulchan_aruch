He must rent him another house. This applies even if he originally said, "I rent you this house," as Rambam states. If a third party damages the house, the landlord is not ordinarily required to provide another house merely because a wrongdoer caused the tenant's loss; the damager pays only the value of the damage he caused. Why then, if the landlord himself sells the house, must he provide another? The answer is that every landlord undertakes a personal obligation to provide the rented use. With "this donkey," the value of the carcass is bound toward providing another donkey, and the arrangement resembles an explicit apoteiki. The same conceptual structure applies to "this house." When the specified house is destroyed through accident, the particular apoteiki is gone and, where the rental was limited to that house, no broader obligation may remain. But when the landlord himself sells, destroys, or otherwise refuses to provide the very property he undertook to make available, his other assets remain bound to fulfill the personal obligation. One cannot use one's own wrongful act to destroy one's debt. This also answers the case where the landlord sells the house to a non Jew who prevents the tenant from entering. From the tenant's perspective the sale may be legally ineffective against the existing rental, but if the non Jew physically bars entry, the landlord cannot say this resembles an outsider who locked the door. The landlord himself created the obstacle and thereby failed to perform his own obligation. Shitah Mekubetzet similarly describes "this donkey" as an explicit apoteiki and a generic donkey as an ordinary apoteiki. Even with an explicit apoteiki, if the debtor himself destroys or refuses to surrender the collateral, his other assets can become liable. Accordingly, if heirs sell or destroy the rented house and inherited other assets, those assets can be used to provide the tenant another house. If they inherited no other assets and merely destroyed the house, they need not pay from their own property beyond whatever value remains from the inherited collateral. If they sold it and received proceeds, the law can depend on the rules governing property sold by heirs discussed in siman 110.
