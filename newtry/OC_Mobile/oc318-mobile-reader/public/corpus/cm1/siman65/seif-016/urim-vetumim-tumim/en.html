There is an opinion that one may collect with it. This is Rambam's view. As Maggid Mishneh understands him, it applies even where the debtor does not admit the debt. The source of this ruling is not obvious. Maggid Mishneh infers it from the wording of the Mishnah: it says the finder "should not return" the document, rather than saying "one cannot collect with it." That inference alone is weak. A stronger basis appears from Baal HaTerumot's question. He asks Rambam from the laws of a woman's get and an emancipation deed. Even when such documents are returned, the woman or slave must later prove when the document reached them before recovering fruits or property from purchasers. All the more so, one might think that a creditor who obtained a fallen loan document without court authorization should have to prove when it reached him before collecting. Rambam's principle is different. A loan note exists primarily for collection. If, after it is already in the creditor's hand, we say that he cannot collect with it, we are stripping the document of its very legal presumption and central function. Rambam therefore says that once the note has actually been returned, we leave it in its ordinary chazakah and allow collection. A get or emancipation deed is different. Its essential function is divorce or emancipation, not recovery of fruits or property. The later monetary recovery is only an incidental consequence of what the document proves. Requiring proof of delivery date for purchaser-facing collection does not strip the get of its essential legal character. It remains a valid instrument of divorce or emancipation. This distinction answers Baal HaTerumot. It also supplies a Talmudic proof for Rambam. In the get sugya the Gemara says that, after return, the woman must bring proof of when the get reached her. Our printed Gemara then asks, "If so, other documents should be the same." Shitah Mekubetzet notes that this question was not part of the original Talmudic text but was inserted by R. Yehudai Gaon. Rambam evidently did not have that gloss and did not accept R. Yehudai's answer that a get is returned because it is needed for remarriage, whereas there is no comparable reason to return a loan note. In truth, even a loan note can be returned for a legitimate present purpose, such as collection from the borrower himself or from free assets. The distinction based merely on purpose of return is therefore weak. Rambam instead resolves the underlying difference by saying that a returned loan note returns to its ordinary evidentiary presumption and can be collected upon, while a get remains subject to proof concerning the separate purchaser-facing consequence. This is why the Gemara did not itself ask that ordinary loan documents be treated like a get. In my opinion this is a clear basis for Rambam's ruling.
