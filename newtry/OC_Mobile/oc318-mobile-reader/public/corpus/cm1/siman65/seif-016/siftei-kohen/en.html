There is an opinion that collection may be made with it. The Sema limits this to a document that was lost, and the Bach writes similarly. There is no basis for that distinction. The Tur and Mechaber speak generally of one who returned a document that by law should not have been returned, implying every such document. The Sema himself elsewhere makes no distinction between loss and deposit. Nor is the limitation attributed to She'erit Yosef persuasive. The earlier case in seif 1 differs because the creditor himself does not make a definite claim.
There is an opinion that even if beit din collected with it. Although in siman 46 the Rosh and Tur rule that possession obtained before the doubt arose is effective, there the doubt concerns the document itself and the document had not been impaired. Here the doubt is external to the document, but the document itself was impaired by having been lost. Therefore the collection is reversed.
Even if beit din collected with it. This means the debtor claimed that he owed nothing. If beit din collected on the basis of his admission, it is obvious that the money is not recovered. Similarly, the later clause, 'if he paid the creditor voluntarily,' means that he continued to deny liability but paid because the creditor held the document. The Tur's reason is that by paying voluntarily he revealed that he knew the document was unpaid, despite his earlier protest. Although elsewhere I established that authorized possession is effective even where the possessor cannot prove the underlying right, here payment compelled by the document would ordinarily be involuntary. It counts as voluntary possession only because his conduct demonstrates that he knew the debt remained due. The Baal HaTerumot's proof from the case of a firstborn who died on the thirtieth day applies only to payment made to heirs whose right was uncertain, yet possession is effective because the money entered their hands with the owner's consent. The Tur therefore correctly understood the Baal HaTerumot.
