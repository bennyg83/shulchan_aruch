Also a deed of gift, and so forth. Sema explains that this means a deed of gift containing a kinyan. Without the fact that the documents were bound together, such a deed would not be returned. When the documents are found tied together, they may be returned to the giver. But if the deed of gift contains no kinyan, Sema says it may be returned to the giver even without the binding circumstance. Shach correctly objects that the opposite is more plausible. A gift deed without a kinyan should not be returned at all unless the binding circumstance is present, because we do not know that it ever reached the recipient from the giver. The fact that it was found bound with the others is what permits us to return the whole bundle to the giver. A gift deed with a kinyan is different. Once the kinyan was made, the recipient has already acquired the gift, and the later location of the document does not ordinarily matter. Whether the deeds are tied together should therefore make little difference. On this point Shach speaks correctly. However, above in siman 56, seif katan 27, Shach appears to hold that the same applies to a loan note made with a kinyan: once the kinyan occurred, the creditor acquired the rights. He therefore challenges Tur's ruling in siman 56 that if a kinyan obligation document is found in a third party's hands, it is not returned because perhaps the debt was paid or perhaps the borrower stipulated that the note be delivered only to himself and not to the lender. Shach compares this to Tur in siman 250, who cites Rambam: if a person gives a loan document to a third party without explanation and dies, nothing follows from it. Rosh comments that this is specifically a loan note, because one may write a loan note for a borrower even when the lender is absent, so perhaps it was written for an intended loan that never occurred. But with a deed of gift containing a kinyan, the recipient has already acquired. Shach therefore answers that siman 250 must concern a case where it is known that the kinyan was made without any condition that the document be returned to the giver. If the circumstances are unknown, he says, even a gift deed with a kinyan might have been subject to a condition. This is very strained, as Shach himself recognizes. If so, why did Rambam and Rosh need to distinguish between a loan note and a deed of gift? They could simply have said the case depends on whether a condition was known. Moreover, Responsa Rosh itself makes clear that nothing was known about the circumstances of the kinyan. A deed of gift containing a kinyan was simply found in another person's possession, and Rosh nevertheless ruled that it should be returned. The basic point is different. Rambam's case of a loan document that can be written for the borrower without the lender present must, according to Rambam's own rule, involve a kinyan. Rambam follows R. Asi that without kinyan one may not write the loan note in the lender's absence. If the note already contains a kinyan, why should we still suspect that the loan never occurred? And if the concern is instead that the borrower ordered the note written but conditioned that it be delivered only back to him, the same theoretical concern could arise with a gift deed. Thus Shach's approach makes the two cases collapse into each other. The correct distinction is the ordinary purpose of the document. A loan note is fundamentally prepared for a future advance of money. The ordinary practice is to write a note for the borrower, even before the lender is present, when a kinyan has been made, so that the document will be ready when the loan takes place. The borrower does not intend the note to be delivered to the lender before he actually receives the money. Therefore he may tell the witnesses or third party to return the note to him until the loan is completed. By contrast, in a deed of gift containing a kinyan, if the giver truly had no intention to give, who forced him to make the kinyan and write the deed at all? The natural inference is that the gift itself was intended to take effect. There is an additional distinction. With a loan note, if the borrower had intended the debt to bind immediately, he could simply have caused the note to be delivered to the lender. The fact that it remained with the third party supports a condition that it not be handed over until money was advanced. With a gift deed, even if the giver later changed his mind about the paper itself, the gift may already have taken effect through the kinyan. He can retract from the document while not retracting from the gift, as Rosh writes in the responsum. This fully explains Tur and Rosh. Accordingly, siman 56 can speak of a loan note with a kinyan and still suspect that the contemplated loan never took place, or that the document was not to be delivered until the money was advanced. This concern applies specifically to loan documents because that is their ordinary commercial function. The fact that Tur additionally mentions concern for repayment does not make the first reason redundant. The first reason teaches that even within the term, where ordinary repayment is not suspected, the note may still be invalid because perhaps the intended loan never occurred. The same must be the point of Rosh in siman 250. He does not content himself with concern for repayment, because the case may be within the term. He therefore adds the distinct concern that the note was written in advance for an intended loan that never took place. A gift deed with a kinyan does not share that concern. Therefore, if a loan note is found bound together with other documents, even if it contains a kinyan and is still within its term, the binding circumstance does not necessarily cure the problem according to Rosh. The possibility remains that the note was prepared for a loan that never occurred. See also what I wrote above in siman 39, seif 13, at length.
