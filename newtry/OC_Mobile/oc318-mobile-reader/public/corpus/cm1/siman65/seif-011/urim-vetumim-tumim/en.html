They should be returned to the borrower even without an identifying mark, because they cannot have fallen from the lender. I am uncertain, however, regarding documents written also "to any bearer," which below in siman 66 we rule are acquired by delivery alone and do not require a written transfer. In such a case, why should we return them to the borrower? Perhaps these lenders sold their notes to another person and they fell from that purchaser. If the Gemara is concerned even with remote possibilities such as that a document fell from the scribe, why not also suspect this, which is common enough, since creditors sell notes to one another? Mechaber and the poskim who say no identifying mark is needed can be discussing ordinary documents, which require both writing and delivery. A purchaser of such a note will have a written transfer document. If he comes to beit din and produces that transfer, there is no greater identifying proof that the note is his. Even if the borrower has the original note in hand, the purchaser can collect through his transfer instrument, and the borrower must prove payment. But with a note that transfers by delivery alone, the difficulty remains. I later found Ritva in Bava Metzia who raises essentially this question: how can such documents be returned, since perhaps they had been sold to another? He answers that in truth they are not returned without an identifying mark. Rather, in the case of one borrower from three lenders, the borrower gives the identifying mark and the notes are returned to him, not to the lenders, because the very fact that the borrower can identify all three documents establishes his claim. Then there is no need to worry about a purchaser who fails to appear. Once the loss was announced and the purchaser does not come to give the identifying mark, we follow Torah law and return the lost object to the one who does identify it. The purchaser caused his own loss by failing to appear. I likewise found in Shitah Mekubetzet, in the name of Maharik, that whenever the Mishnah says the documents are returned, it means they are returned upon an identifying mark; without such a mark they are never returned. I am surprised the poskim did not cite these important opinions at all. It seems, however, that Ritva and Maharik wrote this specifically to explain the Mishnah according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who holds in Bava Batra 169 that documents can be acquired by delivery alone. According to him, their question is strong and therefore they require an identifying mark. But we do not rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. We require written transfer together with delivery. Therefore, in ordinary documents we need not fear that they were sold, because the purchaser can prove ownership with his transfer instrument. This is why the poskim did not cite Ritva's limitation as general law. Nevertheless, in a document that by its terms can be transferred by delivery alone, the original difficulty remains. In my opinion, especially in light of Ritva and Maharik, such documents should indeed not be returned without an identifying mark, because we must fear that they fell from a purchaser.
And if they are not authenticated, and so forth. Sema writes that the identifying mark must be the number of the documents or their manner of bundling. An identifying mark within the body of the note, such as "there is a hole next to a particular letter," does not help, as above in seif 6. That is true when the borrower gives the identifying mark, because he could know the physical characteristics of all the notes that originated with him. But if one lender says, for example, "There is a hole next to such-and-such a letter in the note belonging to the second lender," that is certainly a valid identifying mark. If the bundle had fallen from the borrower, how would this lender know the distinctive feature of the other lender's note? There is therefore no reason to treat such an identifying mark differently from identifying the number or bundling.
Who gives an identifying mark in them. Bach writes that specifically three documents are required. With only two, he says, there is no identifying mark of number, and according to Rosh the bundling itself is not an identifying mark. I do not understand this. Where does Rosh say that bundling can never be an identifying mark? Rosh only explains the Gemara's rejection of the inference that a knot must be an identifying mark by saying that perhaps the real sign is the number. But once we rule that a knot is an identifying mark, bundling can certainly serve as one as well. This is even clearer according to Ramah. He explains that bundling is weak only where both documents belong to the same lender and borrower, because both may know how they were bundled. With two different lenders and one borrower, even Ramah should agree that the bundling is a meaningful identifying mark. Why, then, does the Mishnah speak of three rather than two? Consider the case of one borrower from three lenders. The borrower says the bundle fell from him; the three lenders say it fell from them. Their claim necessarily is that the three lenders had somehow placed their three notes together in one bundle and that bundle was then lost. Why should the borrower be believed? With respect to each individual lender, the other two lenders can testify that the bundle fell from the lenders' side. Two witnesses are enough, and we do not ordinarily suspect witnesses of mutual favor merely because each benefits in part. Above in siman 37, seif 5, where separate debts are established, creditors can testify for one another. One might say each lender is personally interested because his own note is in the bundle. But we can divide the testimony: ignore his testimony regarding his own note and accept it regarding the other two. Why should this differ from the rule above in siman 37 where each person's debt is distinct? The answer must be that the Mishnah is not discussing a case where the three lenders or three borrowers are presently before beit din giving testimony. Rather, the finder comes to court and says what he found. Beit din says: in the case of one borrower from three lenders, return the notes to the borrower, because the ordinary presumption is that they fell from him. We do not wait indefinitely for the possibility that the three lenders may later appear and testify otherwise. Where an identifying mark is required, such as when the documents are not authenticated, the court returns them to the person who supplies that mark and does not postpone the matter merely because later witnesses might conceivably appear. If the three lenders themselves do appear and actually testify, each can testify regarding the others, as in siman 37. This explains why the Mishnah specifically teaches a case of three documents. With only two, once we remove each interested litigant's testimony regarding himself, only one witness remains regarding the other. The evidentiary novelty is therefore much smaller. With three, there can theoretically be two independent witnesses for each other's rights, and nevertheless the Mishnah teaches that absent their actual appearance we follow the ordinary presumption and return the papers accordingly. This also removes Bach's difficulty. The number three is not needed because two papers can never carry an identifying mark. It is needed for the testimonial structure and the novelty of the Mishnah's presumption.
The handwriting of one scribe, and so forth. There is a difficulty. We do not rule that the witnesses' signatures themselves create the lender's lien immediately. If so, when can we reasonably fear that documents found in one scribe's handwriting fell from the scribe? If they fell only after the date written in them had passed, then once the day ended without proper delivery, the documents became antedated and invalid. A careful scribe should not retain invalid instruments, for one should not keep wrongdoing in one's tent. He should tear them. Why should we think he kept them? If instead they fell from the scribe on the very day they were written, while still valid, and were only discovered much later, then a more basic question arises. Why does the Gemara ask regarding a non-kinyan document, "How can we fear that it was written for an intended loan that never occurred, since witnesses may not write such a note unless the lender is present?" Perhaps the note was properly written in the lender's presence, then fell from the scribe that same day and lay unnoticed until much later. We must therefore say that ordinarily we do not suspect that a valid document fell from the hands of a professional scribe or the witnesses. As Ritva writes, they are careful in their work. That revives the original question here. It is strained to answer that once a note has fallen we suspect even that the scribe improperly retained it past its valid date. Falling can justify concern that witnesses happened to write in an irregular manner, perhaps because they trusted the borrower to complete the loan that day. But it is another thing to say that they knowingly retained an invalid document contrary to proper practice. I found, however, in another version of Ritva that he treats as a genuine possibility that the note fell from the scribe on the day it was written and was found only after a long interval. In my chiddushim I explained that this helps clarify Rashi's interpretation of the Gemara. Rashi explains the concern "perhaps he had it written to borrow and did not borrow" as meaning that later he actually did borrow on that note, but not on its written date. Since the instrument was legally defective as antedated, he did not guard it carefully and it fell. Rashi does not explain, like Tosafot, that no loan ever occurred and the borrower now wants to use the old paper for a new loan. Why? Because according to Tosafot's reading one could simply answer that perhaps the note fell from the scribe or witnesses on the day it was written. In that case no improper drafting occurred, and the borrower later wants to use the paper. The Gemara's question, "witnesses may not write without the lender," would not solve anything. Rashi therefore understands that the concern is specifically that the borrower already used the note in a later loan. If it fell from the scribe before any loan occurred, we do not suspect that a lender will now foolishly lend money on a document that has fallen and already become legally suspect. Thus the Gemara can still ask that non-kinyan documents are not written without the lender present. According to Tosafot, another answer is possible. At the stage where the Gemara has not yet introduced concern for collusion, the only motive to reuse the note would be to save the scribe's fee. If the note fell from the scribe before the borrower ever paid any fee or benefited from the document, there is little incentive to engage in the improper reuse. Therefore the Gemara need not fear that case. At the later stage, once the Gemara entertains collusion, such a possibility could have been mentioned, but it chooses the stronger concern of payment. This reasoning also resolves a difficulty in Ran concerning Shmuel. Ran says Shmuel does not hold that the signatures themselves create the lender's rights. If so, why does Shmuel require a found document to be a kinyan document? If he does not suspect that witnesses improperly wrote a non-kinyan note without the lender, even a non-kinyan note should be safe. The answer is that where the borrower admits the debt, and collusion is not suspected, we need not fear a note that fell from the scribe. But where the borrower denies the debt, a note could indeed have fallen from the scribe before any loan took place, and therefore Shmuel requires a kinyan document. Accordingly, if the documents are written on different dates and the facts show they were not all prepared on one day, there is less reason to suspect they fell together from a single scribe. Especially where the instruments state that they were written on their actual dates, or other facts prevent an explanation of delayed dating, the presumption that a careful scribe would not retain invalid papers becomes strong. This aspect therefore requires study in practice.
