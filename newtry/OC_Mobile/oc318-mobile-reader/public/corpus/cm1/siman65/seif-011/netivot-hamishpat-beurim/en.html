Tumim cites Ritva that they are not returned without an identifying mark, because perhaps the debtor sold all of them to one person and they fell from that purchaser. It appears, however, that the Mechaber and all the other authorities do not entertain concern for a sale. After all, a lost object is returned on the basis of identifying marks, and we do not suspect that it originally belonged to the claimant but that he later sold it to another person and therefore knows its identifying mark. Rather, we do not entertain such a concern, because we always establish the item in the possession of its former owner. Where we cannot establish it in the creditor's possession, we establish it in the debtor's possession, since every promissory note stands to be paid, not to be sold. Tumim further asks that every creditor effectively has two witnesses, namely the two other creditors who say that they tied the notes together and deposited them with another person. If the issue is that each is interested in his own claim, why not apply the rule that we divide his statement, accepting the part that is not self interested? This is not difficult in light of the Shach in siman 34, seif katan 28, who writes that wherever there is reason to suspect that a witness is interested, we assume he may be testifying for his own benefit, and we do not divide his statement. Moreover, it is possible that the doctrine of dividing one's statement does not apply at all here, because no portion of the statement can be accepted independently of the portion in which he is interested. Only in the case of one who says, "So and so sodomized me willingly," can we divide the statement, believing him that so and so sodomized him while not believing the added words "willingly." Here, by contrast, there is no way to isolate a meaningful statement in which he has no personal interest.
