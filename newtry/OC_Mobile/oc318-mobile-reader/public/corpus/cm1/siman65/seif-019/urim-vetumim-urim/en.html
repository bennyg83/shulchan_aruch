Even if they had already seen it in his possession, etc. A third party is believed even without a migo because the parties initially trusted him, as explained at length above in siman 56. This is so even if one party denies ever appointing him as third party, as I wrote there. See there.
Specifically when the document is in his possession, etc. Even if the third party himself says that he does not know whether payment occurred, the debt is nevertheless weakened because the receipt is beside the document. See Shach, who cites dissenters. As practical law, the primary view appears to be that of Sema and Bach. See Tumim for my resolution.
If it is not validated, etc. Meaning where it is capable of validation. If it were already validated, the third party's credibility would be unnecessary, because it would certainly belong to the borrower. Rather, it is presently unvalidated, but because it can be validated, the third party is believed by virtue of the trust initially placed in him. If, however, it cannot possibly be validated, then the parties did not rely on him regarding it, because even had he delivered it directly to the borrower it would have had no effect, as I wrote above in siman 46. Shach discusses at length here, following Nimukei Yosef, who holds that if the lender admits the receipt is not forged, then even though formal validation is impossible the third party is believed. I already established above that Nimukei Yosef follows Rashi's approach. According to Tosafos and most poskim, Nimukei Yosef's position is not accepted. Where validation is impossible, the special trust in a third party does not apply at all. See what I wrote above in siman 56.
Because we suspect collusion. Although Rosh holds that where a document was not lost and therefore not weakened we do not suspect collusion, that is because with an ordinary document held by a third party there is no basis to say payment occurred when the debtor admits it did not. Here, however, a receipt lies before us and witnesses say that payment occurred. The borrower's claim that there was no payment contradicts the implication of the receipt, creating an irregularity comparable to a lost document. Everyone therefore suspects collusion. See Shach. Perhaps this is his intention. Shach sought to distinguish based on whether the borrower admits the third party arrangement, but this does not appear correct as law. We concluded above with Sema that even where a document is found in the marketplace and the borrower admits the debt, collusion is suspected. Certainly it is suspected where the document is held by a third party.
