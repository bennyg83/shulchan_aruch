He is believed even if beit din already saw it in his possession. This means where witnesses are signed on the receipt. Otherwise, what force would the word "even" have here? Without witnesses, he would have no migo based on the possibility that he could have handed it to the debtor, because handing over an unsigned receipt would accomplish nothing. This is also explicit in the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, which is the source of this ruling. The reason is stated there: the parties trusted him as a third party. Accordingly, the case must specifically be one where it is known that he was appointed as a third party, or where the creditor admits this. Otherwise, once beit din has already seen the receipt in his possession and he has no migo, he is no better than any other person and is not believed. The later statements, "even if there are no witnesses on it," and, "even if the third party died, etc.," likewise all concern a case where his status as third party is known. Otherwise, even if an unsigned receipt, or even a signed one, is found there, where he lacks the migo that he could have delivered it to the debtor it is ineffective. This also resolves the Tosefta which teaches that whether he found the documents in the marketplace or among his father's papers, if simponot are found with them one acts according to the simponot. Since the Gemara establishes that case as one where the document, according to the Rambam and the authorities who follow him as discussed above in seif katan 54, is found among torn documents, the entire passage appears to concern such a circumstance. If he found the document and receipt among his father's papers, however, why should they have to be among torn documents? Rather, the case there concerns a father who was not a trusted third party. This also explains why the Rambam at the end of chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh writes that one who finds a document among his own papers and does not know its status must leave it until Eliyahu comes, but does not separately state the rule concerning simponot found with it. According to what I wrote above in seif katan 25, the Rambam understands the Mishnah's clause concerning simponot as applying to all of the preceding cases, including one who finds a document among his own papers. He therefore relied upon his ruling in chapter 18 of Hilchos Gezelah, halachah 5, cited by the Tur and Mechaber above in seif 8, concerning one who finds documents and a receipt with them. There is no distinction between finding them in the marketplace and finding them among one's own papers where the finder is not a third party and does not remember having received them in trust.
Even if there are no witnesses on it. This is specifically where the debt document itself is also in the third party's possession, as the ruling continues to explain.
And specifically where the document is in his possession together with the receipt, etc. The Sema, seif katan 65, writes that where the debt document is in the third party's possession together with the receipt, even if the third party does not know whether it was paid, we presume it paid, as in the case where the third party died. The opening words, "and he says that it was paid," concern a signed receipt, where there is no need for the debt document itself to be in his possession. So too write the Bach and the Ir Shushan. They must mean that the debt document is then torn up, for otherwise it is obvious that neither document would be returned to either party, as explained at the beginning of this siman. They wrote this in order to resolve the Beit Yosef's question: if the third party himself says it was paid, why does it matter that he also holds the receipt? But their explanation is not compelled. The same difficulty the Beit Yosef raised against the Baal HaTerumos can be raised against Tosafos at the end of Perek Zeh Borer, in their first explanation that a simpon found in a third party's possession is valid where the debt document is together with the simpon. I cited their words above at the beginning of siman 56. Hagahos Ashri there already answers that the simpon serves merely as a memorandum, and Maharshal says the same. The Baal HaTerumos and Tur can therefore be explained in this way as well. This interpretation fits the wording of Baal HaTerumos better, contrary to the Bach's criticism of the Beit Yosef in seif 27. The Bach writes that Baal HaTerumos also speaks where the third party says nothing, but that is incorrect, for Baal HaTerumos explicitly says, "when he says it is paid, he is believed." Nevertheless, Hagahos Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia cites Rabbeinu Tam's explanation concerning an unsigned simpon where the debt document also emerges from the third party's possession. Even if the third party does not know whether it was paid, but merely remembers that a receipt was delivered to him, we say that because the creditor delivered the debt document to him as well, the debt was certainly paid. It appears that Rabbeinu Tam intended thereby to answer why the simpon was needed, as the Sema and Bach explain the Tur. According to this, the creditor must deny having delivered the receipt to the third party, for otherwise why does the Gemara at the end of Perek Zeh Borer ask, "this proves that a third party is believed, etc."? Still, it is possible that Hagahos Mordechai speaks specifically where the third party is alive. If he died, perhaps the receipt would have no effect, because who says that the creditor delivered this particular receipt to him? Yet the Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh, cited by the Mechaber above in siman 56, end of seif 1, states explicitly that even if the third party died and a written notation is found with him stating that the debt document deposited with him was paid, the debt is treated as paid even though no witnesses signed the notation. It appears that the Rambam follows Rabbeinu Tam's explanation, which is Tosafos' first explanation at the end of Perek Zeh Borer, as I wrote above in siman 56, seif katan 12, and uses it to answer why a simpon is needed. He therefore establishes the baraita where the third party died and holds that since the third party did not tear up the simpon, it is as though he were alive and saying that the simpon is valid. Since the parties appointed him as a third party, he should not have left the receipt intact if it were invalid. The Gemara's question, "this proves that a third party is believed," is therefore appropriate. Tosafos may also have intended this. One should not explain that Tosafos speaks of a simpon written in the third party's own handwriting in the form of testimony by the third party, because that is not the plain meaning of the baraita. See there carefully. Nor is there a difficulty from the Rambam, Baal HaTerumos, Tur, and Mechaber above in seif 8, who rule that where one finds a document, even with a simpon, the debt document must specifically be among torn documents. That case concerns one who found it in the marketplace or among his own papers and is not a third party at all. We may therefore say that the document and receipt both fell from the creditor, who had prepared the receipt for the time when the debtor would pay. Here, by contrast, both are found in the possession of a person who is known to have been appointed as third party, or the creditor admits his appointment. The receipt therefore necessarily came to him from the creditor as well. If the debt were not paid, why did the creditor deliver the receipt to him? He should have deposited only the debt document. See what I wrote above in siman 56, seif katan 12 and seif katan 13.
And if it is not authenticated, it shall be authenticated through its signatures. The Tur means that once it is authenticated, the third party is believed to say that it is paid. The Mechaber omitted this because he follows his own question in the Beit Yosef: if the receipt is authenticated, what need is there for the third party's credibility? His objection is incorrect, as Darkei Moshe, Sema, Bach, and the other later authorities have already noted. The meaning is that at present the receipt is not authenticated. Therefore we need the third party to say that it is paid, and then, if the signatures can be authenticated, or once they are in fact authenticated, he is believed afterward to say that it was paid. This remains true even though beit din already saw the receipt in his possession and he therefore lacks a migo, because he previously could have given it to the debtor. The case concerns one who is known to have been appointed as third party, or where the creditor admits it, and thereby trusted him, as I wrote above in seif katan 63. The receipt is then given to the debtor and the debt document is torn. But if the third party says nothing, the receipt would not be given to the debtor while it remains unauthenticated; it would simply be left deposited. Therefore, if the third party dies, neither document is returned to either side. The case is where the receipt is unauthenticated but capable of authentication. If it were authenticated while in the third party's possession, it would obviously be returned to the debtor, because it would be returned even if found in the creditor's own possession, as above at the end of seif 18. If it could not be authenticated at all, it would obviously be ineffective. This resolves the words of the Raavad cited by Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, part 3: if the debt document itself was not given to him in trust, the receipt is ineffective, because who says the creditor made him a third party? Perhaps he is lying. But if the receipt itself was deposited with him as third party, the third party is believed where witnesses are signed on it and their signatures are authenticated, because the receipt certainly passed from the creditor's possession into the third party's possession. Therefore, even without witnesses to payment, the third party is believed. The Beit Yosef cites this. Gidulei Terumah asks: if the signatures are authenticated, we already know the debt is paid without relying on the third party. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The case is where the signatures are authenticated now, not where a formal authentication was written on the receipt itself. Proof is that otherwise, how could the Raavad say that it certainly passed from the creditor's possession? On the contrary, if it were formally authenticated, it would certainly have passed from the debtor's possession, as stated in the Gemara. Further, why was it necessary to say that it certainly passed from the creditor? Rather, his meaning is that because witnesses are signed upon it, one cannot say, "who says the creditor made him a third party?" The receipt necessarily passed from the creditor to the third party. This is clear. Still, it appears that when the Raavad says that it certainly passed from the creditor, he means that the creditor himself is forced to admit that it came from him once witnesses are signed and their signatures can be authenticated. Even if the creditor does not admit this, the third party is believed that it came from the creditor through a migo that he could have handed it to the debtor, provided that beit din did not previously see the receipt in his possession. If, however, the creditor denies having appointed him as third party at all and says that the receipt, or the document together with the receipt, fell from him and this person found it, and beit din already saw the receipt in the third party's possession so that he has no migo, the third party is not believed to invalidate the debt document. His law is then that of one who finds a document, and neither document is returned to either party. This appears correct to me.
It shall be authenticated through its signatures. But if it cannot be authenticated, it is ineffective, as I wrote immediately above. This is also evident from the Tur, and the Sema, seif katan 66, writes the same, contrary to Maadanei Melech at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Maadanei Melech understands the Tur to mean that even if it is not authenticated, the third party is believed because authentication of documents is rabbinic, while Torah law does not require authentication, and in the case of a third party the Sages left the law at its Torah level. He writes that this follows from the plain meaning of the baraita and the Rosh, and challenges the Sema. His words are incorrect. Nothing of the sort is proved from the baraita or Rosh, for they can be speaking where the document will later be authenticated. He also overlooked the words of the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 56, seif 4, that an instrument is ineffective where authentication is impossible. What Maadanei Melech further writes, that Nimukei Yosef explicitly says at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia that the case concerns a receipt without witnesses to authentication, likewise proves nothing. Even if there is no formal authentication on the receipt itself, the case can specifically be one where its signatures are capable of authentication. Still, in my humble opinion the practical law requires study for another reason. Nimukei Yosef at the end of Perek Zeh Borer explicitly states that even if the witnesses to the receipt are presently unavailable for authentication, the receipt is effective even where the third party has no migo, because once the parties trusted him as third party and the signatures could have been authenticated at one time, he is believed. See there. I also proved above in siman 56, seif katan 1, that Tosafos at the end of Perek Zeh Borer holds the same, contrary to the Bach's understanding of Tosafos here. The Bach's objection to Tosafos from the sugya at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia is likewise no difficulty. Tosafos does not accept Rashi's explanation there. Rather, the baraita's phrase, "it shall be authenticated through its signatures," does not mean that a formal authentication is already written on it, because the language, "it shall be authenticated," would not fit. It means that the witnesses themselves are questioned. See there carefully.
Even though the debtor admits. The Sema, seif katan 69, writes that if the creditor admits that the debt was paid, the receipt is returned to the debtor, as he wrote earlier in seif 18, seif katan 58. I already challenged him there in seif katan 49. Accordingly, here, since the debt document has been impaired as explained below, even if the creditor admits payment the receipt is not returned to the debtor, because perhaps the debtor will return it to the creditor.
Because we are concerned for collusion, etc. The Bach, in seif 28, asks against the Tur. Above in seif 5 the Tur cites the Rosh that with a deposited document that has suffered no defect we do not suspect collusion. The same difficulty arises against the Mechaber, who cites the Rosh's view in seif 3. The Bach answers that there the depositary is alive but does not remember from whom the document came, whereas here he died and the document left his possession. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 52, end of part 3, writes similarly. This distinction is incorrect. The plain wording of the Tur and Mechaber above implies that the same law applies even if the third party died. Rather, there was never any difficulty. Here, the receipt was at least deposited with a third party. If it came from the debtor, the debt document is void. If it came from the creditor, the debt document has been impaired because the creditor deposited the receipt with the third party. What could one answer, that with a deposited item no defect arose? If we say that no defect arose, then all the more the receipt remains valid and the debt document is void. That is why above in seif 18, when the debtor admits the debt is unpaid, the receipt is returned to the creditor. There, no defect arose in the debt document, because perhaps the receipt fell from the creditor after he had merely prepared it in anticipation of payment. Here, by contrast, the creditor at least deposited the receipt with the third party, and it is known that he appointed him as such, or the creditor admits this. The debt document is therefore impaired, because the creditor may have deposited the receipt with him for delivery to the debtor. The Bach himself writes something similar above in seif 23, though briefly and without fully explaining it. It is surprising that his words contradict each other. It appears further that if the creditor does not admit appointing him as third party, but says that he lost the receipt and the deceased person found it, the debt document is not impaired at all. Its law is the same as a receipt found in the marketplace. If the debtor admits the debt remains unpaid, the receipt is returned to the creditor. This appears clear to me.
