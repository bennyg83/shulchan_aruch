They saw it. Although in that case the third party has no migo that he could have given it to the lender, we nevertheless say that since the parties initially trusted him and placed the receipt in his possession, they trusted him concerning all that he says about it. Ibid.
The receipt. Then, even if the third party does not know whether the debt was paid, we nevertheless treat it as paid, just as where the third party died. The reason the ruling begins, 'and he says it was paid,' is to exclude a case where he says it was not paid, because whenever a matter can be clarified we clarify it initially and ask him whether he knows that it was paid or not. Ibid.
Anything. Meaning that even if the third party is alive and says the debt was paid, his statement has no effect. The reason is that the third party cannot have greater force than the borrower himself, who, if he possesses a receipt without witnesses, has nothing. This refers also to a receipt without the lender's handwriting, for if it contained the lender's handwriting it would be effective in the third party's possession just as it is effective in the borrower's possession. Ibid.
Through its signatures. Meaning that the signatures must be authenticated by witnesses. The Tur concludes: 'Then the third party is believed to say that it was paid even if the promissory note is not in his possession, by virtue of a migo that he could have returned the receipt to the borrower. But so long as the signatures have not been authenticated, it is as though no witnesses are signed on it at all.' Thus writes the Sema. The Shach writes that the law requires study, since the Nimukei Yosef states explicitly that even if it is presently impossible to authenticate the witnesses' signatures on the receipt, the receipt is effective even where there is no migo, because once they trusted the third party and at one time it would have been possible to authenticate it, he is believed. See there. The Shach further writes that if the lender denies that he ever appointed this person as a third party, saying instead that the receipt, or the promissory note together with the receipt, fell from him and this person found it, and beit din had previously seen the receipt in the third party's possession in circumstances where he has no migo, the third party is not believed to tear up the note. Rather, his law is like one who finds a document: it is returned neither to this party nor to the other.
Admits. The Sema writes that if the lender admits that the debt was paid, the receipt is returned to the borrower, as stated above. The Shach objects that here, since the promissory note itself has been impaired, even if the lender admits, the receipt is not returned to the borrower, lest the borrower later return it to the lender.
For collusion. The Bach asks that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in seif 3 that with a deposited document, where no defect has arisen, we do not suspect collusion. He answers that there the depositary is alive but does not remember from whom it came into his possession, whereas here the third party died and the document left his control. The Gidulei Terumah writes similarly. Their distinction is incorrect, and the plain wording of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch implies that the same law applies even if the third party died. Rather, there is no difficulty at all. Here the receipt was in any event deposited with a third party. If it came from the borrower, the promissory note is void; if it came from the lender, the promissory note has been impaired by the very fact that the lender deposited the receipt with the third party. If one argues that in an ordinary deposit no impairment arose, then all the more so the promissory note must be void because the receipt remains in force and may have been deposited with him in order that he give it to the borrower. Thus writes the Shach.
They do not collect. The Shach writes that this means they do not collect from encumbered property even if the debtor admits the debt. It appears that if the lender does not admit that he appointed the deceased as a third party, but says that the receipt was lost and the deceased found it, the promissory note is not impaired. It is then like a receipt found in the marketplace, which, when the borrower admits, is returned to the lender. So it appears clear to me.
