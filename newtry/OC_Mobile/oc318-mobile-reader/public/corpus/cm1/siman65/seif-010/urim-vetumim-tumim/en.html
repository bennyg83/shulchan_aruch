It should be returned to the person who states their number. Rosh writes that the documents must nevertheless be bundled together. If they were scattered, the owner would despair, because how would he know that they had fallen together in a manner allowing their number to serve as an identifying mark? This requires explanation. Suppose three documents are now found together, and the lender correctly states their number. We effectively know the papers are his. Why should prior despair matter? At least as to the debt itself, a lender does not seem capable of despairing. The borrower still owes him. If the debt is clearly owed, the finder of the notes should be obligated to return them so the lender can collect, just as a person holding an instrument necessary to establish another's right must produce it before beit din. One cannot answer that only the physical paper is lost to the lender, while the debt itself remains. The paper itself belongs fundamentally to the borrower, not to the lender as an independent object. Nor can we say the lender despairs only of the tiny value of the paper. Once the lender's rights in the paper end, the physical paper returns fully to the borrower. A person cannot effect despair over something that is not ultimately his own property. There is also a difficulty from the rule on Bava Metzia 13 that a note without recourse against encumbered property can be returned to the lender to use as a cover for a small vessel. That case involves an ordinary lost document without an identifying mark. If the lender had despaired of the physical paper, it should belong to the finder. One could say he does not despair there because perhaps the borrower will honestly admit the debt. But if so, the same should apply in the bundle case. The lender can expect the borrower to acknowledge what happened. Rosh himself can perhaps be answered narrowly. The Mishnah speaks generally, whether the lender or borrower gives the identifying mark. If the borrower is the one identifying the bundle and seeks return of the paper itself, despair clearly matters because the physical paper belongs to him. Therefore the Mishnah requires the documents to have been bundled, so that even from the borrower's perspective there was no despair. But Tur and Mechaber state the rule generally without distinguishing. Their wording implies that even when the lender gives the identifying mark, bundling remains necessary. The stronger conclusion is that despair can apply even to a debt held by another person. The lender can despair of collecting the debt after losing the document, particularly from purchasers. That explains Rosh. Still, the scope must be limited. Despair may extinguish the lender's purchaser-facing lien, but not necessarily the underlying right to collect from the borrower's free assets. Otherwise, the sugya in Bava Metzia 13 becomes very difficult. There the Gemara works hard to explain why, even where the borrower admits, the note should not be returned because of repayment and collusion. If the ordinary lost note lacks any identifying mark, the lender should simply have despaired of the debt and no further discussion would be needed. The answer is that at the earlier stage, where the Gemara thinks an admitting borrower allows return, there is no despair. The lender can reasonably expect the borrower to acknowledge honestly, and the note will be returned. Once the Gemara concludes that even the borrower's admission cannot restore a note because of collusion concerns, a lender who loses a note without any identifying mark has little hope of recovering purchaser-facing rights. At that point despair can apply. Thus, as to encumbered property, where the borrower's admission does not help, despair can extinguish the right. But as to free assets, where the borrower may admit and his admission can establish the debt, despair does not necessarily operate. Accordingly, if the lender later supplies a valid identifying mark, even though the documents had not been bundled and he had despaired of purchaser collection, he can still collect from free assets. The mark proves the notes are his and the borrower remains personally obligated. This provides a clear proof for Maharik below in siman 163, who rules that despair can apply even to a debt held by another person. Rama cites him: if people despaired of a debt owed by a ruler and the money was later recovered, it can be treated like found property. Taz and the Gaon R. Tzvi in his gloss and responsum objected that despair should not apply to a debt at all. But Rosh's rule here, properly understood, shows that debt rights can indeed be affected by despair. See what I write below in siman 163 at length.
But mere bundling is not itself an identifying mark, and so forth. These words of Rama come from Ramah as cited by Tur. Early and later authorities offered many interpretations. Most are subtle conjectures without firm proof from the Gemara. I will therefore avoid reviewing them all and instead set out what appears closest to Ramah's language and to the Talmudic structure. Tur quotes Ramah: "Although we rule that either a knot or a number by itself can be an identifying mark, here we require both the number and the manner of bundling or tying. The reason is that these are documents involving the same borrower and lender. Perhaps the borrower paid them all at one time and the lender returned them bundled together, so the borrower knows the bundling. Or perhaps when the loans were originally made, the borrower took the money at one time and handed the lender the notes bundled together, so the lender knows the bundling. The one who made the bundle remembers it, while the other may not have paid attention. Therefore the claimant must know the number together with the bundling or knot. This is where both parties are not before us. But if both are before us, and one knows the bundling, tying, or number and the other knows none of them, we give the documents to the one who knows." Beit Yosef asks three questions. First, why does Ramah speak of "these two documents belonging to one borrower and lender," when the Gemara and poskim discuss three documents? Second, the same possibility should apply to the number. Perhaps borrower and lender both know how many documents there were because the papers passed between them together. Third, if the number alone is a valid identifying mark, why require bundling at all? Rama in Darkei Moshe answers the first question well, and the later authorities largely accept his answer. Ramah begins, "Although either a knot or a number alone is generally an identifying mark." If so, why does the Mishnah require three documents? If number were the only sign, three would be necessary because a count of two has no significance. But if the bundling or knot itself is an identifying mark, then even two documents should suffice. Ramah therefore explains that with only two notes involving the same borrower and lender, bundling is too weak a sign. The two papers naturally pass back and forth together. Hence the Mishnah requires three. This resolves the first question very well. Rama's answer to the second and third questions is that Ramah actually follows Rosh: the number alone is the real identifying mark. Bundling is required only so that the finder does not infer despair, as with scattered coins or fruit. The fact that the claimant can state the number supplies the identification. Rama adds that people pay more attention to the number and may separate the papers, so the concern that both parties know the number is weaker. This is very strained. Ramah's wording clearly indicates that both features are required as identifying marks, number together with bundling, as Rashi, Rambam, and Baal HaTerumot also imply. The claim that people are more likely to distinguish number than bundling is not compelling either. A more serious problem is Ramah's distinction between a case where both litigants are before us and one where they are not. Ramah himself says the reason one party may fail to know the bundling is simply that he did not pay attention. If so, what changes because he happens to appear before beit din now? If he never noticed the arrangement, his present appearance gives him no new knowledge. The later authorities strained greatly over this. But all their answers leave the basic question: in every lost-object case, if one claimant supplies an identifying mark, perhaps another person who is not before us could also supply it if he knew about the finding. Yet we do not indefinitely withhold the item. Once proper announcement was made and the other person did not appear, his absence itself is relevant. Ritva says this explicitly. So why should physical presence at beit din matter uniquely here? R. Tzvi Ashkenazi suggested that a person who supplies an identifying mark is stronger when there is no competing claimant at all. If someone else appears and actively claims the property, the case becomes property with an identified adverse claimant and the evidentiary balance changes. That still does not fully explain Ramah. I therefore propose one underlying approach that can be expressed in two related ways. The Gemara asks whether a knot is itself an identifying mark. R. Chiya answers by teaching: "three documents bundled side by side." This creates a difficulty. We in fact rule that a knot can be an identifying mark. Why then did R. Chiya formulate a special rule requiring three documents bundled together? Would he teach an unnecessary non-halakhic formulation merely to avoid resolving the Gemara's question? There is also a textual difficulty. If the real sign is the number, why does the Mishnah say "a bundle of documents," emphasizing the wrapping? And why does the baraita specify "three documents" when the Mishnah itself does not give a number? The answer appears to be that Ramah's concern, that both borrower and lender may know the bundling, is indeed a real concern, but only a weak one. Normally, we do not worry so far. One of the parties may well have rearranged the papers after receiving them. In fact, a careful person often changes packaging deliberately so the prior possessor cannot later identify it. If we treated every prior possessor's possible familiarity with an item's marks as defeating identification, no purchased movable could ever be returned based on signs, because perhaps the seller once knew the object's features. So Ramah's concern is not strong enough by itself to eliminate a proper identifying mark. However, ordinary intermediate identifying marks are only rabbinically effective. Strict Torah law would not necessarily require return based on them. Chazal instituted return. Where there is even a modest circumstance weakening the mark, we need not extend the rabbinic enactment to its outer boundary. We may require stronger evidence. Now add the distinction I wrote above in seif katan 12. The bundling of a small set of loan notes disputed only between a borrower and lender can be functionally equivalent to a highly distinctive mark. The universe of possible owners is tiny. The chance that these particular two people independently know the identical unusual bundling is very small. If so, even after Ramah's weak concern lowers the evidentiary force by one level, the sign may still remain at least an ordinary intermediate sign. Chazal ordinarily return lost property based on such a sign. But there is still another possibility: perhaps borrower or lender entrusted the papers to some third person, who bundled them in the same manner. Then the possible claimant pool expands beyond just the two litigants. The sign becomes only an ordinary intermediate mark, and Ramah's concern lowers it further. It may then fall below the threshold. This explains why, where the two litigants are not both before us and we do not know the full factual universe, Ramah requires two identifying features, number plus bundling. The chance that both features coincidentally remain identical through the hands of the other party or a depositary is remote. Combining two medium-strength marks can produce one powerful identifying sign. Many great authorities considered even in the severe law of agunah whether two weaker marks can combine into a strong mark. Even those who are stringent in matters of forbidden marriage can agree in monetary law that two medium indicators together suffice. This is Ramah's rule. If both borrower and lender are before us, however, and both agree that they never deposited the documents with a third party, and only each says, "They fell from me," the possible universe again shrinks to those two people. The mark becomes much more distinctive. Even after Ramah's concern that both may have seen the bundling, it remains at least an ordinary identifying mark, and that is enough in monetary law. Therefore a single sign, either number or bundling, suffices when both litigants are present and one knows it while the other does not. This explains the structure very well. R. Chiya's phrase "three documents bundled side by side" speaks about the ordinary case where both possible litigants are not before us. In that case the finder needs the combination of number and bundling. The Mishnah's simpler phrase, "a bundle of documents," can refer to a case where both parties are before us. There the bundling itself may be sufficient, even with only two papers. This reconciles the texts and preserves the halachah that a knot is ordinarily an identifying mark. There is also a second route to the same conclusion based on the Gemara's policy explanation. The Gemara says ordinary identifying marks were accepted because a finder is willing to have others' losses returned by such signs in return for the same benefit when he loses something. It asks: does a borrower really want a regime that helps return lost loan documents to the lender? Rosh offers one answer that perhaps the document case involves a Torah scholar. But, as I explained above, another answer is that in a small two-party dispute the sign can be treated like a highly distinctive mark and does not depend on the loser's consent. Now add Ramah's concern. Because borrower and lender may both know how the documents were bundled, the mark drops from highly distinctive to ordinary intermediate. If the borrower is not before us, we cannot assume he accepted a rabbinic regime that may harm him by restoring the lender's instrument. The Gemara's question returns. Likewise, if only the borrower appears, why assume the lender would accept return to the borrower based on a mark that the lender might also know? Therefore, when only one side is before us, a single intermediate mark is not enough. We require number plus bundling, producing a highly distinctive sign sufficient even without the consent-based rationale. But if both borrower and lender come before beit din and each asks that the property be returned according to identifying marks, they have revealed that they accept this evidentiary mechanism. Each asks to benefit from it. The Gemara's policy concern then disappears. A single ordinary identifying mark is enough. This gives a second explanation for Ramah's distinction between both litigants being present and only one appearing. It also explains why the Gemara's question "does the borrower really want this?" is specifically strongest when the borrower is absent. When both litigants personally invoke identifying marks before court, their conduct demonstrates acceptance of the system. Under either explanation, Ramah's words are coherent. This also answers a further question. If the Gemara's initial question was merely whether a knot is an identifying mark, why does Ramah introduce a new concern that neither the challenger nor the answerer mentions? The answer is that the challenger assumed ordinary identifying marks are of Torah force. If so, once the bundling counts as an ordinary identifying mark, return follows despite Ramah's weak concern. The Gemara answers without deciding the constitutional strength of the mark because, after we conclude ordinary marks are only rabbinic, we need R. Chiya's stronger combination of number plus bundling. Accordingly, Ramah's rule fits the final halachah. One final question remains. Ramah's language sounds as though his concern applies only where the documents involve a single borrower and lender. What about three lenders and three borrowers? In that case one could say the concern is weaker because no single pair necessarily passed all the papers between them. Yet the possible universe of claimants is larger: there are six parties rather than two. Thus the bundling by itself is only an intermediate mark, and the same policy question returns. So Ramah's requirement of both number and bundling can still apply in every configuration. His specific explanation involving one borrower and lender was needed only to explain why bundling alone remains insufficient even in the narrowest possible two-party universe. Therefore his ruling is coherent in all cases.
They are given to the one who knows. Shach writes in seif katan 20 and again in seif katan 31 that when a lender is given the documents based on an identifying mark, he may use them only to collect from the borrower's free assets, not from encumbered property. His reason is that even where the borrower admits, a lost note is not ordinarily returned because of concern for collusion. Perhaps the borrower secretly told the lender the identifying mark so that the lender could receive the note and improperly collect from purchasers. I am surprised that Shach overlooked Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, part 2, seif 3. There, Baal HaTerumot records precisely this question in the name of his teachers, the sages of France: why do we return a lost document based on an identifying mark and not fear collusion, as we do where the borrower merely admits? He gives two answers. The principal one is this. When a note has merely fallen and there is no identifying mark, we remain uncertain whether it fell from the borrower or lender. If we restore it solely because of the borrower's admission, then the fall itself has already weakened the document. Since restoration depends only on his statement, we fear collusion. But where an identifying mark establishes independently whose document it is, the fall no longer creates the same defect. The sign itself clarifies the ownership. The situation is treated as though the note had not become legally weakened through the fall, and we do not create a further collusion concern. Rosh intends exactly this when he writes in the sugya of a bundle of documents that the notes are returned to the one who supplies the identifying mark and "the fall does not weaken them, because the identifying mark determines whether they fell from the lender or borrower." Shitah Mekubetzet likewise cites this answer in Tosafot's name. Perhaps Shach intended to challenge the authorities who hold that even a document in a third party's possession is not returned on the borrower's admission, even though it did not fall in the marketplace. According to that view, absence of a fall alone does not eliminate collusion concerns. Still, there is no real difficulty. Everyone can agree that where there is no legal weakening at all, collusion is not suspected. Those authorities merely hold that placement with an uncertain third party is itself a weakening of the document. The fact that the third party does not know its status is a substantive defect. By contrast, where an identifying mark clearly establishes ownership, that uncertainty is removed. There is therefore no basis to reject the great authorities cited above without proof. I did later find Ritva to Bava Metzia, in the discussion of perhaps a woman sold her ketubah, who writes that documents returned based on identifying marks are returned only where they contain no recourse against encumbered property. If they contain recourse, the mark does not overcome concern for collusion. That indeed accords with Shach. Nevertheless, Ritva's isolated view should not override Baal HaTerumot, Rosh, Tosafot as cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, and the unqualified language of the other poskim. However, one qualification is reasonable in the special case described by Ramah above. Where the lender alone cannot recover based merely on the bundling, but if both borrower and lender are present and the lender knows the bundling while the borrower does not, Ramah says the notes may be given to the lender. In that specific situation, the borrower's presence is doing part of the evidentiary work. His failure to know the mark helps the lender. There Shach's concern has greater force. If purchaser rights are at stake, the borrower's participation cannot be treated as neutral evidence, because we do not rely on the borrower where collusion is possible. His silence or failure to supply the sign should therefore not improve the lender's position against purchasers. For purchaser-facing collection, the case should be treated as though the borrower were absent. If, without the borrower, the lender's single bundling mark would not be enough, then the note should not be restored for purposes of collecting from encumbered property. This is especially true according to the explanation I gave above that, when both appear, the evidentiary mechanism works because both reveal that they are willing to have property returned through identifying marks. Purchasers never expressed such willingness. Thus, in Ramah's special two-party-present case, Shach's limitation may be correct. But in the ordinary case where the lender independently supplies a sufficient identifying mark, the note may be returned with its full force, including against encumbered property.
