He need not say how they were wrapped. The number itself is an identifying mark. The requirement that the documents be wrapped together is needed because otherwise the owner may have despaired of recovery, reasoning that there is no reason to think the documents fell together and therefore no usable identifying mark remains. See Tumim regarding whether despair can affect the underlying debt held by the borrower, so that the borrower too would no longer have to pay, or whether despair affects only the physical papers in the finder's possession. I concluded that the borrower certainly must still pay from unencumbered property. As to collection from encumbered property, it appears that the lien is lost. If number is to function as an identifying mark, there must ordinarily be three documents. If the finder announces, "I found documents," two is not distinctive because the ordinary plural already means two. But if he announces only, "I found a lost item," and the claimant says, "Two documents," it is possible that this too is an identifying mark. The Sages nevertheless did not institute such a vague announcement, so that the owner would investigate his affairs more carefully and identify exactly what he had lost. See Ritva, Bava Metzia.
How they were wrapped. If he states the precise manner of wrapping, then under the law below concerning lost property, a knot or wrapping not used by everyone is itself an identifying mark. According to Rosh this would therefore suffice. Many authorities, including Rashi, Rambam, and Baal HaTerumos, disagree and understand that both number and wrapping are required. See Tumim at length.
The wrapping alone is not an identifying mark. Rama states this according to Ramah, who holds that wrapping alone is not distinctive, because perhaps when the borrower originally delivered his documents to the lender they were wrapped in that manner, and when the borrower later paid the lender they remained wrapped the same way. Thus neither the lender nor borrower proves by describing the wrapping that the documents actually fell from him. Regarding number, Rama holds that such continuity is unlikely, because documents would not always remain in precisely the same number without one being separated or another added. Most later authorities disagree, and I likewise wrote in Tumim that the same concern applies to number. Therefore according to Ramah and Rambam both number and wrapping are required. One alone does not suffice. See Tumim.
Of one lender and one borrower. For the reason explained above. The wording of Ramah and Rama implies that this applies even when the three documents have different dates. One might otherwise have said that the borrower knows how they were wrapped only because he delivered them together, which seems impossible if the documents were created on different dates. Yet we still suspect that even though one document is dated earlier than another, perhaps the borrower received them from the witnesses and later delivered them all together to the lender. This is difficult, because with ordinary documents that contain no immediate kinyan, the witnesses may not write and deliver the document to the borrower for later use; they must themselves deliver it to the lender on the day of the loan. Since the document was lost, why should we assume the witnesses acted improperly? The usual rule that a lost document is weakened applies before it was ever used for a loan, when the borrower would not guard it carefully. Once the loan was actually made, the loss does not justify assuming improper conduct by the witnesses. It therefore appears that Ramah and Rama follow the view that witnesses may sometimes deliver the document to the borrower. According to our ruling otherwise, where the documents contain no kinyan, their dates differ, and the borrower gives an identifying mark based on the wrapping, the documents should be returned to him because the concern above does not apply.
And one borrower. If the documents are against two different borrowers or are held by two different lenders, everyone agrees that a distinctive knot or wrapping can be an identifying mark, because Rama's concern does not apply. See Tumim. It is possible, however, that if both possible claimants are not before us, neither wrapping nor number alone suffices even there. See there. The reason the lender's description of the wrapping does not help in the ordinary case is where the borrower does not claim, "They fell from me before I borrowed from you." But if the borrower makes that claim and the lender describes the wrapping, we return the documents to the lender, because according to the borrower's own account the lender could never have learned the wrapping before payment, since the documents supposedly never reached him. Still, for collection from purchasers, perhaps we do not rely on the borrower's admission and instead raise the alternative concern ourselves. See what I wrote above.
We give them to whoever knows. With a distinctive wrapping, even two documents suffice, because the wrapping and knot constitute an identifying mark. Likewise, if there are three documents and one claimant knows the number while the other does not, they are given to the one who knows. If one claimant gives the number and the other describes the wrapping, Rama holds that they are given to the one who gives the number because it is a stronger identifying mark. Sema and many later authorities hold that the two marks are equal, and therefore we give them to neither claimant.
To whoever knows. Shach holds that wherever an identifying mark is effective, it is effective only for collection from unencumbered property, not from encumbered property. Perhaps the borrower supplied the lender with the identifying mark to help him collect improperly from purchasers, and we suspect collusion just as where the debtor admits the debt. See Tumim, where I established that most poskim disagree. Nevertheless, in this particular case, since absent the borrower's presence we would not have returned the documents to him at all, and only his presence coupled with his inability to give the identifying mark strengthens the lender, there is room for Shach's concern that perhaps the borrower deliberately remained silent as part of collusion. See there.
