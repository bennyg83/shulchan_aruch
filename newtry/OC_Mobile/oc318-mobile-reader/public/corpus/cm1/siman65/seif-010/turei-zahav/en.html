Tur, seif 8: "and Ramah wrote," etc. Beit Yosef left these words unresolved, and I have seen many explanations in different directions. I too will offer my understanding, with God's help. The dispute concerns the interpretation of the Mishnah: one who finds a bundle of documents must announce it. The Gemara concludes that the finder announces, "I found documents," and the owner states their number. Rosh and Tur understand that the Mishnah specifically says the documents were bundled so that the claimant can give the number as an identifying mark. If they were not bundled, perhaps they did not fall together and the owner despairs. Therefore Tur writes that the finder announces that he found documents to the one who states their number, teaching that bundling is what creates the duty to announce. Ramah, however, treats the manner of bundling itself as an identifying mark, as will be explained. It appears to me that Ramah was troubled by the Baraisa: "a bundle of documents means three documents wrapped together." The Gemara asks what the finder announces. If he announces the number, why would two not suffice? It answers that he announces only generally. Ramah explains that the claimant must state both the number and the manner of bundling. Why then does the Tanna require three documents, as the questioner asks, and even with three, why must the claimant give the number? He answers that the Mishnah deals with one borrower and one lender. Where there are three documents, the claimant must also know the number, because the other two documents of that borrower and lender create a situation in which both parties know the identifying features, both the number and how the documents were bundled. Ordinarily, when only one party knows an identifying mark, we return the item to him, as in seif 6 regarding a hole next to a particular letter. Here, however, the other party may also know the identifying mark and yet not appear. One can say that one of them noticed the way the documents were wrapped, while the other never paid attention to the wrapping and therefore did not come. He might also not know the number because he thinks his documents were lost separately, as Rosh explains below. Rosh holds that for this reason he despairs; Ramah holds that he has not necessarily despaired but simply did not come based on that uncertain identifying mark. Therefore Ramah requires the claimant before us to state both the number and the manner of bundling. We then know that at least originally the documents were together in this particular form. The remaining concern is that perhaps this claimant himself assembled them in that manner and they actually fell from the other party. But once we know they had been intentionally grouped in one place and not brought together accidentally, both parties who handled them as a group would know at least that they were grouped, even if one did not notice precisely how they were wrapped. That party should therefore have come and stated the number. If the claimant before us states only the number, we can say the documents were separate and the other party did not come because he did not know they had fallen together. Why must the claimant also specify how they were wrapped rather than merely say that they were grouped? Because saying "they were grouped" could be a guess. He must give the specific identifying feature of the wrapping. Conversely, if he identifies the wrapping but not the number, we can say that perhaps he refers only to two that were wrapped and the rest were separate, and that they later became wrapped together. The other party may therefore not have come because he did not notice the wrapping and there is no proven number. Thus the claimant needs both identifying marks, number and bundling. If you ask why we still give the documents to him, perhaps the other party did not come because he knew the court would not return them to him based upon only one sign. The answer is that he still should have come, because perhaps the other claimant knew no sign at all, in which case his single identifying feature would help him.
The continuation of Ramah's words appears to me briefly as follows. Although ordinarily either the number or the manner of wrapping is sufficient, here we require specifically the number together with wrapping or tying. Therefore there must be three documents. If there are only two, there is no distinctive number, and because the case concerns one borrower and one lender we cannot return them merely because one party describes the wrapping or knot: each party could know that detail because the documents had passed through each one's hands. The main proof is that one party came to beit din and the other did not. But perhaps the one who came happened to notice the wrapping while the other did not, and for that reason the other did not come. If there are three documents and the number itself is a sign, why must the claimant state both number and wrapping? Why not rely upon wrapping alone, since the other party did not come to give the number? Because perhaps the documents were not originally in one bundle. The absent party therefore thinks they may not have fallen together and sees no reason to state the number; if he did so and it turned out otherwise he would appear false. If the claimant states both the number and the wrapping, however, we now see that the documents were indeed originally together. Even if the absent party did not notice precisely how they were wrapped, he knew they formed one group and should have come and stated the number. This applies where both parties are not before us. Whenever the identifying feature itself is uncertain, we can explain that the second party did not come because he thought: why should I go to beit din with an uncertain sign when I see no opponent before me? But if both parties are present and the court asks for identifying marks, and one states only the wrapping while the other says nothing, the first need not additionally state the number even though there are three documents. The second party is standing there and sees an opponent who may receive the documents; he should at least state the number, even if he is uncertain whether they were in one bundle. His silence shows that the one giving the identifying mark is the actual loser, and we return the documents to him. The editor adds an extensive clarification. He notes that the commentators, especially Shach, greatly lengthened the discussion and challenged the Rosh, and he seeks to uphold the Rosh. In his view Ramah was compelled to explain that the case concerns one borrower and one lender not merely because two identifying signs are required, but from Rava's rule in Bava Metzia that where one claimant has identifying marks and the other has witnesses, the item is given to the one with witnesses. Even if one might say that where we know an item once belonged to one person and another later gives an identifying mark we return it to the latter because the original owner did not appear, if both appear and claim it, and the former owner gives no identifying mark, we nevertheless return it to the former owner on the basis of the witnesses. Rosh explains that the witnesses need not have seen the loss itself; it suffices that they testify the item belonged to him, and even a strong identifying mark does not overcome that proof. If the Mishnah's three documents belonged to three different borrowers and three different lenders, how could it categorically say that all three are returned to the person who gives an identifying mark when the other lenders or borrowers may be standing and protesting that each named document belongs to them, and we effectively have witnesses from the document itself that it belonged to the person named? Necessarily the case is one borrower and one lender, so identifying marks can decide between the same two parties. According to Shach's explanation, Beit Yosef's objection remains: if the claimant knows how they were wrapped, surely he also knows the number. Shach answers that once he states both signs we are no longer concerned, but this is merely an external assumption that because he knows the wrapping he also presumably knows the number, whereas the three documents should be treated as a single group. The editor also raises a broader conceptual objection: when only one party appears and gives one identifying mark, Shach would not return the documents, although one might say the absent party effectively conceded by failing to come; but when both are present and one gives the wrapping sign while the other gives no sign, Shach would return them to the former even though perhaps the second simply forgot both the number and wrapping. That would make property with an active claimant weaker than property with no competing claimant. Moreover, if both are present and one gives the number while the other gives the wrapping, Shach gives priority to the number. If only the claimant who gives the number is present, why not return it to him immediately? If the absent party also knew the number he should have come; if he knew only the wrapping, even had he come he would lose to the number according to Shach. The editor therefore explains Ramah differently. The reason the finder must announce only generally and not reveal either the number or the wrapping is that, since the documents concern one borrower and one lender, mentioning either identifying mark would alert a dishonest claimant. If the finder announces the number, the dishonest party can come and supply the wrapping that he remembers from when the documents were in his hands; if he announces the wrapping, the dishonest party knows the number. There are two possible harms. With three documents, both the honest and dishonest claimant may appear, each possessing the other sign, causing the documents needlessly to remain until Eliyahu. With only two documents, the danger is greater: because "two" itself is not a distinctive number, once the finder announces that there are two documents, the dishonest party may remember and supply the wrapping and actually take them while the true loser, who has no further sign, does not come. Therefore both identifying marks must remain undisclosed in the announcement. This also resolves Beit Yosef's objection: certainly one who knows the wrapping knows the number, but the point is that the finder must not reveal either one. When Ramah says that this applies where both are not before us, he means at the time of the announcement, when the finder comes before anyone else. Then both signs must remain hidden. If one or both claimants are already before us, however, any one valid identifying mark may be accepted, because the other party either appears and fails to provide a sign or does not appear at all. The editor regards this as close to Bach's understanding, though with an important distinction. Bach's further assertion that where there are different borrowers and lenders the documents are given to the one who gives the wrapping sign is not compelled; rather, as explained, identifying marks do not overcome the proof represented by the named ownership when the opposing party stands and claims the document.
