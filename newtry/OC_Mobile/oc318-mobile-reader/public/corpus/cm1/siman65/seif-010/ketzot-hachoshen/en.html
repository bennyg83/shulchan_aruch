He need not say how they were tied. Rosh explains that tying and number are mentioned together so that the owner can give an identifying mark in the number. Without being tied, perhaps the documents did not fall together and the owner would despair because the number would not be reliable. This raises a difficulty from the Mishnah concerning a found debt instrument: if it contains property responsibility it is not returned; if it lacks responsibility it is returned. If the lender gives a valid identifying mark, why not return even the first kind, since a mark can identify the owner even for collection from encumbered property? If he gives no mark, why return even a document without responsibility, since he has presumably despaired of the paper? Yet despair does not extinguish the debt itself. The answer follows Tosafot in Chullin. Visual recognition is stronger than ordinary signs. If two witnesses identify a murderer only by signs, he is not executed, while recognition by sight can suffice. Nevertheless a lost object can be returned by signs because no one else presently possesses the object. Money already in another person's hands cannot be extracted by signs. Therefore, where the lost paper has not been lost through despair, the lender can recover the document by its signs. Once the paper lawfully returns to his possession, he can use the instrument under ordinary document law. If he despaired of the paper, however, the paper belongs to the finder. Although the debt itself does not disappear through despair, the creditor no longer has a legal right to the evidentiary paper and cannot force the finder to present it to beit din merely because it would help him. If the finder nevertheless returns it contrary to law, the creditor cannot use an ordinary sign alone to extract another person's money. In the Mishnah where the document lacks property responsibility, the substantive debt can remain recoverable independently, and the evidentiary use differs. This also explains why tying is needed along with number. If the papers were not tied, the lender could not reasonably rely upon the number as an identifying mark and would despair. Moreover, as explained in the preceding seif katan, a creditor who has a reliable identifying mark may guard less carefully because he relies upon recovery through that mark. Without a reliable mark, failure to guard the document is stronger evidence of repayment.
The tying alone. The Mishnah mentions two identifying features, number and tying. The commentators ask why both are necessary. It appears that the answer is connected to the Talmudic doubt whether ordinary identifying marks are Torah law or rabbinic. Rava initially says that if ordinary marks were only rabbinic, why do we return lost property by them? He answers that the owner consents to the system of reciprocal signs. But with debt instruments that rationale is weaker, because the borrower would often prefer the paper remain forever with the finder rather than return to the lender. Rava therefore concludes from the Mishnah of bound documents that identifying marks can have Torah force. Many authorities nevertheless treat ordinary signs as doubtful on the Torah level. The combination of two independent identifying marks, however, can attain the force of a highly distinctive mark and be accepted biblically. This is supported by the passage in Chullin concerning two matching physical signs in hybrid animals. Although ordinary signs may not be Torah law, the concurrence of two can be. Thus the Mishnah of debt instruments deliberately requires both the number and the manner of binding, giving two marks. The borrower cannot object on the ground that the system of signs is merely rabbinic, because two combined signs reach the stronger level. This also explains why a single ordinary mark is enough for an ordinary lost object, where the owner accepts the rabbinic system, but a debt instrument requires greater certainty because returning it can prejudice the borrower. Therefore the tying and number together provide the needed stronger identification.
