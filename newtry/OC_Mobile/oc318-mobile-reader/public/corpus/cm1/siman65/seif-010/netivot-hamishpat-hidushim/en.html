Bound together. Number alone is a distinguishing sign. The reason we also require them to be bound is that otherwise the owner despairs, thinking perhaps the notes fell in different places and he has no usable sign. Therefore there must be a strong binding that would not easily come apart. Darkei Moshe and Sema. See Beurim, seif katan 18. Despair does not transfer the underlying debt. It affects only the physical note, so the finder may retain the note until the lender reaches an arrangement with him. Despair applies only where caravans are common, because without a sign the note would not be returned due to concern for another person with the same name from another place, even if the borrower admits. The lender therefore despairs of the physical note. But where caravans are not common and the only doubt is between lender and borrower, or where two people of the same name are locally known, the lender does not despair even if he knows the borrower or the other namesake found it, because he assumes the other person will admit the truth, like a lost object in a place populated mostly by Jews. If one merely announces, "I found a lost item," and a claimant says, "Two notes," perhaps that too is a distinguishing sign. The Sages initially did not institute such an announcement because a more specific announcement allows the owner to search his affairs more carefully and determine what he lost. Ritva. According to Rama, two signs are required: number and binding. Sema.
Who knows. With binding, even two notes suffice, because the binding and knot together are a distinguishing sign. If there are three notes and one person knows the number while the other does not, they are given to the one who knows the number. If one gives the sign of the binding and the other gives the number, Rama rules they are given to the one who knows the number. The Sema says the signs are equal and the notes should be given to neither. The Shach discusses the matter at length and concludes: with two notes belonging to one lender and one borrower, even if both are present and one gives both the number and binding signs, those signs do not suffice, because with only two notes neither number nor binding is significant enough, and the notes remain deposited. With three notes, if both are present and one gives either the number or a binding and knot while the other knows no sign, the notes are returned to the one with the sign. If one gives the number and the other gives the binding sign, they are given to the one who knows the number, because the other's failure to know the number strongly indicates they are not his. If one gives both signs and the other gives the number, they are equal and the notes remain deposited. If both parties are not present and one comes and gives both signs, the notes are returned to him, because the other's failure to appear indicates that he knows no sign. But if the claimant gives only one sign and the other does not appear at all, they remain deposited. The fact that a binding sign alone does not help the lender applies specifically where the borrower claims, "I paid." If the borrower says, "I never borrowed," and the lender identifies the binding, the note is returned to the lender, because according to the borrower the note never reached him at all, so how would he know the sign? See Beurim, seif katan 19. The primary explanation is that number alone does not suffice even though the notes clearly fell from the lender, because his failure to guard them suggests perhaps he had already been paid. When they are bound together, that concern is reduced. Binding alone is insufficient because it is not a meaningful sign against the borrower, who too once had the note. His failure to remember the binding proves little because a person may simply have failed to notice it. Number is different: failure to know the number cannot easily be explained as failure to notice. Alternatively, binding is needed as the physical sign where caravans are common, while number resolves the lender versus borrower dispute. See Beurim.
