Bundled together. See Sema, seif katan 24, who explains that the documents must be bundled because otherwise the creditor certainly despaired of recovering them, reasoning that they must have been lost elsewhere. His words are very difficult, as Tumim observes. Earlier, in seifim 6 and 7, the finder must return a note when the debtor admits the debt even though an identifying mark does not help and the owner may have despaired. Moreover, despair does not extinguish the underlying debt, as Taz explains in siman 163. It appears that although despair cannot extinguish the debt itself, it can transfer ownership of the physical document to the finder, allowing him to retain it until the creditor reaches an accommodation with him. Tosafot in Bava Batra 75, s.v. Keni Lecha Ihu, indicate that a Torah-level sale of a debt instrument can occur where someone finds it after the owner's despair and then sells it back to the owner. Tosafot to Ketubot 85, s.v. HaMocher, likewise support this. Tumim asks how the creditor's despair can transfer the document when the paper belongs to the debtor. There is no difficulty. Until the debtor pays, the physical paper belongs to the creditor, who may sell it even merely for its scrap value, as Ran writes in Ketubot, chapter HaKotev, concerning a bundle of notes. Where one attempts to sell the debt for collection without the formula assigning the underlying claim, the failed transfer of the debt generally causes even the incidental transfer of the paper to fail. But where one sells only the paper itself for that limited purpose, the sale is effective. Tosafot in Yevamot 116, s.v. Mai Amrat, likewise write that the creditor can sell the paper for its material value. The reason is that when the debtor originally gives the creditor the note in order to create the lien, he must also transfer the physical instrument, like any instrument that itself effects a right. Upon payment, the creditor is obligated to transfer it back, because the original transfer was implicitly made on condition that it be retransferred when the debt is discharged. The Gemara and authorities contain many cases in which a sale or pledge of the physical instrument is effective for purposes of retaining it, even without a written assignment of the debt. Where sale of the paper is effective, despair is likewise effective, because despair operates like a gift, as Tosafot to Bava Kamma 66 state. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that the finder must still produce the instrument in beit din, analogous to one who is told, "The document in your hand contains a right belonging to me." This too is not difficult. Here the finder has no obligation to produce it at all, because the creditor's despair transferred the physical paper to him like a gift, as Tosafot to Bava Kamma 66 explain. A donee is certainly not required to surrender an instrument that was validly given to him merely because another party wants to use it. Moreover, if the creditor wants the paper before coming to beit din to collect, he may have to reach an accommodation with the finder. Ketzot further writes that once the finder acquired the note through despair, the creditor can no longer collect on the instrument, because his position is analogous to witnesses saying that a certain person bearing this identifying mark was the borrower while the claimant himself no longer possesses the note. This is incorrect. Despair is like a gift. The situation is therefore comparable to one who sells or gives a debt instrument, and the transferee may collect according to the applicable law. The earlier question from a note without a property lien, which is returned without concern that the creditor despaired, is also not difficult. Where the only uncertainty concerning the found note is between the known debtor and known creditor, there is no true despair. Just as an owner does not despair of a lost object merely because the finder might dishonestly deny finding it, so the creditor does not despair merely because the debtor might deny possession. Even if the creditor knew that the debtor himself found the note, he would normally expect the debtor to return it. Despair arises in a case such as where several people have the same name, or caravans are frequent and there is a genuine competing possibility of another creditor. In that situation the finder will not return the note because of the possibility that it belongs to another person, and the true owner may despair. The rule concerning a note without property liability, however, speaks where the only genuine dispute is between one debtor and one creditor. There is therefore no difficulty.
But the bundling alone. See Shach, seif katan 31, who discusses at length the Gemara at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia concerning a bundle of promissory notes and explains why two identifying features are needed, namely, the number of notes and the manner in which they are bundled. He raises three questions and offers a difficult resolution. His entire explanation is strained, as Chacham Tzvi already observes in his glosses to the Taz. It is also unclear how the Ramah derives the particular rules he states from the Gemara. Nor do we find elsewhere that a person's failure to respond to a public announcement proves anything, since perhaps he was away from home. On that theory the law should distinguish whether he was present when the announcement was made. In my opinion there is a straightforward approach requiring specifically the two features. Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 52, asks why an identifying mark helps at all. Even if we know the document fell from the creditor, should we not say that his failure to guard it impairs the note? He answers that this concern applies only where the note is found loose, in circumstances resembling one who threw an invalid or paid instrument onto a refuse heap. Where the documents were arranged in a distinctive bundle or tied together, their condition indicates ordinary accidental loss rather than deliberate discard. Accordingly, if the notes are not bundled and the claimant merely gives their number, for example saying that three notes were lost together, even if we know they fell from the creditor the notes remain impaired because their loose condition resembles discarded papers. Bundling is therefore necessary to remove the defect created by the fall. Once they were intentionally arranged together in a distinctive form, the reasoning of Baal HaTerumot applies and the circumstances no longer suggest deliberate abandonment. Bundling alone, however, is insufficient for the reason stated by the Ramah. It may still be that the notes fell from the debtor. The creditor could know the manner in which they had been bundled because they were previously in his possession, whereas the debtor might not know the detail simply because he did not pay attention. A numerical identifying mark is different. If the debtor does not know how many notes were together, that itself is evidence that they did not fall from him. With respect to a number, the Ramah maintains that it is not plausible to answer merely that the debtor failed to notice. The Gemara therefore properly infers that the manner of tying or bundling is itself an identifying mark. If it were not, the defect of a fallen note would remain even if the documents came from the creditor. Only where the arrangement has the character of an identifying mark can Baal HaTerumot's reasoning remove the appearance of deliberate discard. The Ramah offers a different reading of the Gemara. He explains that both bundling and number are required because the Mishnah concerns a place where caravans are frequent and there are known to be two people with the same name, creating the possibility of two different creditors. We therefore need two identifying features. Against the uncertainty between two possible creditors, the bundle is a strong identifying mark. If the notes did not belong to this claimant, he would not know how they were tied. Yet the possibility remains that they fell from the debtor, and against the debtor the bundling mark is not sufficient, because the debtor may simply have failed to notice it. The numerical feature resolves the uncertainty between debtor and creditor. Since the debtor does not know the number, we infer that the documents did not fall from him. As the Shach explains in the name of the Ramah, with respect to number we do not readily say that the other person simply failed to pay attention. Number alone, however, although it helps distinguish debtor from creditor, does not resolve the possibility of another creditor. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 28, and see Shach, siman 262, seif katan 5, explain that a numerical mark is not necessarily sufficient where the object was lost in an ordinary manner. In the discussion at 25, Shitah writes that because objects stacked in a tower-like arrangement appear intentionally placed rather than accidentally dropped, their number can serve as an identifying mark. If they are not so arranged and appear to have fallen, number is not an identifying mark, because several objects may simply have fallen together from some unknown person. Thus, between the known debtor and creditor, number has evidentiary force because the debtor's ignorance of the number affirmatively indicates that the notes were not his. But where the uncertainty is between this claimant and an entirely unidentified second creditor, number alone is not a sufficient identifying mark for loose items that may have fallen together. This also explains the Gemara's inference. The Mishnah's principal need for the identifying feature of a bundle is to address the possibility of two creditors. From that the Gemara properly infers that a knot or bundle is an identifying mark. It makes no comparable inference from the number, because in this context number is not functioning as a general identifying mark but as circumstantial evidence from the debtor's inability to state it. The conclusion of the Ramah's ruling likewise follows. If both persons with the same name are before us, in a place where caravans are not frequent but there are nevertheless two known individuals with that name, and the second one cannot state the number of the notes, then the notes certainly belong to the creditor who knows the number. Just as we do not retain doubt about the debtor when he stands before us and cannot state the number, we do not retain doubt about the second creditor when he too stands before us and cannot state it. The case must also be one where the debtor admits the debt, or some other circumstance removes the independent defect ordinarily caused by a fallen note. In this manner all of the Ramah's words are well explained and the difficulties disappear. As to whether an identifying mark is sufficient to allow collection from encumbered property, the primary ruling appears to be that of Tumim, who cites Baal HaTerumot that the creditor may collect even from encumbered property. Once a valid identifying mark establishes the note and the manner in which it was found no longer impairs it, we do not suspect collusion. The Shach in seif katan 7 apparently overlooked these words of Baal HaTerumot, since he does not cite them. The Shach also raises the possibility that the debtor himself told the creditor the identifying mark in order to help him recover the note. Shitah Mekubetzet, in the discussion of a lost debt instrument, notices this and answers that if so the debtor should simply have given the identifying mark himself and then secretly delivered the recovered note to the creditor. In my opinion that answer is difficult. Bava Metzia 19 explains concerning a get that people can say the Sages returned it merely to prevent the woman from remaining an agunah. With a promissory note, however, purchasers may reason: if the Sages returned the document, it must have been for purposes of foreclosure, and therefore beit din must have investigated the matter and established that the note had properly reached the creditor. Precisely for that reason, the debtor may prefer to collude with the creditor by telling him the identifying mark so that beit din itself returns the note to the creditor. Purchasers will then not demand independent proof, because they will rely upon the fact that beit din returned it. If the debtor instead gives the identifying mark himself, has the note returned to himself, and then secretly hands it to the creditor, purchasers remain free to say, "Bring proof." Therefore the suggested distinction does not remove the concern.
