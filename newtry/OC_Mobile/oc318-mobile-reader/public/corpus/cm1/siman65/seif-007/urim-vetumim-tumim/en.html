And likewise if the borrower has free assets, and so forth. Maharshal, Sema, and Bach already objected: perhaps the borrower owes other creditors whose debts precede the date of the found note. They may take the free assets, while the creditor on this note then collects from purchasers. Or perhaps those other debts are later, and this creditor takes the free assets first, forcing those later creditors to collect from purchasers improperly, when absent this returned note they would have had free assets available. Shach correctly concludes that Mechaber must mean that the borrower possesses free assets in such a configuration that there is no concern for any other creditor. However, Shach's example, that public announcement was made for all creditors to appear and none came, is forced. We do not find such a procedure in the Gemara or poskim. If that were the intended solution, then above in siman 37, in the laws of testimony where a witness is disqualified because his testimony may preserve property for a creditor, we should likewise say that after such an announcement he may testify. The absence of such a rule shows how strained that reading is. A more plausible case is this. Reuven lent money to Shimon. Shimon possesses a field that Reuven himself had previously given him as a gift with warranty, subject to the express condition that no other creditor could collect from that field, and that if any other creditor did collect from it, the gift would be void. See below siman 111, seifim 3 and 4. No outside creditor can therefore collect that field. If an outside creditor attempts to do so, the gift is void and the field reverts to Reuven. But Reuven himself can certainly collect his own debt from the field. Either the gift remains valid and the field belongs to Shimon, in which case Reuven collects from Shimon's property, or the gift is treated as having been cancelled, in which case the field has returned to Reuven. Either way, Reuven has recourse to that field. There is therefore no concern for collusion. Reuven has a place from which to collect that cannot be displaced by other creditors. One might still worry that Reuven himself owes another creditor from before the date of the gift. That creditor could seize the field after the gift is cancelled, leaving Reuven to use the returned note against other assets. But Reuven gave the gift with warranty. Any earlier creditor who disturbs it creates a warranty claim in favor of Shimon, so Reuven's own debt to Shimon offsets that concern. There is no improper purchaser collection created. What if Reuven holds another note against Shimon and could first use that other note to take the gifted field, then use the found note to collect from purchasers? That concern can be eliminated by requiring Reuven, before returning the found note, to execute a receipt cancelling every other note he holds against Shimon except the specific note being returned. Other possibilities are too remote. Collusion itself is already only a concern, and we do not multiply extremely distant contingencies. There is also a simpler approach. Tur follows Rashba that an after-acquired-property clause can be revoked before the property comes into existence. See below siman 112. Suppose on the first of Nisan the borrower admits the found note. At that time his father is dying and he is expected to inherit land at any moment. The note contains an after-acquired-property clause, and according to Tur such a clause may even be implied. The borrower now revokes every after-acquired-property lien contained in all other notes he may have issued, while leaving this particular note's after-acquired-property clause intact, because according to his statement he owes no one else. The next day his father dies and he inherits land. Now there is no concern of collusion. If there really are other creditors, whatever their dates, the borrower can prevent them from taking the newly inherited field by virtue of the revocation of their after-acquired-property clauses before the field came into existence. The returned creditor, however, retains the clause and can collect from that inherited free asset. Nor can purchasers complain. The returned creditor still has free property available and therefore cannot improperly collect from them. If one worries that on the same day as the debtor's admission he incurred another debt with an after-acquired-property clause, that is too remote. Moreover, same-day lending has publicity, and the new creditor should have protected himself. This is a straightforward way to understand the ruling.
And it contains a court authentication, and so forth. See Shach citing Maadanei Melech. Rambam states this rule, and Maggid Mishneh cites R. Yonatan that if we do not rule like Abaye, who holds that a lender acquires lien rights through the signatures of the witnesses, then a court authentication is required. Rif, who does not follow Abaye, likewise requires an authentication. Baal HaTerumot copies the same rule: otherwise we fear that the borrower had the note written for an intended loan that never took place.
In truth, however, the difficulty remains obvious, as Maggid Mishneh reports in Rashba's name. What concern can there be on the very day the note was written that perhaps no loan has yet taken place? The day has not ended. Every moment is still a proper time for the intended loan. We are not in Jerusalem where documents are written by the hour. Baal HaMaor and Tosafot likewise raise this point. Gidulei Terumah's lengthy treatment of Rashba here is very difficult to understand. He appears to maintain that even on the same day there is a meaningful concern that the borrower had the document written but had not yet borrowed. For a scholar of his stature, this requires explanation. If someone asks what to do, we would simply tell him: complete the loan before sunset so that the document's date is correct and the scribe's fee is not wasted. What legal concern remains? I found, however, that Ritva to the beginning of Bava Metzia attempts to preserve the earlier authorities' position. He explains that "he had the note written to borrow and did not borrow" means that the borrower ultimately changed his mind and decided not to borrow at all. He then returned to the witnesses and scribe and cancelled their agency. At that point the document became worthless paper and its lien was extinguished. If he later borrows using that old document, he is borrowing through an instrument that no longer has legal force. The lender may not know that the borrower previously cancelled the witnesses' agency. Therefore, Ritva says, we require a court authentication. Once the document was presented for authentication, the concern that the whole arrangement had been cancelled is no longer present. But according to Ritva, one must ask: why does this concern not apply even according to Abaye? If the borrower cancelled the witnesses' agency, the document became a mere potsherd. What can Abaye's rule that witnesses create lien rights accomplish? The answer is that the cancellation matters only where the document itself creates a property lien and therefore requires valid agency in its drafting. An oral obligation cannot create the same purchaser-facing lien. Once the agency of scribe and witnesses was cancelled, the written instrument can no longer create the lien. A receipt is different. It is merely evidence of forgiveness. Forgiveness can be accomplished orally. A written receipt therefore does not depend upon the same formal agency, and cancelling the scribe's authorization does not undermine the underlying discharge. This explains why the Gemara can say that a receipt operates from its date according to Abaye without raising Ritva's concern. Similarly, when the Gemara asks concerning a found get or receipt, "What if he changed his mind," and answers according to Abaye, the principal question may concern the receipt. And with a deed of emancipation, cancellation is less of a concern because a person is not expected to create such a severe religious stumbling block for his slave by knowingly circulating a wholly void emancipation document. With an antedated loan note, however, the underlying debt can still be valid by Torah law while the document is invalid only rabbinically, so people may be less careful. Still, why does the Gemara itself not answer Abaye's question concerning a found loan document by saying that perhaps the borrower cancelled the witnesses' agency? Ritva's approach must be that witnesses and scribes are careful not to release a document after their agency was expressly cancelled. So long as the paper remains with them, there is no meaningful concern. If they were instructed that the whole transaction was abandoned, they would not hand the instrument to the borrower, knowing that he might later use the now-void note. According to R. Yochanan, once a document has actually fallen, however, its very falling creates a defect in its history. We may then fear that perhaps the witnesses happened to act improperly and gave the cancelled note to the borrower despite the rule. This parallels the concern that perhaps witnesses accidentally wrote a note without proper authority. The fall weakens the ordinary presumption that they acted correctly. Abaye, however, does not accept that general inference from falling. Therefore he does not create the additional concern that the witnesses released a cancelled instrument. On his view there is no problem. Rif must therefore hold, like R. Asi, that once a document has fallen we do fear that witnesses happened to violate normal drafting rules, even though Rif elsewhere explains the non-return rule through payment and collusion. Rif's use of those broader reasons does not show that he rejects the possibility of an improperly prepared note. Indeed, his treatment of receipts shows that he is willing to entertain such concerns where relevant. Although this reconstruction gives Ritva's answer internal coherence, his approach remains strained. He effectively treats ordinary loan documents like gittin, as though writing must be performed as formal agency and cancellation of the agency can void the writing itself. Rashba expressly writes in responsum cited by Beit Yosef above in siman 49 that ordinary monetary documents do not require such formal agency. Tosafot Bava Batra 170b say the same. Most other authorities do not accept Ritva's model, and R. Yonatan himself, as quoted by Maggid Mishneh, understands the issue as depending directly on whether we follow Abaye, not on cancellation of agency. I therefore searched for a more satisfactory explanation and, with Hashem's help, found one that resolves the earlier authorities broadly. We first need to address a difficulty from Rama's ruling above in siman 48, based on Mordechai. A borrower who previously used and repaid a note may, on the same day, borrow again through the same instrument if it is redelivered before delivery witnesses. The document can become effective again. If so, why does the Gemara fear that a borrower might later reuse a paid note? When beit din returns the note to the lender at the borrower's instruction, should that not itself count as delivery before witnesses? The court members can serve as delivery witnesses. The renewed lien should become valid. This is exactly parallel to Tosafot in Bava Metzia 19b, who explain that when a husband admits the found get belongs to his wife and the court returns it to her, the divorce can take effect now through that return. The judges or those present function as delivery witnesses. Even if one says formal court return is not itself delivery before witnesses, why suspect collusion based on the cost of a scribe? The borrower can simply redeliver the note before ordinary witnesses and thereby save the cost of writing a new note. Why would he need a false collusive scheme? One could perhaps answer, according to the Geonic view cited by Rambam and Mechaber above in siman 40, that even with delivery witnesses one must still write and sign a memorandum stating that the document was delivered before them. If so, new delivery witnesses do not eliminate the cost of a scribe. But we do not rule like that view. Rama's version, which is primary, does not require the original signing witnesses to serve as the delivery witnesses. The same question applies to making a new kinyan. Rama above in siman 48 says that a renewed kinyan can restore the lien. Why would a borrower not simply do that instead of attempting collusion? One can force an answer that the borrower may not want to make a new kinyan, because a kinyan can impose a binding lien whether or not he later receives the money. Rosh says that this unusual feature itself creates publicity. A person may therefore be reluctant to enter such a structure merely to save the scribe's fee. But the delivery-witness problem remains severe. The proper answer is that the Gemara's case does not concern a document found on the same day it was written and then, on that very day, brought before beit din with both lender and borrower appearing and admitting that it fell from the lender. Such a case is simple. Before sunset the parties remain free to recreate or renew the obligation in any valid way. Rather, the Gemara speaks of a note whose date is today and which was found today, but the finder does not necessarily reach beit din and locate both parties until several days later. The phrase "a note whose date is that very day" describes the time of the finding, not necessarily the day of the later admission. This distinction is crucial. If the note was found on the day it was written, repayment that same day is unlikely. Indeed, if the borrower repaid it that day, the note necessarily would have fallen that same day. Rav Kahana therefore says that, when the borrower later comes and admits the debt, the note may be returned. One might still ask: perhaps the borrower had repaid it and is now colluding with the lender to harm purchasers who bought in the interval between the day of the finding and the later admission. But concern for collusion arises primarily when the fall itself indicates the document was defective. Here the fact that the note fell on the very day it was written is not much of a defect. If the lender had the note and the debt was unpaid, he certainly would be careful with it. If the borrower had repaid it that very day, he too would still have reason to guard the paper until the day ended, because he might wish to use it again that day by redelivering it before witnesses or by making a new kinyan and thus save the cost of a scribe. Therefore, the fact that it fell that day does not strongly indicate payment. There is an additional reason. Ordinarily the Gemara says that if a borrower had paid, he should have torn the note. The lender can sometimes defer by saying, "I do not have it with me now; I will give it tomorrow." But with a document written and allegedly repaid within moments on the same day, such an excuse is difficult. It is implausible that the lender could immediately say the note is unavailable. So again, same-day payment is not a strong concern. This explains why the Gemara asks, "What novelty is there?" The note's falling on the day it was written does not meaningfully weaken it. The Gemara answers that one might still fear the borrower intends to reuse the paid note later. The novelty is that we do not suspect such a scheme, because the lender himself would not accept a defective instrument if the Sages might later discover the facts and deprive him of collection. Now all the earlier questions disappear. Redelivery before witnesses or a renewed kinyan helps only on the same day. If the borrower returns to court days later, the old date would make the note antedated. A new witnessed delivery cannot make a note dated days earlier collect from the proper date. The same is true of a new kinyan. That is why one cannot simply cure the document after time has passed. This also explains why the lender might care about the expense of a new instrument. The borrower may want to reuse the old paper rather than pay for a replacement. But because its date is now old, the lender would gain an improper lien and therefore risks rabbinic sanction.
However, one might still ask: if days have passed between the note's date and the borrower's later admission, then the lender gains the benefit of those intervening days by using the old note. If he gains something, why should the Gemara say the lender himself will refuse because there is no gain? The answer is a two-sided analysis. If property was sold or another creditor acquired rights during those intervening days, then the borrower himself will not ordinarily collude merely to save the scribe's fee and cause unlawful loss to purchasers. Tosafot say that a borrower who already benefited from the original loan generally will not harm purchasers merely to save the document-writing expense. If, on the other hand, there were no purchasers or intervening creditors, then the lender gains nothing from preserving the old date. A new note dated today would put him in the same position. In that case, the lender has no reason to accept the legal risk of using the defective old paper. Thus, either there were intervening rights, in which case the borrower is unlikely to engage in the scheme, or there were none, in which case the lender gains nothing from it. This is the force of the Gemara's reasoning. Now we can understand why a court authentication is still required. Even though the note was found on the same day it was written, the borrower's admission may occur only later. One must therefore still fear that the note was originally written for an intended loan that never occurred. If the lender already had the note and later completed the loan, the old date can create real benefit. Since the borrower had not yet actually received the loan on the written date, he may be willing to use the old paper to save the scribe's fee, even though this can harm purchasers. Tosafot explicitly say that where he has not yet benefited from the original scribe's fee through an actual loan, his willingness to reuse the document is more plausible. Therefore we require an authenticated document, which removes the concern that the paper was merely prepared for a contemplated loan that never took place. Rambam, Rif, and the other authorities are now precise. There is another point in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, part 2, law 8, that requires explanation. He writes that if a note dated that day is found, the borrower admits, and the note bears court authentication, it may be returned. He gives two reasons why repayment is not suspected: same-day repayment is uncommon, and the borrower admits he did not repay. Why mention both? Rav Kahana's interpretation itself uses the borrower's admission. Once he admits, why is the unlikelihood of same-day repayment needed at all? The answer is that Baal HaTerumot understands Rav Kahana as relying on the combination of two weak factors. Collusion is already an uncommon concern. Same-day repayment is also uncommon. We do not combine two uncommon possibilities and say: perhaps he repaid on the very day the note was written, and perhaps he is now falsely admitting the debt in collusion with the lender. Rav Kahana disagrees with R. Elazar only in how much weight to give the same-day presumption. R. Elazar would return the note even without the borrower's admission because same-day repayment is itself too unlikely. Rav Kahana requires the admission as an additional factor. Once both are present, return is allowed. This reading also explains Rosh's responsum, siman 76, law 3, concerning a receipt that purports to cancel all documents. Rosh writes that although in the found-note sugya we say same-day repayment is not suspected, that is where the borrower is not before us, but here the debtor stands and cries that he paid. At first glance this is difficult, because Rav Kahana specifically requires an admitting borrower. His view seems to reject the very principle that same-day repayment is not suspected. But according to what I have explained, Rosh accepts that Rav Kahana too recognizes the presumption. He merely does not rely on it alone when the note has fallen. Where the borrower affirmatively says he paid, that direct claim can overcome the weak same-day presumption. Likewise, Baal HaTerumot's two reasons are deliberate and cumulative. This also clarifies another passage in Baal HaTerumot. He writes that the same-day rule applies only on that day. For a document of an earlier date, even if there is authentication and the borrower now admits, it is not returned because we suspect repayment. Gidulei Terumah asks why Baal HaTerumot needs to cite a separate statement of R. Yochanan for this. Could he not derive it from the same-day law itself? If the same-day note is returned, the obvious implication is that an older note is not. According to what I have written, the implication is not automatic. One could have understood R. Yochanan as saying a same-day note may be returned because collusion is never suspected when the borrower admits and the document is authenticated. If so, an older note should likewise be returned. The separate statement is therefore needed to establish that once the date is old, we do suspect repayment despite the borrower's admission. The same-day rule depends specifically on the added factor that same-day repayment is unlikely. There is no redundancy. Gidulei Terumah further asks that Tosafot interpret R. Yochanan's other statement as a case where the borrower denies liability. Baal HaTerumot, however, understands it even where the borrower admits. There is no serious objection here. Tosafot were forced into their reading by a particular proof structure, and Maharshal and Maharsha already struggled greatly to explain why. Baal HaTerumot was not bound to accept that strained interpretation and could understand the statement in its broader form. Indeed, there is another reason to reject Tosafot's limitation. The Gemara initially explains the rule "all judicial acts are returned" through a case where the borrower had previously been established as a liar. Rashi explains that he once falsely denied the debt and therefore is no longer believed to say it was paid. If even a debtor who presently stands in court and says, "I did not pay," is still disregarded because we fear collusion, then why would his status as a liar matter? Being a liar only prevents him from credibly saying "I paid." It cannot make his present denial of payment stronger than an express admission in court. This shows that Tosafot's limitation belongs only to an earlier stage of the discussion. Once the Gemara abandons the liar explanation and resolves the judicial-act clause differently, we can return to the broader reading that R. Yochanan's rule applies even where the borrower admits. This is likely Baal HaTerumot's intent when he says that even if the borrower had previously been established as a liar, the document should not necessarily be returned. Thus the entire framework is consistent.
And there is an opinion that says, and so forth. The wording is not arranged very clearly, but the intent is that the borrower's admission is not required. Even if he is not before us and therefore is not presently denying the debt, the note may be returned to the lender. If, however, the borrower stands before beit din and cries, "It was paid," then it is not returned. This opinion follows Responsa Rosh, which holds that we do not ordinarily suspect repayment on the very day of the loan. The later authorities already objected that Rav Kahana specifically establishes the case where the borrower admits. Without such an admission, the Gemara appears to suspect payment. See what I wrote in the preceding seif katan in defense of Rosh. Mechaber can be understood as follows. Rosh states generally that same-day repayment is not suspected, indicating that he regards this as a real presumption. One might ask: if so, why does Rav Kahana require the borrower to admit? Rosh may have considered the Gemara's reason, "the lender himself will not leave a valid arrangement and rely on an improper one lest the Sages discover it and cause him to lose," somewhat difficult in a note written and repaid on the same day. How would the Sages or witnesses ever know which particular instrument had been repaid? Documents are not written by the hour. Everything happened on the same date. Even if witnesses remember a payment, how can they distinguish whether the note presently used was the one repaid earlier that day or a new obligation created later that same day? Bava Batra 35 says that when two deeds bear the same date and hours are not written, it may be impossible to determine which came first. Therefore, in a true same-day case the lender may not seriously fear that the Sages will expose the earlier payment. Rosh can therefore hold that Rav Kahana's concern is different. The found note might actually be postdated. If so, its apparent date is today, but the loan and payment occurred earlier. Then the same-day repayment presumption does not apply. Rav Kahana requires the borrower's admission to remove that concern. In the era discussed above, postdated documents were not ordinarily written unless the document expressly indicated that it was postdated. But once a document has fallen and thereby become defective, we sometimes fear that the witnesses may have deviated from proper practice and written a postdated document without the required notation. R. Asi may distinguish between the common error of writing a loan note for a borrower without the lender present and the more unusual error of silently postdating a document. Rav Kahana may not distinguish as sharply and can entertain both. This resolves the Gemara. If the note is truly dated the day it was written and was repaid that same day, repayment is not suspected. If it is actually postdated, then the loan and payment may be older, and ordinary repayment concerns return. Rav Kahana's reason that the lender fears the Sages works especially well in the postdated scenario. Because the loan, payment, and later note date are separated in time, there may be witnesses or other circumstances proving the earlier history. The lender risks exposure. Thus, on either side of the dilemma, return is allowed where the borrower admits. Accordingly, when Mechaber speaks of a document in which there is no possibility of postdating, then even the borrower's admission is unnecessary. If the borrower is not before us and does not presently claim payment, the same-day presumption suffices. This could be a note that expressly says, "We wrote this on its actual date," or a note whose terms make postdating impossible, or a document whose factual description proves it could not have been written earlier, such as identifying the creditor as a son-in-law when that relationship did not exist before that day. In such a case, there is no postdating concern, and same-day repayment is not suspected. The ruling of Mechaber is therefore defensible. One might still ask how the Sages could discover the lender's scheme in the true same-day case. Perhaps the borrower later admits the truth and forces the lender to swear. The lender will not swear falsely. Even if he has a migo that there was a new loan, the old document itself is now invalid for collection from purchasers. A migo cannot transform a worthless note into a purchaser-facing lien. This also sheds light on Tosafot's statement that the mention of court authentication may itself be a novelty. If the borrower must later redeliver the note before new witnesses, one can write a separate memorandum of the delivery without obtaining a new court authentication. But if days have passed, a wholly new document is needed and the existing authentication can save expense. Therefore the incentive structure differs depending on whether the borrower returns immediately or later.
And regarding what Gidulei Terumah asked, Tosafot interpret R. Yochanan's statement as involving a borrower who denies liability, whereas Baal HaTerumot understands it even where the borrower admits. There is no serious objection here. Tosafot were forced into their reading by a particular proof structure, and Maharshal and Maharsha already struggled greatly to explain why. Baal HaTerumot was not bound to accept that strained interpretation and could understand the statement in its broader form. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes that the authentication and borrower's admission help specifically on the same day, while an older note is not returned. Gidulei Terumah asks why he needs a separate proof for the older note. According to the analysis above, the same-day rule could have been understood as a broad rule that an authenticated note is returned whenever the borrower admits, because collusion is not suspected. The separate teaching establishes that the leniency depends on the additional same-day presumption. Once that factor disappears, repayment is again suspected.
