Likewise, if the borrower has other creditors, etc. Shach says this means it is known that the borrower owes no one except this particular lender. Otherwise, what difference does the existence of free assets make? Earlier creditors may take those assets, leaving this lender to pursue encumbered property. See Tumim, where I describe several arrangements in which this lender, and not any other creditor, would necessarily collect those free assets. Without such circumstances, the mere existence of free assets does not solve the problem.
And it bears an authentication, etc. See Tumim. The case is where the document was found on the same day it was written. Before the finder reached beit din, however, the day had already ended, and only then did the borrower appear and admit liability. Therefore authentication is required, lest the deed have been written for a contemplated loan that never occurred. Once the day passed, the instrument cannot simply be used for a new loan. If the borrower admitted on the same day, no authentication would be needed because there is still time for the loan to occur validly that day. See there at length.
Some say, etc. The wording is somewhat disordered. The intended meaning is that the Gemara's rule, “If it is found on the day it was written, return it,” is not limited to a borrower who admits liability. According to this opinion, even if the borrower is absent, it may be returned because we do not suspect repayment on the very same day. But if the borrower stands and protests, “I paid,” it is not returned. See Tumim for my reconciliation of Rosh. Although I worked to defend Rosh, virtually all later authorities rule like the first view, requiring the borrower's admission. All of this assumes we cannot say the deed was intentionally postdated, for example because it states, “This document was written on its proper date.” Otherwise even the latter view agrees it should not be returned.
