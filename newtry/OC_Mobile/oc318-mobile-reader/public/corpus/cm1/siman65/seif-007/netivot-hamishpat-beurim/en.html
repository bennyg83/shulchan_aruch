Unencumbered property sufficient for collection. See the Sema, through his statement that otherwise there is a definite loss to purchasers. The primary explanation follows the Gemara's discussion of a gift deed, which may be written for the donor even when the recipient is not present. The Gemara asks that perhaps the donor will borrow money in the interim, so that a creditor should be entitled to collect from this land but will be prevented by the earlier-dated gift deed. The Gemara distinguishes that concern from a case where a creditor would wrongfully seize land, because in the latter case the land would actually be held through robbery, whereas a gift recipient who uses an earlier-dated deed to prevent a creditor from exercising his lien certainly prevents collection improperly, but the field itself is not literally stolen property in his hands. The Gemara therefore did not suspect collusion merely because it would prevent enforcement of a lien. It was concerned only where the consequence would be that property itself is held through outright wrongful taking. It is possible to fit this into the Sema's words as well: preventing foreclosure is not called a "definite loss" of the same kind. This also answers the difficulty from the law of a gift deed. As to the Shach's question that perhaps there is an earlier note, the creditor will collect from the debtor's unencumbered property, and the holder of this found note will then seek to collect from purchasers, the matter can be resolved from Bava Metzia 19. There the Gemara says that a lost note has publicity. When its holder later seeks to collect from purchasers, they can tell him, "Bring proof of when the note came into your possession." In the case of a get, by contrast, people may say that the Sages returned it merely so that the woman not remain an agunah. But with a debt instrument, purchasers can reason that if beit din returned it to the creditor, the obvious purpose was collection, and might infer that beit din investigated the matter and established that the note reached him before the relevant date. Where there is other unencumbered property available for a creditor, however, the purchaser can still demand proof. He can say: perhaps beit din returned the note only so that this creditor could collect from the debtor's unencumbered property. Beit din knew at the time that unencumbered property remained available, while it did not know of another earlier debt that would consume it. Therefore, when the holder later seeks to collect from purchasers, they may demand proof of when the note actually came into his possession. If he cannot prove it, he will not collect from them, and no loss results. Taz writes similarly regarding a receipt, that purchasers can say the document was returned only to enable the debtor to defend against the particular creditor, not to support collection from purchasers. The authorities can be understood the same way here. Where unencumbered property remained available, the return of the note proves at most that it may be used to collect there, not that it may be used to foreclose upon purchasers. Only where the debtor had no unencumbered property at all could purchasers argue that by returning the note beit din must necessarily have determined that it was valid for foreclosure. If, when it was found, there was known to be unencumbered property, purchasers can say that beit din did not know of the earlier debt and returned it only for collection from that property. They may therefore still demand proof.
If the debtor admits the debt, it should be returned. See Sema, seif katan 22, who answers the Maggid Mishneh's question why validation by beit din is required. The Sema writes that once the note has been impaired by having fallen, we may suspect that the creditor presently lacks funds and will not actually lend until some later time, yet will collude with the debtor to use the earlier date. The debtor may cooperate in order to avoid losing the scribe's fee. If the note has already been judicially validated, however, this concern does not apply. At first sight this is difficult. Even if the note has been validated, perhaps the original debt was paid and the parties now intend to use the old note for a new loan several days later because the creditor currently lacks money. The creditor would have an economic reason to do this, as the Gemara indicates there and on 17, and the debtor may cooperate to save the scribe's fee. The Sema's intent appears to be as follows. According to Rashi's view, cited above by the Shach in siman 39, a note is not written for the debtor even when the creditor is present unless the witnesses actually see the money delivered, because perhaps the money will not be lent until Tishrei. On that view, where the found note has not been validated it certainly cannot be returned. It is no better than a newly written note that cannot be given to the creditor without the witnesses seeing the money. Once it has been validated, however, the special concern that an already paid loan will be revived through the old note is weaker. Tosafot to Bava Metzia 13, s.v. Hai Ta'ama DeChayshinan, write that a debtor will not cause purchasers to lose a large sum merely to save the small fee paid to the scribe. The distinct concern that a note was written for a contemplated loan that never occurred exists because the debtor has already paid the scribe and does not wish to waste that expense. This explains why on Bava Metzia 17 the Gemara, when discussing a debt that had already been paid, needed to add that the creditor himself would not agree to reuse the note. It did not suffice to say that the debtor would not cooperate merely to save the scribe's fee. As Shitah explains, where no purchaser can be harmed, such as a same-day reuse, the debtor may indeed cooperate to save the fee. The Gemara therefore needed the creditor's lack of incentive as an additional reason. Accordingly, when a note is found on the same day, we need not suspect that it was already paid and will be reused. If the new loan is to occur that same day, the creditor has no gain from using the old note rather than making a fresh arrangement. If the new loan is to occur later, the debtor will not agree to impose a serious loss upon purchasers merely to save the scribe's fee, as Tosafot write. This differs from the concern that the note was written for a loan that never occurred. In that case the debtor has already incurred the scribe's cost and may permit the loan to take place later rather than waste it. Even according to Tosafot, who permit writing a note for the debtor when the creditor is present although the witnesses did not see the money delivered, there is no contradiction. In the ordinary case we do not suspect that debtor and creditor intentionally write an antedated loan document as a collusive device, because absent the concrete saving of a previously incurred scribe's fee the debtor will not willingly harm purchasers. This is also implicit in the Sema's wording that collusion is feared because the debtor will do it to save the scribe's fee. Where there is no such saving, we do not suspect him of colluding. Nor need we fear that the creditor promised to lend immediately but did not actually deliver the money until the following day. In such a case the loss of the scribe's fee would fall upon the creditor, not the debtor, because the creditor would have to reimburse the debtor for the writing expense, as explained by the Sema in siman 39, seif katan 46. The debtor therefore has no reason to cooperate. Only where the creditor is not present do we fear that time may pass naturally because the debtor cannot find someone to lend to him. The debtor may then ultimately borrow even after the date written in the note so that the scribe's fee not be wasted. Ordinarily, where the note never fell, we do not suspect intentional collusion. Here, because it fell and was impaired, we entertain the unusual possibility that the debtor himself had the note written and now wishes to make use of it to avoid that expense. This is precisely what the Sema indicates by saying that because the note fell we suspect that the creditor presently has no money and may lend only later. This distinction is clear, and the many objections raised to the Sema on this point are unnecessary. There is, however, another sound approach, based upon Ritva's explanation of Bava Metzia. Ritva explains that "he wrote a note in order to borrow and did not borrow" means that the debtor ultimately decided not to borrow at all and went to the scribe and witnesses to revoke their agency. Once he did so, the instrument became like a broken shard and its lien was nullified. If he later borrows using that same paper, he is effectively lending on a document without force. The creditor may not know that the debtor previously revoked the witnesses' agency. That is why validation is required. Tumim raises several objections. First, if that is the concern, why did Abaye later need to invoke the concern of repayment rather than simply the possibility that the witnesses' agency was revoked? Ketzot HaChoshen answers that according to Abaye, who holds that witnesses acquire rights for the creditor through their signatures, it is as though the note reached the creditor retroactively, after which revocation of the agency cannot help. Tumim's attempted answer to another objection of Pilpula Charifta is difficult. Tumim also questions Ritva's apparent treatment of a monetary document like a get, where revocation of agency may prevent later delivery, even though monetary documents do not require preparation specifically for the recipient in the same manner. At first sight that objection is powerful, but after examination Ritva's words are correct. The key is not that a loan document requires the same personal preparation as a get. Rather, revocation can prevent the document from operating against encumbered property because the debtor has the ability to withdraw from having the document issued in a manner that creates publicity. To explain this, consider the Gemara in Bava Metzia 19. It asks: if the debtor admits the debt, why not return the note even where it contains a property lien? Rabbi Elazar answers that perhaps the debtor had the note written in Nisan but did not borrow until Tishrei. The Gemara asks that if it is an acquisition document, the debtor already subjected himself through the acquisition, whereas if it is not an acquisition document, witnesses do not write the note when the creditor is absent. It answers that because the note fell, we suspect the exceptional occurrence that it was improperly written. Abaye says that witnesses, through their signatures, acquire for the creditor, because he found it difficult to accept the concern that witnesses would improperly write in the creditor's absence. The Gemara then challenges Abaye from the Mishnah concerning found gittin, where we suspect that the sender changed his mind. If the witnesses' signatures themselves acquire for the recipient, why should such reconsideration matter? The Gemara answers that with a get actual delivery is required. It then challenges Abaye from the Mishnah of a found promissory note that is not returned and answers that we suspect payment and collusion. Tosafot ask why Abaye needed the doctrine that witnesses acquire through their signatures. He could simply have accepted Rabbi Elazar's framework and explained that the note is not returned because of concern for payment. The question is substantial. There is another difficulty. The Gemara asks regarding an acquisition document, "he has already obligated himself." Yet the Sema in siman 243, seif katan 16, explains the principle that one may retract from the deed but not from the underlying field. Ordinarily, witnesses to an acquisition create publicity because every acquisition stands for writing. But if the transferor protests to the witnesses that they not write the deed, so that no public notice emerge, the witnesses will honor that protest and purchasers will not know to guard themselves. The recipient therefore cannot collect from purchasers, although he can still collect from unencumbered property. The same applies to a promissory note, even if the deed has already been written but the debtor protests against its delivery. See Sema, siman 39, seif katan 13. Why, then, does the Gemara not establish its case as an acquisition document where the debtor does not presently admit the note, which itself functions as a retraction concerning the written instrument? Such a protest could cancel collection from encumbered property while leaving the underlying debt effective against unencumbered property. The note should therefore not be returned if it contains a lien, but might be returned if it has no lien and serves merely as evidence. One cannot answer that once a person may retract from the written instrument, even a note without a property lien should not be returned because the debtor can say that he does not wish to have many documents outstanding against him. Siman 39, seif 4, provides that he may go before beit din and have evidence written, and all the more so an existing note serving only as proof may be returned where it cannot affect purchasers. Even if we require a protest specifically before the signing witnesses because the Sema's reason is that those witnesses then suppress publicity, the difficulty remains. Since the note fell and is impaired, perhaps the debtor had previously protested to those witnesses, thereby preventing publicity and canceling the note's force against purchasers, and the document then fell from the scribe or witnesses. Bava Metzia 20 expressly considers loss from the scribe or witnesses in the case of several borrowers. Alternatively, perhaps by an unusual occurrence the witnesses delivered it to the debtor after his protest and it then fell from him. Ritva's language that perhaps he revoked their agency must likewise mean that the document fell from the scribe or witnesses, or that by an exceptional occurrence they gave it to the debtor, as Tumim notes. This is Ritva's true intent when he says that after revoking their agency the note is like a broken shard and cannot later be used for a new loan. He does not mean that a monetary document is invalid for lack of recipient-specific preparation, like a get. He means that with respect to encumbered property the written instrument has lost its force, because the debtor can retract from the deed even though he cannot retract from the underlying acquisition. The Sema's rule establishes precisely this. This also explains an apparent difficulty with Shmuel's ruling that one who finds an acquisition note should return it. Why are we not concerned that the debtor previously protested against the deed and thereby canceled its ability to bind purchasers? The answer must be that with an acquisition document we ordinarily do not suspect that the debtor instructed the witnesses, "Give it to me," as the Shach states in siman 56, seif katan 28, even though with a non-acquisition document we may suspect such a direction. Just as we do not ordinarily suspect that at the time of the acquisition the debtor said, "Give it to me," so we do not suspect that he later returned to the witnesses and instructed them to give it to him, and all the more so we do not suspect that he told them not to give it to the creditor at all. Even if one were to say that a protest made before beit din not to deliver an acquisition document can cancel foreclosure against purchasers, there is still no difficulty when the debtor presently does not admit the note. One might have thought that his present protest itself could cancel the document if it had not yet reached the creditor. But with an acquisition document there is no realistic concern that the note fell from the debtor, because the witnesses deliver it only to the creditor. The only possibility would be that it fell from the scribe or witnesses. With a valid document already belonging to the creditor because of the acquisition, the witnesses are especially careful not to lose it. A person is more careful not to cause another's loss than to avoid his own loss. It is true that Bava Metzia 20 and the later seif 11 entertain the possibility that a document fell from the scribe or witnesses. That applies to a note without an acquisition, which does not yet belong to the creditor before actual delivery and has no monetary value in his hands. With an acquisition document, by contrast, the note belongs to the creditor in a meaningful sense even before delivery, because the underlying obligation cannot be canceled. The witnesses are therefore careful with it. This framework also resolves Tosafot's question. At first the Gemara understood Abaye's doctrine, that witnesses acquire for the creditor by their signatures, to apply even before the note actually reached the creditor and even if no loan was ever made. Yet even if that were so, if the debtor protested to the witnesses against delivery, at least the note's force against purchasers would be canceled. It would be no stronger than an acquisition document, which itself does not require actual delivery, and nevertheless loses publicity where the transferor protests, as the Sema states in siman 243. Although an acquisition ordinarily suffices to collect from purchasers, once the debtor protests the witnesses do not publicize it. Thus, on Abaye's initial premise there would still be reason to suspect that the debtor changed his mind and protested to the witnesses in order to prevent collection from purchasers. Indeed, he would have no other way to protect encumbered property, because the witnesses' signatures otherwise transfer the note to the creditor even without a loan. He would need to protest and notify the witnesses so that purchasers not be exposed. The witnesses would not simply hand the document back to him, since on Abaye's theory they had already acquired it for the creditor even without a loan. His only protection is protest that suppresses publicity. Accordingly, even without concern for repayment and collusion, a note containing a property lien should not be returned, because perhaps the debtor protested and thereby canceled collection from encumbered property. A note without such a lien may be returned, since the protest would not destroy the underlying obligation against unencumbered property. This is exactly the distinction of the Mishnah. The Gemara then challenges Abaye from found gift documents and receipts, where we do suspect that the maker changed his mind. There the concern affects even rights against unencumbered property, showing that witnesses' signatures do not themselves complete the transfer. The answer is that actual delivery is required. Once actual delivery is required and the document is ordinarily given to the debtor for delivery, the debtor need not spoil the note or waste the scribe's fee by formally protesting before witnesses. He can simply retain the paper and borrow upon it whenever he wishes. We therefore do not ordinarily suspect a protest that would cancel publicity. At that point, the Mishnah concerning a found loan note again becomes difficult: if the debtor admits the debt, why not return it? The Gemara must then invoke the separate concern of repayment and collusion. This also clarifies Ritva's statement that validation is required. Without judicial validation, we may suspect that the debtor originally instructed the witnesses to write and deliver the note to the creditor, but later protested to those same witnesses that they not deliver it. In Rav Asi's framework, one ordinarily does not write and deliver a note to the debtor when the creditor is absent, but because the document was found after having fallen we suspect the exceptional case that it was written in the debtor's absence from the creditor and then he protested against delivery. The debtor could not simply take the note for himself, because according to Rav Asi the witnesses would not deliver it to him. His available method was to protest that it not be given to the creditor. Once he protests in the presence of the witnesses, they cease generating publicity. If the note is later found and returned in their absence, no new publicity arises. Thus, as against purchasers the instrument has lost its force, even though the debtor presently says that he never protested. The debtor may now cooperate because he saves the scribe's fee, while the creditor, unaware of the prior protest, may be willing to lend on the old document. Judicial validation removes this concern. Once validated, the only remaining concern would be that the debt was paid and the parties want to reuse the note the same day. In that situation the creditor himself, who knows of the payment, lacks sufficient gain to participate, as the Gemara states. Everything therefore fits well, with Hashem's help. The earlier authorities left room for this explanation. Tumim further asks according to the opinion in siman 48 that a note whose lien has been extinguished may later be used for a new loan. If so, why does the Gemara say that we fear it was paid and the parties wish to borrow again on it? Why not simply renew the transfer in the presence of witnesses? According to what I established in siman 48, however, the signing witnesses themselves must serve as the witnesses to the subsequent delivery for such reuse to be effective. See there, and the difficulty falls away.
