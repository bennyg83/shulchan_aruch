It shall remain deposited. If the third party later says, "I remembered," it appears that we act according to his statement. This is unlike an ordinary witness who says, "I do not know," and cannot later change his testimony, as explained by Sema in siman 29. Certainly according to Rashba, cited by Shach, seif katan 7, the reason the item remains deposited specifically in the hands of a third party is that the parties trusted him, and the third party's uncertainty is therefore like the uncertainty of a litigant. A litigant himself who says, "I do not know," may later say, "I remembered," as explained above in siman 59. The third party's uncertainty cannot have greater force than that of the litigant. Even according to the Mechaber's ruling in seif 2 that the item remains deposited even when the parties are heirs, all agree with Rashba's principle that a third party is treated like a litigant and that his uncertainty is like a litigant's uncertainty, as I will explain below. Just as the creditor appointed the third party to stand in the position of a litigant, so too did the debtor. He may therefore say later that he remembered. If there is one witness who knows the nature of the arrangement and testifies that the document belongs to the creditor, the debtor must swear against the witness. Although the third party's doubt is equivalent to the creditor's doubt, this is no weaker than a doubtful claim based upon a single witness, which can require an oath. If the debtor admits that according to the condition made in the witness's presence the document belonged to the creditor, but claims that he paid afterward, he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear, and must pay. This is unlike siman 51, seif 2, concerning a document supported by one witness. There the principle of, "Why is your document in my hand?" does not operate with a document signed by only one witness. Here the document itself is signed by two witnesses, so that principle certainly applies. How could the debtor have known in advance that the third party would later forget the arrangement and not know its nature? Likewise, if the debtor claims that after the witness departed the parties made a different condition before the third party, then since, had there been two witnesses to the terms at the time of deposit, the debtor certainly would not be believed to claim that a later different condition was made, the same rule applies with one witness. So long as the debtor has not sworn to contradict him, one witness has the force of two. With a document containing a lien on property, however, one witness certainly cannot establish liability against purchasers. If the single witness testifies that the document belongs to the debtor, the witness is treated as though absent and the document remains deposited. This is unlike siman 267, seif 14. Even Rosh agrees here. There, the reasoning is that the claimant is no worse than if the object were in the opponent's hands and the opponent refused to swear against the witness, in which case he would have to return it. See Tur and Rosh there. Here, if the document were in the creditor's hands and he refused to swear, he would not have to return the document, because the required oath is rabbinic and we do not descend to his property for failure to take it. We therefore return to the reasoning stated there, that since even if the creditor swore the debtor would not thereby be required to pay, the creditor need not swear. This appears correct.
He could have collected. See Sema, seif katan 1, that the holder must swear against the witness who contradicts him. Tumim objects that a person cannot render himself wicked, and therefore the holder is not believed to say that the document was delivered to him unlawfully. See there. This objection applies only according to Tosafot in Sanhedrin 9, who hold that we do not divide a person's statement concerning one and the same actor in the case where one says, "So and so sodomized me willingly," so as to accept only that it occurred under compulsion. According to the authorities who do apply division of a statement even in such a case, here too, although the holder says that the other voluntarily gave him the document, we can divide his statement and accept only that he obtained the document, treating it as though he seized it from him under compulsion. See Shach, seif katan 3. Even according to what I wrote in siman 58, seif katan 21, that Tosafot's view implies that regarding a claim that a payment was for another debt there can be a case of one obligated to swear who cannot swear, I find a difficulty. Baal HaTerumot in the name of Ramban writes that such a claimant is not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear because the oath involved is only rabbinic. He then asks that since the creditor is believed to say that the payment was for another debt only through a migo that he could have denied receipt, he should still have to take the same oath required upon a denial. He answers that the creditor is treated as already appeased and standing upon his document. If so, Tosafot and Ran, who hold that there can be an obligation to pay for inability to swear even though the underlying oath is rabbinic, could still agree with the principle that in the claim of another debt the creditor is already appeased. They may hold only that because his credibility rests on a migo from an outright denial, and an outright denial would certainly require the Mishnah's oath, he is treated as one who cannot swear and must pay. Perhaps Shach holds that once we accept the principle that the creditor is already appeased when he claims another debt, the case is treated as though no oath exists at all, so the rule of inability to swear is inapplicable. Shach further concludes that the holder's contradiction of the witness is no worse than if he admitted the witness's statement but asserted with certainty that the other party still owed him the debt, in which case no oath would be required, as explained below. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that since the holder presently could swear, he must swear, and cannot be believed through a migo that he could have admitted the witness. Otherwise, where one witness says a document is paid and the creditor denies this, the creditor should be believed through a migo that he could have claimed the payment was for another debt according to Baal HaTerumot. Necessarily, a migo against one witness is ineffective. These words are astonishing. Here the holder makes two assertions: first, that the debt on the document remains due; second, that the witness is wrong in saying that the document was not delivered to him lawfully. Shach's point is sound. If the holder admitted the witness's statement and nevertheless made the same substantive monetary claim he makes now, namely that the debt on the document is still due, he would be exempt from an oath on that claim. What is gained by requiring him to swear merely because he adds a denial of the witness concerning a matter that does not itself involve a denial of money? If beit din instructs him to go swear, he can simply admit the witness and be exempt. It cannot be that if he admits before beit din and says that his earlier statement was false he is exempt, but if he maintains that his original statement was true he becomes liable.
And if he admits. See Shach, who asks why he should not be believed through a migo. Sema and Shach further ask from the later ruling in siman 16. See there. Sema gives an answer, and in Derishah he cites the same answer but himself writes that it is strained, because if so a document that became invalid should likewise be left in beit din. See there, where Derishah gives a broader resolution. I independently arrived at that resolution, with Heaven's help, and do not know why Sema omitted it. The distinction is that here the document is held by a third party. Rashba, cited by Shach, seif katan 7, writes that a third party is literally treated as a litigant because the parties trusted him that the matter should be as he says even if that harms them. When a doubt arises for the third party, it is therefore as though the doubt arose for the litigant himself. The creditor is accordingly a doubtful claimant while the debtor makes a certain claim, and the debtor is exempt, as explained in siman 59. This differs from the later case where someone else found the document. Tumim objects that a third party is not believed against witnesses, while a migo is equivalent to witnesses. There is no difficulty in light of Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer, siman 141, seif katan 14, who writes that a migo is not believed against a third party. In siman 56 and in my novellae to Even HaEzer there, I proved this clearly. Tumim further objects that we do not rule like Rashba in seif 2. This too is no difficulty. Even those who disagree with Rashba there agree with his principle that the third party's uncertainty is like the uncertainty of a litigant. Their disagreement concerns another matter, as I will explain below in seif 2. Tumim also asks that the entrusted item has already left the third party's possession, so he is no longer especially precise. This too is no difficulty. A third party loses that status only when the entrusted matter leaves his hands in a way that removes him from the role. Here it was given back to him merely as a deposit, relying on his trustworthiness to return it, so he remains the third party. See Shach, siman 56, seif katan 34, that even if the third party transferred it to beit din or to a guardian, he still remains the third party, all the more so here. Moreover, the creditor admits that when the item was originally delivered to the third party, while he was certainly still exacting in his recollection, the third party said that he was uncertain. At that moment his doubt was certainly equivalent to the doubt of a litigant, and the document was thereby impaired.
And he returns the land to its owners. See Sema, seif katan 3, who writes that the possessor cannot claim purchase, because the document held by the third party establishes that the property came into his hands as collateral. At first glance this is astonishing. Even if the collateral document were in the debtor's own hands and the possessor had held the land for three years, the document alone could not destroy his chazakah. The possessor can say, "You wrote the collateral document yourself," since one may write a document for a borrower or seller even when the creditor or purchaser is not present. Even if one witness testifies that it was written with the creditor's knowledge, a single witness cannot destroy a three year chazakah. Accordingly, here too, although the third party asserts with certainty that the document came into his hands from both parties, he cannot destroy the chazakah. Regarding this issue the third party is only like one witness. The special credibility of a third party applies only concerning a result he could have accomplished by delivering the document to whichever party he chose. He never had power merely by such delivery to destroy a land possessor's chazakah. Even if he had handed the collateral document to the seller, that document would not destroy the chazakah for the reason stated above. A collateral document can undermine the chazakah only where its date falls after the possessor's three year period began. Then, even if the debtor himself wrote it, the writing may operate as a protest made before witnesses. It is also necessary that three years not pass from the date of the document, because a protest must be renewed at the end of each three year period. Even this is only according to the view in siman 146 that a statement before witnesses, "This field is in his hands as collateral," constitutes a protest even if the owner does not add, "Tomorrow I will sue him in beit din." See there. Necessarily, Sema follows the authorities in siman 39, seif 13, who hold that without a kinyan one does not write a document for a borrower in the lender's absence. In that setting we do not suspect that it was merely written and happened to come into existence independently. Since the document has not otherwise been impaired, as Sema writes in seif katan 11, the only remaining concern is payment, and Sema's ruling is understandable. But where there was a kinyan, even according to those who hold that the witnesses' signatures acquire on the creditor's behalf, the possessor can still claim purchase. This is clear.
