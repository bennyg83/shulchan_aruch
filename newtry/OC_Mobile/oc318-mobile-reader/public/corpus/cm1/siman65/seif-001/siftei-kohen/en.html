And if he returned it, etc. See above, siman 54:4, the gloss to siman 55:1, and the gloss to siman 386:3.
He could have collected with it, etc. The Sema infers from the words ‘he could have collected with it’ that collection is not certain, because in any event the creditor would have to take a stringent oath resembling a Torah oath to contradict the person who says that the document had previously been in his possession. In my opinion, the Tur and Mechaber did not intend this. If so, ultimately he would still collect. Rather, their meaning is that even if the creditor admits that he gave the document to the third party and the third party returned it to him, the third party acted improperly, because the creditor could have collected with it and might have denied the third party’s account. The Sema and Bach also assume that the creditor must take a stringent oath to contradict the witness. This requires study. It is unlike a single witness who testifies expressly that the document was paid. Here the witness does not testify expressly to payment. Although the document was in his possession, it may nevertheless have belonged to the creditor. No such oath is found in the Mishnah or Gemara. This resembles Baal HaTerumot’s ruling in the name of the Ramban, cited by the Beit Yosef above at the beginning of siman 58, that where one witness says the debtor paid and the creditor claims that the payment was for another debt, no oath is required. The Sages instituted an oath where one witness testifies that the debt was paid in order to appease the debtor. Where the witness does not expressly testify that this document was paid, the debtor’s concern is already allayed and the witness does not support him. A rabbinic enactment not established by law is construed leniently, not stringently. The same applies here. Even according to what I write in siman 58, seif katan 21, that Tosafot, the Ran, and Ramban in Chazakat HaBatim imply that where the creditor claims the payment was for another debt, it is a case of one who cannot swear and must pay, that is because the witness clearly testifies that payment occurred. Here, even on the witness’s account, the document may still belong to the creditor. At minimum, according to Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Ramban, no oath is required. Although the creditor contradicts the person who says he gave him the document, his position is no worse than if he admitted the delivery and asserted with certainty that the debt remained due, in which case no oath would be required, as explained below. Certainly where he denies the delivery and also asserts that the debt is due, the matter requires study.
And if he admits that he gave it into his hand, he must return it to him, etc. The Tur attributes this ruling to a responsum of the Rosh. I searched the responsa of the Rosh and did not find it. The ruling itself is astonishing. What does it matter that the creditor admits giving him the document? The creditor says that the document remained his. Why should he not be believed through a migo that he could have denied the delivery? This is certainly so according to Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Ramban, cited in the preceding seif katan, because if the document was not impaired there, all the more so here. Even under Tosafot and the Ran, a distinction can be drawn as explained above. Even if one says there is no distinction, the Tur and Rama themselves rule without qualification in siman 58 like Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Ramban, yet here say that admission of delivery requires returning the document. Why? Just as there the creditor admits the witness’s statement that payment was made but claims it was for another debt and the document is not impaired, here he admits delivery but claims that the document was his. The document should not be impaired. This requires study. It appears that the Tur and Mechaber address a case in which the creditor himself does not make a certain claim that the borrower still owes him; he too is uncertain whether payment occurred. If he denied having given the document to the third party, he could collect where the borrower was likewise uncertain, as explained below in siman 82:2. But if he admits delivery and merely wishes to retain the document, then, since the borrower is also uncertain and the document was previously in another’s hands, he must return it. This also explains their statement that the third party ‘acted improperly by violating the words of the Sages.’ Otherwise, they should have said that he acted unlawfully by enabling the creditor to extract money without right. It also explains the wording ‘he could have collected with it,’ which troubled the Sema. It means he could have collected if the borrower also remained uncertain, in contrast to a borrower who makes a certain claim, where the document is void under siman 59. It further resolves the question of She’eris Yosef, Sema, and Bach from seif 16 below, where the authorities record a dispute. Their strained distinctions are unnecessary. According to this explanation, all the rulings are consistent.
