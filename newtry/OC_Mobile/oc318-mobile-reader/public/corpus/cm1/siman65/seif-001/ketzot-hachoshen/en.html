He could have collected. The Sema says he cannot collect on the instrument until he takes a severe oath. The Shach asks why any oath is required if the situation does not resemble one witness testifying that the document was paid. Ramban's rule concerning an "other debt" claim says that where the claimant cannot take the oath he collects without it because the required oath is only rabbinic. But that explanation itself shows the answer. Ramban exempts only because the person cannot swear. Here he can swear directly against the witness. Therefore he must. One cannot say he should be believed through a migo that he could admit receiving the money and claim it applied to another debt. If one witness says the document was paid and the creditor flatly denies receiving payment, he must also take the Mishnah oath. We do not allow a migo that he could have admitted receipt and claimed another debt. Until he swears against the one witness, that witness has the force of two. A migo cannot be used against witnesses. Thus the Sema is correct. According to the Mechaber, who requires return even if he admits receiving it, there is no migo in any event.
And if he admits. The Shach asks that he has a migo that he never received it, following the Tur and Rama in siman 58 concerning an "other debt" claim. Knesset HaGedolah answers that a document found in a third party's hand is already weakened like a fallen document. Even a clause of special credibility and even the presumption of nonpayment within the due date do not overcome the defect of having fallen. A migo is no stronger. The fact that elsewhere a person can claim payment within the due date through a migo does not prove otherwise, because that law itself is only an unresolved Talmudic question and possession prevents extraction. Here the creditor is the one seeking to extract. His migo is no stronger than the presumption of nonpayment within the due date, and that presumption does not repair a document already weakened by falling.
