Passed. See above, siman 56, seif 6, in the gloss.
Was. The Sema infers from the wording "was" that the rule is not absolute. The reason is that the lender cannot collect until he takes an oath resembling a Torah oath against the third party's statement. Even though the instrument is no longer in the third party's possession, the third party has the status of one witness, as explained in siman 56. The oath is not technically a Torah oath, because every Torah oath is taken by one who swears and is exempt, while here the lender swears and collects, as discussed in simanim 89 and 108. Nor does this fall under the rule that one swears only heset concerning documents, as in siman 95, because the lender is not swearing against the document itself but against the witness who says the document had been deposited with him. The Shach writes that in his view this requires study. It differs from the ordinary case of one witness saying a document was paid, because there the witness expressly testifies to payment. Here the third party does not expressly say it was paid; the fact it passed through his possession does not prove payment, since perhaps he held it on behalf of the lender. No such oath is found in the Mishnah or Gemara. It resembles the Beit Yosef's citation from Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of siman 58, where one witness says payment was made and the creditor claims it was for another debt, and according to Baal HaTerumot no oath is required. All the more so here. Even according to the poskim there who treat the creditor as one obligated to swear but unable to do so, that is because the witness clearly testifies to payment, unlike here. Even if no distinction is made, according to Baal HaTerumot there should still be no oath here. Indeed, although the creditor contradicts the third party, his position is no worse than if he admitted the third party's statement but asserted with certainty that the debt nevertheless remained due, in which case no oath would be required, as explained below. The matter therefore requires study. Shach.
To return it. The Shach writes that the Tur attributes this law to a responsum of the Rosh, but he searched the responsa and could not find it. The law itself is difficult: why should the lender not be believed through a migo that he could have denied the third party's account? This is especially difficult according to Baal HaTerumot, cited in seif katan 2. Rama also states Baal HaTerumot's rule without qualification in siman 58, so why here must the lender return the document? The Shach suggests that the Tur and Mechaber refer to a case where the lender himself does not state with certainty that the debt remains unpaid, but is also unsure whether payment occurred. In such a case no migo applies. See there.
Will be explained. The Sema notes that the Tur and Mechaber in siman 17 record a dispute on this question, whereas here they state the rule without dissent. He distinguishes that where a document had been entrusted to a third party, it can be returned to that same custody as before. By contrast, where a document fell and another person found it, no one had trusted the finder with possession, and certainly the court should not simply return it to the ground where it was found. The Shach writes that no such strained distinction is necessary. According to his explanation above, this case concerns a lender who himself is uncertain, and therefore no contradiction exists.
A claim. Meaning not a claim of outright purchase, because the document itself shows that it came into the holder's possession as collateral. Nor is he believed to say that it secures the full amount implied by the document, because since it is in the hands of a third party perhaps the debt was fully paid and the borrower deposited it there. Sema.
To its owner. Even though the present possessor physically holds the property, where such a defect exists we apply the presumption that land remains in the possession of its established owner. Sema.
Himself. Meaning that if the father asserts that the borrower still owes him money, he may collect using the document. But if he too does not know whether it was paid, siman 59 already establishes that the document must be returned to the borrower even if formally authenticated, because the lender should know whether his own instrument was paid. Sema.
