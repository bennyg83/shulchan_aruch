He could have collected with it. Sema and Bach explain the doubtful language as follows. If the creditor now denies the third party's statement, he must swear to contradict a single witness. Once the third party no longer holds the entrusted item, he has only the force of one witness. Therefore the ruling is expressed as uncertain, because perhaps the creditor will refuse to swear falsely. Shach objects. The third party is not really a single witness who says the note was paid. Even according to his account, the creditor's present claim on the note may be legally proper, because the third party himself no longer knows what the document's status is. Moreover, Ramban writes in the set-off case that the special oath there was instituted merely to reassure the debtor, not because one witness formally testifies to payment. All the more so here. There is, however, some room to defend Sema and Bach. In an ordinary payment case, the document itself is properly in the creditor's hand. The presumption, "If it had been paid, why is the document still in his hand?" supports the creditor. Therefore, absent the special concern of reassuring the debtor, Chazal would not have imposed an oath merely because one witness claims payment. Here, by contrast, the third party testifies that the creditor obtained the document improperly. We are not dealing with a note that remained naturally in the creditor's possession. The ordinary presumption supporting the note is therefore absent, and an oath could be justified. Even so, I do not understand the case on a more basic level. We rule that witnesses who say, "This document was a trust document," are not believed once the writing is valid because they thereby make themselves wicked: signing such a document was itself wrongful. The same applies where they say they were minors or otherwise disqualified. A person does not make himself wicked. Here too, according to the third party's own statement, he violated the words of the Sages. Shulchan Aruch says explicitly that he acted improperly. He is therefore making himself into one who transgressed a rabbinic prohibition. I explained above in siman 46, and I discuss further in the rules of migo, that even concerning a rabbinic prohibition we say a person does not make himself wicked. If so, I do not understand what formal witness-status can be assigned to this statement at all.
And if he is, and so forth. All the later authorities ask: why does the creditor not have a migo? He could have said, "I never received the document from the third party." He should therefore be believed to say, "I did receive it, but in truth the borrower still owes me." Sema answers that denying receipt would be a brazen claim. But it is well known that in monetary law we generally do recognize a migo even where the alternative claim involves brazenness. Shach answers that the case is where the lender himself is uncertain whether the debt remains. This is strained. Mechaber would not normally state the law without qualification if the entire ruling depended on the lender himself admitting uncertainty. And if he does not know that he is owed money, it is obvious that he must return a note he admits did not properly belong in his hand. There is another question. Here Mechaber rules simply that the document must be returned to the person from whom it was received. But later, in seif 16, he cites a dispute concerning a found note that was returned improperly: can the creditor collect with it or not? Why is this case treated differently? One possible answer begins with the general law of a third party. When the husband says, "I gave the get merely for safekeeping," and the third party says, "It was given for divorce," the third party is believed. The authorities explain that at the outset the owner intentionally subjected himself to the third party's determination. If the third party changed the entrustment and delivered the get as a divorce, the husband is treated as having accepted that result from the beginning. Rashba therefore holds that even if the woman already remarried and witnesses later come and prove the get had originally been deposited only for safekeeping, the divorce can nevertheless remain valid. The husband initially accepted that the matter should follow the third party's disposition. The same can apply in monetary law. If the creditor deposited the loan note with a third party, then even if the borrower had not actually paid, the creditor accepted from the outset that the legal status of the note would follow the third party. If the third party later says it was paid, that status becomes accepted. If he says he does not know the note's status, then from the outset the creditor accepted that the note should become unusable and remain in suspense. On this approach, there is no meaningful migo. We can accept the creditor's claim as completely true that he was never actually paid. Even so, he had already accepted that the note's legal force would depend on the third party's statement. Since the third party says its status is unknown, the creditor loses the right to use it. His factual truth is irrelevant because he subordinated the instrument to the third party. This would also explain why the law differs from a found document. In the found-document case there was no entrusted third party and no prior acceptance of another person's determination. Therefore, if the creditor later shouts, "It is my valid unpaid note and it fell from me," Rambam can hold that after improper return the document may still be collected. This interpretation finds some support in Responsa Rashba, siman 1035, where Rashba says that in a genuine third-party arrangement, the parties' own later claims are subordinated to the third party. This appears to be what Sema and Perishah intended, although they stated it too briefly.
However, I am still not satisfied. Even in third-party law, everyone agrees that if witnesses come before the wife remarries and prove that the husband gave the get merely for safekeeping, the third-party status is defeated. I explained above in siman 56 at length that Ramban holds this and Rashba concedes before the legal consequence is completed. A valid migo can function similarly to witnesses. If the creditor presently has a migo establishing that he was not paid, why should the original entrustment continue to override that proof? One can say the owner accepted the third party only so long as stronger proof would not arise, just as we say regarding witnesses. This is especially true once the third party has already released the document from his possession. His special credibility has weakened, as explained above. It is unlikely that the creditor originally accepted that the third party should override even later equivalent proof after the entrusted item had left his control. There is a second problem. If Rashba's broad trust rationale were the true basis, then when the note is found in the hands of the third party's heir the law should be different. Rashba himself says explicitly in responsum 1035 that the reason of prior entrustment does not apply to an heir. The heir was never trusted by the creditor. Yet Shulchan Aruch in seif 2 rules that even when the note is found in the heir's hands, it is not returned. This shows that our halachah does not rest on Rashba's broad theory of entrustment. Therefore Perishah cannot solve the issue by relying on Rashba's rationale, especially since we do not follow Rashba there. The first question is better answered another way. Once the creditor admits that the document had been deposited with a third party who did not know its status, the document has already become a worthless potsherd. Its documentary lien has been extinguished. The creditor now attempts to revive it through a migo that he could have denied having received it from the third party. That is a migo used to extract money. We find the same below in the rule following R. Yehudah concerning money: once the note is treated as worthless paper, a migo cannot revive it because the creditor is trying to extract. This is especially clear according to what I establish in the rules of migo. The reason we generally reject a migo used to extract with a document is not that a document is considered as already collected, for we do not hold that a document standing for collection is the same as money already possessed. Rather, the usual document is supported by the chazakah, "If it had been paid, why is it still in his hand?" Here that chazakah is absent. The creditor admits the note came into his hand improperly from a third party. Thus the natural documentary presumption disappears. He is trying to use only a migo to revive an extracting instrument. That migo does not work. See the rules of migo. The principle is clear: where the ordinary presumption "your document is in my hand, what is it doing there?" does not apply, we say that a migo cannot be used to extract with the document. The second question, from the found-note case, can also be answered independently. Bava Metzia 13 says that according to Shmuel, who does not ordinarily suspect payment, the Mishnah "if one found a loan note, he should not return it" can be explained with a non-kinyan document, because perhaps the note was written for a contemplated loan that never occurred. Although according to R. Asi witnesses should not write such a document without the lender present, once the document fell we say perhaps the witnesses happened to violate the rule. But consider our Mishnah: "If a loan note is found in the hands of a third party and he does not know its status, it shall remain." How can Shmuel explain this? He does not suspect payment. Nor can we say perhaps the borrower wrote it but never borrowed, because with a non-kinyan note witnesses do not write it without the lender present. Here there is no added defect of falling in the street. The fact that a document is in a third party's hand is not itself reason to assume the witnesses acted improperly. And if it was never a real loan note at all, what meaningful "status" could there be for the third party to be uncertain about? We must therefore say that even Shmuel, who does not suspect ordinary repayment merely because a note fell, does suspect payment when the note was intentionally placed with a third party. A debtor often pays subject to conditions and deposits the note with a third party pending fulfillment of those conditions. This is exactly like the familiar case of one who partly pays and places the document with a third party. Thus a note in a third party's hand raises a stronger concern of payment than a note found in the street. Even Shmuel agrees. This resolves the apparent contradiction. When a note merely fell and was later returned improperly, Rambam can rule that after the fact the creditor may collect, because we rely on Shmuel's general position and do not strongly suspect repayment. We also presume witnesses acted properly and did not write a false note. But a note deposited with a third party is different. There, even Shmuel acknowledges a real concern of payment or conditional discharge. Therefore, if the third party says he does not know its status, and the creditor later obtains it, the creditor still cannot collect. This is clear and resolves both difficulties.
