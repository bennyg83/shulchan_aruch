Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam equates a ketubah with other debt instruments. Shach expresses surprise, saying there is an explicit Gemara in Bava Metzia 7 that if one finds a ketubah and the husband admits, it is returned to the woman. But the Gemara actually says exactly that. The later authorities then ask why a ketubah differs from other debt instruments and why we do not fear collusion. They answer that the case concerns a ketubah with no recourse against encumbered property. Maggid Mishneh's point is therefore correct. Rambam treats a normal ketubah like other debt instruments and does fear collusion. The Gemara's permissive case must be limited, as the commentators explain, to a case without property warranty. In my opinion Rambam can also establish the Gemara's case as a ketubah from erusin. Rambam holds that a ketubah from erusin is not collected from purchasers. There is then no collusion concern. In any event, Rambam's rule that an ordinary ketubah is subject to the same concern as other debt instruments is derived from the general law, and Maggid Mishneh's explanation is sound. All this, however, follows Rif, Rambam, and Rosh, who hold that the rule "one who pleads payment after a condition of beit din is not believed" applies only in a place where ketubot are not normally written. In a place where they are written, if the woman has no ketubah, the husband may say, "I paid." According to Tosafot and those who agree with them, however, even where ketubot are written, if the woman says, "My ketubah was lost," the husband is not believed to say he paid the basic ketubah. The Mishnah itself says that when she produces a get without the ketubah, she can still collect because she says the ketubah was lost. According to that view, what difference is there between her saying, "I lost my ketubah," and a ketubah actually being found in the marketplace? In either case the husband cannot claim payment of the basic amount. The baraita concerning the dispute of the Sages and R. Yose must therefore concern the additional ketubah, as several authorities cited in Shitah Mekubetzet explain. It cannot refer to the basic maneh or two hundred. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 17b nevertheless write that the baraita can concern the basic ketubah as well. They say the husband may be believed to say he paid the maneh because he has a migo that she was a widow when he married her, or that he paid everything because he could deny the marriage entirely. With due respect, I have been unable to understand this. The ketubah itself is before us. It is merely in the hands of the finder rather than the wife. When husband and wife appear, the document proves she was married to him as a virgin and that the ketubah obligation existed. What migo does he have to say she was a widow or was never his wife? The ketubah contradicts those claims. If he must pay the full two hundred immediately because the ketubah proves the obligation, what difference is there whether we physically return the paper to the woman and she collects later, or require payment now? Leaving the ketubah forever with the finder does not create a migo where the document itself remains in existence. And if, despite the ketubah's existence, he somehow has such a migo, then why does the Gemara challenge R. Yose from his position elsewhere and the Sages from theirs on the question whether repayment is suspected? There would be no difficulty. The husband would simply be believed because of his migo, and migo to retain property is recognized throughout the Gemara. This requires study.
