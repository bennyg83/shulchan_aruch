Because we suspect repayment and collusion. Bava Metzia says that one who finds a debt instrument in the marketplace may not return it even if it bears court validation, because of concern for repayment. Rabbi Jeremiah asks from the rule that every judicial instrument is returned. The Gemara first suggests a case where the debtor was established as a false denier, but Rava objects: does one episode of false denial mean he never repaid? Tosafot comments that although elsewhere a person established as a false denier regarding that very money cannot later claim payment, a fallen document is different. The fall itself indicates that the creditor may no longer have guarded it because it was paid. This seems to contradict the episode of Shabtai son of Rabbi Marinus. His daughter in law lost her ketubah. He denied ever writing the silk garment in it, witnesses proved he had, and when he later claimed payment Rabbi Chiya treated him as a false denier. The loss of the ketubah did not help him. Shitah Mekubetzet answers either that witnesses established the ketubah was destroyed or lost through a circumstance that did not indicate repayment, or that his denial concerned only the garment while she still needed to guard the ketubah for the basic amount and other rights. The Tur, however, rules broadly in siman 41 that where a debt instrument was lost, the debtor denied the loan, and witnesses proved the loan and the writing of the document, he is established as a false denier and cannot later claim payment. Beit Yosef derives this directly from the Shabtai case and treats ketubah and ordinary documents alike. We must therefore distinguish two kinds of established false denial. First, one who says, "This never happened," and is then contradicted by witnesses. His statement is also an admission that he never repaid, because one who never borrowed cannot have repaid. Even witnesses later saying he paid do not help against his own judicial admission. In that case the defect of a fallen document is no stronger than witnesses and cannot overcome the litigant's own admission, which is like one hundred witnesses. This was Shabtai's case and the Tur's case. Second, one whom beit din ordered, "Go pay," and who later says he paid, but witnesses establish that he did not pay at that time. He becomes a false denier concerning that money, yet if he later brings witnesses that he subsequently paid, they are effective. His disqualification does not make repayment logically impossible. In that case a fallen document creates the very kind of evidence that can support later payment. Therefore the Gemara says that one episode of false denial does not mean he never paid afterward. This is also why the Mishnah's phrase "judicial instruments" cannot refer to the first kind. That form of false denial need not arise specifically through beit din. The phrase points to the second kind, where the status of false denier arises specifically from disobedience or denial after a judicial order. It is therefore that weaker form of false denial, and a fallen document can still create a repayment concern. Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch do not expressly record this limitation on collecting from a fallen document where the debtor had the second kind of false denial, and this requires study.
There is a hole in it. Whether the borrower or lender gives the identifying mark, the document is ordinarily not returned, because both could know marks appearing on the instrument. The borrower saw it before delivering it, and the lender held it until repayment. This assumes the borrower's claim is payment. If he instead says, "I prepared the document to borrow but never borrowed," then a distinctive mark given by the lender should prove that the document reached the lender, for otherwise how would he know the mark? Perhaps the borrower could still be believed through a migo that he paid, but in siman 298 the Shach rules that a migo does not overcome a distinguishing mark. This requires study.
The letter peh. A highly distinctive mark that is not part of the ordinary body of the document certainly helps, as the Mishnah says concerning documents bound or tied together. The Shach says this identifies the claimant only as against another finder, but does not permit collection from encumbered property, because even if borrower and lender both agree, we fear collusion. The same concern would apply that the borrower informed the lender of the identifying mark. Shitah Mekubetzet already addresses this regarding a bundle of documents. If collusion were the plan, the borrower could simply supply the mark himself and transfer the paper secretly. The fact that the court acts on an independent identifying mark means the result rests upon the mark, not upon the parties' admission. The collusion concern that defeats simple admission therefore does not apply in the same way. Accordingly, a sufficiently distinctive mark can support return even where the document can later be used against encumbered property.
