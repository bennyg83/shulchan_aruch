There is a hole next to such-and-such a letter. Baal HaTerumot gives the reason cited by Sema and Shach: the borrower may know the identifying mark because a loan document can be written for the borrower even when the lender is not present. The lender, on the other hand, knows the mark because the document had previously been in his possession before repayment and was afterward returned to the borrower. Shach objects that this reason does not suffice for a document without a kinyan, since such a document may not be written unless the lender is present. He answers that Baal HaTerumot's wording is not exact, and means merely that in the ordinary course the borrower sees the document when it is prepared for the lender. It is possible, however, that Baal HaTerumot follows Rashi's interpretation: we fear that the borrower had the document written for an intended loan that never occurred, and afterward used it for a later loan. Since the document is then antedated, he may not have guarded it carefully after it fell. Many authorities cited in Shitah Mekubetzet hold this way. Accordingly, even with a document without a kinyan, once the note was found we can fear that the witnesses happened to write it improperly in advance, that the borrower intended to borrow in Nisan but did not actually borrow until Tishrei, and that he therefore knows the identifying mark. We cannot return it to the borrower merely because he supplies that mark, even though the document may still support a valid claim against the borrower himself. This fits Baal HaTerumot well. One might ask why Baal HaTerumot also says that the lender knows the mark. Even if the item was found inside a vessel and the lender supplies identifying marks on the vessel, we still would not necessarily return the note to him, because even where the debtor admits, the note may not be returned if there is concern for collusion. Perhaps the note belonged to the borrower, who deliberately told the lender the identifying mark so that the note would be restored to the lender and used improperly. The answer is that Baal HaTerumot teaches the law even where the note expressly says that there is no recourse against encumbered property, a case where an admitting debtor ordinarily allows return. Even then, if the only basis is the identifying mark, it should not be returned, because the borrower may also know the mark. This is the novelty.
However, I saw a puzzling passage in Baal HaTerumot. He writes that although with a get we return the document based on this type of identifying mark, a get is different because it does not remain with either the husband or the witnesses, and because the husband can divorce her again. I do not understand his reference. If he means the cases in Gittin and Bava Metzia where the husband says, "There is a hole next to such-and-such a letter," the concern there is that perhaps the get is not his at all and fell from someone else. In that case, of course the identifying mark works. How else could he know the mark if the get belonged to someone entirely different? If he means the case at the end of the second chapter of Bava Metzia, where both husband and wife give identifying marks on the get and it is returned to the wife, then in truth the husband's identifying mark does not help him because the get was originally in his possession. If the question is why the wife's identifying mark should prevail when the husband also knows the mark from having possessed the get, the answer cannot be merely that he has the ability to divorce her again. We are specifically entertaining that perhaps he already divorced her and she denies it. What appears possible, though strained, is this. The Gemara's answer that he says, "There is a hole next to such-and-such a letter," applies even according to the opinion that frequented caravans create concern despite no second person of the same name having been established. One might still fear that this husband has another wife of the same name whom he married without our knowledge and to whom he previously gave this get before one witness. The get would then already have been used and void. Now that it was found, he could identify it and attempt to use it for the second wife who bears the same name. Baal HaTerumot would answer that a man does not normally retain a used get for a future divorce. If he had already used it, he would no longer know its identifying marks in the practical way required. In addition, since he can simply divorce the present wife lawfully with a new get, he has no reason to engineer such a fraud against her. Still, this interpretation is difficult and requires study.
And if the document expressly states that he is not liable, and so forth. See what I wrote above in siman 43 at length concerning the Gemara according to R. Meir. Yet in Shulchan Aruch itself the difficulty remains. Mechaber rules below in siman 104 that liens are of Torah force, and therefore an earlier oral loan takes precedence over a later written loan. If so, there can still be a collusive scheme. Suppose this debtor already owes money on a written note and afterward sold property, but still owns free assets. If the holder of the written note seeks to collect from purchasers, the purchasers can say, "You have free assets from which to collect." The debtor can now collude with another person by having an earlier-dated note returned to him, one that expressly contains no property warranty. Because that earlier obligation precedes the later written loan, the first creditor can take the free assets. The later written creditor can then turn to the purchasers and collect from them. The purchasers are harmed improperly. Thus even according to Sema's principle in seif katan 19 that Chazal were concerned mainly for loss to purchasers, that concern exists here. The answers I gave above in siman 43 do not solve this case. One could answer, with some difficulty, that although an ordinary oral loan has precedence by Torah law, where the creditor expressly waived property warranty and agreed that the debt would have no recourse against assets, the lien itself was relinquished. Such a debt has no priority over a later written creditor. By expressly waiving the lien, he removed the Torah property encumbrance. A better answer is that the concern for collusion is itself only a remote concern, as all the authorities say. Chazal were especially protective of purchasers where the purchasers could say, "We relied upon the public record. We heard no report of this note, because in truth it had either been written for a loan that never occurred or had already been paid. We purchased in reliance upon the absence of any kol." Therefore Chazal refused to restore such a note, because its use would unfairly defeat purchasers who reasonably relied upon the lack of publicity. But in the hypothetical concern described above, purchasers cannot say they relied upon any public record concerning earlier oral loans. Oral debts have no kol. The seller might genuinely have owed thousands in earlier oral debts. The purchaser knowingly entered into the transaction despite that uncertainty. Since the purchaser voluntarily assumed that background risk, we do not create an additional remote concern of collusion merely to protect him from a danger he accepted from the outset. Therefore the document may be returned. This resolves the matter.
