Rambam interprets the Mishnah this way, as appears from his commentary to the Mishnah: a note found among other documents means among torn documents, and the note itself was found in the marketplace and therefore remains suspended. If a receipt is found with it, the receipt is followed. Lechem Mishneh asks: if so, why does the Gemara use this Mishnah to challenge the rule that a receipt found in the creditor's own hands is merely a joke and has no force? The Mishnah, according to Rambam, does not concern a receipt in the creditor's hands at all. It concerns a note and receipt found in the marketplace. Shach answers that the question still works. If a receipt in the creditor's possession has no force, then even when a note and receipt are found together in the marketplace we should worry that both fell from the creditor, and the receipt should not determine the result. This is strained. When the note is in the creditor's hand, the note has a strong presumption of validity and the receipt found with him does not overcome it; we can say the receipt was merely written as a draft or joke. But when the note itself has fallen, the fall weakens it and suggests cancellation or payment. A receipt found beside it can naturally strengthen the inference that it was paid. The two situations are not comparable. There is also a separate question: from where does Rambam derive that the receipt can help even if no witnesses signed it at all? See what I write below in seif 18. The matter appears clear in accordance with Bach. At the initial stage, the Gemara understood the Mishnah like Rashi: the clauses are independent. This is why the challenger could treat the receipt as one in the creditor's possession. The reason he was forced into that reading is the Mishnah's use of the word "simpon" rather than "receipt." When a paper is in the creditor's hand and therefore has relatively weak force, it is often called a simpon. A formal receipt in the borrower's hand is called a receipt. Shitah Mekubetzet likewise indicates that a paper without witnesses may be called merely a simpon rather than a full receipt. At the Gemara's initial stage, if the phrase did not refer to the creditor's hand, why call it a simpon? We cannot answer that the paper was unsigned, because an unsigned paper would ordinarily be meaningless. Therefore the Gemara initially assumes it is signed, and the reason for the weaker term "simpon" must be that it is in the lender's possession. That yields the question: a receipt in the lender's possession should be treated as a mere joke. The Gemara answers by explaining the entire passage differently. The Mishnah is not discussing a receipt in the lender's hand. Rather, the document is found among cancelled or torn documents. In that setting, even an unsigned paper can have evidentiary significance because the surrounding circumstance itself weakens the note. It is therefore called a simpon precisely because it lacks formal signatures. The phrase "among his documents" is then understood as "among his torn documents," consistent with what I wrote above in siman 59. Otherwise the phrase "among his documents" would add little. Thus Rambam is correct. The Gemara's answer does not mean that a signed receipt found in the lender's possession becomes effective merely because it lies among torn papers. Rather, the entire case is reinterpreted as a found-note situation, and the paper's placement among torn or cancelled documents supplies the significance that formal witnesses would otherwise provide. This resolves both Lechem Mishneh's question and the source of Rambam's ruling.
