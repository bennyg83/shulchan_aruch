This one shall swear, etc., and pay, etc. This is the Rambam's view, that no distinction is made between parties who are holding the essential text of the document and parties who are holding its standard form. The Maggid Mishneh and the Beit Yosef had difficulty with this and wrote that perhaps the Rambam had a different textual version. In my humble opinion, the opposite is true. He had the version found in our books, but did not accept Rashi's explanation, because Rashi's explanation presents many difficulties. First, the wording, "Rabbi Yochanan said," implies that they disagree. Further, according to Rashi, one must say that the words, "even if one is holding the standard text, etc.," were said by the questioner, which is strained. Further, if so, the Gemara should not have said, "meaning," but rather, "Was it not taught: this one takes, etc." Further, the main resolution, that one authority stated one point and the other stated another and they do not disagree, is entirely absent from the text. Further, the expression, "they always divide," is difficult to explain as referring specifically to a case where the essential text stands in the middle. Further, it appears that the statement, "it is necessary only where the essential text stands in the middle," refers back to the baraita. Further, the Gemara later says, "according to Rabbi Elazar, who says that this one takes the standard text and this one takes the essential text, etc." From the wording, "according to Rabbi Elazar, who says, etc.," it appears that Rabbi Yochanan disagrees. Therefore, it appears that Rabbi Yochanan holds that only with respect to a garment do we say that each takes up to the place reached by his hand, because the portion that each alone holds has independent monetary value. With a document, however, if each were to take the piece in his hand, it would be worth nothing, since a torn document is worth nothing, and the document itself serves only as evidence and is not intrinsically money. Consequently, his physical grasp has no legal significance, and they always divide equally, even if one holds the standard text and the other holds the essential text. The Gemara asks from the baraita that even regarding a document each takes up to the place reached by his hand. It initially assumes that the baraita applies in every case, even when one holds the standard text and the other the essential text, so that each takes the portion he holds, which contradicts Rabbi Yochanan. It answers that the baraita is needed only where the essential text stands in the middle. Each takes the paper up to where his hand reaches, for use as a cover for a small bottle, while the remainder of the document, meaning the essential text, is divided. The Gemara asks: if so, what is the novelty? It is obvious that each takes up to where his hand reaches, so why does the baraita state it? It answers that the baraita teaches this to exclude the thought that where the essential text is closer to one of them, beit din should divide the document at its midpoint and award that party the essential text. Rather, proximity to the essential text gives him no advantage at all; he receives only up to where his hand reaches. Although even one who actually holds the essential text does not receive it by reason of his grasp, the baraita nevertheless teaches the case where it lies in the middle, because one might have thought that physical grasp alone is ineffective since the body of a document is not money, but that beit din itself should divide according to the relative value of the document and award the essential text to the party closer to it according to its appraisal. The baraita teaches otherwise. Accordingly, there is never any distinction, and they always divide. This is why the Rif, Rambam, Baal HaItur, and Semag did not distinguish between the cases.
And some say, etc. So appears in the Tur. The Beit Yosef writes: "It is surprising that the Tur, whose practice is to follow the Rosh, appears here to disagree with him." I do not understand his intent. Even if one assumes that the Rosh holds that the authorities disagree and rules like Rabbi Elazar, how does the Tur appear to disagree with him? The Tur writes precisely like Rabbi Elazar. Moreover, there is no proof from the words of the Rosh. On the contrary, it appears more likely that he follows Rashi and holds that they do not disagree. That is why he cited only Rabbi Elazar, since Rabbi Yochanan does not disagree with him, and there was no need to cite Rabbi Yochanan because he adds nothing. Rabbi Yochanan's ruling that they divide where the essential text lies in the middle is included within Rabbi Elazar's words. But if the Rosh had held that they disagree, it would be difficult why he did not rule like Rabbi Yochanan, since the halachah generally follows Rabbi Yochanan against Rabbi Elazar. What the Bach writes here to resolve the Beit Yosef's difficulty is also astonishing. It appears that he did not understand the Beit Yosef's question and did not examine the Gemara carefully, for neither Rabbi Elazar nor Rabbi Yochanan mentions parties holding the margin. It is self evident that holding the margin has the same law as both holding the standard text or both holding the essential text.
This one shall swear, etc. Some say that they divide without an oath, because this oath was instituted only so that every person would not seize his fellow's garment. Therefore, it was instituted only for a common occurrence, but not for documents, which are uncommon. These are the words of the Tur. The Mechaber omitted this second view, indicating that he considered the first view primary. Perhaps this is because the Mechaber did not find the source of this second view. In the Beit Yosef he records the ruling but does not identify its source, and he overlooked the Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, who records this opinion. Alternatively, he may have considered it incorrect because the Gemara and the poskim made no such distinction. The Rambam and the Semag also write explicitly in this case that each swears, and the Mechaber cited the Rambam's wording above. This is the primary ruling.
He takes the excess monetary value. Meaning, the extra value of the essential text because of the date that appears in it. The party holding it first takes that added value. If there are witnesses to delivery, then both the essential text and the standard text can validate collection without signed witnesses. Still, the standard text is worth more because of the witnesses signed upon it, since the witnesses to delivery might travel overseas or die. Therefore, one appraises the added value of the standard text because of its signed witnesses and the added value of the essential text because of its date, offsets one against the other, and divides the balance. These are the words of the Tur. This follows the view that witnesses to delivery effect validity even with monetary documents. I have already written above, at the end of siman 51, seif katan 7, that many authorities disagree and hold that we do not rule that witnesses to delivery suffice for monetary documents. See there.
That the essential text is worth more. Because of the date, since the document is valid even without a date. So is stated by Tosafos and the Rosh. See the Ir Shushan, who discusses the Tur's words at length. In my humble opinion his explanation is incorrect. He understood that even where there are no witnesses to delivery, one appraises the standard text because of the signed witnesses, and then appraises how much more the essential text is worth because of its date, comparing it to a garment decorated with gold at both ends, where one end has much gold and is held by one party, while the other end has less gold and is held by the other, so that the value of the lesser gold is deducted from the greater. This is incorrect. Where there are no witnesses to delivery, the standard text is not appraised because of the witnesses. Even if a party is physically holding the signatures, that grasp has no value, because without witnesses the document would be invalid and we do not assign a separate value to the signatures. But where there are witnesses to delivery, the added value of the standard text because of its signatures is also appraised. The Tur's words, "therefore, we appraise the added value of the standard text, etc.," refer to the case where there are witnesses to delivery. This is explicit in Tosafos, Bava Metzia 7b, and is also implied by the Rosh. See there carefully. See also Responsa Mahari ben Lev, siman 40.
And they divide the remainder equally with an oath. So writes the Tur. It implies that the portion actually held in one's hand is taken without an oath, and the Ir Shushan writes this explicitly, adding that nevertheless an oath may be imposed upon him through gilgul, as below in siman 138, seif 3. I am surprised by the Tur, because that is true only according to the Rambam. The Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, and the Tur himself below in siman 138, rule that even regarding the portion one physically holds, he must swear. One may answer that the Tur holds that this applies specifically to a garment, whereas regarding a document all agree otherwise. Even according to the first view, which holds that the oath was also instituted for a document because the Sages did not distinguish, that applies when the parties take equal shares. But the unusual situation where one party holds specifically the essential text is not common enough for such an institution. It is also possible that the other poskim who require an oath regarding a document and make no distinction were speaking according to their own view, that no distinction is made between holding the essential text and the standard text, and the document is always divided equally. But where the party holding the essential text first receives its added value, he indeed receives that value without an oath, and swears only because of gilgul. Alternatively, perhaps the Tur was concise and relied upon what he writes below in siman 138. This requires study.
