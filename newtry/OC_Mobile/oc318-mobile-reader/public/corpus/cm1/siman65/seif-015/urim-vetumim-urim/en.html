The value of the debt, etc. If the borrower is poor or violent, so that the document could be sold for only fifty, then the lender receives only twenty five gold coins. The case is treated as though two unrelated people, rather than the actual lender and borrower, were physically holding the document.
He takes the excess value, etc. The standard text is merely the body of the document. The witnesses' signatures are part of the operative portion, together with the identities of lender and borrower, the amount, and the date. The rule is this: if a person holds a portion whose removal would invalidate the entire document, that portion creates no special added value, because if either party removed what he holds the document would simply be destroyed. Therefore the one holding the witnesses' signatures receives no special amount merely for that. But the one holding the date does have an advantage, because even if the date is cut away the document remains valid, only it can no longer be collected from encumbered property. The added value created by the date is therefore awarded to the one holding that portion. Likewise, if the document has witnesses of delivery, then even if the signatures are removed the document may remain valid through those witnesses. A document supported by signed witnesses is nevertheless worth more than one depending only on witnesses of delivery, because those witnesses may die or leave. The holder of the signatures therefore receives the amount of that added value. If one party holds the date and the other the witness signatures, and there are also witnesses of delivery, beit din evaluates which feature creates more value according to the circumstances, including the quality of the document, the witnesses, the debtor, the existence of many creditors, whether the witnesses of delivery are elderly or likely to travel overseas, and similar factors. Whoever holds the feature with greater value must compensate the other for the difference as appropriate, Tur and later authorities.
