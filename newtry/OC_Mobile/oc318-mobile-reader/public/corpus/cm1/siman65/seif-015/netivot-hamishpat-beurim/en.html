And the debtor pays half. At first sight this is difficult. When the parties come before beit din both holding the document, and the debtor is holding either the standard text or the essential operative portion, and immediately upon arriving before beit din says that the document has been paid, then before he came before beit din he had the ability to tear the portion of the document that was in his hand and thereby invalidate it. Why is he not believed by virtue of the migo that, had he wished, he could have destroyed it, just as in siman 46 a debtor is believed through the migo that he could have destroyed the instrument? Even according to the view that disputes that rule there, as cited by the Shach in seif katan 6, that dispute applies specifically to a creditor. A debtor should certainly be believed through such a migo, just as he is believed against a document to claim payment through a migo of forgery, or to claim that he returned an item through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances, for the reason I explained there. Likewise, at the end of the chapter Zeh Borer in Sanhedrin, even Rav Nachman, who does not accept the special credibility of a depositary, agrees that where the depositary has a migo that he could have destroyed the document, he is believed. The same should seemingly apply here. There is a further difficulty with an unverified document. The Mechaber rules that the debtor takes a hesset oath and departs, which implies that beit din removes the document from the creditor and tears it once the debtor takes the oath. Why? The debtor is believed only through the migo of saying that the document is forged. Had he actually claimed forgery while admitting that the instrument belongs to the creditor, the creditor could certainly say, "Leave the document in your custody until I find witnesses to verify it." With respect to half the document, the creditor is treated as being in possession, just as where the entire document is in his hand we do not take it away merely because the debtor claims forgery. At minimum, beit din should grant the creditor a period of time in which to verify it. I initially wished to say that we do not apply a migo to impair a document, as Knesset HaGedolah writes in his rules of migo. The presumption that a document in the creditor's hand has not been paid is stronger than the presumption that a debt has not been paid within its term, regarding which there is already a question whether we say migo against a presumption. Proof of this may be brought from the many authorities who hold that one who admits having written a document need not verify it. Nevertheless, our accepted rule is that with an unverified document the debtor is believed to say, "I paid," by virtue of the migo that he could say, "It is forged," and is believed to say, "I returned it," through the migo that he could say, "It was lost through unavoidable circumstances." The explanation is that where the defendant can defeat the document by another claim, such as forgery or unavoidable loss, the claim of payment against that very document itself becomes a strong claim. The argument "why is your document in his hand?" no longer carries its usual force, because a person is not concerned to leave such a document with the creditor when he relies upon another defense that would defeat it. Sema in siman 46, seif katan 2, writes explicitly that the debtor may say, "I left it in his hand because if he sued me I would say it was forged." Indeed, this must be the explanation. Even in a setting where such a migo would not ordinarily operate, such as against one witness, where migo does not remove the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay, as in the case of Rabbi Abba's ingot, nevertheless with a document the debtor may claim payment through a migo of forgery. This remains so even where one witness attests to the verification of the document and even where the document contains no property lien, or the creditor waived the lien. Had the debtor claimed forgery he would have been required to take a Torah oath against the witness, yet the migo is still effective. We are compelled to say that this is because whenever the debtor can exempt himself by another defense against the document, the claim of payment itself is substantial. Accordingly, in our case, where the creditor is in possession of half the document, it is as though he possesses a document for half the amount. The debtor is not believed to claim payment merely because he had a migo that he could have destroyed the document. With an unverified document, however, the debtor is believed because as long as it remains unverified it is not considered a valid document, and a migo may be used against it. This resembles what the Shach writes at the end of simanim 57 and 83. Although a creditor can sometimes extract money by means of a migo where he has a valid document, when the document is unverified it is treated as a mere shard and he is no longer believed, even if he later verifies it. So too here, where the debtor has a migo that he could have destroyed an unverified instrument, the instrument is treated as a shard for this purpose, and later verification does not restore the earlier evidentiary position. This approach could also resolve the words of Rashi at the end of Zeh Borer and of Sema in siman 47, seif katan 3, who interpret the phrase "and it was established in beit din" to mean that it was verified. Tosafot there and the Shach ask that even after verification the depositary still has the migo that he could have destroyed it together with its verification. According to what I have written, it is well understood. Once the document is verified, a migo does not defeat the presumption of a valid document, even while it is in a third party's hand, and there is also the Shach's reasoning there in seif katan 6. Only where it is unverified does the migo operate for the reason stated above. Yet the words of the authorities imply that a debtor may use a migo even against a verified document. See what I wrote in siman 82. Therefore, the correct explanation here is that the case is one where both parties came before beit din holding the document, beit din took custody of it, and the debtor did not say within the period of immediate speech, "It has been paid." At that point the migo would be retroactive, and a migo operates only when asserted within immediate speech, as is clear in many places in the Gemara and the authorities. Alternatively, the case is one in which the document could not be torn. Once beit din has taken custody of the half that supports the creditor, the debtor is no longer believed. If, where both are holding the document, the debtor claims, "I never borrowed," they likewise divide it. We treat the half held by the creditor as though it were in his possession alone, against which the debtor is not believed by any ordinary claim. This is contrary to what Pnei Yehoshua writes in Bava Metzia 7.
Half of its value. Bava Metzia 7 states that when two people are holding a garment and divide it, division means according to monetary value. Otherwise, the physical division itself could cause loss. The same applies here. At first sight, since the Gemara compares the document to a garment, there is a difficulty. Even when a garment is appraised, one party can tell the other to purchase or sell his share at a price above the appraisal. Certainly one partner cannot force the other to sell his half at the appraised value. Why, then, can the debtor not be told by the creditor: "Why should you appraise my half? Since we each hold the document equally, write a receipt for half the debt instrument. I do not wish to sell my half of the debt instrument." There is no difficulty where the instrument is appraised below face value because the debtor is poor. If the note is for one hundred and the debtor owns only fifty, the note is worth no more than fifty. If the creditor merely writes a receipt reducing the debt by fifty, the division is not equal. The creditor would receive the entire fifty that is collectible and thus worth fifty, while the debtor would receive the remaining fifty of face value that is not worth even a perutah. Similarly, if a note for one hundred is appraised at fifty because the debtor is known to be difficult or dangerous to collect from, it is clear that a note for fifty is worth proportionally more than half the value of a note for one hundred. A smaller debt instrument does not necessarily have the same proportional valuation as a larger one. Thus, writing a receipt for half the face amount would not divide the value equally. However, if the note is appraised at less than face value solely because payment is due in the future, so that the discount rate is proportionally the same for a smaller and larger note, then it appears that the creditor may say, "I wish to wait for my half and I do not want an appraisal." In that circumstance they can divide equally by having him write a receipt for half the debt instrument.
