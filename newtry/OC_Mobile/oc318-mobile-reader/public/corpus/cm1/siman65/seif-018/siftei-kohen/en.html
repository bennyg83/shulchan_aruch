When the creditor admits, etc. The Sema writes as follows: The wording specifically says that the creditor admits, because then there is no concern for collusion by which he seeks to remove his debt from one to whom he himself owes money. This too is for the reason stated above in seif katan 19, that since there is a valid receipt before us supporting his admission, the Sages were concerned only where there is a definite loss to a creditor, as written below in siman 86, seif 5. See also what I wrote above at the beginning of siman 47. But if the debtor admits that he has not yet paid, the receipt is not returned to the creditor, because it was impaired by having been lost, and we are concerned for collusion intended to remove property from purchasers who bought from the debtor, as written above in seif 6. End of the Sema's words. His words are incorrect from beginning to end. What he writes about collusion intended to remove the creditor's debt is certainly incorrect. We would not suspect such collusion even where there would be a definite loss to his creditor, because if the creditor wished, he could simply tear up the debt document, burn it, or hide it. The Bach's words in seif 23 are likewise not satisfactory on this point. What the Sema writes based on his earlier reasoning in seif katan 19 I have already rejected above in seif 7, seif katan 22. Moreover, in the Gemara, at the end of Bava Metzia 19a, the Gemara asks that perhaps the creditor sold the document, and answers that he can in any event forgive the debt at any time, as below in siman 66, seif 23. That reasoning applies here as well according to the view that one may forgive a debt even against his own creditor, as I wrote above in siman 47. What the Sema further writes, that if the debtor admits the debt has not been paid we do not return the receipt because its loss impaired it, is astonishing. On the contrary, because the receipt was impaired by having been lost, the debt document is all the more presumed intact, since no defect occurred in the document. Why should we suspect collusion? Proof is that the document is in any event in the creditor's possession, so how would purchasers from the debtor know of the receipt? Further, when the creditor admits payment we return the receipt to the debtor and do not fear that the debtor will return it to the creditor or destroy it. One cannot answer that when it is returned to the debtor we tear the debt document, because then the ruling should not say, "return it to the debtor," but, "return the debt document to the debtor." The implication is that the debt document is not before us. The Gemara and poskim likewise prove this explicitly, because they discuss whether to fear that the woman sold her ketubah, and answer that she can forgive it. Only according to Abaye does the Gemara answer that the ketubah itself is before us. Thus, a receipt is returned even when the ketubah is not present. Rather, the law is certainly as I have written. The Tur's wording, "when the creditor admits," is not intended to exclude a case where the debtor admits. On the contrary, the case of the creditor's admission needed to be taught, because one might have thought that perhaps he had sold the debt document, as discussed in the Gemara. The Bach, at the end of his discussion, also reaches the conclusion I have written, although he needlessly expressed uncertainty at the outset. The matter is clear as I have explained.
He does not admit, etc. This concerns a receipt that has not yet been formally authenticated, but can be authenticated, such as where beit din recognizes the signatures or can authenticate it by one of the methods explained in siman 46. If it were actually authenticated, meaning that an authentication certification was written on the receipt itself, then even if the creditor does not admit payment it would obviously be returned to the debtor, because it would be valid even if found in the creditor's own possession, as explained below. If it cannot be authenticated, then even if it were in the debtor's possession it would have no effect, as I explained.
It is nothing. This applies even if witnesses are signed on the receipt and their signatures can be authenticated. In the next seif katan I cite the Yerushalmi that with respect to a gift document, such as a document stating, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so," or the like, if a receipt is found in the possession of the holder of the gift document stating that the gift document was paid, the gift document in his possession is void. The reason our Gemara does not answer in this manner is that our Gemara understood that the Mishnah presumptively concerns all types of documents. The Baal HaItur also cites this Yerushalmi.
Even if it is in the debtor's handwriting. The Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, at the end of part 3, writes that Rabbi Yehudah Barceloni was uncertain where the receipt is in the debtor's handwriting, and the Baal HaTerumos ruled that it is ineffective. One who examines his words will see that this conclusion is not compelling. I found that the Yerushalmi at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia states explicitly that a receipt in the debtor's handwriting is valid. It says there: Rav Yirmiyah said in the name of Rav that a receipt found in the creditor's possession, written in the creditor's handwriting, is invalid, because we say that he was merely occupied with arranging his documents. But if it is found in another person's possession it is valid. Rabbi Yitzchak bar Nachman said in the name of Shmuel: a receipt is never valid until it leaves the creditor's possession in the debtor's handwriting. The Yerushalmi then asks from the oath, "Father did not instruct us, father did not tell us, and we did not find among his documents that this document was paid." This implies that if they did find that it was paid, the notation is effective. Rabbi Yose bar Bun answers that the case can be established with a testamentary gift document, because a person is not ordinarily careless so as to impair his own testamentary document. End of the Yerushalmi. It appears to me that its meaning is as follows. When the writing is in the debtor's possession it is called a shover, a receipt, while whenever it is not in the debtor's possession it is called a simpon, as Tosafos write at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia and at the end of Perek Zeh Borer. The Yerushalmi says that when it is in the possession of another, meaning a third party, it is valid. Rabbi Yitzchak bar Nachman says that even when it is in the possession of a third party it is invalid, following the second version of Rav Nachman's ruling at the end of Perek Zeh Borer, whereas we rule like the first version there because the second version is refuted. He continues that a simpon is not valid until it leaves the creditor's possession in the debtor's handwriting. It cannot mean that even a receipt in the debtor's handwriting is invalid. If so, where would one find the case of the Mishnah, which says that if simponot are found among the documents one acts according to them? The Yerushalmi does not establish that Mishnah where the notation was found among torn documents. It then asks from the oath concerning the father's records. If a notation that the document was paid were found, why should that matter, since it is found in the creditor's possession? Certainly we do not ordinarily suspect that there might have been a receipt in the debtor's handwriting requiring the heirs to swear about it, for why should such a possibility occur to us? The Yerushalmi answers that the case is a testamentary gift document, meaning a gift document stating, for example, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so," and a notation is found there that this gift document was paid. A person does not ordinarily impair his own testamentary dispositions. Only with a debt document arising from a loan do we say that the creditor may merely have been occupied with preparing his papers, but with a gift document, since it is only a gift, he is especially careful and would not casually prepare a notation impairing his testamentary gift. I later saw that the Baal HaItur, folio 25c, also cites this Yerushalmi, except that in his text Rabbi Yitzchak bar Nachman is quoted as saying, "A simpon is never valid until it leaves the creditor's possession," followed by the question from the heirs' oath. This is clearly a scribal error, as is evident even from the Baal HaItur's own words. Otherwise, how could he say, "A simpon is never valid, etc."? He should instead have said, "A simpon found in the creditor's possession is valid." Further, the question from the heirs' oath should then have appeared before the words of Rabbi Yitzchak bar Nachman, as an objection to Rav Yirmiyah, not afterward. Rather, the Baal HaItur's text must be corrected to read: "A simpon is never valid until it leaves the creditor's possession in the debtor's handwriting," precisely as in the Yerushalmi I cited and as I have explained.
Or the creditor. This appears to refer back to the preceding case, where a receipt is found written in the form of testimony and witnesses are signed upon it. In that case we say that even though the creditor wrote it in his own handwriting, showing that he knows how to write, he may nevertheless have feared that at twilight he would be unable to find witnesses, as appears from the Gemara. But if the document is written in the form of his own admission, such as, "I admit that this document has been paid," in a legally effective form as explained below at the beginning of siman 69, it should be valid. Why would he write such an admission if he knows how to write for himself? It is strained to say that he feared he might later be unable to write it. Still, the Rambam's wording in chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh, cited by the Mechaber above in siman 56, end of seif 1, appears at first glance to make no distinction. This requires study. See also below in siman 81, seif 17, seif katan 44.
Unless the document, etc. Rashi writes that in his view the Gemara's statement, "it was found among torn documents," means that the receipt itself was found among torn documents. We may therefore say that the debt was collected and this receipt is no longer needed by the creditor, but belonged to the debtor, who forgot it in the creditor's possession. The creditor therefore placed it among documents he no longer needed. If the creditor had written it merely so that it would be ready when the debtor came to pay, he would not have placed it among useless documents. End of Rashi's words. Responsa Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 185, cites the Ritva in the name of Tosafos to the same effect, as I cite below in seif katan 60. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Nesiv 30, part 1, likewise writes that the Mishnah's rule, "if simponot are found among them, one acts according to the simponot," concerns documents on which such notations were written that are therefore presumed paid. Tosafos explain this where he found the receipt among torn documents; otherwise, perhaps he had ordered it written merely so that it would be ready when the debtor came to pay. The Baal HaItur's view is necessarily the same, for in the letter shin, entry Shovar, folio 25a, he cites: a simpon found in the creditor's possession, even if written in his own handwriting, is treated as mere preparation and is invalid; but if found among torn documents, one acts according to the simponot. This explanation appears correct in the Gemara. The Gemara says simply that it was found among torn documents, which naturally refers to the receipt under discussion. If it referred to the debt document, it should have said that the debt document was found among them. Further, in the case of documents belonging to several men named Yosef ben Shimon, the Gemara likewise answers that it was found among torn documents, and does not say "they were found" in the plural. Thus it clearly refers to the receipt. Rashi's explanation there, that the debt document was found among paid documents and each person says, "this is my document," is unclear to me unless there is a textual error in his words, because the case necessarily involves documents differentiated by a third identifying generation, as Rashi himself explains immediately before. Further, it appears that the Gemara's answer, "found among his documents," means among his torn documents, and the case there concerns a receipt. This also explains why, regarding a simpon bearing witnesses, the Gemara did not answer that it was found among torn documents, because there the baraita does not say that it was found among his documents. It appears that the case specifically concerns a receipt bearing witnesses, as Rashi there implies, namely an ordinary receipt. Otherwise it is merely a worthless scrap, and it would make no sense to say that it belonged to the debtor and that the debtor forgot it with the creditor, for why would the debtor take an unsigned receipt that has no legal value? See what I write below in seif katan 65.
Among his documents, etc. See Responsa Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 185, who is uncertain whether all the surrounding documents must be torn or whether a majority is treated like the whole. See there.
The torn ones. The Tur's text reads "the paid ones." It appears to me that this is a scribal error, because the Tur afterward says, "even though this one is not torn, etc.," as does the Mechaber. If the text were "paid," it should have said merely, "even though it is not torn." By saying, "even though this one is not torn," the wording implies that the other documents are torn. Moreover, what novelty would there be in saying that this one is not torn? Rather, the case is that it lies among torn documents, and one might have thought it is superior because unlike the others it is not torn. The ruling teaches otherwise. This appears correct, unlike the Beit Yosef and Bach, who read the Tur as "paid" and therefore write that the Tur apparently had a different text in the Gemara. The Bach is then forced to say that when the Tur later says, "even though it is found among torn documents it is not impaired," he writes "torn" only as an added novelty. That is incorrect. Nevertheless, as a matter of law, paid documents appear equivalent to torn ones. In the Rambam at the end of chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh, and in the Maggid Mishneh there, the word "paid" appears in place of "torn."
Nevertheless, the document has been impaired and we tear it. So too is stated in the Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, parts 2 and 3, and this is also clearly implied by the Rambam at the end of chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh.
And we rely upon the receipt. This applies whether the receipt is in the creditor's possession or in the possession of his heirs, as stated by the Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilchos Malveh and by the Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, part 3, and as is implied by the Tur and Mechaber. It appears that this is so even if the creditor claims with certainty that the debt is not paid, because if he does not know, the debt document is invalid in any event, as explained above in siman 59. The Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, part 2, writes explicitly: if the creditor knows with certainty that the debt is not paid, then even if the debt document is found among torn documents, and beit din knows that fact, his power is not impaired, because perhaps he was not careful and the circumstance happened accidentally. The same applies to his heir if the heir knows the status of the debt and knows with certainty that it is not paid; he collects with the document, because the document is impaired only where it is found among torn documents together with a simpon. In that case, because there are two defects, both a simpon and its placement among torn documents, even if the creditor or his heirs claim, "we know it was not paid; I prepared this receipt only so that it would be ready in my possession," they are not believed, because the document has been impaired and it is immediately returned to the debtor. It is reasonable that any document whose status is unknown, or which is found among torn documents together with a simpon, is impaired even if it contains a credibility clause. End of his words, cited by the Beit Yosef.
Even if there are no witnesses on the receipt. This ruling is from the Rambam, followed by the Baal HaTerumos, Tur, and Mechaber. It appears that the Rambam derived it from the Gemara at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, which asks concerning a simpon that bears witnesses and does not answer, as it did earlier, that the document was found among torn documents. Apparently, if that were the case, why should the presence of witnesses matter? Even without witnesses the receipt would be valid. Still, this is not a conclusive proof even according to those who explain that "found" refers to the debt document, because the baraita there does not simply say "found," as the earlier cases do. This also disposes of the proof advanced by Gidulei Terumah from the Gemara's statement, "this too is reasonable, etc. If there are no witnesses at all, need it be taught?" He argues that the novelty would have been that even when found among torn documents it is ineffective. But there too the baraita does not state that it was found. In any event, that proof is independently weak, because the Gemara could still have regarded it as obvious that an unsigned receipt is ineffective. According to Rashi's interpretation, cited in seif katan 54, that "found" refers to the receipt, none of these proofs exists. Although I write below in seif katan 60 that according to Rashi the same defect arises when the debt document itself is found among torn documents, that applies only regarding the heirs, not where the creditor himself claims with certainty that it is unpaid. Accordingly, one could say that an unsigned receipt does not impair the document in such a case. Nevertheless, the Rambam and Baal HaTerumos clearly imply that even here the debt document is void despite the lack of witnesses on the receipt, and the Tur and Mechaber imply the same. Their ruling requires study in practice.
Even though it was found, etc. Meaning, even if the debt document was found among torn documents, that alone does not impair it, and the heirs may collect with it. The Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 53, part 2, records a second view that because it was found among torn documents the document is impaired, no collection may be made with it, and it should remain deposited until Eliyahu comes. The Gidulei Terumah there is surprised by this view. Rav Safra, at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, establishes the Mishnah in Shevuos concerning the oath, "we did not find among our father's documents that this debt document was paid," in a case where the document was found among torn documents. The case must concern heirs who do not know its status, because Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah teaches that even if the son was born after his father's death, he may swear and collect. If so, asks Gidulei Terumah, what benefit is there in the absence of a receipt if the debt document itself lies among torn documents? He leaves the matter difficult. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. This second view explains, as Rashi does, that it is the receipt that was found among torn documents, as I wrote above in seif katan 54. Responsa Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 185, likewise cites the Ritva in the name of Tosafos that the interpretation is that the receipt was found there. The Ritva adds that the same law applies if the debt document itself is found among torn documents, for it is invalidated by that presumption alone. This is clear.
It is not impaired, etc. I have already written above that some authorities hold that the debt document is impaired merely by being found among torn documents and should remain deposited until Eliyahu comes. This is in truth a sound view according to Rashi and the authorities following him whom I cited above in seif katan 54, who explain that "found" refers to the receipt. Just as when a receipt is found among torn documents we say that because he cast it among the torn papers he certainly discarded it as no longer needed, the same should apply to a debt document. I have already written in seif katan 54 that this approach appears primary in the Gemara, and Mahara ibn Sasson, siman 185, likewise rules in an actual case that at least no collection may be made with such a document. Nevertheless, it appears that this applies specifically to the creditor's heirs. If the creditor himself is alive and claims with certainty that the debt is unpaid, he may collect even if the debt document was found among torn documents. The Baal HaTerumos clearly implies that even those authorities agree to this, as cited by the Beit Yosef. Although Rashi's explanation at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, that the Mishnah's rule concerning simponot is an independent case where one finds among his own papers a receipt written against one of his debt documents, appears to imply that the Gemara's answer, where the receipt was found among torn documents, applies even to the living creditor, Rashi's interpretation there is not compelled. I already gave another explanation above in seif katan 25. Further, even if a receipt found among torn documents impairs the debt document despite the creditor's certain claim, the same does not necessarily follow where the debt document itself was found among torn documents. The creditor can plausibly say that when he placed it there he mistakenly thought it had been paid, but he now remembers that it was not, as above in siman 59. Such an explanation is not available regarding a receipt. Proof is that the creditor himself may collect with the debt document while his heirs may not according to the second view in Baal HaTerumos. Ordinarily we advance on behalf of heirs every claim their father could have made. Rather, here, since the father died without asserting, "I was mistaken when I cast it among the torn documents," the document remains in its state of impairment and no collection is made. See carefully.
And they collect with it. Meaning, even his heirs may collect, as the Tur writes. This is so even where they do not know whether it was paid. It follows the first view in the Baal HaTerumos. The Tur proves this according to his approach, following the Rif and Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, that "found" refers to the debt document itself. If so, Rav Safra's establishment of the Mishnah proves that the heirs can collect. The Tur and Mechaber likewise rule this below in siman 108, seif 5. But when the creditor himself is alive, he may collect only if he claims with certainty that it was not paid, as explained.
