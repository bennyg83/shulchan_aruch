When the creditor admits it. See Shach, seif katan 49, who writes concerning the Sema that his words are incorrect from beginning to end. In my humble opinion, however, all of the Sema's words are clear and correct, and I will answer the Shach's objections in order. The Shach first questions the Sema's statement that we suspect collusion intended to remove the creditor's own debt obligation. The Shach objects that such collusion surely need not be suspected even where another creditor would definitely lose, because this creditor could simply have torn or burned his own note. There is no difficulty. The novelty is that the rule applies even where the instrument has already been established in beit din, so that the creditor no longer has a migo that he could have destroyed it. If one asks that he still has a migo that he could write a new receipt, Drishah answers this in his discussion beginning "one who finds a receipt." It also appears to me from what Drishah writes under "or the debtor has a handwritten document," that collusion is suspected only where returning this document will enable someone to remove the very field already acquired by a purchaser. We are not equally concerned about mere extinguishment of a lien against property that the creditor has not yet acquired. Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia 19 states the same principle: collusion is suspected where, if the field is removed, it will be held through wrongful taking, but not merely to frustrate a lien. Accordingly, even where this creditor owes money to another creditor and seeks to extinguish that debt by admitting that a found receipt belongs to the debtor, his admission should be effective and the receipt should be returned to the debtor. This is also indicated by the fact that the authorities do not distinguish in the rule that where the creditor admits, the receipt is returned to the debtor. They do not say that if another creditor is present and this admission would deprive him of collection, the creditor is not believed. Rather, he is still believed for the Sema's reason, because a valid receipt supports the admission, and where the consequence is merely preventing a lien from being enforced we do not suspect collusion. This may in fact be the Sema's intent. When the Sema concludes that we are concerned only where there is a definite loss to a creditor, he means a case where there is no valid receipt supporting the admission and the creditor merely says orally that the debt was paid, as discussed in siman 47. In such a case, once another creditor is before us, he no longer has the migo that he could simply write a new receipt. According to Taz, who writes that there is no injury to others here because without the creditor's admission the other creditor could not collect from the debtor in any event, since a receipt was found and the debtor insists that it fell from him, then certainly even where another creditor is present and the admission would prevent his collection, the receipt is returned to the debtor. It is also possible that this is what the Sema means by saying that it is not a clear loss. Since without the admission the rule is that the receipt is returned to neither party, the matter is doubtful, and the creditor of the creditor certainly cannot collect on the basis of that doubt until the matter is clarified. Thus, at the moment of the creditor's admission there is no definite loss. Beit Shmuel at the end of Even HaEzer siman 110, however, writes that when a receipt is found the debt instrument remains in force. One must therefore say that when the creditor himself admits payment the case is stronger, because the receipt supports his admission, while the creditor's creditor claims only doubt and the debtor claims certainty. The Shach also challenges the Sema's statement that when the debtor finds the receipt we do not return it to the creditor because the receipt has been impaired by falling. The Shach argues that the reverse should be true: the receipt, not the debt instrument, has been impaired by the fall, and the debt instrument should remain in full force. This is likewise not difficult in light of Rashi's explanation, cited by the Shach in seif katan 54, regarding a receipt found among torn documents. Rashi writes that this indicates that the debt was collected and the creditor no longer needed the receipt, so it was placed among the torn documents. If the creditor himself had prepared the receipt, by contrast, he would have guarded it because he prepared it for a purpose. The same applies to a fallen receipt. Since the creditor admits that he did not guard it, we infer that the debt was actually collected, the debtor left the receipt with the creditor, and the creditor no longer needed it and therefore was not careful with it. If it had been a receipt prepared by the creditor for future use, he would have guarded it. Thus, the defect created by the receipt's fall itself supports the conclusion that the debt was collected, exactly as the Sema writes. The Shach's further proof can likewise be reconciled with the following rule. He asks that if so, when the creditor admits payment, why do we return the receipt to the debtor? We should fear that the debtor will later return it to the creditor or tear it. There is no difficulty. Bava Metzia 19 states regarding a get that it may be returned because when the woman later seeks to collect from purchasers they can demand proof of when it reached her hand. By contrast, with a lost monetary instrument purchasers may not come to challenge it, because they may say that since the Sages returned it, beit din must have investigated and established its validity. We must infer that the discovery of a document in beit din receives publicity and that everyone knows when a found document has been returned by beit din. Otherwise, even in the case of a get purchasers could not demand proof, because the ordinary presumption is that a document was delivered on the day it was written, as Tosafot explain. In the case of a monetary document, the purchasers do not challenge because they assume that by returning it beit din determined the matter. Accordingly, once a receipt has been found and the rule is that it is returned to neither party, the creditor cannot use the debt instrument against purchasers, because the matter remains doubtful. Therefore we do not return the receipt to the creditor. If we did, purchasers would not challenge him, for they would reason that beit din must have returned the receipt to the creditor after determining that it fell from him. Returning it to the debtor, however, causes no harm. On the contrary, if purchasers hear that beit din returned a valid receipt to the debtor, they will certainly challenge any later collection attempt. Returning the receipt to the debtor therefore creates no greater impairment than leaving it in beit din, and may actually clarify the matter further. If the debtor later gives the receipt back to the creditor or tears it, that is irrelevant. Once the receipt was found, its discovery is public knowledge. Indeed, it is precisely because the discovery and return are public that the Gemara allows a get to be returned. Returning the receipt to the creditor would worsen the purchasers' position, because they could assume that beit din determined that it had fallen from the creditor, whereas leaving it in beit din creates no such inference. This is clear to me, and I am surprised that the Shach did not address it. Even according to Beit Shmuel at the end of Even HaEzer 110, who holds that the debt instrument remains in force, once beit din itself returns the receipt to the debtor, that judicial act certainly has publicity and establishes that the debt instrument is treated as paid.
The debtor's handwriting. See Shach, seif katan 12, whose interpretation of the Yerushalmi is strained, as Urim VeTumim notes. The Shach also writes that Rabbi Yose bar Nachman, who holds that a receipt emerging from another person's hand is invalid, follows the second version of Rav Nachman at the end of Zeh Borer. At first sight this is difficult. Rav Nachman there disputes only a receipt that cannot be verified or that lacks witnesses, where the document and the receipts emerge from the depositary's hand and have already been established in beit din, so that the depositary now lacks a migo and merely once had one. In the Yerushalmi, however, the receipt appears to be one whose witnesses can be verified, because it is taught in parallel to a receipt emerging from the creditor's hand, and where the signatures cannot be verified even a receipt in the debtor's hand is ineffective, as the Shach proves in seif katan 3. Where witnesses can verify the receipt, even Rav Nachman in Sanhedrin agrees that the depositary is believed, since he has a migo that he could have returned it to the debtor. A prior establishment in beit din does not negate the effect of a verified receipt, as the Shach writes in siman 56, seif katan 1. According to what I wrote there, however, that statement of the Shach was made only according to Maharsha's interpretation, and the matter is explained there. Tumim also struggles with the Yerushalmi. In my opinion, the Yerushalmi simply does not accept the Bavli's rationale that the creditor merely prepared the receipt in advance. Proof is that the Yerushalmi says that a receipt emerging from the creditor's hand in the creditor's handwriting is invalid because perhaps he was merely practicing his writing. It does not give the reason that he may have prepared it. Evidently, it does not rely on that rationale. Rather, the Yerushalmi speaks of a receipt without witnesses and without the creditor's signature, which would have the status of witnesses, but merely in the creditor's handwriting, stating that the particular debt instrument has been paid. Such a writing is invalid because perhaps he wrote it only as a writing exercise. If it is in another person's handwriting, however, that other person is believed through a migo that he could have delivered it to the debtor. In that setting we do not say that he merely wrote it as an exercise, even without a signature, as explained in siman 69, seif 1. Rabbi Yose bar Nachman holds that even there one can claim that it was written as an exercise, in accordance with the view of Ritva cited by the Shach in siman 69, seif katan 4. The Yerushalmi then challenges this from the rule that where it is found among the creditor's records that a particular note was paid, the entry is effective. The Yerushalmi was reluctant to establish that case as a receipt signed by witnesses, because then the debtor should know of it, since a receipt would belong to him. Nor does the Yerushalmi assume that the creditor himself writes and signs witnesses' names on a receipt. Rather, the case must be that it was found written in the creditor's own handwriting that the note was paid, which shows that such a writing can be valid. The answer is that the entry was found in his testamentary record, meaning a will in which all his testamentary matters are set forth. Even if he did not sign his name, people do not ordinarily write a detailed testamentary document merely as a writing exercise. A person is not likely to blemish his own will in that fashion. It appears that this rule is correct even according to the Bavli, which does employ the rationale that the creditor may have prepared a receipt. Where a lengthy will contains many matters, it is not plausible that he wrote all of it merely in preparation to hand a separate receipt to someone. That concern applies specifically where an isolated receipt is found.
Unless the debt instrument. See Shach, seif katan 54, through his statement, "and this also resolves why the Gemara does not answer..." His meaning is that according to their interpretation, in which the debt instrument itself was found, there is no difficulty at all as to why the Gemara did not answer that it was found among torn documents. As for the Shach's objection to Rashi, who says that each party claims, "This is my document," there is no difficulty. Rashi refers to the document mentioned in the receipt, namely the note described as paid. Each claimant says that the note referred to in the receipt is his own. Tumim explains it the same way.
Even though it was found. See Shach, seif katan 60, who cites Bach as questioning the opinion in Baal HaTerumot that where a note is found among torn documents, even heirs must specifically claim with certainty that it was not paid. Bach asks from Rav Safra's explanation of the oath that the heirs did not find among their father's torn documents an indication that this note was paid. That explanation must apply even where the heirs do not know the matter's circumstances, because Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah teaches that even a son born after his father's death may swear and collect. If so, what is gained by swearing that no receipt was found? Bach does not simply ask that if the heirs claim certainty, why do they swear only that their father did not instruct them, since when they themselves make a certain claim they would ordinarily need to swear to that claim just as their father would, as explained in siman 75, seif 16. It therefore seems that they need not claim certainty that the debt was never paid. It is enough that they know with certainty that the note was not placed among the torn documents because of payment, but for some other reason. Once they know the reason it was placed there, that suffices, even if they do not know whether it was paid afterward. Similarly, heirs who know the reason their father placed it there are sufficient, even if they do not know whether payment later occurred. Since they need not swear to the certain matter that they themselves know in this setting, they swear only that their father did not instruct them. Bach therefore had to challenge from a son born after the father's death, who cannot possibly know why the note was placed among the torn documents. Even this question is not so strong. Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah's phrase "he swears and collects" need not refer to the specific oaths enumerated earlier in the Mishnah, because the oath that the father did not instruct them is not required in every instance, as explained in siman 108. It is possible that the son in fact swears only that he did not find the note among the torn documents, an oath heirs must also take, as the Shach explains there. A different difficulty remains against the authorities who require both a found receipt and the note's presence among torn documents. A note that fell and was found in a refuse heap seems to suffer a greater defect than one simply found among torn documents in the creditor's house. If a note fell in the marketplace, even where we know it fell from the creditor, the rule is that it is not returned because the fall impairs it. Yet a note merely found in his home among torn documents remains in force absent additional evidence. Thus, a marketplace fall is a stronger defect. Why, then, where the note is among torn documents and a receipt is also found in the marketplace do we return the receipt to the debtor? Indeed, even if the receipt is found in the creditor's home or in a third party's hand, it is returned to the debtor. A receipt in a third party's hand where he does not know its circumstances is comparable to one found in the marketplace. Yet if the debt instrument itself is found in the marketplace, not among torn documents, and a receipt is also found in the marketplace, the rule in seif 8 is that neither is returned, as explained by the Shach in seif katan 5. There is also a difficulty from the implication that if the creditor himself finds a note that had fallen, the note remains fully in force, even if a receipt is also found, since the authorities do not distinguish. Yet if the creditor finds the note among his own torn documents together with a receipt, the receipt is returned to the debtor. Likewise, according to the authorities who say that merely finding the note among torn documents requires the creditor to make a definite claim, even where the creditor himself finds it there he must make that claim, while if he himself finds a note that fell, no such distinction is stated. I later found that Mishneh LaMelech also leaves Rambam's formulation, adopted by the Mechaber in seif 8, in difficulty on the basis of this very question: from where did Rambam derive that the note must specifically be found among torn documents? It appears that finding it among torn documents is different because that is a deliberate placement, and the creditor himself created the defect. Therefore, when a receipt is also present, even if the creditor himself wrote that receipt, had the debt not truly been paid he would not have deliberately created two recognizable indications that it was paid. The combination therefore proves actual payment. A document that fell, by contrast, may simply have fallen accidentally, and the creditor did not create that defect himself. This also explains those authorities who require the creditor to claim certainty where the note is found among torn documents. Since he himself created the suspicious circumstance and now claims only uncertainty, that combination is strong evidence that the debt was paid.
