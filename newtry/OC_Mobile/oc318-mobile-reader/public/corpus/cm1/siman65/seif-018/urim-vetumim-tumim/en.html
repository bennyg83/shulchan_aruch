When the creditor admits, etc. The Sema asks that perhaps the creditor owes others and is returning the receipt merely to deprive them. His answer depends upon his approach above and is not convincing. The Shach's answer through a migo that the creditor could destroy the document also fails where the debt has already been established in beit din. The author of Chiddushei Gershuni already raised this. Nor can one invoke Shmuel's rule that a seller of a debt can waive it, because under Rabbi Natan's rule the debtor cannot waive to the prejudice of his own creditor, as explained below. Maharshal already raised the same difficulty regarding a receipt found for a ketubah and answered that Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply to ketubah. That answer does not help with an ordinary debt instrument. The difficulty is even greater because if the document was transferred through maamad sheloshtan, the transfer cannot be waived, and such transfer can be imposed without the debtor's consent. Tosafos's answer that maamad sheloshtan does not apply to ketubah again does not apply here. There is also the question of the Mechaber's ruling below that one who waives a sold debt must reimburse the purchaser for the full value of the debt. What migo is there to waive if waiver may require paying a large amount, while returning the receipt may cost only the amount actually received for the sale? This is particularly serious where the creditor sold the debt cheaply and afterward became wealthy. The explanation that a wife would always waive a ketubah for her husband also does not apply to an ordinary promissory note. Nor can one say that returning the receipt is itself an act of waiver rather than merely a migo, because in Even HaEzer the Mechaber rules that a woman who sold her ketubah and then says that it had already been paid is not believed by a migo that she could waive it, since she fears liability for the full value. If the statement itself were a waiver, the absence of a migo would be irrelevant. The correct explanation follows what I wrote above in siman 54. A person who wishes to defeat a purchaser or creditor can now have witnesses write a receipt without a date. Once undated receipts were instituted, the concern of collusion disappeared because the creditor always has that means available. The baraita's concern belongs to the earlier period when one could not write an undated receipt because a later dated receipt was invalid according to those who permitted receipts at all, especially with a ketubah, where late dating is particularly problematic. At that time the creditor could not simply create such a receipt. Nowadays, when an undated receipt may be written, there is no special concern that the present receipt is a collusive device, for he could always create an equivalent one. This does not mean that above in siman 47 a creditor who says, "the debt was paid," should be believed whenever that admission harms others, based on a migo that he could write an undated receipt. A person may hesitate to fabricate a new receipt because the collusion could be exposed and he would be caught as a liar. Here, however, there is already a receipt before us. The only alleged collusion is the same type of act that the creditor could have accomplished by writing another undated receipt. Moreover, where the receipt itself supports his statement, even a weaker migo can suffice. One may also say that where a receipt is delivered through witnesses it constitutes an actual waiver and thereby harms the creditor himself, as the Rosh and Sema indicate, so the collusion is less attractive. If the receipt is found only after the creditor's death and his heir wishes to return it, the situation is different. The heir cannot now write an undated receipt effective against a sale made by the father during his lifetime. Such a receipt would merely show an act of the heir, not of the original creditor, and would not defeat a prior purchaser except perhaps through waiver, returning us to all the earlier problems. Nevertheless, where the document contains no special credibility clause, concern for collusion because of the creditor's other debts can ordinarily be dismissed for the reasons explained in siman 47. The remaining concerns are less substantial because an ordinary promissory note is usually sold at close to its value, so a creditor generally gains little from such collusion. Tosafos explicitly says that where there is no significant gain the Sages did not ordinarily fear collusion. This is also why the Mishnah's rule concerning found receipts is not challenged in the same way as ketubah. Maharshal explains that the concern is much greater with a ketubah because the woman may have a special incentive to benefit her husband. There is an apparent contradiction in Maharsha. In one place he derives from the Mishnah concerning receipts that according to Abaye, if the creditor says to return the receipt, it may be returned because the witnesses' signatures create rights from the beginning. Elsewhere he says that ordinary receipts are less subject to collusion. The two statements are reconciled because the Mishnah says "receipts" generally and therefore includes ketubah receipts. With ordinary debt receipts, the fact that the creditor instructs return is generally enough. With a ketubah receipt, where collusion is a real concern, Abaye must rely on the rule that the witnesses' signatures create rights from the first moment. Thus Maharsha's words are consistent.
Now, etc. Those who hold that witnesses' signatures create rights from the outset, including the Tur in siman 39, say that with a receipt there is no need for it ever to reach the debtor. The Shach asks: if so, why does the Mishnah say that a found receipt is not returned because perhaps the creditor reconsidered? If delivery is unnecessary, reconsideration should not matter. From this he rejects the Tur's position. The matter can be explained. If when the creditor orders witnesses to write the receipt he expressly conditions its effect upon actual payment, then obviously it does not take effect until the condition is fulfilled. Every monetary condition is valid, and even a shtar haknaah can be made conditional upon a future loan. Therefore, a creditor can have a receipt prepared in advance for delivery when payment occurs without thereby immediately waiving the debt. A found receipt may therefore have been written on that express condition, while no payment ever occurred, and the creditor may have reconsidered giving it. This also explains why witnesses were permitted to prepare receipts in advance. Without such a condition, writing the receipt could itself extinguish the debt under the doctrine that signatures create rights. The ordinary assumption must therefore be that the creditor's instruction means: write it so that if I later accept payment or choose to waive, the receipt will be effective retroactively. With a sale or gift document, actual acquisition depends on the instrument, so even according to Abaye it does not operate fully without ultimate delivery. With a receipt or waiver, however, the substantive act of waiver can be accomplished orally. Thus the creditor can say: if I later waive or accept payment, let that waiver be treated as having taken effect from the date of the receipt. The document is not itself the sole means of acquisition. This distinction also resolves the Tur's difference from the Rif concerning a gift or sale document written in Nisan but delivered later after the donor had meanwhile transferred the property to someone else. The Rif says the earlier document may no longer operate because the donor's intervening disposition uprooted it. The Tur does not necessarily follow that rule regarding receipts, because a receipt does not depend upon the paper as an instrument of conveyance. The future oral waiver can activate the earlier written receipt retroactively. Therefore, even if the creditor sold the note in the interim, his later effective waiver can relate back to the date of the receipt if that was the original condition. Thus the Mishnah can properly say that a found receipt is not returned because perhaps it was written conditionally and the condition never occurred. If the creditor now says, "Give it," his present waiver activates the earlier receipt, and the rights relate back. The Shach's objection therefore falls away. This distinction between receipts and dispositive instruments is clear and resolves the difficulties that have troubled many authorities.
He admits, etc. If the debtor says that the receipt should be returned to the creditor because the debt has not yet been paid, the Sema writes that it should nevertheless not be returned, just as a found promissory note is not returned even where the borrower admits the debt, because of possible collusion. The Shach objects that a fallen receipt weakens only the receipt, while the creditor's promissory note itself remains in his possession and was never weakened. But below, seif 19, where a receipt is held by a third party and its status is unknown, even if the debtor admits that the debt was not paid we do not return it to the creditor because of possible collusion. The Shach attempts to distinguish that case because the creditor admits having placed it with the third party. That does not answer the point. If a receipt lay in the creditor's possession without payment, why would he fail to guard it carefully, knowing that if the debtor found it the debt could be lost? The fact that it fell from him itself weakens the promissory note, because it suggests that perhaps he had already been paid and therefore ceased guarding the receipt. Chiddushei Gershuni writes likewise. There is no need to pursue strained distinctions.
If it is in the borrower's handwriting, etc. The Shach cites the Yerushalmi, which says that a receipt is valid only in certain circumstances and asks how this accords with the heirs' oath, "we did not find among our father's papers that this debt was paid." He explains the Yerushalmi to mean that if the father wrote a testamentary gift of two hundred zuz to someone and a receipt is found beside it saying that the recipient had already received it, the receipt is valid because a person does not ordinarily impair his own testament. This explanation has no footing. The Mishnah there concerns heirs collecting a debt due to their father, not recipients collecting under a testamentary gift. The Yerushalmi means instead that the heirs swear that they did not find in their father's testament, called a deyatiki in rabbinic language, a statement that this particular promissory note had been paid. If the father himself wrote in his testament that the debtor had already paid, that is certainly reliable. A separate receipt might have been prepared in advance for possible future use and could later have been torn up if payment never came. But a person does not ordinarily write into his formal testament that a debt was paid unless it actually was. He would not expect to erase and spoil his testament later because a payment failed to materialize. Thus the heirs' oath includes the statement that they found no such entry in the father's testament. This is simple and reasonable. The Shach also argues from the Yerushalmi that if the receipt is in the borrower's handwriting it can be valid even when found in the creditor's possession. Even if such a reading appears in the Yerushalmi, our Bavli deliberately resolves the Mishnah differently, namely where the receipt is found among torn documents, and does not distinguish based upon whose handwriting appears on the receipt. This indicates that the Bavli rejects that distinction. The Yerushalmi also has variant readings, as the Ittur records. We should therefore not depart from the straightforward ruling of the Bavli.
That the document, etc. The Shach writes at length that many authorities follow Rashi, who holds that if the receipt itself is found among the creditor's torn documents, the receipt is valid, because had the creditor still needed it he would not have thrown it among discarded papers. He asks about Rashi's explanation of the case where a receipt says that the note of one Yosef ben Shimon was paid, so both same named persons' documents are treated as paid. Rashi explains that the promissory notes themselves were distinguished, while the ambiguity lies in the receipt. When the Gemara answers that it was found among torn documents, Rashi says that each one says, "this note is mine." The Shach asks that the notes themselves were identified, so what is the uncertainty? There is no difficulty. Either the word "note" can refer to the receipt itself, or, more accurately, each person says, "the note referred to in the receipt as paid is mine." That is Rashi's intent.
There are no witnesses on the receipt. The Shach explains that Rambam derived this from the Gemara's challenge that a receipt bearing witnesses can be authenticated by their signatures, implying that a receipt in the creditor's possession can be valid. Why did the Gemara not establish it as one found among torn documents? If so, why specify that witnesses signed it, since even an unsigned receipt might help? The Shach rejects this proof by saying that the baraita does not use the word "found," so the Gemara did not understand it as a receipt found among torn documents. That rejection is strained. Above I already established a strong proof for Rambam that even an unsigned receipt can be valid under the relevant circumstances.
It is no worse, etc. The Shach suggests that the alternative opinion in Baal HaTerumot, which says that heirs who make only an uncertain claim cannot collect with such a document, may depend on how one explains "found among torn documents." According to authorities who understand that the promissory note itself was found among torn documents, one can argue that a note lying there is not automatically invalid; otherwise, what need would there be for the receipt? The combination of both creates the defect. But according to Rashi, who understands that the receipt was found among torn documents, perhaps the promissory note itself would also be invalid if found there, because the creditor would have been expected to guard it just as he would guard the receipt. This does not follow. Even if a receipt found among torn papers weakens the debt, a promissory note found there without any receipt need not be treated as paid. The receipt contains an explicit statement that payment occurred; the only question is whether it was prepared in advance. Finding it among discarded papers supplies the missing indication that it was used. A promissory note by itself says nothing about payment. Why should its poor storage alone create an affirmative presumption of payment? Indeed, the heirs are required to swear that they found no receipt among the father's papers, but they are not required to swear that they did not find the promissory note itself among torn documents. If merely finding the note there weakened it, that should have been included in their oath. This is a compelling indication that the receipt and the note are not equivalent in this respect.
