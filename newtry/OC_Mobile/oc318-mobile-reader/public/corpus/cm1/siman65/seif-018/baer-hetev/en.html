Admits. The ruling specifically states that the lender admits, for then there is no concern for collusion intended to remove his debt from one to whom he himself owes money. This too follows the principle above: since a valid receipt is before us and supports his admission, the Sages were concerned only where there would certainly be a creditor's loss, as stated above in siman 86, seif 5. But if the borrower admits that he has not yet paid, the receipt is not returned to the lender, because its having been lost creates an impairment and we are concerned for collusion intended to defeat purchasers who bought from the borrower, as stated in seif 6. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach objects that the statement about concern for collusion intended to defeat purchasers is incorrect. Certainly we are not concerned for that, even where a creditor would definitely lose, since the lender could simply tear up or burn the note. Likewise, the statement, 'but if the borrower admits... because it was impaired by being lost,' is difficult, for on the contrary, since the receipt was impaired by being lost, all the more so the promissory note remains in force. Rather, the reason the text states that the lender admits is not to exclude a case where the borrower admits, but because it was necessary to state the lender's admission lest we think that perhaps he had sold the note, as stated in the Gemara. The Taz asks: If the lender admits, why is the receipt returned? We should be concerned that he wrote the loan note in Nisan but did not deliver it until Tishrei, and in the interim the lender collected land from the borrower on account of the debt. Now the borrower produces a receipt dated in Nisan and improperly removes that land. The Gemara raises a similar question in the first chapter of Bava Metzia regarding a bill of manumission. See there for his resolution.
For this. This refers to a receipt that has not yet been formally authenticated, but can be authenticated by one of the methods explained in siman 46. If it were actually authenticated, it is obvious that even if the lender does not admit, it is returned to the borrower. Conversely, even if it were in the lender's own possession, the receipt is valid only if it can be authenticated; if it cannot be authenticated, then even in the borrower's possession it has no effect. Shach.
Anything. This is so even if witnesses are signed on the receipt and their signatures can be authenticated. The Yerushalmi implies that in the case of a gift document, such as one stating, 'Give two hundred zuz to so-and-so,' and the holder of the gift document is found with a receipt stating that the gift document has been satisfied, the gift document in his possession is void. Ibid.
The borrower. The Shach rules that if the receipt is in the borrower's handwriting it is valid. See there. He further writes that when the Mechaber says, 'or the lender,' this refers to a document written in the form of testimony by witnesses. But if the lender wrote it in the form of his own admission in a manner effective under the rules at the beginning of siman 69, it is effective. The words of the Rambam, however, cited by the Mechaber at the end of siman 56, seif 1, appear at first glance not to distinguish between the cases, and this requires study. See also siman 81, seif 17.
The torn documents. Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 185, raises the question whether all the documents must be torn or whether a majority is treated as the whole. See there. Shach.
Impaired. We tear up the note. There is also no legal distinction here between documents that are torn and documents marked as paid. Ibid.
And they collect. The Sema writes that this means even the lender's heirs may collect, because anything found in the lender's house is presumed to be his, and we say that it happened to become mixed among the torn documents. The Shach cites poskim who hold that if it is found among the torn documents alone, the note is impaired and should remain deposited without collection. He writes that this is a sound view. See there.
Valid. It is not ordinarily the lender who authenticates a receipt, but the borrower. We therefore presume that the borrower had already paid him, received the receipt from the lender, delivered it for authentication, and later deposited it again with the lender. Sema.
