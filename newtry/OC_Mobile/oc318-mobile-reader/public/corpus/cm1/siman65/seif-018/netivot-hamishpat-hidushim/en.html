He admits. Even if by his admission the lender causes loss of his own debt to someone who is owed money by him, the note is nevertheless returned to the borrower, as Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer. The lender's admission is stronger. See Beurim. But when the lender does not admit, the creditor of the holder of this note may collect from the borrower and from purchasers. Likewise, if the holder of the note owes money to another person and a receipt is found against this note, that creditor may collect from the borrower. If the borrower admits payment, however, the note is not returned to the lender and he cannot collect from purchasers because we suspect collusion. The Shach disagrees and holds that where the borrower admits, the note is returned to the lender. The primary ruling follows the Sema that it is not returned. See Beurim, seif katan 27.

The borrower. The Shach rules that if the receipt is in the borrower's handwriting it is valid. If it is in the lender's handwriting and phrased as his own admission in a legally effective manner, he remains uncertain whether it is valid.
Among the torn documents. The same applies if the receipt itself is found among torn documents. It is valid because if the lender had not yet been paid and still needed the receipt, he would not have left it among documents he regarded as discarded. "Torn" is not literal. The same applies if they are marked paid even if not physically torn. Responsa Mahara Sasson was uncertain whether this requires all or merely a majority.
