When the lender admits, etc. See Sema, who strained to explain that perhaps the lender himself owes money to another person and seeks to return this receipt so as to defeat his own creditor, creating a duty of restoration that harms the creditor. His explanation is difficult, as I wrote above in seif 7. Shach answers that the lender could in any event burn his own debt document. He gives another answer based on the lender's ability to waive the debt, but whether a person can waive a debt subject to R' Nasan's lien is itself disputed. The first answer is good where the document had not previously been publicly established, because then he could destroy it. See Tumim, where I raised an additional difficulty that perhaps the lender sold the debt to another through maamad sheloshtan. I concluded that if the lender is alive, he is believed in every case because he can issue an undated receipt. If the lender died and his heir instructs that the receipt be returned, that answer no longer applies. Therefore, if the document contains no clause of trust, the receipt should be returned in every case. We do not ordinarily suspect collusion in the sale of debt documents, and there is no concern that the lender harms others because collection would require an oath, while the heir cannot swear in light of the receipt. But if the document contains a trust clause effective even against heirs, then the receipt should not be returned, lest the heir thereby harm other parties and defeat their rights. See what I wrote above in siman 47.
He does not admit, etc. If the receipt is validated, it is returned to the borrower because the lender does not validate a receipt against himself, Shach.
Neither to this one. If the borrower says to return the receipt to the lender because no payment ever occurred, Sema nevertheless holds that it should not be returned to the lender because of concern for collusion, just as with a found debt document where even the debtor's admission does not justify return. Bach and Shach disagree. They say the cases are different: a lost debt document itself was weakened by the loss, whereas here only the receipt was weakened and the debt document remains safely in the lender's possession. See Tumim, where I concluded that Sema is correct. See there.
Even if he himself wrote it, etc. Not only where a scribe and witnesses prepared the receipt, because they may have written it in advance so that if the borrower later came to pay there would be no delay in finding a scribe and witnesses. Even where it is in the lender's own handwriting, or even in the borrower's handwriting, which could easily have been written at the time of payment, we still say it may have been prepared earlier because perhaps payment would occur in urgent circumstances when there would be no opportunity to write a receipt. Shach writes that where it is in the borrower's handwriting the matter is doubtful. In Tumim I wrote that this does not appear correct from the unqualified language of the poskim.
Or the lender himself. Shach questions a case where the receipt is not written as witness testimony, but as a complete personal acknowledgment in the lender's handwriting, such as, "I acknowledge that so and so paid me," whether such a receipt can really be treated as merely prepared in advance, since the lender can always write such an acknowledgment if the borrower later produces money. His words do not appear correct. Otherwise, why did the Gemara have to explain the case of a document found among torn documents as one that was paid? It could simply have said that the heirs must swear they found no handwritten acknowledgment from their father. The unqualified language of all poskim likewise indicates otherwise.
The lender himself. If a person possesses a document stating that someone gave him a gift of two hundred gold coins, and a receipt is found in his possession stating that the gift was paid, Shach, seif katan 52, holds that the receipt is valid. See Tumim, seif katan 31, where I wrote that this does not appear correct. I was inclined, however, to say that if a person wrote a will stating that a certain debtor had paid, and after his death the will was found in his possession, we do not say that the debt remained unpaid and that he wrote the statement only so it would be ready if the debtor later paid, intending to erase it if he did not. People do not commonly erase and alter language within a will. This appears correct as law.
Unless the document, etc. Shach writes in the name of Rashi and other poskim that the same applies if the receipt itself is found among torn documents. It is valid, because had the lender still needed the receipt for a debt not yet paid, he would not have placed it among documents treated as cancelled. See there.
Torn documents. The same applies to paid documents even if they are not physically torn.
It is weakened. And the debt document itself is torn up entirely, Baal HaTerumos as cited by Shach.
We rely on the receipt. Even if the lender or his heirs make a definite claim that the debt was never paid, we nevertheless treat the debt as presumptively paid, Baal HaTerumos and Shach. It is doubtful, however, where we know that the receipt was found among torn or paid documents only from the lender or his heirs themselves, and they claim definitely that the debt was not paid. Do they have a migo because they could have denied finding it among torn or paid documents, or is that a migo used to extract money, because once it is found there the debt document is like a mere shard? This resembles the case below in siman 82 and requires study.
Even if the receipt has no witnesses. Even the lender did not sign it. Even if the debt document contains a trust clause, in such circumstances the document is nevertheless weakened, Sema.
