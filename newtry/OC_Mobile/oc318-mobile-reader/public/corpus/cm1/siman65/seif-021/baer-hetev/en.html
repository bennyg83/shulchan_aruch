And it was found. The Ir Shushan writes that this refers to a separate writing found lying within the promissory note stating this. See there. There is no sound reason for his explanation. Rather, the Mechaber teaches that even if the receipt was written in a place from which it could be cut off, we act in accordance with the receipt, since the holders are heirs who plead only a doubtful claim, and we do not advance a contrary claim for them in this matter, unlike where the lender himself is alive and makes a definite claim, as stated above. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach, however, writes that this explanation does not appear correct. Rather, the Mechaber means the ruling literally. Although he already wrote above that a receipt written on the body of the promissory note is valid, he repeats it here to teach that the same law applies to heirs. One might have thought that only when the lender is alive is the note invalid, but not in the hands of his heirs, since in several areas heirs are in a stronger position than the lender himself. Therefore the Mechaber teaches otherwise. The Shach further writes that the Bach explains this as referring specifically to the lender's own handwriting, and that the wording is needed for an inference: although if the lender were alive we would tear up the note even if the receipt were in another person's handwriting, here there is no presumption that a person would not impair his own document. This is unpersuasive, for once a receipt is written on the body of the promissory note, even if it is in another handwriting, we tear it up based on the presumption that the lender would not have impaired his own note. The Taz likewise objects to the Bach and writes that his words are difficult, because whenever the note has been impaired in any manner, the debt is treated as paid. See there.
