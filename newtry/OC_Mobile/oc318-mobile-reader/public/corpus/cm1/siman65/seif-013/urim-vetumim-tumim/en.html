However, if the slave comes, and so forth. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe struggled with Tur and Rosh. They accept Abaye's doctrine that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the recipient, and we also rule like the Sages that emancipation is a benefit to a slave because it takes him from his master's control into freedom. The Gemara says explicitly that according to the view that emancipation is a benefit and Abaye's doctrine applies, the slave need not bring proof of when the deed reached him. Why then do Tur and Rosh require proof? Shach answers that once the Gemara concludes that the principal function of an emancipation deed is to free the slave, Abaye's doctrine does not apply. He criticizes Bach, but in truth his own answer is essentially Bach's, only stated at greater length. In any event, the explanation has no foundation. Of course everyone always knew that the primary purpose of an emancipation deed is to free the slave and permit him as a free Jew. That was not hidden from the earlier stage of the sugya. The actual issue was whether the deed's delivery has publicity and whether purchasers can demand proof. The proper explanation is as follows. Tosafot in Gittin 17 and Bava Metzia 19 ask how a get may be written for a husband when his wife is not present. Perhaps it will be written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei, allowing her to recover fruits from the earlier date improperly. They answer that because a get must be delivered before witnesses, the delivery has publicity. Purchasers can say, "Bring proof of when it reached you." Rosh adds that this differs from an ordinary loan document because everyone understands a get was written for the purpose of being delivered for divorce, and delivery witnesses create a kol. This answer depends on the Gemara's final distinction between a get and an ordinary debt document. At the earlier stage, before the Gemara introduces that distinction, this answer is unavailable. One must instead use Tosafot's other answer, that a person does not ordinarily bring punishment upon himself in advance by writing a get long before he intends to divorce. That reasoning works for a wife's get but not for a slave. A master has no comparable reluctance to prepare an emancipation deed. Now Tosafot in Bava Metzia 20 write that Abaye's doctrine that signing witnesses create rights is itself only a rabbinic enactment, instituted to make life easier for witnesses. Otherwise witnesses could never safely sign unless they observed immediate delivery. Because it is a rabbinic enactment, Tosafot question whether Chazal strengthened it so far in the case of a slave that he is immediately permitted to a free Jewish woman before receiving the deed, or whether it applies only to the monetary aspects. This uncertainty depends on the two explanations just mentioned. At the earlier stage, before we distinguish between a get and a debt instrument through publicity of delivery, an emancipation deed could not safely be written for the master unless the slave was present. Therefore the same policy reason for Abaye's enactment applies. Chazal had reason to recognize the slave's rights from the signing, at least for monetary purposes. Thus the Gemara can say: according to the view that emancipation is a benefit and Abaye's doctrine applies, there is no need for proof. After the Gemara's conclusion, however, we can use the first answer of Tosafot and Rosh. An emancipation deed, like a get, is delivered before witnesses and delivery has publicity. Witnesses can therefore write it even though the slave is absent, because purchasers can later require proof of the delivery date. Once that mechanism already solves the drafting problem, there is no reason to extend Abaye's special rabbinic enactment to emancipation deeds. The enactment was unnecessary there. This is why Tur and Rosh, following the final conclusion, do not apply Abaye's doctrine to emancipation documents and require proof of when the document reached the slave.
However, Mordechai writes that with an emancipation deed it is a liability to the slave, and therefore we say, "Bring proof of when the deed reached you." Bach quotes a version reading "there is an opinion that it is a liability." Our printed Mordechai lacks those words. Either version can be explained. Tosafot themselves are uncertain whether, since Abaye's doctrine is only a rabbinic enactment, Chazal strengthened it sufficiently to permit the slave immediately to a free woman. Ritva decides that they did not. According to authorities who hold that Chazal cannot uproot a Torah prohibition through a positive act, as Raah, Nimukei Yosef, and Ran maintain in the relevant contexts, the slave certainly cannot be permitted to a free woman merely through this rabbinic device. At most, from the date of signing he is treated stringently as though partly free: he can become prohibited to a maidservant and disqualified from eating terumah, while still not being permitted to a free Jewish woman until actual delivery. That intermediate status is unquestionably a detriment to him. He acquires the stringencies of freedom without its full benefits. Accordingly, even if full emancipation is ordinarily a benefit, this partial rabbinic status is not. The witnesses cannot acquire it for him against his will. This explains Mordechai's statement that emancipation is a liability for this purpose, and therefore the slave must bring proof of delivery. This also explains why the Gemara's earlier statement can still say that according to the view that emancipation is a benefit and Abaye's doctrine applies, there is no problem. According to R. Meir, who holds that signing witnesses themselves effect the document on a Torah level, the legal effect is complete. There is no half-status. If the witnesses acquire for him, he is fully free and therefore the arrangement can be treated as beneficial. But according to our halachah that delivery witnesses effect the get and Abaye's rule operates only as a rabbinic enactment, the partial-status problem remains, and we do not acquire for the slave in that manner. This provides a coherent explanation of Mordechai, Tur, and Rosh. One might still ask: perhaps after the master wrote and signed the emancipation deed, he cancelled it, just as a husband can cancel a get. If so, even according to Abaye, the earlier acquisition should be gone. Perhaps the master then sold the slave's property and now colludes by returning the cancelled emancipation deed to defeat those purchasers. The answer is that our concern for collusion applies where the instrument itself remains a valid get but is being used to recover fruits improperly. We do not suspect a master of knowingly handing his slave a wholly void emancipation document and thereby causing him to marry a free Jewish woman unlawfully. This is like the rule at the beginning of Gittin that a husband is not presumed to create a severe religious stumbling block for his wife. This also answers Shach's earlier question in siman 39 according to Rif as explained by Rosh. If Abaye's doctrine gives rights from signing, but the owner transferred the slave to another between signing and delivery, that intervening sale cancels the prior effect. Why then is the Gemara satisfied with Abaye? Perhaps the master sold the slave and later colludes by reviving the old emancipation deed. Once the slave was sold, the old emancipation deed cannot simply be used as a valid current instrument. Returning it as though it were fully valid would create a religiously invalid emancipation and expose the slave to forbidden marriage. We do not suspect the master of that. There is, however, a further complication. Whether a cancelled get can later be reused is disputed by Rav Nachman and R. Sheshet at the beginning of Perek HaSholeach. See Rosh, the poskim, and Even HaEzer siman 141. If we rule like Rav Nachman that even a cancelled get can later be used again, then the concern returns. The get is not permanently worthless. Still, Abaye's original acquisition cannot simply continue, because the first agency and legal arrangement were cancelled. If the master later decides to use the document again, this is a new act with a new legal basis. According to R. Sheshet, who holds that a cancelled get cannot later be reused, everything is simpler. We do not fear cancellation because the master would then be returning a worthless instrument and causing a religious stumbling block. Thus Tur and Rosh can also be understood as reading the Gemara's "this works well" according to the view that a cancelled get cannot later be reused. According to our practical halachah, which follows Rav Nachman, one may still need proof because the earlier signing-based effect does not automatically survive cancellation. In any event, the requirement of proof is well founded.
From the date written in it. Sema and Shach dispute the required proof. Sema reads Mechaber precisely: it is not enough merely to prove that the document was in the claimant's possession before it fell. He must establish that it actually reached him on the date written in the document. Shach strongly rejects this. Once the claimant proves he possessed the document before the loss, the defect created by the fall has been removed. There is then no reason to require more. This dispute depends on a major disagreement between Rashi and Ritva on one side and Tosafot on the other. Rashi and Ritva explain the concern "perhaps it was written to borrow in Nisan but the loan did not occur until Tishrei" to mean that the loan really did occur, only later than the written date. Since the instrument was antedated and invalid, the holder did not guard it carefully, and therefore it fell. According to this approach, merely proving that the lender held the note before the loss does not answer anything. We already know it may have been in his hand before the loss. The concern is that it did not reach him at the proper time. He must therefore prove that he received it on the date written in it. This supports Sema. One might ask: in Bava Metzia 16 the Gemara implies that a court authentication removes the concern that the note had been written earlier for a loan that occurred later. According to Rashi's approach, perhaps the lender obtained the note only later and then had it authenticated. Ritva already sensed this and answers with some difficulty. A simpler answer is that the whole reason we infer delayed delivery is that the holder's careless loss indicates he knew the note was defective. If he took the trouble to have the note formally authenticated, that conduct shows he regarded it as a valid instrument and was careful with it. The inference from loss therefore weakens substantially. This reading of Rashi also resolves Tosafot's question in Gittin. Rashi explains that where a get is in the wife's hands and she seeks to recover property, she must bring proof of when the get reached her, because a get can be written for the husband while the wife is absent. Tosafot ask: in Bava Metzia, when a lost get is found, why does the Gemara still worry that it was written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei? If the woman must always prove delivery date in any event, what additional problem does the loss create? According to Rashi's approach, there is no difficulty. Rosh writes that when a get is written while the wife is absent, it should ordinarily be delivered through a proper agent or before witnesses. If the wife later proves she had the get in Iyar, we generally presume it was delivered lawfully and can relate the legal consequences to that delivery. We do not suspect that the husband handed it to her privately in an invalid way, because people are not presumed to create religiously invalid divorces. But once the get itself has fallen, the loss creates a special defect. We can say: perhaps the husband indeed delivered it improperly and antedated, so the wife knew it was defective and did not guard it carefully. Thus even proof that the get was in her possession before the fall does not solve the problem. She must prove lawful delivery on the actual date of the document. That is exactly the Gemara's concern and exactly Sema's requirement. Tosafot disagree. They hold that if the lender actually received the note at any earlier time, even after its written date, he would still guard it. The only concern is that no loan ever occurred at all and he now seeks to use the document for the first time. According to Tosafot, Shach is correct. Once he proves the note was already in his possession before the fall, we know it was part of an actual transaction and the falling no longer creates the original concern.
And further, in my chiddushim I initially sought to prove Sema from Tosafot in Bava Metzia 18. Tosafot discuss R. Zeira's inference from a baraita: if one finds a get and the husband admits, return it to the wife; if he does not admit, do not return it. R. Zeira understands the case as one where substantial time has passed. Tosafot ask why he cannot establish it as an immediate finding. They answer first that if it were immediate, the rule would be obvious, unless the novelty is the concern that it was written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei. But the Gemara already answers that a fallen get has publicity and the woman can be required to bring proof. Tosafot then suggest perhaps R. Zeira follows Reish Lakish that the husband loses fruits from the signing date, eliminating that concern. They give another answer: if the get was seen in the woman's hand immediately before it fell, then where the husband admits, return is obvious. Even if the found get were another document, we already saw that she was divorced. A gloss asks: perhaps the get in her hand was a different get with a later date, while this earlier-dated found get could now allow her to collect fruits from an earlier time improperly. Maharshal answers that this concern is eliminated by requiring proof. If Shach is right that the proof need show only that she possessed some get before the loss, then the gloss's concern remains. We already know she had a get. What needs proof is whether this particular earlier date was valid. That seems to support Sema's position that the proof must establish receipt on the date written in the document. However, this proof is not decisive. The gloss's question is relevant mainly to Tosafot's statement that the permissive case would otherwise be obvious. The novelty could simply be that in an immediate finding we do not fear another get. But in the clause where the husband does not admit, the Mishnah's own rule that an immediately found get is valid already defeats a concern for a different get. The baraita cannot be speaking as though immediate loss itself created such an additional concern. Thus Maharshal's answer need not mean that the woman must prove receipt on the document date. The whole structure can still fit Shach. Once that proof falls away, the straightforward reading of Tosafot supports Shach. There is also an additional reason in the case of an emancipation deed. Above I explained that an emancipation deed may be written for the master even when the slave is absent because delivery must occur in a manner that creates publicity. If the slave later proves that the deed was in his hands before it fell, and there was no contrary public information, that itself strongly suggests proper delivery. Unlike an ordinary loan note, the emancipation deed is not rendered invalid merely because delivery occurred later than the writing date in every respect. It can remain a valid emancipation document, with disputes focused on monetary consequences from the earlier date. Therefore the inference from falling is weaker. For practical halachah, I therefore agree with Shach: proof that the document was in the claimant's possession before the fall is generally sufficient.
And further, there remains a related question concerning Rambam. In Hilchot Gezelah VaAvedah chapter 18, Rambam simply rules: if one finds a woman's get and the husband admits it, return it to the woman; likewise, if one finds an emancipation deed and the master admits it, return it to the slave. Rambam does not mention the Gemara's requirement that they prove when the documents reached them. Why omit such an important law? Regarding a woman's get, the answer may be that Rambam follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's post-danger enactment that a woman may collect her basic ketubah without producing the get. Rambam writes in Hilchot Ishut 16:26 that after the period of danger Chazal abolished the earlier documentary requirement and allowed collection without the get. If so, the woman has little reason to guard the get for ketubah collection. A fallen get is therefore not necessarily defective at all. The Gemara's concern arose under the earlier Mishnah's regime, where a woman could not collect her ketubah without the get and therefore ordinarily would guard it carefully. Under Rambam's practical law, losing it does not create the same suspicious inference. Therefore he need not require special proof of delivery time. One might say she should still guard the get in case the husband sells her fruits and she later needs to establish the divorce date. But a woman does not normally plan around the possibility that her husband will wrongfully sell such rights after divorce, and this is a weaker reason for careful preservation. Regarding an emancipation deed, Rambam may hold like R. Yochanan that in found documents we fear repayment and collusion but do not generally fear that witnesses improperly wrote a document for an absent party. In his view, with an emancipation deed there is no normal concern that it was written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei if the drafting rules required the proper party to be present. The Gemara's proof requirement therefore follows R. Asi and those who, once a note falls, fear that witnesses happened to depart from ordinary drafting practice. Rambam follows R. Yochanan and does not create that concern. This explains his omission. Accordingly, the practical conclusion remains with Shach: where proof is required, establishing that the document was already in the claimant's possession before the fall is sufficient. There is no need in ordinary cases to prove delivery on the exact date written in the instrument.
And in a place where the signing witnesses acquire rights for him, and so forth. Sema and Shach agree that Rama cannot mean the opinion that, as a general rule, the signing witnesses automatically acquire for the recipient from the time of signing. Rama did not rule that way above in siman 39. Sema therefore suggests two explanations: either the document expressly says that the obligation should take effect "from now," or the witnesses actually acquired on behalf of the recipient. I do not understand either explanation. If a borrower says, "I hereby encumber my property from now even though I will lend the money only later," a monetary lien can indeed be created immediately. There is then no problem of antedating. But an emancipation deed is different. If the master says, "When this get reaches you, you will be free retroactively from now," is the slave actually free immediately? Certainly not. Even if the master says, "Your property rights are yours from now," through what legal hand can the slave acquire them while still a slave whose property belongs to his master? He has no independent hand of acquisition until emancipation takes effect. Even according to R. Meir, a gift to a slave "from now for after emancipation" does not simply give him full present title, and before emancipation the master may still be able to retract. Every dated emancipation deed in any event contains the equivalent of "from now," because we rule like R. Yose that the date in a document demonstrates present intent. Yet the Gemara still worries that it may have been written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei. So Sema's first explanation cannot be right. The second explanation, that the witnesses actually acquired for him, is also difficult. If the master explicitly said "acquire on his behalf," then we rule that he can no longer retract even if he later merely said "give it." All the more so with an express acquisition. The slave would be legally free at once. What role remains for the master's later admission, and why would any proof of delivery time be required? Even if the paper were later thrown away, the acquisition had already taken effect. The correct explanation of Rama is based on what I established above in siman 39, Tumim seif katan 23, regarding Rosh. Even R. Asi can accept the doctrine that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the recipient according to R. Meir. His disagreement can concern only whether that mechanism has sufficient publicity to affect purchasers. Many authorities rule like R. Meir regarding monetary documents. Therefore, with respect to a creditor collecting from the borrower's free assets, everyone can effectively agree that the signing witnesses create the lender's right from the written date, because no purchaser-facing publicity is needed. Apply this to an emancipation deed. Suppose the master had an oral creditor, and the former slave now seeks the fruits of property that belonged to the slave between Nisan and Tishrei. The slave produces an emancipation deed written in Nisan. For purposes of a dispute involving only the master and free assets, there is no need for the special kol required against purchasers. Under the R. Meir framework, the witnesses can be treated as acquiring for the slave from the signing date. Emancipation is a benefit to him for these monetary purposes. Therefore the slave need not prove exactly when the document reached him for that limited monetary issue. This, in my opinion, is Rama's intent. The qualification is thus not a general ruling that emancipation always takes effect from signing. It concerns the legal consequences in a setting where publicity to third parties is irrelevant and the signing-based acquisition doctrine can operate. Even so, I have already shown in several ways that in the final halachah the doctrine of signing witnesses acquiring for the recipient should not be broadly applied to emancipation documents. The practical reach of Rama's clause is therefore limited.
