He should return it to the slave. See Sema, seif katan 38, who alludes to seif katan 19, which is a scribal error and should read seif katan 18. There he asked that perhaps the master has creditors, and the same concern should apply here. His words are difficult to understand, because in any event we do not ordinarily suspect collusion, since the slave must bring proof of when the emancipation document reached his hand. Tumim raises the same question. It appears that the Sema means as follows. The requirement that the slave bring proof applies only when he seeks to extract property from another. If the slave is already in possession of property and the master's creditor comes to remove it from him, the slave can certainly say, "I acquired it for myself, and the property covered by my emancipation preceded your lien." Tosafot in Gittin 50, s.v. Katav BiKetav Yado, similarly write that if the woman seizes the produce she is believed. Accordingly, as to movables in the slave's possession, the master's creditor certainly cannot take them from him. Even as to land of which the slave is in possession, it appears that the creditor cannot remove it from him merely because of the defect created by the document's having fallen. Likewise, where a deed of sale fell, it appears that a creditor of the seller cannot remove the property from the purchaser merely because the deed was impaired by the fall and claim that it was written to be delivered in Nisan but was not delivered until Tishrei. Since the purchaser is in possession, we do not remove property from him on the basis of that defect. Even where the purchaser's claim is based on possession alone, there is considerable room to analyze whether the creditor may remove the property from him when the purchaser claims that he bought it before the date of the debt instrument, if the purchaser does not bring proof that the property was in the debtor's hands at the time of the loan. With Hashem's help, this will be explained in the laws of collection of debts. Accordingly, there is reason to fear that the master owes money to others, that the slave acquired property, and that the master instructs that the emancipation document be returned to the slave through collusion so that the creditor will not be able to seize it. The answer is that we do not entertain such a concern, for the reason stated there, namely that we do not suspect collusion merely to prevent a creditor from enforcing his lien. See my novellae there. Nor need we fear that the slave himself sold property and now wants the emancipation document returned so that he can use it to remove the property from the purchaser. He would not do this. Once the emancipation document has become invalid and can no longer effect emancipation, he certainly would not place it in the slave's hand for such misuse. A person does not deliberately cause another to sin before Heaven, as Tumim writes.
And in a case where witnesses acquire it on his behalf. See Sema, seif katan 41, who explains that this means either that the document states that the transfer is effective immediately, or that the witnesses immediately acquired it on behalf of the creditor. Tumim asks that once the witnesses acquired it for the creditor, the grantor can no longer retract, and therefore it should be returned even if the master does not admit it. There is no difficulty. The master can stipulate with the witnesses: "Acquire this document on his behalf from now, on condition that it ultimately reaches his hand with my consent." Delivery to the recipient then serves merely as fulfillment of the condition.
