Likewise, the heirs of the depositary, etc. The Sema asks why, in siman 243:13, the Mechaber rules that a deed of gift found among a depositary’s heirs is returned to the donor’s heirs, whereas here no document is returned. He suggests distinguishing between a case where it is unknown from whose hand the document came and a case where it is known that the donor gave it to the depositary but the purpose is unknown. He then proposes that a gift deed without a kinyan may not yet have vested, so it returns to the donor’s heirs, while a deed supported by a kinyan is returned to the recipient, as the Rama states at the end of siman 250. A loan document is different. In my opinion, this is incorrect. The Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia, pages 13a and 19a, expressly states that even a gift deed without a kinyan that is found is returned to neither party. The Rambam and Maggid Mishneh codify this in Hilchot Gezelah 18:9. A document found among the father’s papers is plainly equivalent to one found in the marketplace. The Tur and Mechaber’s phrase here, ‘they shall return no document,’ likewise includes a gift deed. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia expressly teaches that deathbed instructions, apotiki documents, and gifts are returned to the recipient when the donor admits, but otherwise are returned to neither party, whether found in the street or among the father’s documents. The correct distinction is that siman 243 concerns a case where it is known that the donor delivered the deed to the depositary, as explained there. The Sema calls this forced, but it is not forced at all. The Rosh expressly holds that where it is known to have come from the donor, it is returned to him, as I write below in seif 5.
They shall not return it, etc. This is the principal ruling, contrary to Rashba, responsum 1035. The Rashba writes that Rav Nachman’s ruling in Zeh Borer concerning a woman who produced a document and said it was paid, whom Rav Nachman did not believe once the document had been established in court, proves that a document found in another’s possession is not impaired merely because its status is unknown. Otherwise, apart from her credibility, it would be a document found with a third party whose purpose is unknown. Likewise, when Rava asks that she should be believed because she could have burned it, why is a migo necessary? The document should already be impaired. The Rashba concludes that a document is impaired only when it comes from a recognized stakeholder who discredits it by virtue of his status and credibility from both parties. In my opinion, this is no proof. Even without her credibility, the document would indeed remain deposited and unresolved. Rav Nachman refused to believe her only for purposes of returning it to the borrower and having it destroyed entirely. Rava therefore asks that since she could have burned it, she should be believed that it was paid and return it to the borrower for cancellation. According to that version, once the document was established in court she was no longer believed, so it remained deposited. That version, however, is rejected at the end of Zeh Borer. We follow the first version, under which even after establishment in court the stakeholder is fully believed to nullify the document. A stakeholder is believed even without a present migo, as I proved at length at the beginning of siman 56, where I also cited the Rashba himself as ruling so. See there.
Before it came into another’s hand, etc. The Sema writes that ‘another’s hand’ is not exact. The decisive time is before the doubt was heard from that other person. The Bach challenges him. See their discussions.
Nevertheless, etc., a woman’s ketubah, etc. The Sema explains that a ketubah is not ordinarily collected during the husband’s lifetime. He adds that when the Rosh and Mechaber speak in seif 5 of a woman’s dowry, they mean dowry specifically, not ketubah. This is inaccurate and unnecessarily strained. As a matter of law, even a ketubah found in another’s possession after the woman is widowed is subject to concern for payment, as stated expressly in Bava Metzia 7b and the authorities, and by the Ritva himself, as I write below in seif 12, seif katan 32. Here the ruling rests on local custom. The Ritva cited by the Beit Yosef writes that in his region the heir returns the ketubah to the woman because it is presumed that she placed it with the depositary for safekeeping. The Ir Shushan likewise writes that women ordinarily deposit their ketubot. The Rama abbreviated the matter. Therefore, where no clear custom exists, we do suspect payment. This is the correct distinction.
