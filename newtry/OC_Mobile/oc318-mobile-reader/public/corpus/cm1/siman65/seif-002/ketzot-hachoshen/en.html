And likewise the heirs of the custodian. Rashba disagrees. He points to the woman who produced a document and said it was paid. Rav Nachman did not believe her once the document had been validated in beit din. If every document found in a third person's hands without a known explanation were automatically weakened, why was her special credibility relevant at all? Rava challenged Rav Nachman only with the migo that she could have burned the instrument. This implies that mere possession by an uncertain third party does not itself weaken the document. The Shach answers that without the migo the document indeed could not be used to collect, but Rav Nachman refused to let her return it to the borrower and destroy it altogether. Rava's migo then proves her credibility to declare it paid and hand it back for destruction. Yet this answer is difficult. The Gemara asks whether Rav Nachman relied on Rabbi's rule that debt instruments are acquired by delivery alone, and Rav Nachman answers that the case is different because she could have burned it. According to the Shach, even under the Sages who require writing and delivery, if she had claimed purchase she might at least own the paper and could return it to the borrower. But if she says purchase, the legal character of the instrument is known and the creditor can still collect the underlying debt. The document is weakened only when its nature is unknown. Thus the purchase migo cannot establish repayment. This provides the needed distinction and leaves the Rashba dispute intact.
