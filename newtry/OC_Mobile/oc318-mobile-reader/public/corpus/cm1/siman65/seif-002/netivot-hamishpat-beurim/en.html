They should not return it. See Shach, seif katan 7, who rules contrary to Rashba. Rashba's reasoning at first appears difficult to understand. If the holder does not know whether the document came from the debtor or from the creditor, why should it be returned to the creditor? Why are we not concerned that the debt was paid? Rashba's reasoning must be that Shmuel, even where a note was actually lost, does not suspect payment, as stated in Bava Metzia 13, because had the debt been paid the debtor would ordinarily have torn the instrument. Therefore, even according to the authorities who do suspect payment in the case of a lost note, where the document is in the hands of an entrusted third party and has not been impaired by loss, we do not suspect payment. Those who disagree with Rashba hold that when a document is in the hands of a third party we must suspect that the debtor placed it there subject to some condition of the entrustment. This also explains the rule at the end of siman 41. If a note was deposited with another person and the debtor says, "It fell from me," he is believed by virtue of a migo, as the Sema, seif katan 30, and the Tur state. Urim VeTumim writes that once the note has been established in beit din the debtor is no longer believed. In my opinion that cannot be correct. If it were so, consider a sale of a promissory note effected by delivery without the required written assignment, where the purchaser cannot collect and the seller remains entitled to collect. Why should the debtor be believed to say that the note fell from him? Even if the purchaser says that the note is valid, the debtor should have no migo that the purchaser could simply return it to him secretly, because the purchaser wants to collect the debt for himself. It is true that the Sema in siman 66 writes that the debtor is discharged if he claims that the note fell from him, but that does not appear correct, as I wrote there. Moreover, common practice is to collect upon debt instruments that are sent through an agent even after the instrument has plainly been seen in the agent's hand by beit din. Evidently, we do not ordinarily suspect that the document fell from the debtor, or that after payment the debtor deposited it with someone else. We suspect payment only where we actually see that the note fell and was thereby impaired, even according to those who disagree with Shmuel. There is further support from Yevamot 116. The Gemara says that with respect to loss, a person is careful to guard a document, and with respect to deposit, one does not ordinarily deposit with another person a document whose name could be confused with his own. If ordinary loss from the debtor had to be suspected, the Gemara's reasoning would be insufficient. Likewise, according to the opinion in Bava Batra, chapter Get Pashut, that individual loss is a meaningful concern, we still do not routinely suspect that the document fell from the debtor. The distinction must be that payment and loss from the debtor are considered only where we actually observe a circumstance that impairs the note, such as finding it after a fall, or the later case in which debtor and creditor are physically grasping the instrument from opposite sides. Otherwise, we do not suspect it. When a note is found in another person's possession, however, we do suspect that it was entrusted there subject to a condition. This is clear because even Shmuel, who does not suspect payment when a note is found after loss, agrees that if a note is found with an entrusted third party who does not know its status, it is not returned. Necessarily, the concern is a condition of entrustment. In this respect, being found in another's possession creates a stronger concern than ordinary loss. Accordingly, when the Tur says that the debtor is believed because the custodian had a migo that he could have returned the note secretly, the meaning is not that the custodian presently has a conventional migo. Rather, because at some point while he held the instrument he had the practical ability to return it, he remains believed as an entrusted third party, in accordance with the Shach in siman 56, seif katan 11. Even according to the authorities cited by the Shach in siman 56, seif katan 5, who hold that where the principal denies the existence of an entrustment the alleged third party is not believed, here we do not suspect ordinary loss from the debtor. The only real concern is that the debtor entrusted the document on some condition, and on that question the holder has the status of an entrusted third party. Therefore, where a note was delivered to another without written assignment, even though that person has no present migo because he seeks to keep the claim for himself, he is nevertheless believed when he says that the note is valid. We do not suspect loss or post-payment deposit from the debtor, but only conditional entrustment. Since the parties trusted him to state the condition, he is believed, even though he now lacks a conventional migo. Nevertheless, although the Shulchan Aruch rules contrary to Rashba, as the Shach notes, that rejection concerns only Rashba's conclusion that we do not at all suspect that the document came from the debtor. On that point we do not follow him. Thus, even where the note is found among the custodian's heirs, it is not returned to the creditor, because perhaps the debtor delivered it to the custodian subject to some condition. But Rashba's more basic principle, that uncertainty in the mind of an entrusted third party has the legal status of uncertainty in the mind of the litigant, appears to be accepted by everyone. Otherwise there is a major difficulty with the Mishnah in Bava Metzia 20, which rules that when a note is found with a third party who does not know its status, it remains deposited until Eliyahu comes. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 2, s.v. Veyachloku, explain that with respect to a third maneh deposited by two disputants, the custodian is holding for both of them, so the parties are treated as though both physically possess the disputed property. The reason the third maneh nevertheless remains deposited rather than being divided is that an equal division cannot represent the truth, because property in another person's possession cannot ordinarily be acquired in that arbitrary way. By contrast, Bava Metzia 7 rules that when two parties are physically holding a promissory note, they divide its value just as when they hold a garment. If a note is held by an entrusted third party, then, under Tosafot's principle, both debtor and creditor should be treated as possessors because the third party holds for both. Here division can correspond to the truth, since the very uncertainty may be whether part of the debt was paid. Why, then, is the rule that the note remains deposited rather than being divided as in the case of two holding a garment? One might distinguish the third maneh because the custodian definitely received it from both parties, whereas here perhaps only one party deposited the note, so that they are not both considered possessors. Shitah Mekubetzet suggests such reasoning. Even so, the Mishnah should at least have distinguished a case where the custodian knows that both parties jointly entrusted the note but no longer knows its precise status. In that circumstance they should divide. No authority states such a distinction. It must therefore be that the reason the note remains deposited is Rashba's principle. The uncertainty of the entrusted third party is treated as the uncertainty of the litigant himself and thereby impairs the note. The law is therefore to leave it deposited. As to the debtor, we cannot reason in reverse that the third party's uncertainty improves a paid note into a valid one. An uncertain litigant cannot create a valid document out of a paid one. Accordingly, if the custodian has died and his heirs know that the note had been entrusted jointly by both parties, the parties are treated as jointly in possession. The heirs themselves do not have the special status whose uncertainty impairs the note, as Rashba explains. In that case the proper rule is to divide. The Shach also seeks to answer Rashba's difficulty from the incident in chapter Zeh Borer, where Rav Nachman did not believe the custodian for purposes of returning the note to the debtor so that it could be torn. According to one version, once the note had been established in beit din the custodian was no longer believed and the document remained deposited. This explanation is very difficult. If "once established in beit din" meant that the custodian was no longer believed and the note therefore remained fully valid for collection, one could understand that after beit din had seen the note, the creditor could still collect even if the custodian later tore it. The custodian would then truly have lost the migo that he could destroy it. But if the only consequence of establishment in beit din is that the note must remain deposited, then even after beit din sees it in the custodian's hand and the custodian says nothing, the note would in any event continue to remain in the custodian's possession, because property that is to remain deposited is ordinarily left with the custodian, as explained in siman 300. He would therefore still have the practical ability to say nothing, destroy it the next day or later, and prevent collection. Even if he said that he did not know its status, beit din would still leave it with him. His migo would not be gone. In my opinion Rashba's question can be resolved differently. So long as the note remains with the entrusted third party and he says nothing in beit din, beit din cannot collect upon it even according to Rav Nachman, just as where the third party says he does not know its status. The fact that beit din happened to see the note does not strengthen it. But in the Sanhedrin incident the woman's statement was, "I know that this note is paid." The wording implies that she did not say that the debtor entrusted it to her, nor that the creditor delivered it to her for return to the debtor. Rather, the case must have been that she said the creditor had deposited the instrument with her, so that by the terms of the deposit she was obligated to return it to the creditor, but she independently knew from another source that it had been paid, for example because she overheard the debtor pay the creditor. Had she said this to beit din immediately upon producing the note, she would have been believed by virtue of the migo that she could have destroyed it. But once the note had been established in beit din, meaning that she first produced it and said that the creditor deposited it with her and that it belonged to him, and only after the time for immediate retraction had passed did she add, "I know that it was paid," her migo was gone. A migo operates only within the period in which a litigant may immediately retract or supplement the prior statement. Rav Nachman therefore did not believe her. Rava, by contrast, held that she remained believed by virtue of her status as entrusted third party, since at some earlier point she possessed the practical power associated with that status. It is possible that even Rashba agrees that where the heirs of the entrusted third party do not know whether the note came from the debtor or creditor, the note remains deposited. His disagreement may concern only a case in which it is known that the note came from the creditor and the sole uncertainty is whether the debt was later paid. In that situation he holds that payment is not suspected absent some impairing circumstance, as explained above.
Before it came into another person's possession. See Sema, seif katan 7, through his statement that so long as the uncertainty of the later holder has not yet been heard from his own mouth, the note remains unaffected. Rashba's words indicate that this rule applies specifically to a note in the possession of an heir, which resembles a note that was lost. Until it comes into another person's possession and the defect is thereby revealed, the note is not impaired. The entrusted third party himself is different. His authority derives from the parties having trusted him and thereby made him functionally equivalent to a litigant, so that his uncertainty is treated as the litigant's uncertainty. Therefore, if another person seizes the note from the entrusted third party in the presence of witnesses, even before the third party has voiced his uncertainty, the seizing person must return it to him. The third party's uncertainty retains the status of the litigant's uncertainty, because whenever the object must legally be returned to the entrusted third party, his special credibility has not ended. Shach writes similarly at the end of siman 56 concerning one who gives the entrusted object without qualification to beit din or a guardian. Even if one were to disagree with the Shach there, where the entrustment may have passed to another custodian, here, where the object was seized from the entrusted third party, the result is certainly as stated. By contrast, where a note was lost, or where it was taken from an heir, even if another person found or seized it in the presence of witnesses before the heir had expressed any uncertainty, the note remains valid. Rashba expressly compares seizure from an heir to finding a note that fell from him. The special rule applies only to the original entrusted third party whom the parties deliberately trusted.
A woman's ketubah, etc. See Sema, seif katan 9, and Shach, seif katan 9, who question this ruling from the Rambam's position in seif 4 and from Bava Metzia 7. In my opinion the matter is simple. Even where a note was actually lost, Raavad, cited by the Shach in siman 41, holds that if the debt is still within its term, the creditor may collect upon proof that the due date has not yet arrived. Accordingly, even the Rambam, who disagrees there, does so because the document was lost and thereby impaired. Here there is no such defect, as the Sema explains in seif katan 11. Therefore, certainly one may collect upon proof that the debt is still within its term. A ketubah is no weaker than a debt within its term. That is why the woman may collect on it. The rule in siman 41 that a note found in another person's possession cannot be collected even within its term must necessarily concern a case where the holder himself is uncertain. If the holder knows the status of the document, his statement is believed. When he is uncertain, his uncertainty has the status of the litigant's uncertainty and therefore prevents collection. Even proof that the debt is within its term cannot overcome the creditor's own effective uncertainty. But where the note is found among the heirs of the entrusted third party, Rashba explains that this doctrine does not apply, and there is also no defect of loss. Everyone then agrees that proof that the debt remains within its term suffices for collection. This also answers the difficulty from the dowry document in seif 5. That case concerns an acquisition document, where we must additionally suspect that the husband instructed the witnesses to write the instrument but said, "Give it to me," and never delivered it, or that it was placed in the third party's hands subject to a condition. A ketubah, by contrast, represents an obligation that certainly came into existence. The only uncertainty is whether it was paid, and where payment is still within the presumptively unexpired period, collection is allowed. The Sema writes that the holder cannot claim, even according to the Rambam, "She sold it to me." The reason appears to be that Bava Kamma 89 states that with respect to a woman's claim against her husband, she is presumed eventually to waive it to him, and therefore no ordinary person would purchase it from her. The holder is accordingly not believed to say that he purchased it. There is an additional distinction. Yevamot 116 explains that we generally do not suspect loss of debt instruments because a person is careful with them. A ketubah is different. Under the rule that one who claims contrary to an established beit din enactment accomplishes nothing, a woman may have protection even without retaining the physical ketubah, and therefore people are not equally careful with it. Loss of a ketubah is thus a more realistic concern.
