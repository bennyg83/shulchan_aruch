They should not return it. Sema asks from siman 243, where if a gift deed is found among the heirs of a depositary it is returned to the giver's heirs. He distinguishes a gift without kinyan from a loan document. Shach rejects that and adopts Sema's second answer: here it is unknown from whose hands the document reached the depositary, whether from borrower or lender, so it remains deposited. There it is known that the giver delivered it, and therefore it returns to the giver. The same rule applies to a debt document: if it is known from whose hands it reached the depositary, it is returned to that person. This is correct.

It should be returned to the woman. One cannot argue that perhaps it had been pledged to the depositary's father or sold to him, according to Rambam, who does not require proof of acquisition against the creditor. Sema limits the rule to the principal ketubah of a virgin or widow, because so long as she remains married it cannot ordinarily have been paid without making continued marital relations improper. He excludes additional ketubah obligations and dowry, which may have been paid. On his view, if a widow or divorcee's ketubah remained in the father's possession for some time after the marriage ended, it also should not be returned. Shach objects and says that by strict law even the principal ketubah should not be returned. The leniency rests only on the custom of women depositing ketubot with third parties. Therefore if the local custom extends also to dowry documents, or continues after divorce and similar circumstances, everything follows the custom. This is correct.
