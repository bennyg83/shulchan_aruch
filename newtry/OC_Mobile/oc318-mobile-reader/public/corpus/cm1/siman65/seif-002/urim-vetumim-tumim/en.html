He should not return any document, and so forth. Rashba asks a strong question. In the Sanhedrin incident involving the elderly woman who said, "I know this document was paid," there are two versions of R. Nachman's ruling. According to one version he believed her because she had a migo that she could have burned the document. According to the other he did not believe her after the document had already been established before beit din. Why was any special credibility needed? Even if we entirely ignore her statement that the note was paid, the situation should be no better than a third party saying, "I do not know the status of this document." Such a note cannot be collected at all. What then was the entire dispute about? Shach answers that the issue was not whether the creditor could collect from the borrower. Certainly, if the note were merely one whose status was unknown in the hands of a third party, it could not be collected. The question was whether the document should actually be returned torn to the borrower or instead remain deposited. The elderly woman, by virtue of her migo that she could have burned it, might be believed strongly enough to destroy the physical instrument. This is extremely strained. If the creditor can never collect with the note anyway, what practical importance justified the entire Talmudic discussion over whether it should be torn or merely stored? One could suggest a difference if the woman later changes her mind and says, "I now remember that it was not paid." If she was originally believed as a third party through migo, perhaps she cannot retract, like a witness who has testified. But if the note was withheld only because she had said, "I do not know its status," perhaps she can later say, "I have now remembered." Perhaps Shach intended something like this. Yet I am uncertain whether one who says in beit din, "I do not know the status of this document," can later say, "I now remember." A litigant or witness is expected to be precise before beit din. See siman 29, seif 1, Rama. The phrase, "It shall remain until Eliyahu comes," also suggests indefinite suspension, not merely until the third party happens to remember. So this distinction requires study. There is a better answer based on what I wrote above in siman 56, seif katan 15, from Responsa Rosh, klal 68. If Reuven's note against Shimon is in Levi's hands, and Levi says it was deposited with him for full forgiveness and destruction, then even if Shimon himself knows nothing of any payment, if Reuven admits he appointed Levi as a third party, Shimon is exempt. We do not say: remove the note, and Reuven still has an oral claim while Shimon says, "I do not know whether I paid," which would ordinarily obligate him. The third-party entrustment itself can discharge even the underlying debt. I explained the reason there. Accordingly, the Sanhedrin case may involve a borrower who does not know whether the debt was paid, perhaps heirs or another person lacking knowledge. If the elderly woman is not believed as a third party, and the only rule is that the document is withheld because its status is unknown, then the borrower may still have to pay the underlying debt. Remove the note. The creditor says, "You owe me," and the debtor says, "I do not know whether I paid." Ordinarily the debtor is liable. But if R. Nachman believes the third party because the parties originally entrusted her and she had the power to burn the note, then the borrower is exempt even though he himself does not know whether payment occurred. The original entrustment means the creditor accepted that her statement would determine the status of the debt. This solves Rashba's question. However, it does not fully fit the version that believes her only because she has a present migo to burn. On that version, the rationale is not necessarily that the creditor originally accepted her as a binding third party. If the borrower remains uncertain, why should her current migo exempt him from the underlying debt? Perhaps there is one witness who supports the creditor. In ordinary circumstances, where the borrower does not contradict the witness, the witness is treated like two and the creditor can collect. But a third party is believed more strongly than one witness. According to the first version, she is believed because of her original entrustment; according to the other version, she is believed through the present migo that she could burn the note. Either basis can outweigh the single witness. See the beginning of siman 56. Still, there is another, even clearer resolution based on Tur and Baal HaTerumot above in siman 56. They explain "the document was established before beit din" to mean that the elderly woman had brought it to beit din for authentication and said nothing. Only afterward did she say, "It was paid." According to the first version, R. Nachman still believes her because she is a third party. According to the second version, once she allowed it to be authenticated in court without protest, she is no longer believed. This directly answers Rashba's question. If we did not believe her by third-party law, we could not now treat the note as one whose status was merely unknown. By allowing it to be authenticated without saying anything, she effectively acknowledged that it was not paid. Shach himself writes above in siman 56 that this removes the category of "I do not know its status." The document would therefore remain fully valid. Only because she is believed as a third party can she later say that it was paid despite her earlier silence during authentication. Accordingly, the Talmudic dispute has major practical meaning. This also gives a clear proof for Tur's ruling. Once a third party voluntarily presents a note for authentication without saying anything, he can no longer later claim merely, "I do not know its status." Only where the special third-party credibility remains available can he still assert actual payment. This is, in my opinion, the correct resolution.
