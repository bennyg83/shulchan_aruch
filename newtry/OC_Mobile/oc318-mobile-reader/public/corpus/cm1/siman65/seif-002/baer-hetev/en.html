The heirs. The Sema writes that this teaches that one should not say that since the heirs could not have known the matter, they should return the document to the lender. It teaches otherwise. This requires study, because in siman 243, seif 13, regarding a deed of gift found among the heirs of a depositary, the Mechaber rules that it is returned to the donor's heirs. Perhaps one can distinguish that there the deed contains no kinyan, so the recipient had not yet acquired the gift, whereas here there is a valid loan document before us and the borrower admits that he borrowed, but we fear that he may have repaid. Therefore it is returned to neither party. The Shach disagrees and writes that the Gemara explicitly proves that even a deed of gift without a kinyan that is found is returned to neither party. Certainly a document found in their father's possession is like one found in the street, and this is also explicit in the Tosefta. The correct explanation is that in siman 243 the case is where it is known that the donor gave the document into the depositary's hand, and the Rosh expressly holds that in such a case it is returned to him. Thus the Shach; see there.
Another. The Sema writes that this is apparently not literal. Even if the document came into another person's hands, so long as that person never stated that there was uncertainty about it and that he did not know its nature, if the lender took it from him we do not say that it must remain deposited. The Bach disagrees. Thus the Shach. The Taz agrees with the Sema and writes that the Rashba clearly implies this, contrary to the Bach, who emended the Rashba's words; see there.
Ketubah. The reason is that a ketubah is not ordinarily payable until after the husband's death. The Ritva concludes that this itself is the reason the heir is not believed to say that she sold him the ketubah by writing and delivery, transferred it incidentally with land, or pledged it to him. As for the Mechaber's ruling in seif 5 that a dowry document that is found is treated like a promissory note, it appears to me that this refers specifically to a dowry obligation, which she gave him when he married her and which is more immediately subject to payment. A ketubah, however, may not be paid during the husband's lifetime if he will continue living with her without a ketubah, as stated in Even HaEzer siman 100, end of seif 2, and siman 102, seif 5. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that this distinction is unnecessary. As a matter of law, even with a ketubah we would ordinarily suspect payment if it is found in another's possession after she became widowed. Here the ruling is based on local custom, as the Ritva writes: it is the custom in our regions that the heir returns it to the woman, because the presumption is that she deposited it. The Rama abbreviated the matter. Accordingly, where there is no such custom, we do suspect payment. Thus the Shach.
