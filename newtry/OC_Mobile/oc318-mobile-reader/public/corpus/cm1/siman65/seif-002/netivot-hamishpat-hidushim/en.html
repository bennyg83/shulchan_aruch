His heirs. In siman 243:13, concerning a gift deed, the Mechaber rules that it is returned to the donor's heirs. A gift without a kinyan is different because the recipient had not yet acquired the gift. A debt note, by contrast, indicates that the loan was actually made, and the only concern is repayment, so it is not returned to either side. The Shach disagrees, because the Gemara explicitly states that even a gift deed found in the marketplace without a kinyan is not returned to either party, and a note found in their father's possession is like one found in the marketplace. The Shach therefore distinguishes siman 243 as a case where it is known that the donor himself delivered the deed to the custodian, so it is returned to the donor. Likewise with a debt note, if it is known from whose hand it came into the custodian's possession, it is returned to that person. This is correct. If the custodian knows that it came to him from the lender, while the borrower claims, "It fell from me and you found it," even if it had already been held by beit din, the custodian is believed, unlike Tumim at the end of siman 41. If heirs of the shalish know that their father was shalish for both parties but do not know under what arrangement, then although two people holding a garment divide it with an oath, here the document remains deposited. Rashba explains that uncertainty of the shalish is like uncertainty of the litigant and itself impairs the note. See Beurim, seif katan 10.

A woman's ketubah held by a custodian. Specifically a ketubah, which is not ordinarily payable immediately but only after the husband's death. A dowry note in seif 5 is more immediately payable, and therefore is not returned. Sema. The Shach writes that even a ketubah is returned only because of custom. See Beurim, seif katan 8: with any note still within its due date, one may collect through witnesses establishing that it is within its term, because a document held by a shalish is not impaired in the same way as a fallen document. By contrast, the dowry note in seif 5 is speaking of a note with a kinyan, where there is concern that the giver may have said, "Give it back into my hand," or that it was deposited subject to a condition.
