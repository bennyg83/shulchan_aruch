See Shach, seif katan 12, through his statement that if the debtor refuses to swear, the note is returned to the creditor, and if the creditor refuses to swear it is returned to the debtor. I find it difficult why this rule is not stated in every case where the Mishnah says that disputed property remains deposited. For example, concerning the third maneh in siman 300, why cannot either claimant say to the other, "You swear and take it, or I will swear and take it," so that if one refuses to swear the property is delivered to the other? This does not resemble siman 267, seif 10, where even after the oath the object remains deposited. Here the very proposal is "swear and take." One cannot simply answer that this case is different because the debtor can say, "I instruct that the note be returned to you. Swear and collect, and if you refuse to swear, return my note." There is still a problem from the opposite direction. If the debtor refuses to take the rabbinic oath of denial, how can the creditor collect as though he held an entirely valid note? That seems like judicial seizure of the debtor's property, yet with respect to an ordinary rabbinic oath of denial we do not seize assets when a defendant refuses to swear. It may be that this is not considered judicial seizure. The note is already deposited in the hands of the finder, and the immediate adjudication concerns possession of the document itself. Returning the document to one side therefore does not constitute descending into the other's property. This also explains why the rule differs from other cases of property left deposited. Here the creditor independently has an oath claim against the debtor even if the loan were oral. If the debtor refuses to swear, the note can be returned to the creditor without that return itself constituting seizure of the debtor's property. Conversely, the debtor has an oath claim against the creditor. Since the debtor does not direct that his note be returned, the money he previously paid is treated as a loan to the creditor, for his payment was made only on the understanding that the note would be returned to him. The debtor therefore has an enforceable oath claim against the creditor. If the creditor refuses to swear, the note is returned to the debtor, again without the return itself constituting judicial seizure. The third maneh in siman 300 is different. The two depositors have no independent oath obligation against one another at all. Therefore neither can force the result merely by offering to swear while the other refuses.
