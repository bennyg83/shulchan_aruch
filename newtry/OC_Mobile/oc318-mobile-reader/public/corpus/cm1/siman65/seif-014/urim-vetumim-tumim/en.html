A deed of gift made by a healthy person, and so forth. This too is difficult according to Tur, who accepts Abaye's doctrine that the signing witnesses acquire for the recipient. If so, even with a gift document we should return the deed. Even if the donor had it written but did not yet deliver it, the recipient's rights should already arise from the signing. I wrote above in siman 43 that there is still another concern. Perhaps on that same day the donor sold or gave the property to another person and delivered a deed to that other party. If we now return this lost gift deed to the first intended recipient, there may be a case of shuda between same-day documents, and returning it can deprive the party who actually received a valid transfer first. I later saw a Tosafot gloss cited in Shitah Mekubetzet raising this very question. Tosafot answer that such a same-day competing sale is too remote and is not suspected, just as in the receipt case. Perhaps this is exactly where Rosh and Tur disagree with Tosafot. They may hold that a gift differs from a receipt for the reason I explained in siman 43. Another possibility is that Rosh does not accept Tosafot's same-day-remoteness answer even for receipts. Once a document has fallen and thereby become defective, we can suspect even an uncommon event. One might ask: according to Abaye, who allows a receipt to operate from the signing date, perhaps the woman sold her ketubah later that same day. Why is the receipt returned? Rosh cites an opinion that Shmuel's rule allowing assignment of a debt does not create a practical concern in this setting because anyone who buys a ketubah knows that the woman can forgive it to her husband. Such purchases are inherently weak and purchasers protect themselves. One can refine this. If purchasers were genuinely exposed to an immediate same-day forgiveness with no way to protect themselves, few rational people would buy a ketubah. Therefore actual purchasers can be expected to supervise the woman through the remainder of the day and prevent her from executing a receipt or forgiveness after the sale. This makes the same-day concern too remote. But a gift deed is different. No such market structure forces the recipient to monitor the donor continuously for the rest of the day. The concern that the donor transferred the property to someone else that same day is therefore more meaningful. There is also another way to understand Abaye's answer. When a receipt is written, the original debt already exists. The receipt merely cancels or releases it. Where R. Meir holds that letters are acquired through writing and delivery, the sale of the ketubah may itself require a formal acquisition document. If the sale and receipt bear the same date, one can treat them as taking effect together at the end of the day for certain purposes, leaving the burden of proof on the purchaser who seeks to extract from the husband. This structure does not translate directly to a deed of gift. This also helps answer why Abaye needed to say in the sugya that the Mishnah's gift documents concern a gift by a healthy person that resembles a deathbed gift, from which the donor can retract. Rosh explains that Abaye needed this so that the rule "if the donor says give it, we give it" should not create a contradiction. One might object that according to Abaye's signing-based doctrine there is no collusion concern in any event. But according to what I have written, there remains the same-day competing-transfer concern. Abaye therefore still needs to establish the Mishnah in a form where the donor can retract and where return depends on the specific circumstances.
And Shach writes that Rosh's statement that Abaye is coming to explain the Mishnah is really language taken from Rif. That is forced. If so, later when Rosh derives from Rif's precise wording in the receipt case that intervening sale can defeat Abaye's doctrine, he should have derived the same conclusion more directly from this very discussion. The fact that he does not shows that Shach's reading is not persuasive. In any event, the concern with a healthy person's gift deed is real: the donor may have made an intervening transfer that defeats the effect of the lost document. Therefore it is not simply returned merely because the donor now admits.
It should not be given to him. Bach writes that this remains true even if the document is authenticated. The recipient's own authentication does not allow return. His concern is that perhaps the donor later reacquired the property from the recipient and sold it to a second purchaser. Now the donor and original recipient collude by returning the old gift deed so the recipient can recover the property from the later purchaser improperly. This creates difficulties. Tur rules that a kinyan document may be returned. Why does Bach's concern not apply there as well? Perhaps the donor reacquired the property, thereby ending the original acquisition, and now uses the old instrument to recreate an earlier lien. There is also a direct Gemara problem. Bava Batra 169 says that when a sale deed is lost, a replacement deed may be written without the warranty clause. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel disagrees. The Gemara explains that his concern is that documents can be acquired by delivery alone. Perhaps the purchaser returned the deed to the seller and thereby transferred the property back. A replacement deed would then allow the first purchaser to reclaim improperly. The first Tanna disagrees because he holds that letters require written transfer together with delivery. Mere return of the deed does not transfer the property back. According to Bach, however, even under the first Tanna we should fear that the original purchaser reacquired or retransferred through a valid transaction and that the parties now collude. If so, no replacement deed should ever be written from the original date. Tosafot there say explicitly that the replacement deed is written from the original date. This contradicts Bach's broad concern. The answer must be that according to the view requiring written transfer together with delivery, we do not suspect such collusion. If the original recipient truly sold the property back to the donor, there would normally be a written transfer or other formal acquisition. Such a transaction has publicity. People sell openly. We do not create a remote conspiracy that contradicts a public transaction. The whole concern of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel is specifically that mere delivery of the old deed can transfer the rights back quietly, without a new document and without comparable publicity. Therefore, where the law requires written transfer and delivery, there is no serious concern that the donor secretly reacquired and then colludes by returning the old deed. This also explains the discussion above concerning Rosh and Abaye. Abaye himself holds that letters can be acquired through delivery alone. Therefore, according to him, Bach's type of concern is real: perhaps the recipient silently returned the deed to the donor and thereby transferred the property back. That is exactly Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's concern in Bava Batra. Consequently, even according to Abaye's signing-based doctrine, an admission by the donor does not automatically allow the lost gift deed to be returned. There may have been an intervening quiet reacquisition through delivery alone. Tosafot, who say that according to Abaye the concern does not arise, can still agree with Abaye's signing rule while not extending his delivery-alone acquisition rule in the same way for the practical halachah they are discussing. But for our halachah, which requires written transfer together with delivery, the broad concern stated by Bach should not be applied. Thus, where a deed of gift has already been validly delivered and later lost, and there is no evidence of a subsequent formal reacquisition by the donor, we do not presume an undisclosed transaction and collusion merely because the donor now admits. The principal concern remains the more basic one: perhaps the deed was written for a gift that was never completed. That is the proper distinction.
Unless he expressly stated, and so forth. Nimukei Yosef cites a difficulty in the name of Ran. Why does the Mishnah say that found deeds of gift are not returned? If they are kinyan documents, they should be returned. If they are not kinyan documents, the witnesses should not have written them until the recipient was present, just as witnesses may not write a loan document for the borrower unless the lender is present. Ran answers that scribes do write gift deeds even when the recipient is absent and even without a kinyan, because it is uncommon and remote that the donor will retract. Derishah and other later authorities were troubled, because the Gemara above explicitly says that once a document has fallen we may suspect that the witnesses happened to write improperly even though they were not authorized to do so. Why then can we not say the same here? Shitah Mekubetzet preserves Ran's own words and clarifies that, according to the conclusion where the concern is repayment and collusion, we do not generally say that the witnesses happened to write improperly. This is clearly connected with Tosafot Bava Metzia 15b, who explain that according to R. Yochanan, who attributes the Mishnah to repayment and collusion, if he does not follow Abaye he need not accept the concern that the witnesses happened to write improperly. The question on R. Yochanan from the baraita of gifts is therefore real. The same applies according to Abaye if we follow Rav Zevid's explanation and Rif's view that if the donor meanwhile sold or gave the property to another, the earlier signing-based right can be displaced. Then the question returns: how could witnesses write for the donor in the absence of the recipient? Ran's answer is that Chazal did not fear so remote a possibility in advance. The witnesses may therefore write. Once the document is found, however, the fall creates enough weakness that we do suspect such a later event. Ran himself asks another difficulty. Perhaps after ordering the gift document written, the donor did not sell the field but instead borrowed money from someone. When the gift is later delivered, the earlier date may defeat the creditor's lien. This is more common than a repeated sale and does not look like collusion, since the donor may expect to repay the loan from other assets. At first I thought the distinction might depend on whether liens are of Torah force. When a gift deed is written in Nisan but not delivered until Tishrei, there is already publicity from Nisan that the property may be given. If the donor borrows in Iyar, the lender heard the kol and cannot reasonably rely on the property. If liens are only rabbinic, Chazal imposed collection from land primarily so lenders would not refrain from lending. Here that policy is weak, because the lender knew of the earlier contemplated gift. Thus giving effect to the gift does not produce a Torah wrong. R. Asi, however, holds that liens are of Torah force. Under his view, the creditor who lent in Iyar acquired a Torah lien before the gift actually took effect in Tishrei. Delivering the Nisan-dated gift document later would therefore wrongfully displace an earlier Torah lien. This could explain why R. Asi's concern differs. But after finding Ran's own explanation in Shitah Mekubetzet, I withdrew from that approach. Ran explains the law even according to R. Yochanan, who holds liens are of Torah force, so the distinction cannot be built on that alone.
Therefore, it appears that Ran understands the signing witnesses as creating an immediate right in principle even according to R. Yochanan and R. Asi. The defect is that there is no publicity until delivery. Thus, from the Torah perspective, the earlier date can already establish the priority. Chazal alone limited purchaser-facing collection because there was no kol. If the donor borrowed in the meantime and the later gift displaces that creditor, the creditor loses only the rabbinic protection that depends on publicity. That is not the kind of direct Torah theft for which Chazal required us to anticipate a remote scheme. But where the donor gave the property to one person and afterward seeks to give it to another through the earlier-dated document, the later recipient would physically take the field from someone who already acquired it. According to Rif, even Abaye's signing-based right is displaced by an intervening actual transfer. That would be a direct wrongful taking, and Chazal were more concerned about it. This explains why the Gemara emphasizes the case where he gave and then gave again rather than merely borrowed in between. It also answers why R. Asi can still fear collusion in such a case even though elsewhere he does not broadly fear collusion. His reluctance to suspect collusion is strongest where a creditor collecting from purchasers would first have to swear. Jews are not presumed to collude in a scheme that requires a false oath. Here, however, the earlier-dated donee would simply take the field from the later donee under the apparent strength of his deed. No comparable oath necessarily blocks the scheme. Therefore even R. Asi can be concerned. This also helps explain Rif's wording concerning a note written in Nisan but not borrowed upon until Tishrei, where he adds the possibility of collusion. Rosh objected that R. Asi does not generally fear collusion. But if the note is already past due and purchaser collection would require an oath, collusion is indeed unlikely. On the very date of the loan, however, no such oath is required, and a collusive scheme becomes more plausible. Rif can therefore distinguish by circumstance. The same framework resolves Tosafot's question concerning a deathbed gift. If the father first gave the property to one person, and the son now asks that an earlier deathbed gift be returned to another, the son may be trying to displace his own creditor or another recipient. A debt of the son can attach to the inherited property, and the later use of the father's earlier gift deed can remove that property from the son's creditor. This is not necessarily the same type of collusion as ordinary repayment, and the concern can therefore exist even according to R. Asi. Rambam's distinction also becomes understandable. In Hilchot Malveh 24 he rules that ordinary non-kinyan loan documents may not be written for the borrower unless the lender is present, but sale and gift deeds may always be written for the seller or donor. Maggid Mishneh says the precise reason for the distinction is unclear. According to the analysis above, it is because with a sale or gift the underlying transaction and its publicity operate differently. Chazal did not initially fear the remote possibility that the donor would later use the earlier writing to displace another party. With a loan document, however, an earlier date used for a later loan can directly create an improper lien and purchaser collection, a positive act of extraction. That concern is stronger. One might ask that a sale deed written for a seller could likewise become dangerous if the seller first made the field an apotiki for a creditor, later sold another field, and only afterward completed the first sale. If the creditor later collects the apotiki, the purchaser could shift the loss to the later-sold field even though his own purchase was in fact later. Perhaps that scenario is too uncommon, and an apotiki generally has publicity. Chazal did not build the drafting rule around such a remote possibility. The distinction therefore remains plausible, though the matter still requires study.
