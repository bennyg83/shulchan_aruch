It shall not be returned to him. This too is because of concern for collusion. Perhaps the named recipient does not truly intend to acquire by this instrument, but the person who now produces it gives it to him only so that he can use the earlier-dated document to dispossess someone to whom the giver later sold or gave the field. Therefore it appears that here too, if the recipient or purchaser named in the document proves that the instrument reached his possession on the date it was written, it should be returned to him. The Mechaber did not need to state this because he relied on the preceding law. A loan document, by contrast, is not returned to the lender even if he proves it reached his hand from its stated date, because there remains concern that the borrower later paid it, since a promissory note is made to be repaid. What the Tur and Mechaber write above in siman 43, seif 15, that a document is returned when proof is brought, which appears to include a loan document, concerns a case where the lender himself found it after it had fallen. See Perishah and Derishah.
Unless the gift document expressly reserved, etc. The same applies if he reserved such a right in a sale document. The reason is that if he later sold or gave the property to someone else, he thereby retracted from the first transfer and could not use the earlier document to dispossess the later transferee.
Where it expressly states that he transferred it immediately, etc. In that case it is like the deed of emancipation or gift discussed above: when proof is brought that the document reached the recipient at the time of transfer it is returned. Here it need not actually have reached his hand, because the immediate kinyan gives it the same status as if it had reached him.
One who finds a deathbed gift document, etc. With a deathbed gift, even if the giver did not expressly reserve the power to retract, the law treats it as though he had, as explained below in siman 250.
Even if his son says, etc. The reason is that we are concerned that the father wrote the document intending to give it to this recipient but never actually delivered it. After the father's death, the son may have given or sold this field to someone else and now regrets it and seeks to dispossess the later recipient. He therefore says to give the old document to the person named in it and falsely claims that his father already delivered it and never retracted, making the old transfer appear earlier than the son's later transfer.
Unless it is a kinyan document. Then it has the status of a healthy person's gift, and the named recipient acquired as soon as the document was written even if it never reached his hand, as explained nearby above.
