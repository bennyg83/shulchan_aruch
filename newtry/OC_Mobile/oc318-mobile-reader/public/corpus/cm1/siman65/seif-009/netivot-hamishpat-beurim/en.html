One who finds a note inside a utensil. At first sight this is difficult. The primary concern with a fallen promissory note is that because the instrument was invalid or paid, its owner was not careful to guard it. The note is therefore presumed to have fallen from the creditor. Indeed, the Gemara appears not ordinarily to suspect that it fell from the debtor. Bava Metzia 16 states that Shmuel does not suspect payment, because had the debt been paid the debtor would have torn the note. The opposing view answers that perhaps the creditor delayed returning it, or retained it as leverage for the scribe's fee. That latter explanation applies only while the note remains with the creditor. As to the debtor, Shmuel's reasoning remains strong: if he had paid and recovered the note, he would have torn it. I did, however, find that Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 52, writes that we also suspect payment and loss from the debtor. He explains that the debtor may have kept the intact note because he wished to show others that his document was in his hands. See there. In any event, when the note fell from the creditor, everyone agrees that the fall impairs it. Baal HaTerumot himself already raises the present difficulty and answers in the name of Tosafot. The concern applies where the note is simply found loose, because the circumstances resemble one who cast it onto a refuse heap after it became invalid or was paid. But where it is found in one of the protected forms described here, such as inside a utensil or bundled with other documents, it is more likely that the notes were validly stored and then accidentally lost. That is why the ordinary defect of a fallen note does not apply in the same way. A further question remains. Why does the Sema in seif katan 18 need to say that an identifying mark consisting of a hole in the note is ineffective because both debtor and creditor know that mark? Even if only the creditor knew it, a note that was found after falling is ordinarily invalid once we know it fell from the creditor, because the fall itself impairs the note and suggests that he discarded it due to invalidity or payment, as Bava Metzia 13 states. The answer is that the Sema needs his reason for a more limited claim. Suppose the note is still in the creditor's hand, he admits that the debt was paid, but demands reimbursement of the scribe's fee and says that he retained the note as leverage for that fee. He is certainly believed up to the value of the paper as scrap. This is not because of a migo that he could have demanded the entire debt. Once he admits that the lien has been extinguished, he is not believed through such a migo to revive the debt. Rather, the paper itself functions as collateral in his possession for the small amount owed for the writing fee. Even if he says that the physical paper itself belongs to him, he may be entitled to receive the paper for its material value if he explains that he retained it as security for the scribe's fee. One cannot say that because he failed to guard the document and it fell, this proves that nothing at all was owed to him. Bava Metzia 17 itself explains why payment may be suspected even though the note was not torn: sometimes the creditor retains it as leverage for the scribe's fee. Evidently, loss of the paper does not disprove such a limited claim. The Sema therefore adds that a hole known to both sides is no identifying mark at all. The note may have fallen from the debtor, who had no reason to guard the paper carefully after payment. Accordingly, even the torn paper is not returned to the creditor on the strength of such an identifying mark. As for the Shach's discussion whether in monetary matters we suspect that an item was borrowed and therefore an identifying mark may be known to another, with Hashem's help this will be explained in the laws of lost property, where the subject properly belongs.
