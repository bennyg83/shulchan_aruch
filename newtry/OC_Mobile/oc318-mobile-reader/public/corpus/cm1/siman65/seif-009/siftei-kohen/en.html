He shall give it to the person who identifies. He gives it to the one who identifies the container. We do not suspect that the container was borrowed, as we do in matters of marital prohibition, because in monetary law the Sages permitted return of lost property even by non-exclusive identifying marks. Tosafot and the Rosh explain so. In my view, however, their proof is not compelling. The Gemara distinguishes prohibition from monetary law regarding the adequacy of identifying marks, not necessarily regarding concern for borrowing. A case involving a casket or satchel may concern an article people do not ordinarily lend, like a purse or wallet. Alternatively, the finder announces merely that he found documents or produce, and the claimant identifies the particular container. Even though people place documents in such containers, they are not invariably placed there, and the identification is therefore meaningful. Regarding prohibition itself, the Rosh rules that even exceptionally distinctive marks on borrowed clothing are ineffective because of concern for borrowing, and the later authorities follow him. Yet this requires substantial study. Nimukei Yosef and Ran rule that we do not suspect borrowing where the marks are exceptionally distinctive, and the Rif, Rambam, and Baal HaItur do not clearly disagree. Their silence suggests that marks on clothing and on the body have the same law. The Maggid Mishneh, citing Ramban and Rashba, likewise states that exceptionally distinctive marks are effective even there. The conclusion of Tosafot also indicates that at the final stage of the Gemara we do not suspect borrowing even in a matter of prohibition. This therefore requires further study.
