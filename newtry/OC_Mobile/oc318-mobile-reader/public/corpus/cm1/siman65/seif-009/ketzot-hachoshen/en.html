An identifying mark on the utensil. This is difficult. If the very reason a fallen document is weakened is that the creditor's failure to guard it suggests repayment, why should proof that the document fell from the creditor help? Even if we watched it fall from his hand, perhaps he had already been paid and therefore was careless. Rashi's statement that a document held by a judge should not be removed until Elijah comes unless witnesses saw from whose hand it fell seems to imply that proof it fell from the lender does help. Yet other passages in Rashi say explicitly that the fall itself is evidence of repayment because a valid document would have been guarded. The broader authorities likewise imply that even where we know it fell from the lender, we can still fear repayment unless the creditor himself found it before another took possession. The Shach's discussion of a document lost before witnesses similarly assumes that proof of loss alone is insufficient unless the loss was unavoidable, such as through military capture. How then can the identifying mark on the creditor's utensil help? Perhaps where the document fell together with the creditor's personal utensil, the circumstances demonstrate an accidental loss affecting both items. The fact that he lost the entire utensil shows an unavoidable mishap rather than carelessness because the debt was paid. Chiddushei Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi uses a similar rationale in the Shabtai ketubah case. This may answer our seif. But a later seif concerning a bundle of documents still requires explanation. Rosh writes that when tied documents bear an identifying mark, the mark clarifies whether they fell from lender or borrower. Perhaps a creditor who possesses a highly distinctive mark relies upon his ability to recover the documents through that mark and therefore guards them less carefully. In that setting, the very existence of the mark explains the lack of care and removes the inference of repayment. This remains somewhat difficult.
