They should give them to the one who provides an identifying mark on the vessel. Tosafot, Rosh, and many authorities cited in Or Zarua write that in monetary law we do not generally suspect that the vessel was borrowed. Just as Chazal rely on ordinary, non-extraordinary identifying marks in monetary matters, so too we do not create a concern that the claimant merely knows the vessel because he once borrowed or lent it. Shach questions this, but his objections are not strong enough to reject Tosafot, Rosh, and the other authorities. There is, however, a difficulty from the Gemara itself. The Gemara explains that Chazal return lost property through ordinary identifying marks because the owner is willing to accept that arrangement in order that his own lost property also be returned to him. It then asks from a bundle of loan documents: why should a borrower be pleased with a rule that returns the documents to the lender? The Gemara does not give a clear answer. Rosh writes that although the Gemara leaves the point unresolved, one can say that ordinary identifying marks are only rabbinic, and some other reason must apply in the document case. Alternatively, the Mishnah may concern a Torah scholar, to whom the documents are returned based on visual recognition after he supplies even a minimal sign. If that second answer is necessary, then one cannot generally return a loan document based merely on an identifying mark on the vessel, because the borrower has no reason to accept a regime that helps the lender recover a note. Only a Torah scholar with reliable visual recognition would be different. The first answer of Rosh creates no difficulty, but the second does. This also resolves Maggid Mishneh's question why Rosh inferred from the fact that the Gemara asks concerning an animal identified by its saddle, rather than from the more direct Mishnah of a document found inside a case. According to the second answer, the document-in-a-case law may concern a Torah scholar and does not prove that borrowing is ignored generally. The fruit-in-a-vessel case, however, plainly says the item is returned to whoever gives the identifying mark on the vessel and therefore supports the broader principle. Still, Tur is difficult. He rules without qualification both that a document found in a vessel is returned to the person who identifies the vessel and that a bundle of documents is returned based on identifying marks. Yet according to one of Rosh's own explanations, the latter should be limited to a Torah scholar. Were I not hesitant to say so, I would suggest a more fundamental analysis. Rava initially attempts to prove that identifying marks are of Torah force from the verse requiring one to investigate a claimant before returning lost property. The Gemara rejects the proof by saying that perhaps the verse refers to witnesses. Why not answer instead that the verse speaks of an especially strong identifying mark, which everyone agrees has Torah force? The likely answer is that when Rava asks whether "identifying marks are of Torah force," he is asking about the entire category of marks, including those normally called significant. In Chullin 79 as well, the phrase "identifying marks are of Torah force" can refer to highly distinctive marks, as Ran explains. Rava's proof therefore concerns the overall evidentiary institution of identifying marks, and the Gemara rejects it only by substituting witnesses. This yields an important distinction. A mark is called ordinary or intermediate when it is found among some people, while a highly distinctive mark is one so unusual that it is virtually unique. In a normal lost-object case, the entire population is within the possible field of owners. An ordinary identifying mark may therefore match another person's property by chance. But in a bundle of loan documents, if the only realistic possibilities are the borrower and the lender, then the same ordinary mark becomes highly distinctive for purposes of that limited universe. The chance that precisely these two people share the same unusual mark is extremely small. Thus even if ordinary marks are only rabbinic in general, the identifying mark in a two-party document dispute can function like a fully distinctive mark and does not depend on the policy reason that the owner is pleased to have lost property returned through such signs. This removes the difficulty from the bundle-of-documents case. The Gemara's question is strongest only under the assumption that no class of identifying mark has Torah force. Once one accepts that highly distinctive marks are of Torah force, the limited two-party setting can naturally qualify. This also explains why the case of a found get is different. A lost get can raise concern not merely between this husband and this wife, but with respect to any other person of the same name. Rashba writes that once another person of the same name is established, we can become concerned even for others elsewhere. The possible universe is broader. Therefore a truly distinctive mark is required, such as a hole next to a particular letter. R. Elazar, who was uncertain whether identifying marks are of Torah force, therefore requires a highly distinctive mark in a get. This also helps explain the Gemara's question regarding a get found tied to a purse: perhaps the purse was borrowed. In the get case, where many possible owners may be relevant, concern for borrowing can be a general concern. In a monetary document dispute limited to the borrower and lender, however, borrowing would have to be precisely between those two people. That is a much narrower and less likely possibility. Accordingly, even if we sometimes suspect borrowing in ordinary monetary cases, a loan document found in a case can still be returned based on the identifying mark because the concern would require a very specific one-person borrowing scenario. Still, this line of reasoning is somewhat forced. It is therefore likely that Rava's policy explanation was stated only at an earlier stage and is not the final rationale. Once the Gemara concludes that ordinary identifying marks are rabbinic, their legal force rests on the authority of beit din to regulate property and on the rule of hefker beit din hefker, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 19b. In that framework, Chazal could validate identifying marks in monetary disputes even without relying on each possible owner's personal satisfaction with the rule. This better supports the unqualified rulings of Tur and the other authorities.
