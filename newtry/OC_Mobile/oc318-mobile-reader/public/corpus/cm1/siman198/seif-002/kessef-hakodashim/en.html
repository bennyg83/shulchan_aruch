Some say that lifting one handbreadth is sufficient. One bought three utensils from another, paid the money, strung them on a cord, lifted them from above the table and threw them to the floor. The seller told him to leave them there. When the buyer later returned, the seller said that they had been stolen. No handshake agreement had been made. Nevertheless the buyer acquired by lifting, even though they do not remember whether he lifted them three handbreadths, one handbreadth, or less than one handbreadth. It appears that the final opinion in siman 198:2 is the halachah, as Sema in siman 203, seif katan 19, treats the final opinion as primary, and as I wrote elsewhere. This is especially so because Rabbeinu Tam is later than Rashi, and it appears that the halachah was fixed in accordance with him; see Pri Megadim, Yoreh Deah, introduction. Even if it is uncertain whether there was a lifting of one handbreadth, possession taken with permission is effective after the doubt arose against the prior owner’s presumption. It appears that here the buyer was otherwise in a position of possession against the seller. Moreover, according to those who remove property from a possessor on the strength of the prior owner’s presumption combined with a double doubt, there is a double doubt here: perhaps the halachah is that money acquires, and perhaps he lifted a handbreadth. In addition, a person’s hand acquires even without conscious intent, like a guarded courtyard. There is, however, a decisive basis that this was a valid acquisition. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 98a offers two answers regarding moving an object downward, one of which holds that lowering does not acquire unless the object was already in his hand. But Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, in the discussion of an object that can be pulled away, resolves this dispute and rules that once the object was already in his hand he acquires even by lowering it. Accordingly, in our case there was a complete acquisition. At first glance the seller might be deemed a paid custodian because he profited from the transaction, somewhat like a craftsman. But according to Sema at the beginning of siman 306 it is clear that a seller is no better than a day laborer. Likewise, according to Shach there, his discussion does not establish paid-custodian status for a seller, and even a craftsman becomes an unpaid custodian once he says, “I have finished.” So too here, after the item had been in the buyer’s hand after acquisition, he subsequently deposited it with the seller. As to the oath issue, I arranged a compromise with their consent.
