Even through major unavoidable accidents. Tosafot at the beginning of Perek HaMafkid writes that according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that by Torah law money acquires, the rabbinic institution of meshichah was made because of the concern that a seller might say, "Your wheat burned in the attic." The Sages left the seller's acquisition of the buyer's money on its Torah basis. Thus the seller may use the money after receipt. According to Reish Lakish, however, meshichah is itself the Torah mode and the seller has no right to use the purchase money until the buyer performs meshichah. See also what I wrote in siman 66.
Come and take what is yours. The reason a custodian can tell the depositor, "Come and take your property," is that a custodian has no independent obligation to deliver the deposit into the owner's hand. The duty of affirmative restitution is written regarding a robber. A borrower must likewise repay because the borrowed money was given to be spent and the object owed at repayment is not the original object. A custodian, by contrast, merely holds the owner's own property. Wherever the deposit stands, it remains legally the owner's. Therefore, when the custodial obligation has ended, the custodian may ordinarily say, "Your property is here, come take it." Nimukei Yosef's statement that where an animal died through negligence the custodian must bring the carcass to beit din is limited to a case of negligent loss, where his liability arose through wrongdoing. If it died through unavoidable accident, he can say, "Here is yours before you." All the more, when the deposit itself remains intact, no delivery duty exists. A borrower or debtor is different because repayment itself must reach the creditor.
