One who gives, etc. Bava Batra 49a. The Rif writes in the name of a Gaon that we do not follow the first incident there, but the later report, where one of the Rabbis said that the incident never occurred. Since the seller would not accept the curse of Mi Shepara, why should he accept the money? The money therefore becomes a loan in his hands, making him liable even for unavoidable accidents, as stated in Bava Batra chapter 4 and Kiddushin chapter 2. From this the Rambam derived that the rule applies specifically when the seller retracts. But if the buyer retracts and the seller says, “Take your money,” since the seller need not accept Mi Shepara, the Gemara’s ordinary rule remains: the money is like a deposit, meaning he is an unpaid bailee. Although the Gemara says that he is not even an unpaid bailee, that follows Rava’s view in accordance with Reish Lakish, who prohibits use of the money. According to our ruling he is an unpaid bailee. See Tosafos there, s.v. “rather, etc.”
(Collected note) One who gives, etc., like a deposit, etc. Although a borrower is liable because he has already benefited, here he has not yet benefited. See Rashi, 81a, s.v. “they were lost through accident, etc.,” and s.v. “upon return, etc.” A mere prior status as buyer does not itself impose liability, but the money is at least a deposit, like a case of “take what is yours,” since at first he was permitted to spend it. See Rosh, chapter 4, siman 13. His first explanation parallels Tosafos’ first answer on 43a, s.v. “what, etc.,” while his later distinction parallels Tosafos’ second answer. See further there regarding Rabbi Yochanan. This is also the view of the Rif and the Gaon. (End of note.)

But, etc., some say, etc., and some say, etc. Maggid Mishneh. See below siman 120 seif 2. The basis of the Gaon’s ruling is Bava Metzia chapter 5: “He may contract with him against the heap, etc.” This shows that he has permission to use the money, for otherwise what benefit does he gain from receiving payment early? Since he may use it, he is like a borrower even before using it, as stated in Bava Metzia chapter 2 concerning a lost object. Although at the end of chapter 3 we rule like Rav Nachman that unavoidable loss does not impose liability in a deposit, the Rosh cites the Raavad that an ordinary bailee is afraid to use deposited money, unlike here, where the money was given to him with this understanding. Furthermore, according to Rabbi Yochanan, who says meshichah was instituted lest a seller say “your wheat burned,” the same concern would exist with money unless the seller is liable because he may use it. See Tosafos 43a, s.v. “what,” in their further answer according to Rabbi Yochanan. This avoids the strained answers there. According to Reish Lakish those answers are necessary. Rabbi Yochanan must also explain the case of payment to a barber, and Tosafos on 48a, s.v. “bath attendant,” writes that Rabbi Yochanan establishes it accordingly. We likewise rule that meshichah acquires from a non-Jew. Rava’s incident concerning sesame follows his own view that the Mishnah supports Reish Lakish, and therefore there the seller had no right to use the money. Otherwise misuse would create liability even before actual use. Since he had no right to use it, he was not even a paid bailee before retracting. Tosafos 49a notes this. But according to our ruling like Rabbi Yochanan, we do not follow that incident, certainly not according to the latter version, which says the first incident never occurred. Even after the seller says “take your money,” he remains an unpaid bailee, like any bailee told “take what is yours.” In the case of a borrower, after the loan period he is a paid bailee because he previously benefited, but here, where the seller has not yet benefited, he is only an unpaid bailee. This remains so until Mi Shepara is accepted and the seller says “take your money,” provided he has not spent it. If he spent the money, everyone agrees it is a loan in his hands. The Rif writes that although it began as a deposit, once he spent it it entered his domain and became a loan. The same is stated at the end of HaMafkid: if he did not use it, the halachah is like Rav Nachman; if he used it, it is a loan. In that case “take your money” does not suffice even after Mi Shepara, because a deposit remains in the owner’s domain, while a loan is given to be spent and belongs to the borrower until repayment. The Rif writes likewise. The dispute among the poskim from Gittin concerns only whether involuntary tender counts as repayment. We rule like the latter version that it does, and even the first version agrees regarding a loan. Tosafos proves this from the case of lending in a settled place, etc. Likewise the Tosefta in the chapter HaGozel teaches that one who encounters an unavoidable danger on the road and says to his creditor, “Take what I owe you,” is exempt if the creditor accepts, but otherwise remains liable specifically because of the danger. Without that factor, tender suffices. This explains the two opinions recorded here. Although the Maggid Mishneh and Tur appear to treat them as disputing, there may be no practical dispute where the creditor actually receives the money. The first view would also agree there. They are expressed separately only because this condition is not explicit in the earlier authorities and they may have followed the first version. According to what was explained above, however, since involuntary tender is valid, there is no practical disagreement. Until the money is actually given, all agree that the seller remains liable, certainly for theft or loss, and even for unavoidable accidents, as above. So too Bava Kamma chapter 9 and the end of Gittin chapter 1 show that even after money leaves his hand and he sends it, he remains responsible, apparently even for accidents. This differs from the earlier case, where he was not even a paid bailee, because here the money has been treated as a loan. See above siman 120 seif 2.
