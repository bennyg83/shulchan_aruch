Sema writes similarly in siman 201, seif katan 4, cited by Baer Heitev there, seif katan 3, that the Sages were concerned about this only where the seller had already received the entire purchase price. See there. Netivot HaMishpat writes, however, that with kinyan sudar and situmta, even if the money was already paid, the acquisition is effective. Since ordinarily the money is not paid at the time of these kinyanim, the Sages did not distinguish. With money alone, by contrast, even if only part of the money was paid, so that the concern that the seller might say, "Your wheat burned in the attic," does not fully apply, the purchaser still does not acquire and is entitled only to the remedy of Mi Shepara, because when the Sages uprooted acquisition by money they uprooted it entirely. He proves this from Chullin 83a, and Shulchan Aruch below, siman 199, seif 3, concerning one who gave a dinar toward an animal, where acquisition occurs only at the four designated seasons and not on other days. In my humble opinion, there is another proof from Rambam and Shulchan Aruch below, seif 15, concerning one who gave the price of movable property or part of it and then retracted. Although the words of Sema imply otherwise, the practical law follows Netivot HaMishpat. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 134, likewise writes that Sema's statement in siman 201, that the concern applies only where the seller has received the entire purchase price, might imply that if he has not received all the money, money itself acquires. In his opinion that is incorrect. Monetary acquisition is ineffective everywhere once the Sages abolished it, and because the normal mode of monetary acquisition is to pay the full amount, they made no distinction. Only with kinyan sudar, where payment is not ordinarily made at the time of the kinyan, does the kinyan remain effective even where all the money was in fact paid. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 99, likewise understands Sema as holding that where all the money was paid, even kinyan sudar and situmta are ineffective, and notes that the same view appears in Sema's Drishah to siman 201. Chatam Sofer argues at length. He writes that kinyan sudar and situmta are comparable to acquisition by hagbahah, and nowhere do we find a distinction based on whether the money was paid. Since the Sages ordained that hagbahah acquires and leaves the buyer secure in his purchase even without payment, if the purchaser voluntarily pays before or together with the kinyan, he caused his own problem; the Sages had no reason to undo the acquisition because of that. The same applies to situmta, which acquires by established commercial custom and has the legal force of an ordinary kinyan. At least according to Beit Yosef and Rama at the end of this seif, who rule that if the parties explicitly stipulated that money should acquire, it does acquire, it is clear that situmta likewise acquires even though the purchaser paid the entire amount. Even according to Shach, seif katan 10, who disagrees with that ruling, his objection concerns a stipulation to acquire by money itself, which resembles a stipulation to acquire through an ineffective mode. With situmta, however, Shach would agree. Sema's implication therefore requires study. Chatam Sofer further writes that the matter depends upon the two answers of Tosafos at the end of the chapter Chalon in Eruvin, cited by Sema and Baer Heitev at the end of this seif katan, concerning why the Sages entirely uprooted monetary acquisition rather than merely ruling that acquisition does not occur until neither concern is present. According to the answer of Rabbeinu Yitzchak, one must adopt the reasoning stated above, that the Sages do not legislate based on unusual circumstances. According to the Rivan, however, this reasoning is not accepted, and Sema was concerned for Rivan's view. Consequently, to remove money from one who presently holds it, as in the case addressed there, where the Sages ordained meshichah so that the purchaser would not need to pay first and the purchaser nevertheless paid before meshichah, it is difficult to act against Rabbeinu Yitzchak's view in Tosafos. Chatam Sofer further writes that according to Sema's implication that situmta is ineffective after the entire purchase price was paid, one might argue that even where the purchaser first performed hagbahah, as in the case addressed in that responsum, although hagbahah certainly acquires and is not undone by payment, if he afterward stamped or marked the barrel he revealed that he wished the acquisition to occur through the commercial mark and not through hagbahah. This would resemble the rule in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, where by falling upon an object one reveals his preference for that mode of acquisition. See below, siman 268, seif 1. If so, the earlier hagbahah would not acquire and the only possible acquisition would be situmta, which according to Sema would fail where the full purchase price was paid. In truth, however, this argument is incorrect in light of Nimukei Yosef there, who limits the concept of revealing one's preference to acquisition through four cubits, which is rabbinic. Although Rashi at the beginning of the chapter HaKotev appears to hold that even regarding a Torah based acquisition a person may say that he does not wish to acquire through that mechanism, in any event one cannot remove property from the current possessor on that basis.
