So that movables should not be acquired. Maharam Alshich, responsum 6, writes simply that one who acquired only by payment, where payment creates no completed acquisition but only the sanction of Mi Shepara, cannot transfer that item to another even by kinyan sudar. Perhaps he wrote this only according to those who rule like Reish Lakish that payment does not acquire by Torah law. If, however, payment acquires by Torah law, then until the first seller accepts Mi Shepara the acquisition may perhaps be a real one. This is somewhat implied by the language of the authorities at the end of siman 198 concerning responsibility for the money, although the implication may be the opposite: the seller is liable because the money was given to him for expenditure, like money of a permitted deposit, and ordinarily he will not retract and accept Mi Shepara. Once he does accept Mi Shepara, the basis of his borrowing or custodianship ceases. This requires study. Perhaps a present-tense kinyan sudar before an important beit din can transfer the item, and Maharam Alshich means only that the transfer is conditional, since the first seller may still retract subject to Mi Shepara and the item is not yet fully the buyer’s. Or perhaps until such retraction there is no acquisition at all, and only from the moment the seller refuses and accepts Mi Shepara did the Sages vest ownership in the buyer. If so, where the money has already been consumed, how can an acquisition arise retroactively? It is difficult to say that the enactment itself makes every ordinary payment operate as though made “from now.” Where the money was lost in a circumstance exempting the seller, such as where the owner was with him in his work, the question is even sharper. Likewise, when one who paid money sells to a second buyer, there is an additional basis for cancellation if the money has already been consumed, because the first buyer had no power to sell to the second until the first seller refused under Mi Shepara, and an acquisition set to take effect later cannot arise when the money is no longer extant. All this requires study, including whether, if the money was lost while the owner was with the seller in his work, the later refusal converts the money into an ordinary loan or merely ends the seller’s status as custodian. From Maharam Alshich’s reasoning there may also be a lesson regarding acquisition by money of a lien. If one says, “Give so-and-so money and such-and-such movable property shall be pledged to you,” while undertaking no personal obligation, a kinyan sudar appears capable of creating a lien directly on the movable, just as an express apotiki creates no personal liability yet the designated land or movable remains encumbered. Perhaps this is not considered conditional, because collection is ultimately regarded as retroactive, and even according to Abaye an express apotiki may be treated as collected retroactively, with the lien ceasing only from the time it is displaced. No requirement of an important beit din is mentioned for an express apotiki. This requires careful study. Rather, by lifting. If one lifts merchandise merely to inspect its quality after the price was fixed, it is comparable to the dispute regarding measuring, whether acquisition occurs when he did not intend to acquire. Indeed, this case is weaker, because in measuring he at least knows that the object he lifted belongs to the transaction, whereas here when he lifts it he does not yet know whether he will approve and choose it. One sold his fellow barrels of honey that were not at the buyer’s premises. They made no handshake agreement, only payment. Later the seller borrowed the honey from the buyer, undertaking to repay honey, because the market price of honey had been established. The Shach writes that one need not know the precise market price. The seller now wishes to retract from the original sale. It requires study whether the loan gives the sale any greater finality than Mi Shepara. The fact that there is now no object upon which retraction can operate is insufficient, because the original sale never became fully effective, and the borrowing was a pulling for the borrower’s own need rather than an act intended to complete the sale. Nor does his statement of obligation automatically create a binding obligation contrary to the law unless he said “You are my witnesses” or executed a document, as explained in siman 40. Therefore it appears clear that the borrower has no obligation to repay specifically in honey or in money at the honey’s present value. In the actual case, however, he had already repaid in honey and only afterward sought to retract. In such a case he plainly cannot retract, for if one who toiled and purchased in reliance upon the other party’s trust acquires on that basis, all the more so after he has already performed in accordance with the trust of the transaction.
