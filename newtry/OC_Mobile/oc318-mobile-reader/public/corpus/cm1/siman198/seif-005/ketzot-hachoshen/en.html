Likewise one who rents a place. Shach writes that the courtyard must first be rented to the purchaser and only afterward may the movables be transferred into it, because if courtyard and gift arrive simultaneously the courtyard cannot acquire. His view follows what he wrote in siman 202. This is difficult from the first chapter of Gittin, where a woman's courtyard and her get can arrive simultaneously, just as a slave's hand and his emancipation document can arrive simultaneously. One could distinguish that a woman's courtyard in divorce is derived from her hand, whereas an ordinary man's courtyard works through agency. Perhaps simultaneous acquisition is possible only under the hand model. Yet Bava Metzia concerning Rabban Gamliel and the elders appears to show simultaneous acquisition even through ordinary courtyard agency. Rabban Gamliel rented the place of the tithes to the recipients while transferring the tithes themselves. The Gemara asks why they need not stand beside the courtyard and answers that where another person is transferring the property, the rule is different. The transactions must be simultaneous, because the very reason there is "another person transferring" is that Rabban Gamliel is transferring both the courtyard and the tithes in the same act. Mordechai makes this particularly clear. Since the sugya there treats discretionary benefit as nonmonetary, how can Rabban Gamliel be a person transferring the tithes? Mordechai answers that because he is transferring the courtyard to them at the same time, the case qualifies as another person's transfer. That necessarily assumes courtyard and gift can arrive together. Nevertheless, Shitah Mekubetzet expressly writes like the Shach, that a courtyard acquires only if it belonged to the recipient before the movable entered. Hagahot Ashri says similarly in the case of an old wall. The question therefore remains difficult.
Likewise if they stipulated. Shach rejects the view that parties can stipulate that money should effect acquisition where money is not ordinarily a valid mode. He says a stipulation can help only in a place where deeds are customary, because there money is a valid acquisition in principle and only lack of settled intent delays completion until the deed is written. An express stipulation removes that hesitation. But where the law itself does not recognize the mode, a condition cannot invent an acquisition. In my view Beit Yosef and Rama are correct. The first chapter of Bekhorot discusses one who buys scrap from a gentile and discovers idolatry among it. The Gemara asks why meshichah is needed if money acquires from a gentile, and answers that the gentile accepted upon himself the laws of Jewish transactions. Thus even according to the view that meshichah is not normally the gentile's acquisition, a stipulation to follow Jewish law makes meshichah effective. This proves that where a mode of acquisition is legally recognized in some commercial framework, the parties can stipulate to use it in their transaction. The same should apply to money for our movable sales, since money is unquestionably an acquisition mode in other contexts, including land and, according to many authorities, transactions with gentiles. An express stipulation can therefore make it operative.
