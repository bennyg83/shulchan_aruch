With equal-value barter, however, the concern of "your wheat burned in the attic" is inapplicable in any event, because the same concern can subsequently arise against the buyer. I later found this stated in Shitah Mekubetzet. With situmta and kinyan sudar, since ordinarily the money has not yet been paid, the Sages did not distinguish, and the acquisition is effective even if the money was in fact already paid. Conversely, by money alone, even if only part of the money was paid, so that the concern of "your wheat burned in the attic" is weakened, the purchaser nevertheless acquires only to the extent of mi shepara, because once the Sages uprooted money as an acquisition they uprooted it entirely. Proof appears from Chullin 83, where one who gives a dinar toward an animal acquires only on the four special market days, but not during the rest of the year.
In a place where there is no concern of fire. At first glance this is difficult, for the Mishnah expressly says that silver does not acquire gold because silver is currency, even though the concern "your money burned in the attic" does not apply to money, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 43. In particular, each side would face the same concern of fire. Yet acquisition still does not occur, showing that the Sages did not distinguish within their enactment. There is no difficulty. According to Tosafot's second answer there, R. Yochanan holds that the Sages never uprooted the seller's d'oraisa acquisition through money. The seller therefore remains liable for accidents to the silver, which is the money, even after the enactment that money does not complete the purchaser's acquisition. Accordingly, the seller cannot say, "Your silver burned," because his original responsibility over that money was never removed. Regarding the gold, which is merchandise, if money were held to acquire it, the seller would indeed become exempt from accidents, and the concern would apply. Even according to Tosafot's first answer, that one does not speak of "your money burned" because money is guarded by placing it in the ground, that answer relates only to money received by the seller. At minimum, he is an unpaid custodian over funds that entered his house. But if money acquisition had fully transferred the wheat to the buyer, the seller would not even be an unpaid custodian over the wheat, as Beit Yosef writes at the end of this siman. The two cases are therefore not comparable.
