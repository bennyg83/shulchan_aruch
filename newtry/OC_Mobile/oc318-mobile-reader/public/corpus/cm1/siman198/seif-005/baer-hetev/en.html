One may answer that so long as the seller has not received the full value of the produce, he exerts himself to save it because he does not wish to enter litigation with the buyer. For this reason customary commercial acquisition, situmta, also works, as stated in siman 201, and we do not fear that the seller will say, "Your wheat burned." One might ask that now that the Sages ruled money does not acquire, after the buyer has paid but not yet performed meshichah the seller could say to him, if the buyer retracts, "Your money burned." That is, when the buyer retracts, the seller might say this. Ordinarily we do not suspect the seller of doing two wrongs, both retracting from the sale and allowing the buyer's money to be lost. See Shach. The answer is that money requires little effort to rescue, so the seller will certainly save it. Why then did the Sages uproot acquisition by money entirely instead of requiring meshichah together with payment? For the benefit of commerce, so that purchases can be completed quickly by meshichah alone. Alternatively, they feared that the buyer might first perform meshichah before paying, a fire might then break out in the buyer's premises, and he would not save the goods but would tell the seller, "Your wheat burned in the loft." The renter. The same applies to one who borrows the place: he acquires the movables within it. See what I wrote in siman 202. Ritva at the beginning of the ninth chapter of Bava Metzia writes that a courtyard in which a person has only a right of passage to reach a house he owns elsewhere is not considered his courtyard for purposes of acquisition. Likewise, if one lends another his courtyard only for passage, it is not a borrowed courtyard that acquires for him. A borrowed courtyard acquires only when it is lent for use; when it is lent only for walking, he has only a right to walk there. The same appears to apply to rental.
