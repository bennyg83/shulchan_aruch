The seller may delay and fail to save them. One might ask why, for this reason, a kinyan sudar should not likewise be ineffective. The answer is that so long as the seller has not received the purchase price of the produce he will save it, because he does not want to have to litigate later with the buyer to collect his money. For the same reason situmta effects acquisition, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 201, and we do not fear that the seller will say, "Your wheat burned in the upper story." One may also ask: now that the Sages ruled that money does not acquire, why should the seller not say to a buyer who paid but did not pull, "Your money burned in my upper story"? The answer is that money requires little effort to save, so the seller will certainly rescue it. Produce, by contrast, may require substantial effort to save. The Sages abolished acquisition by money entirely rather than requiring both payment and meshichah, both because of market convenience, so that goods can be acquired quickly by meshichah alone, and because otherwise the reverse danger might arise: the buyer may pull the goods before paying, a fire may then occur in the buyer's house, and he may refuse to save them and tell the seller, "Your wheat burned in the upper story." Therefore, if the house belongs to the purchaser, etc., because he is commonly present at his house, etc. The Tur gives this reason as well. His meaning is that even if the purchaser himself does not live in the house like the seller, the house belongs to him and he is commonly present there even if he rented it to another. Because he is present, he will save his own house and the produce will be saved incidentally. This differs from produce located in a place that is not his house. Even if he has already purchased the produce, he is not yet commonly present there, and not everyone knows the law. People may assume that the seller must save the goods because they remain in his possession. The Tur gives the same reason in the second case, where the seller rents the purchaser a room in the seller's house and the purchaser resides in that room. Even if the produce is not in that particular room, because the purchaser is present in the same house he will certainly take care to save it. The Mechaber, however, follows the Rambam, who speaks in the latter case of renting to the purchaser the very room in which the produce is stored. His reason is that the produce is then considered placed in the purchaser's courtyard rather than the seller's. Therefore he requires the rented place to be guarded for the purchaser or the purchaser to be standing beside it, since otherwise it lacks the law of a courtyard that acquires the contents within it. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam this way. According to this, the Mechaber should not have introduced the second case with the word "likewise," because the first and second clauses rest upon different reasons. There is no difficulty in the Rambam himself. His first clause also may not rely on the purchaser's physical presence at all, but on the fact that the house is his. Although he rented it to the seller, rental for a period is like a temporary sale, yet for this purpose it still counts as his property so that the merchandise is regarded as being in the purchaser's domain. The Maggid Mishneh explicitly explains the Rambam this way. The Mechaber, in the Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef, rejected that explanation and understood "the merchandise is in the purchaser's domain" to mean only that the purchaser is commonly present there and will save it with the house. That is strained because it leaves the two clauses on different rationales although the Rambam connects them with "likewise." The Beit Yosef also cites the Maggid Mishneh's requirement that the place be guarded for the purchaser or that he stand beside it while discussing the Tur, even though that requirement belongs specifically to the Rambam's acquisition through the purchaser's courtyard. According to the Rosh and Tur, where the reason is merely that the purchaser is present in the house and will save the produce, there is no need for the rented place to be guarded specifically for the purchaser. See siman 200. The Ir Shushan combines the two approaches and thereby confuses the matter. Examine this carefully.
