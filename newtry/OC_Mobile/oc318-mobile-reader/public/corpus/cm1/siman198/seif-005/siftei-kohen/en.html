The seller may delay, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 7, through his question. The intent is that when the purchaser retracts, the seller may say this, for we ordinarily do not suspect that the seller will do two things to his own detriment, both retract from the sale and lose his money. We must therefore say that the case concerns retraction by the purchaser. In that event the seller is exempt from unavoidable loss even though he did not say, 'Take your money.' If he did say, 'Take your money,' he is exempt even from theft and loss, as stated at the end of this siman. Only when the seller retracts does the Sema write that he remains liable for unavoidable loss. Examine this carefully. This is the corrected text of the Sema printed at the end of the volume in the table of errata.
Therefore, if the purchaser's house, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 9, who writes that according to this explanation it is difficult why the Mechaber introduced the latter law with the words, 'and likewise one who rents the place,' since the first and second clauses do not rest upon the same reason. This is no difficulty. The meaning is that likewise in such a case we find that although he did not perform meshichah, he acquired. The Rambam too must be understood this way. His intent in the first clause cannot be, as the Maggid Mishneh sought to explain, that the purchaser's courtyard acquires for him. If so, why did the Gemara need the reason that if a fire breaks out the purchaser will exert himself and save the goods? It should have said simply that his courtyard acquires for him. Moreover, why would payment be necessary? Even without payment the courtyard should acquire. The Rambam himself writes in the first clause that because the article is in the purchaser's domain, once he pays the price the sale is acquired. Further, since the seller rented the place, it is now like the seller's courtyard, for a tenant's courtyard acquires for the tenant even though the underlying property belongs to the landlord. The Semag likewise writes that when the purchaser's house containing the article is rented to the seller, the Sages did not require meshichah because the article is in the purchaser's domain; if a fire breaks out he will exert himself to save it, and once he pays the price the sale is acquired. He then adds that one who rents the place in which the movable property lies acquires it, as taught in Perek HaSefinah. Thus, in the first clause he gives the reason that the purchaser will save the goods, while in the second he gives the reason that he rented their location, as taught explicitly in Perek HaSefinah. The Maggid Mishneh himself later retracted and wrote that the Rambam did not state his earlier explanation explicitly. The Sema further writes that according to the Rosh and Tur the courtyard need not be guarded except with the seller's awareness. I do not understand his difficulty, because on this matter the Mechaber follows the Rambam, as below at the beginning of siman 200. A stronger question would have been that according to the reason that the purchaser lives in the house and will certainly save the produce, the area need not be guarded at all, even with the seller's awareness, according to every view. We are not relying upon acquisition by courtyard, but upon the fact that he will exert himself to save the goods. Perhaps this is the Sema's intent, and his statement concerning the Rosh and Tur is a separate point. See Tosafot Yom Tov, Perek HaZahav.
And likewise one who rents the place, etc. One who borrows the place likewise acquires the movable property within it. See what I wrote below at the beginning of siman 202. The Ritva writes at the beginning of Bava Metzia, regarding the Gemara's statement that the Torah gave him the right only to walk there and gather peah but did not make it his courtyard, that a courtyard in which a person has only a right of passage to reach a house elsewhere is not his courtyard for purposes of acquisition. One who lends another his courtyard solely for passage has not lent him a courtyard that acquires for him; a borrowed courtyard acquires only when it is lent for use, whereas permission merely to walk through grants only passage. It appears that the same rule applies to rental. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 10.

A courtyard guarded with the tenant's awareness, etc. According to the dissenting opinion below at the beginning of siman 200, it likewise suffices if the courtyard is guarded with the seller's awareness. This is also explicit in the Beit Yosef here and is straightforward. It is therefore difficult that the Rama rules here without qualification like the Mechaber, yet in siman 200 he decides like the dissenting opinion. This requires study.
The movable property in the place, etc. The Beit Yosef writes at the beginning of this siman that this limitation does not appear from the words of the poskim. This is also correct in principle, because the Sages did not make their enactments dependent upon variable measurements. The Bach adds the same conclusion, and it appears to me to be the primary ruling. This is also implied by the Rambam's words as copied by the Mechaber.
And likewise, if they stipulated explicitly, etc. This ruling does not appear correct to me. The Beit Yosef derived it from siman 190, seif 8, but the cases are not comparable. There, money or a document effects acquisition by law; only in a place where a deed is normally written does the buyer lack final reliance, and when he expressly relies upon the money, it therefore acquires. Here, however, since the Sages enacted that money does not acquire movable property, they made no distinction. Proof may be brought from the ruling in accordance with Rabbi Yehuda in Ketubot 56a, that even in a monetary matter a condition contrary to a rabbinic enactment is void, for the Sages strengthened their enactments even more than Torah law. Indeed, even Rabbi Meir would agree here. A person can stipulate concerning a monetary right only where waiver is applicable, so that his condition is equivalent to relinquishing the right. He plainly cannot stipulate that something which is not an act of acquisition shall become one. Tosafot write similarly regarding inheritance: one cannot declare that a person who is not an heir shall inherit. Careful examination shows that in the present case no authority would uphold the condition. This is why all the poskim state without qualification that money does not acquire movable property. I later found the Ritva state this expressly in Kiddushin 32b: wherever the Sages said that a particular mode does not acquire, even if seller and buyer expressly agree that it shall acquire, it does not, just as a bare verbal declaration cannot create an acquisition. The Gemara's principle that an express stipulation is effective applies only to money for land, where money is inherently a valid acquisition but, in places where a deed is written, the parties ordinarily do not rely upon it. If they expressly rely upon the money, it acquires according to its law. See there.
