See Sema, seif katan 19, who explains that the case must be one where it is visibly clear that the expenditure exceeded the appreciation, and therefore the improver takes the smaller amount, namely the appreciation. I find this difficult. If the expenditure is visibly greater than the appreciation, why does Mechaber emphasize that the improver's hand is on top? Even where his hand is on the lower position, he always receives expenses up to the amount of appreciation without an oath. Moreover, if the expenditure is visibly greater, why limit him to the appreciation merely because that is the smaller amount? He should at least be able to recover the demonstrably lower bound of the actual expense. In my view the rule is different. A planter or sharecropper ordinarily first recovers the expenditure and then shares the net appreciation according to the local arrangement, often one half. Suppose the improver says, "I do not wish to claim my expenses at all. Appraise only the total appreciation as though no expenditure were deducted, and give me my share of that appreciation." In that case he need not swear to the amount of his expenses, because he is not claiming them. This is why Mechaber had to say that his hand is on top. If his hand were on the lower position, his right would ordinarily be limited to reimbursement of expenses up to the amount of appreciation, and to collect those expenses he would need to establish them.
