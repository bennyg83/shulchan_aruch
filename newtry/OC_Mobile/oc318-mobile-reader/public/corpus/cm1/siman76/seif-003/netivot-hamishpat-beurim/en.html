He is exempt even from the duty to satisfy Heaven. See Sema and Shach, who give strained explanations distinguishing this case from seif 1. In my view the reason is simple. Siman 222, seif 4, in Rama explains that if one says, "I sold a field but do not know to whom," and one person comes and says, "I am the purchaser," he is believed, because a person would not brazenly assert such a claim. But if one sold a field and does not know whether it was the larger or smaller one, and a person says, "I purchased the larger one," he is not believed, as explained in siman 223, seif 5. The reason must be that since he certainly purchased something from this seller, he is willing to brazenly claim the larger one. Accordingly, the reason here is straightforward. Where he borrowed from only one of them, his failure to remember was not serious negligence, because he had no reason to fear that someone would falsely say, "I am the lender," since a person generally would not brazenly make such a claim. He could give the money to whoever asserted a definite claim, as in siman 222, seif 5. He can therefore say to each claimant, "Even if the truth is with you, your own forgetfulness caused your loss, because had you remembered and made a definite claim, you would have collected even if the other claimant remained uncertain." This follows both from the principle just stated and from the rule that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one, as explained above. Thus the depositor's or lender's negligence caused the loss more directly than the guardian's negligence, and the guardian is exempt. In seif 1, however, he borrowed from two people. There the guardian was significantly negligent, because he should have feared that one might claim, "I deposited or lent the larger bundle," since a person can brazenly make that sort of claim. Therefore he is liable. If both claimants make definite claims, the depositor was not negligent at all, so the guardian is liable for his own negligence even though it was not gross negligence. See also siman 365, Shach seif katan 5, who writes similarly that when a robber is before us he is likewise exempt from the duty to satisfy Heaven. In my view, however, a robber is liable to satisfy Heaven in every case, because a robber must, in fulfillment of his heavenly duty, return the stolen item specifically into the victim's hand. Bava Kamma 103 states that to satisfy Heaven he must give to each claimant so that the stolen property actually reaches its owner, since in the case of robbery he must follow the victim even to Media in order that return reach him. Only when the victim acknowledges the debt is the robber exempt, because it is as though the victim says, "Leave it in your hands for me." The Gemara compares this to one who robbed one of five, where to satisfy Heaven he must give to each so that actual return reaches the victim. Therefore the robber is liable to satisfy Heaven even if no one claims from him, and even when the robbery occurred in their presence, just as he must follow the victim to Media. In such a case it cannot be said that the victim effectively agreed to leave the property in the robber's hands.
According to the requirements of Heaven. See Shach, seif katan 17, who writes that if he says, "The father of one of you lent me one hundred, but I do not know which," he is liable to satisfy Heaven. See also siman 300, Sema seif katan 12, who calls this negligence. I do not know the basis for calling it negligence, for how was the borrower supposed to know at the time of the deposit that the depositor would die? Rather, the principal reason that he is exempt here when they make no claim is as I explained above. The guardian can say to the depositor, "You were more negligent by forgetting, and your forgetfulness caused your loss, because had you remembered and made a definite claim you would have collected." Where he says, "The father of one of you deposited with me," there was no negligence by the depositor himself. The Rosh explicitly gives the reason that the depositor should have been precise. For this reason, in the case of two separate bundles above, the guardian is liable to satisfy Heaven even if they do not claim from him. There too the depositor was not negligent, because he remembers the deposit and had no reason to remember its exact amount after handing over a sealed bundle, assuming that the same bundle would be returned to him, as the Rosh expressly writes.
