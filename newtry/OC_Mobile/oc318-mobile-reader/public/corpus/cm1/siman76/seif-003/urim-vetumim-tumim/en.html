And some say that to fulfill one's duty toward Heaven, and so forth. Shach asks from siman 300, where Rama appears to follow the first opinion that even the heavenly obligation can be absent. The cases can be distinguished by custodial responsibility. When a deposit is obstructed from return because another person falsely claims it and beit din cannot identify the owner, the custodian has suffered something akin to an unavoidable armed intervention. He cannot safely return the object because a competing claimant has legally blocked him. If the custodian had no reason to anticipate that dispute, the event is an ones. An unpaid or paid custodian can be exempt from appropriate categories of ones, and therefore may be exempt even toward Heaven. A borrower or debtor is different. He remains liable for the money despite unavoidable events until repayment actually occurs. Thus even if another claimant's false assertion makes return practically impossible, that external obstacle does not extinguish the debt itself. That is why in a loan there can remain a heavenly duty to satisfy the true claimant, while in a deposit the custodian can be fully exempt. This also helps explain the Gemara's comparison to the case of a purchaser who does not know from whom he bought. There the buyer remains responsible for the purchase money until it reaches the proper seller. His monetary debt is not extinguished by the confusion. A shepherd or custodian can sometimes invoke ones, but where he should have kept track of the owners the event is no longer considered unavoidable. That is the force of the Gemara's statement that he "should have been precise." Once negligence replaces ones, the deposit case returns to the same practical result as the purchase-money case. The distinction therefore depends on whether the confusion itself was avoidable and on the underlying level of responsibility.
