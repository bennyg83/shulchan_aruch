And when they come to claim from him. The principal meaning of "claim" throughout this siman depends on whether the claimant speaks with certainty or doubt. A definite assertion is called a claim; an uncertain assertion is considered as though he did not claim. Shach:
To give. The Sema writes: although in siman 300 the Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule that when two people deposited with him at one time, even without a document and even if they make a claim against him, he is obligated only if he wishes to satisfy the laws of Heaven, while if they do not make a claim he is exempt even under the laws of Heaven, a loan is different. Money lent is given to be spent, so he should have paid close attention, particularly since they informed him. With a deposit, even if they informed him, the custodian can say that he did not pay close attention because a deposit is not given to be spent; he assumed that each person would later take back what he had placed, and the bundles became mixed without his knowledge. The Shach writes that one must distinguish this way according to the Tur, but asks what source there is for it, and leaves the matter requiring study. See the Beurim for my resolution. This applies specifically where he certainly owes one of them. But regarding a document whose nature he no longer knows, discussed above in siman 65, meaning that he forgot whether the lender or the borrower entrusted it to him, since he is exempt toward the borrower, then even if it was deposited with him and he was negligent he is also exempt toward the lender. The same law applies where a Jew and a non Jew deposited with him: since he is exempt toward the non Jew because the laws of custodians do not apply to a non Jew, he is also exempt toward the Jew. But with a loan where doubt arose between a Jew and a non Jew, he is liable to the Jew even though he is exempt toward the non Jew because one may retain a loan owed to a non Jew:
That he swear while holding a sacred object. We do not say that the borrower is one who admits part, since he admits owing one hundred to each claimant but does not know concerning the other hundred, which would ordinarily require payment without an oath. Since he knows with certainty that he does not owe both of them more than two hundred in total, he is considered to have a definite claim as to that point. Shach. See the Beurim, where I explain that even if he makes a definite assertion against one of them and says, "You are the owner of the hundred," he need not swear against that person. It follows that when he is uncertain, the rule that one who cannot swear must pay does not apply:

It remains deposited. This is because each claimant makes a definite claim and one of them is necessarily fraudulent, so the money is not divided unless both agree. If each says, "I know about one hundred and do not know about the other hundred," he gives one hundred to each and thereby satisfies even his obligation under the laws of Heaven. See below, siman 300, in the gloss at the end of seif 1. The third hundred remains deposited because each claims part of it and there is concern for fraud. But if neither makes a claim at all, we say that the third hundred should be divided, since there is no fraudulent claimant. See the Beurim. This rule of division applies specifically to a deposit or stolen property that is physically present. With a loan and similar obligations, neither claimant can demand anything, for the debtor can repel each one individually unless they come with authorization from one another. The Shach further writes that many authorities hold that the money should remain with him and that he must swear that he does not know, even if he wishes to place it with beit din. See the Beurim. Regarding a loan, all agree that it remains with him. The Shach also writes that whether he is obligated under the laws of Heaven depends on the dispute in siman 300, end of seif 2. See the Beurim, where I explain that here all agree he is exempt:
