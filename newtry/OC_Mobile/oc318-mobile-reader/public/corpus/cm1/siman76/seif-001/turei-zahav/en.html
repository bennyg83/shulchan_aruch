(At the end of seif 1 in the Shulchan Aruch.) "And there is an opinion that with an oral loan," etc. This is the Rosh's position as reflected in the Tur. He holds that with an oral loan, even if the lender does not presently demand payment, the borrower remains obligated to satisfy his duty toward Heaven. This excludes Ramban's view cited by the Tur, who holds that even here the borrower is exempt from the obligation toward Heaven. Although the Tur cites Ramban in the case where someone borrowed from one person but does not know which person lent to him, the same reasoning applies. Baal HaTerumot explains Ramban's position: there is an obligation toward Heaven only where the defendant bears a degree of wrongdoing, such as theft or negligence where the custodian alone was negligent and not the depositor. Where there is no negligence attributable solely to the custodian or borrower, there is no such obligation. Accordingly, in the case where two people lent to him at the same time, one one hundred and the other two hundred, the lenders themselves were also negligent, and therefore the borrower is exempt even from his duty toward Heaven. Thus there are three positions in the Tur. The first opinion holds that in every such case there is an obligation toward Heaven. Ramban exempts in every such case. The Rosh distinguishes: with an oral loan there is an obligation toward Heaven, while with a written document there is not. The Sema does not explain it this way. I have written what appears correct to me, and I saw that my father-in-law, of blessed memory, understood it similarly.
