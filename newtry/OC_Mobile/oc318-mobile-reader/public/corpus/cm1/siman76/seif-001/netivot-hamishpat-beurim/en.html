Introduction.
Before explaining this siman, I will copy the Gemara in Bava Metzia 37 that pertains to it and resolve what appears difficult to me there. The Mishnah states: one who says to two people, "I robbed one of you of one hundred and I do not know which one," or, "The father of one of you deposited one hundred with me and I do not know whose father," and so forth. The Gemara infers that we do not extract money because of uncertainty, and challenges this from the case of two people who deposited money with one custodian. The Gemara answers, "Are you comparing robbery to a deposit?" Study the sugya carefully. All the commentators ask what the Gemara means, since the first clause itself also mentions a deposit. Perhaps the challenge was really from one deposit case to another. Why did the Gemara not simply ask what distinguishes the first clause from the later clause? Tosafot, s.v. Dekatani alah, also ask why the Gemara could not have challenged the matter directly from the baraita itself.
The matter can be explained through Bava Kamma 103. There the Mishnah teaches that one who robbed another of a perutah and swore falsely must follow the victim even to Media to return it. The Gemara asks whether the Mishnah follows Rabbi Tarfon or Rabbi Akiva, because in the case of one who robbed one of five people, Rabbi Akiva requires payment to each claimant. The Gemara initially reasons that Rabbi Akiva should require even one who did not swear to pursue the victim, and later distinguishes the cases. At first glance, the comparison is difficult. Rabbi Akiva's requirement to pay each of five claimants cannot simply arise from the command to return the stolen object specifically into the victim's hand, because in cases of a deposit or sale, where there is certainly no obligation to travel to Media, Rabbi Akiva nevertheless requires payment to each claimant. Rather, he appears to hold that money can be extracted because of doubt when the other party makes a definite claim, as indicated in Bava Metzia 37 concerning the house that fell upon a person and his mother. If so, why does Bava Kamma equate the obligation to pay each of five claimants with the obligation to travel to Media, where the rationale of extracting money from doubt does not apply? Likewise, the proof from the incident with the pious man, that even without an oath one must travel to Media, is difficult because in an ordinary sale one plainly need not travel there.
The answer is that robbery and deposit differ from a loan. With stolen or deposited property, the custodian can fulfill restitution by saying, "Here is yours before you," even where the owner cannot presently take physical possession. Proof appears in Bava Kamma 45 concerning an ox that was returned before or after sentence was entered. The Gemara places the ox in the custody of beit din, and Tosafot explain that even while it is held by beit din, the custodian can say, "Here is yours before you." Beit din certainly would not release the ox before sentencing, lest its owner hide it, and after sentencing they would not release it because the mitzvah of stoning must be carried out. Nevertheless, legal restitution can be satisfied. This does not mean that a custodian whose deposit was later stolen by a robber may simply point to the robber and say, "Here is yours." Tosafot explain that with a tam ox that caused damage, the prior owner cannot say this where the object has already entered the injured party's legal domain. Likewise, once the article is in a robber's possession, the original custodian no longer has it within his legal control. We rule that where an object was stolen and the owner did not despair, neither the owner nor the robber can consecrate it, one because it is not his and the other because it is not in his possession. In addition, restoration must at least be made into the custody of beit din, as indicated in Bava Kamma 98 regarding one who pushes another's coin into the sea. Thus, the significant point is that an object placed under beit din's control can satisfy the return obligation even if the owner cannot immediately take it.
This resolves Bava Kamma 103. In a loan or purchase, the borrower cannot merely say, "Here is yours," because the money currently belongs to the borrower until it is transferred to and acquired by the creditor. Therefore, where the creditor asserts a definite claim, one can speak of extracting money because of doubt. In robbery or deposit, however, once the object is restored to beit din and the custodian says, "Here is yours before you," his obligation is definitively discharged. There is no remaining uncertain personal debt from which money is being extracted. Rabbi Akiva's requirement that a robber pay each claimant therefore shows something different: the Sages penalized the robber and required actual restoration into the victim's own hand, even at substantial expense. That is why the Gemara can compare the case to the duty to travel to Media. When the Gemara later limits Rabbi Akiva's ruling to one who swore, the anonymous formulation of the Mishnah concerning one who robbed one of five still requires analysis where the stolen object no longer exists. In that situation the liability has become a monetary debt and "Here is yours" is no longer possible. If Rabbi Akiva held that money is extracted from doubt whenever the claimant is definite, he should require payment to each claimant even without an oath. It follows that Rabbi Akiva ordinarily does not extract money from doubt. The Gemara can therefore challenge him from Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar and the incident with the pious man. Since the Mishnah groups together cases in which the stolen object exists and cases in which it does not, if Rabbi Akiva requires payment to each even where the object exists, the reason must be the special penalty requiring restoration into the victim's hand, and that naturally raises the question why he should not also require travel to Media.
This also resolves the Bava Metzia sugya. At first the Gemara could not challenge one deposit case from another. Shach in siman 76, seif katan 7, explains that where both claimants make definite claims the disputed deposit remains deposited, whereas where both are uncertain the money is divided. Since a deposited object can be returned by saying, "Here is yours before you," the later clause, where both claimants are definite and the object is placed with beit din, does not involve extracting money from doubt. The custodian has discharged his duty. The first clause, however, where he says, "The father of one of you deposited with me," is a case of uncertainty on all sides and the law is division. Property subject to genuine doubt is treated as though both claimants are in possession, as Tosafot explain in Bava Metzia 97, and even the Sages who disagree with Sumchus concede this where no party has an established monetary chazakah. In such a case, placing the property before them and saying, "Here is yours," would itself transfer it into the joint legal possession of both claimants, and the custodian is not discharged merely by that declaration. The Gemara therefore initially challenges specifically from robbery, because the wording "I robbed one of you" implies that the claimants themselves assert certainty, unlike the later formulation referring to their father. In a definite claim case the normal rule would be that the property remains deposited, so if the robber is nevertheless not discharged by saying, "Here is yours," the Gemara may infer that money is being extracted from doubt. Once the Gemara answers that robbery was specially penalized, the questioner accepts the ordinary principle that money is not extracted from doubt and can then ask directly from one deposit case to the other. Tosafot's question from the baraita itself is also resolved, because the baraita could have been understood as speaking where the two claimants themselves were uncertain, while the Mishnah's language implies definite claims. This concludes the introduction.
He must give. See Shach, seif katan 3, who asks how Tur derived this from the Rosh. In my view it can be demonstrated from the Rosh's statement that even a shepherd in one city must take care to know whose animal he received because he is obligated to return each animal to its owner. At first glance, why should a shepherd have a greater duty to identify the owner than any other custodian? Tumim distinguishes a paid shepherd from an unpaid custodian, but neither the poskim nor the Rosh's language supports that distinction. Rather, an ordinary custodian need not personally deliver the object into the depositor's hand. He can place it before him and say, "Here is yours." If two people deposited separately, the custodian must at least remember who the depositors were, because otherwise the property could leave his control improperly. If he later says, "Someone deposited with me and I do not know who," an outsider might falsely claim it and receive it, as explained in siman 222, seif 4, in the gloss. Moreover, if he forgets the depositor's identity, even when the true depositor appears he cannot safely say, "Here is yours," because he does not know that this is the person. That failure is negligence in the very obligation of return, and he is liable. But when two people deposited together, one one hundred and one two hundred, and he remembers both depositors but not which owned the two hundred, he can place the money before both and say, "Here is yours," and has not been negligent in the core duty of return. A shepherd is different because local practice requires him to deliver each animal specifically to its owner. This is indicated by the law of animals normally entrusted to a shepherd, which can be subject to chazakah only because the shepherd is expected to return them. Since the shepherd must restore the animal to its particular owner, he must keep track of that owner's identity. This explains the Rosh's precise phrase that the shepherd must know whose animal it is "in order to return it to the owners." It follows that wherever the custodian must restore specifically into the owner's hand, even where the claimants dealt with him in one another's presence, he is liable for failing to distinguish them. All the more so with a loan, where saying "Here is yours" does not discharge the debt. Tur and the Shulchan Aruch therefore correctly rule that he is liable even if the two loans were made in one another's presence, unless they were embodied in one document for the reason Rama gives.
This does not create a difficulty from siman 65, concerning one who finds a promissory note and does not know whether it was deposited with him by the lender or by the borrower, where the note remains deposited. Even Rambam, who imposes liability for negligence concerning documents in siman 301, would not require payment to both. If the borrower gave him the paid note, the custodian owes the borrower nothing merely by having held it, because negligence concerning a paid note does not create the same claim and even returning a paid note to the lender may not create liability as explained in siman 54. His possible obligation exists only toward the lender, if the lender was the depositor. He therefore says in effect, "I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you," and is exempt. Nor should one infer that if one person borrowed from one of two people and does not know which, and afterward one claimant gave or bequeathed his possible claim to the other, the borrower becomes exempt. Once the doubt arose while he was certainly obligated to one of them, the later transfer does not transform the case into uncertainty about whether the original obligation existed. One may, however, infer a distinction where the uncertainty is between a Jew and a non Jew. If the law permits the debtor to withhold the non Jew's loan, and ordinary custodial liability toward a non Jew is absent, then regarding a deposit the debtor may be exempt even toward the Jew because he can say, "I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you." With a loan there is room to distinguish, because the debt to the non Jew still exists until actually withheld, so the borrower may still be viewed as certainly liable to one of the two. But with a deposit, exemption appears correct.
After each one swears. See Shach, seif katan 5, whose wording implies that if one claimant definitely says, "I lent you two hundred," and the debtor definitely answers, "You lent me only one hundred and your fellow lent me two hundred," the debtor would be obligated to take a Torah oath against the first claimant because he admits part of that claimant's demand. If so, when the debtor instead says, "I do not know whether the two hundred belonged to you or to the other claimant," he cannot take the oath. Why would this not be the rule of one who must swear but cannot swear and therefore pays? Shach's suggestion that a definite answer is required against that claimant is difficult, since inability to swear can impose payment even where the defendant otherwise makes a definite claim, as explained in siman 75. In my view, even with a definite answer there is no Torah oath. A complete partial admission would ordinarily require an oath notwithstanding the chazakah that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor, because we assume he is temporarily evading payment, as Tosafot explain in Bava Metzia 3. Here, however, the denial is not for the defendant's own benefit. He says that the other claimant is the owner of the additional hundred. Both claimants themselves agree that only three hundred total was lent and merely dispute ownership of the larger portion. The rationale of temporary evasion does not fit, and there is also the chazakah that a person does not sin for no personal benefit. His statement resembles that of a neutral third party testifying for another. Therefore no Torah oath is required even on a definite answer, and when he says that he does not know, the rule of one who cannot swear does not arise.
He gives one hundred to this one and one hundred to that one. See Shach, seif katan 6. His word "we penalize him" is not precise. The obligation is by law, from the verse requiring return to the person to whom it belongs. Bava Kamma 105 states that after a false oath there is no distinction between purchase and robbery for this purpose, and even if the victim does not demand the money the wrongdoer has no atonement until the property is actually restored into the victim's hand. This is a Torah obligation, not merely a penalty.
And the remainder shall remain deposited. See Shach, seif katan 7, who writes that if neither claimant demands it, the third hundred is divided, and the Mechaber similarly rules later in siman 365. This is difficult from Bechorot 49. There, one who gave redemption money to two kohanim and one kohen died cannot simply let the surviving kohen collect part of the money unless he comes with authorization, because the debtor can defer him. Why is that not like saying, "One of you deposited one hundred with me and I do not know which," where the claimants divide? Likewise in Bava Batra 127, where it is uncertain which of two sons is the firstborn, they cannot collect the double portion without authorization. Why is there no division? One might first ask how ordinary heirs can defer the possible firstborn at all, since they are not more in possession of the firstborn share than he is. The answer is that the Torah calls the double portion a gift. The heirs have a personal obligation to give that gift to the firstborn, and according to one view the firstborn acquires nothing before division. Even according to the view that he has a right before division, it arises when he seeks to acquire it, as Rashbam explains. The underlying structure is therefore a personal obligation on the heirs, and a possible claimant can be deferred. But the original difficulty remains: why should the possible firstborn not at least extract one half of the disputed amount, and why in siman 49, where a receipt is found against a note held by one of two people with the same name, are both notes treated as paid unless they come jointly with authorization? At the least the disputed money might be removed from the debtor and held by beit din until Eliyahu comes. The distinction is that division or continued deposit applies specifically to an existing deposit or stolen object. Such property legally remains in the owner's domain, and once the holder places it before the claimants and says, "Here is yours," his personal obligation ends. The remaining dispute is over the object itself, so we can apply the rules of division or continued deposit. A debt is different. The loan money became the borrower's property, and only a personal obligation remains. The same was true of the five selaim in Bechorot, which the kohen was free to spend, and the firstborn share is likewise a personal obligation placed on the heirs. Each possible claimant can therefore be deferred because neither has established that the debtor's personal obligation runs to him. There is an additional reason. With an existing deposit, once the custodian places it before them and says, "Here is yours," he has fulfilled restitution even if later division means the true owner does not receive the full amount. With a loan, if the disputed debt were divided, the borrower would still owe the true creditor the missing half, because he is not discharged until the actual creditor receives full payment. If later witnesses identify the true creditor, the borrower would have to pay the half mistakenly given to the other person. He can therefore say to each one, "Perhaps the other claimant is correct, so I cannot safely pay you anything." Also, an existing deposit may be regarded as held on behalf of both possible owners, so that they are each treated as in possession and division becomes possible. A mere personal debt cannot be held in that fashion. Although division can sometimes occur where no third party holds the object, such as certain found property cases in Bava Batra, that rests on derara demamona, an objective doubt arising before beit din independently of the parties' claims. That feature is absent here. Shach's application of division to the loan case is therefore difficult. The Gemara's question in Bava Metzia regarding the shopkeeper's ledger does not undermine this. It asks why money should leave the employer's possession at all. Even if the workers and shopkeeper later join by authorization or consent, the issue remains whether they have enough force to extract the money from the employer so that it can be deposited pending resolution. It shall remain deposited. See Shach, seif katan 8, who discusses whether the borrower may choose to deposit one hundred. In my view, with a loan everyone agrees that the money remains with the borrower rather than with beit din. In robbery or deposit, handing the object to beit din can end the holder's personal responsibility, even if the object is later lost while held by beit din, as Rashi explains in Bava Metzia 37 concerning Rabbi Tarfon. A borrower is different. If money deposited with beit din is lost and later the true creditor is identified, the borrower is not discharged. Beit din therefore cannot compel him to surrender the money before the claimants agree, and he may defer each claimant until they come with authorization, as explained above. If one claimant is definite and the other uncertain, then where the loans or deposits were separate the debtor must certainly pay the definite claimant. As to the uncertain claimant, it appears that he is not liable even to satisfy the Heavenly obligation, as will be explained shortly. If both claims were embodied in one document, it appears that a definite claimant is believed, not only where he makes his claim before the custodian admits uncertainty, which resembles siman 222, seif 4, where a person would not brazenly make a false definite claim for fear the custodian will contradict him, but even where the custodian first says he is uncertain and only afterward one claimant asserts certainty. Although the latter claimant no longer fears contradiction from the custodian, the principle that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one applies. When property is held by a custodian, the custodian's possession does not create the ordinary chazakah of money, as shown in siman 292, seif 10, regarding rings. For the same reason nothing need be paid even to satisfy a Heavenly obligation to the claimant who admits uncertainty. This applies when both possible owners are present, one definite and one uncertain. If only one appears and asserts certainty, the custodian may not pay him, because the absent claimant may also assert certainty. Likewise, if one claimant died and his heirs are uncertain, the definite claimant is not paid because beit din pleads the possible definite claim on behalf of the heirs, and the property remains deposited. Sema in siman 300, seif katan 3, explains exemption where neither claimant demands by saying that the custodian can claim, "I originally knew which one owned the two hundred and later forgot, and the proof is that they too forgot." That reason is not exclusive. Even where he says, "The father of one of you deposited with me," so that this rationale does not apply, he is not civilly liable. The basic reason is that when all sides are uncertain, we do not extract money from doubt, as explained in Bava Metzia 37.
