He must give, and so forth. Sema explains that although Rosh holds that two people who jointly deposit property can sometimes be treated like one bundle, a loan is different because loan money is given for expenditure. Rosh agrees that the borrower must distinguish the lenders' claims. Shach asks what basis Tur had for attributing that distinction to Rosh. Rosh's own explanation of the shepherd case supplies it. The Gemara says that where two people deposit animals with a shepherd, the shepherd cannot simply place the disputed animal between them and withdraw. Rosh explains that even if the animals were delivered together, the shepherd was obligated to know which animal belonged to whom because identifying and returning animals is part of his paid work. This differs from a gratuitous deposit. Baal HaTerumot explains that an unpaid custodian does the owner a favor merely by accepting the deposit. If the owners placed their property together without distinguishing it, they cannot demand that he undertake additional investigation beyond what they themselves required. A paid shepherd is different. Guarding and identifying the property is his livelihood and responsibility. A borrower is more like the shepherd than the gratuitous custodian. He receives the benefit of the loan and cannot say he was merely doing the lenders a favor by accepting their money. Therefore he must keep adequate track of whose money is whose. This distinction is fully consistent with Rosh, and Tur's formulation is well founded.
After each one swears. Why do we not say that the debtor admits one hundred to each claimant and is uncertain about the second hundred claimed by each, so that he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and they should collect without oath? Ramban, Maggid Mishneh, and Nimukei Yosef answer that the debtor knows with certainty that he owes no more than three hundred in total, and the money is before them. His uncertainty is only about allocation. Still, the opposite question bothers me. Suppose one claimant alone says, "You owe me two hundred," and the defendant says, "I know about one hundred, and I do not know about the other hundred." If he tenders the admitted hundred as heilech, the Torah partial-admission oath can disappear, and the uncertain second hundred need not be paid. Why then, when two claimants each demand two hundred and the debtor tenders one hundred to each, must he pay the additional uncertain amount? The fact that he knows he owes another hundred to someone does not itself tell us he owes it to this claimant. Rashba asks this very question. The answer is that in the ordinary one-claimant case, exempting the doubtful hundred may cause no definite injustice because perhaps it was never owed. Here, however, the debtor knows absolutely that one of the claimants is owed the disputed amount. If we exempt him from paying either one, we know with certainty that someone is being deprived of money truly owed. Yet if we choose only one claimant, we have no basis to identify which one. The legal solution is therefore to satisfy both definite external claims under the special rule governing this uncertainty, with each claimant taking the required oath. That is the distinction intended by the early authorities and Tosafot.
It shall remain deposited. Shach understands that beit din becomes the custodian of the disputed balance. The Tosefta supports him: the debtor gives each claimant the undisputed hundred, while the remainder is not given to either until they compromise. There is further proof from the enactment for a robber seeking repentance. Where returning the stolen amount to the true owner is uncertain, Chazal sometimes allow the robber to place the property aside rather than incur multiplied repayment. If "leave it" meant the robber simply keeps the stolen property in his own possession indefinitely, what repentance enactment has been achieved? The stolen property remains with the robber exactly as before. The enactment makes sense when he removes the property from his own control and places it with beit din. That supports Shach. On the other hand, the beginning of Bava Metzia has language implying money is actually removed from the possessor, but the textual traditions differ. The best distinction follows Ritva. A deposit remains legally in the depositor's domain wherever it is. If the exact deposited object is still intact and the custodian cannot determine which claimant owns it, there is little reason to burden beit din with physical custody. The custodian can continue to hold it safely until the claimants resolve the matter. A loan, sale proceeds, or consumed stolen property is different. The debtor or taker holds money that legally belongs to himself until repayment. Merely leaving it in his own pocket does not constitute return. To discharge his obligation, he should place the disputed amount with beit din. This also explains the Talmudic contrasts. Accordingly, for an intact deposit the disputed property can remain with the custodian. For a loan, sale debt, or similar monetary obligation, the disputed amount should be deposited with beit din until compromise or clarification.
And some say regarding an oral loan, and so forth. Sema and Shach ask why Mechaber writes this as "some say" when no explicit dissent is known. Rosh distinguishes several uncertainty cases. Where one person says, "One of you lent me one hundred," and neither claimant can prove which one, he can be exempt even from the heavenly obligation in some circumstances. Where he knows one claimant lent one hundred and another lent two hundred but does not know which is which, he must do more to fulfill his duty toward Heaven. The reason can be understood from Rashi's distinction between deposits and paid custodianship. With a deposit, the depositor may reasonably think: "I handed over my sealed property. I expect to receive back exactly what I gave. I had no reason to memorize every detail of the custodian's internal sorting." The custodian bears the greater duty to distinguish. But if a single person cannot even remember whether he deposited anything at all with this particular custodian, his own failure of memory is substantial. The custodian is not solely at fault. A loan is different from a deposit because the money is given for expenditure and will not remain as a sealed identifiable package. Both sides know from the outset that repayment will later be an accounting obligation. The lender therefore has more reason to keep track of the amount and identity of the transaction. Accordingly, one can argue that where both lenders themselves are uncertain in an oral loan, the borrower's duty toward Heaven is weaker than in the classic deposit case. That possible distinction justifies Mechaber's cautious "some say" formulation.
