He says that he does not know and is exempt. Responsa Beit David, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, discusses one who delivered one hundred sacks of flour to a shipowner for transport, each supposed to weigh four pud, for a fee of one hundred Reichsthaler. The shipowner did not weigh them in the depositor's presence. At destination the owner claimed that a substantial quantity was missing, more than the freight charge. The shipowner answered, "I do not know; what you placed aboard is what you received." One dayan wished to require the shipowner to return the flour without collecting his fee if the owner swore that the shortage certainly equaled the fee. The other two ruled that the shipowner could swear that he did not know and collect his full fee. Beit David explains that if the compensation were solely for guarding, the question would be balanced. In siman 72, seif 14, where a borrower says that he repaid an uncertain amount on a pledge and the lender admits receiving payment but does not know how much, the borrower is believed. Yet in seif 27, where the lender says that he does not know how much the pledge declined in value, the lender swears hesset and still collects his debt. The distinction is between a claim of payment, which directly weakens the evidentiary force of a document or pledge, and an independent counterclaim of "you owe me against it," where each side's claim remains separate and the creditor's security is not weakened. Here one can argue either way. If the debt is a pure guarding fee, a claim that the property was lost may be equivalent to payment of that fee, since where the custodian is not liable for the loss he may nevertheless forfeit his compensation. On the other hand, if the loss itself would obligate him to reimburse an amount exceeding the fee, then the owner's principal claim is independent: "because I hired you as custodian, you became liable to me for the loss." That resembles an independent counterclaim and does not weaken the shipowner's possessory claim. Since the question is evenly balanced, once the majority of the beit din ruled that the owner must pay, the ruling stands under the rule of following the majority. In the actual case, moreover, the principal payment was freight for transporting the goods and the shipowner incurred substantial expenses; his custodial liability was only incidental. Therefore the shortage claim is clearly an independent claim, and the majority correctly ruled that the shipowner swears that he does not know and collects his freight. This assumes that his possession of the goods gives him the status of one holding security under the rules of siman 72. If he is not considered a secured possessor, the first dayan is correct: the owner may swear that the shortage certainly equals the freight and be exempt from paying it, while the shipowner swears that he does not know whether the shortage exceeded the fee and is exempt from the balance. Beit David further discusses whether a shipowner may be exempt from theft and loss because it is customary that he does not sail personally but appoints another on the vessel; see above, siman 225, seif 6, seif katan 5.
