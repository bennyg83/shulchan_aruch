He says that he does not know, and is exempt. See Responsa Beit David, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, concerning one who delivered one hundred sacks of flour to a shipmaster to carry to a specified port, each sack said to weigh four pud, for a freight charge of one hundred Reichsthaler. The shipmaster did not weigh them in the depositor's presence. At the destination the owner claimed that he had delivered substantially more flour and that the shortage exceeded the freight charge. The shipmaster responded, "I do not know; what you deposited is what you took." One judge wished to rule that the shipmaster must return the flour without receiving freight, provided the owner swore that the shortage definitely equaled the freight charge. The other two judges disagreed, and the majority ruled that the shipmaster could swear that he did not know and collect his full freight. Beit David explains that if the only compensation were a fee for guarding, the issue would be evenly balanced. Siman 72 distinguishes between a debtor claiming partial repayment against a creditor holding collateral, where uncertainty about repayment weakens the creditor's possession, and a debtor claiming, "You owe me an offsetting amount," where the second claim is separate and does not weaken the creditor's possession. In a case of pure custodial wages, a claim that the custodian lost the property might be viewed as a repayment defense to the wage claim, because if the loss is established the custodian may forfeit his compensation even where the lost item itself is exempt from damages. On the other hand, where the custodian would have to pay the full loss even if it exceeds his wages, the owner's principal claim is not merely to defeat the wage claim, but that the custodian independently owes damages. That resembles an offsetting counterclaim, which does not weaken the custodian's claim to his wages. Since the issue is balanced, if a majority of judges has already ruled that the owner must pay, that ruling stands under the Torah principle of following the majority. In the present case, however, the principal compensation was freight for transporting the goods, including the ship's expenses, while custodial responsibility arose only incidentally from the benefit of employment. Accordingly, the shortage claim is plainly an independent counterclaim and does not undermine the shipmaster's possessory claim. The majority therefore ruled correctly that the shipmaster swears that he does not know and takes his freight. This assumes, however, that the shipmaster is legally in possession of the goods in a manner equivalent to collateral, under the rules in siman 72, seif 17 through 22. If he is not a possessor in that sense, then the first judge was correct: the owner swears that the shortage certainly equals the freight and is exempt from paying the freight, while the shipmaster swears that he does not know whether the shortage exceeded it and is exempt from any excess payment.
