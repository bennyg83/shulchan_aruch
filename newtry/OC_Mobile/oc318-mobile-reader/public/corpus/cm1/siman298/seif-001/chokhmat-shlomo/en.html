Seif 2, gloss. A depositor says, 'Some of the deposit was stolen from me,' while the custodian says, 'I do not know'; the custodian swears that he does not know and is exempt. Note: See the Shach, who directs us to the Sema in siman 291, seif katan 12, where the Sema asks why the Shulchan Aruch there did not teach that even an 'I do not know' claim exempts the custodian. In my view, one must distinguish whether the depositor claims, 'You knew,' or, 'I told you at the outset that the deposit contained a specified larger amount,' from a case where he makes no such claim. Here the depositor does not claim that the custodian knew, nor that he told him at the time of deposit exactly how much was present. Since the custodian had no reason to know, his claim of ignorance exempts him, though he must swear that he does not know, as the Shach writes elsewhere, because the deposit was in his hands and a custodian ordinarily examines what is entrusted to him. But where the depositor claims, 'You knew from the outset how much was there,' or, 'I told you exactly how much there was,' then even if the custodian says, 'I do not know,' he must pay. This is like 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' As long as he does not return the entire deposit, it is not considered a complete return. This differs from a custodian who says that he does not know whether the loss occurred through unavoidable accident or negligence. There he is exempt, because liability for unavoidable events begins only when the accident occurs, and the custodian's sworn claim itself functions as the legally recognized return, as reflected in the verse 'the owner shall accept it and he shall not pay,' which the Sages interpret as acceptance of the custodian's oath. Thus an oath can itself be treated as receipt of the deposit, and where the Torah credits his claim even without an oath, the claim likewise serves as a legal return. But where he does not claim that the excess was lost, he effectively concedes that if the excess existed it remains with him. Until he returns the whole amount, he has not completed the return, and his uncertainty is therefore one of possible nonpayment. If, however, the depositor never told him how much there was and does not claim that the custodian knew, he had no duty to know and is exempt. Although ordinarily 'I do not know whether I repaid you' creates liability even without prior reason to know, a deposit is not literally that case, and here there is room to distinguish whether he should have known. Alternatively, once the depositor expressly told him how much was present, failure to verify the amount may itself constitute negligence. I believe this distinction is correct in practice. While considering this, another doubt occurred to me. If a person says, 'I do not know how much was in the deposit,' or, 'I do not know whether you lent me money,' but inwardly he believes the claimant is truthful, must he pay? In ritual law, Yoreh De'ah 127 indicates that one may not say, 'I do not believe you,' when he actually believes the witness in his heart. Does the same apply in monetary law? There is some indication from Bava Metzia, where the Gemara resolves a difficulty by saying that the other party believes him, but that case may involve no present monetary extraction, only concern that the deposit may later reappear and create disqualification or desecration of God's Name, so he may waive the protective enactment. Yet the Gemara then asks how a lender can know how much the borrower's collateral was worth and answers that the lender trusts the borrower because of the verse condemning treacherous people. Why not answer that the borrower's trust in the lender concerns no monetary extraction, while the lender's trust in the borrower does? That suggests that even in monetary matters inward belief can create an obligation to pay. If so, why do all the poskim simply write that one who says 'I do not know' is exempt upon swearing that he does not know? Why is he not required also to swear that he does not inwardly believe the claimant, at least by way of a rolled oath or a ban where the claimant has grounds to assert that he is believed? The omission suggests that inward belief does not obligate payment, because possession of money is stronger than the analogous rule in ritual prohibition. The Gemara in the chapter HaMafkid therefore still requires analysis. Ketubot 85, concerning a judge who says 'I know her to be trustworthy,' is not decisive, because there the lender himself does not believe her; only the judge does, and a certain opposing monetary claim remains. Understand all this carefully.
