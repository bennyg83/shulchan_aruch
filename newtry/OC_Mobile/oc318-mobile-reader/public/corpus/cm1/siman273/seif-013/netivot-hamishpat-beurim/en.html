One who takes fish. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that when one spreads a net and fish lift themselves to take bait or a dead fish lying in the net, this constitutes a full acquisition because raising through one's causal force is a valid hagbahah, even in the public domain or the seller's domain. He therefore treats another's taking from the net as full theft. In my view this is not full theft for that reason. If a person holds a bundle of vines while an elephant raises itself to eat them, the elephant's elevation occurs through his immediate act and can be attributed to him. But if he hangs the vines on a post and leaves, and only afterward an ownerless elephant comes and raises itself to eat, or he leaves food and an ownerless animal later comes and eats it, that is not called meshichah or hagbahah by his force. Acquisition requires some act by the acquirer at the time of the pulling or lifting, like calling the animal and causing it to come. A trap left earlier is at most indirect causation. Ketzot also writes that a vessel does not interrupt the courtyard's acquisition where the vessel was placed there without permission, so the courtyard owner acquires. I do not know his source. The object is still located only in air that is not destined to rest on the ground because the vessel intervenes. Bava Metzia 102 appears explicitly to show that if one places a vessel in another person's domain to receive ownerless property, the courtyard's air does not acquire because the vessel interrupts. There is no difficulty from Rambam and Maggid Mishneh because they interpret that case as one where the courtyard was rented to the vessel owner, so he had permission to place the vessel there and it certainly interrupts. It follows that where one spreads a net having an interior and the fish are trapped entirely inside the vessel, the law may depend on the dispute among the poskim. According to Rashi and Tosafot, the owner of the vessel acquires. According to Rambam, the owner of the courtyard acquires. It is possible that even Rashi and Tosafot agree in our case where the field owner is present and standing beside his field and can remove the vessel. Then the vessel may not count as a legally effective interruption. See my novellae to Gittin 79.
