One who takes fish from another person's net. Tosafot distinguishes between ordinary commercial pursuit, where one who interferes with another's pending transaction can be called wicked, and ownerless property or a found object, where no such rule ordinarily applies because the second person can seek another opportunity. They ask from the rule requiring one fisherman to distance his net from another's net by the distance fish can swim. Rabbi Meir, the father of Rabbeinu Tam, explains that the first fisherman places a dead fish in his net, causing other fish to gather around it. Since the other fish assemble because of the first fisherman's action, another fisherman who sets his net there is taking the direct product of the first person's effort. The first fisherman can say, "You can make the same effort elsewhere."
Were it not for Tosafot's framing, I would say the first fisherman has actually acquired the fish through a complete mode of acquisition and beit din can remove them from the second fisherman. An elephant can be acquired by causing it to lift itself toward bundles of vines held above it. Tosafot, citing Rabbi Meshullam, explains that because the animal raises itself from the ground as a direct result of the purchaser's action, the purchaser acquires through lifting. Likewise, when the first fisherman places the dead fish and the other fish rise and gather around it because of his action, their movement is directly caused by him. This can count as acquisition through lifting, not merely a moral right to prevent interference.
If the sea has the status of a side street or similar acquisition domain, the case is even clearer. An animal that moves because of a person's call can be acquired through meshichah in such a domain. Here the fish move because of the fisherman's dead fish, which is an even stronger causal movement. Even if the water is treated like the public domain, acquisition can still occur because the relevant act is lifting. Tosafot's elephant case treats the animal's raising itself through the purchaser's force as lifting, and lifting acquires everywhere, even in the seller's domain or the public domain. As I wrote in siman 197, the purchaser need not physically raise the object with his hands; it is enough that it is raised through his force. Thus the fish's rising and gathering because of the first fisherman's act can acquire them in any location.
Rashba challenges the elephant explanation. If the elephant raises itself only because of the purchaser's action but remains within the seller's airspace, why should the purchaser acquire? A seller's courtyard includes its airspace, and an object destined to rest can be treated as though already resting there. In my view Rabbi Meshullam's position remains sound. The Gemara concerning striking a bird's nest so the birds rise allows acquisition through the resulting lifting. There too the animals are raised through the person's force while in a domain belonging to another. Tosafot in Bava Kamma asks why pushing a coin into the sea is not acquisition through lifting by one's force and distinguishes only between lifting upward and movement downward. They do not say the act fails because the object is in someone else's domain. Tosafot likewise questions the case of a poor person striking olives from the tree, asking why the resulting fall is not acquisition through his force, even though the olives are still in the owner's domain. The rule is therefore that lifting, whether by one's hand or by one's causal force, acquires everywhere. If the fish raise themselves because of the fisherman's action, his acquisition can operate despite the location.
This is also implied by the general statement of Abaye and Rava that meshichah acquires in a side street while lifting acquires everywhere. Once lifting through one's force is recognized as a form of lifting, it falls under that universal rule. Accordingly, Rabbi Meshullam's elephant can be acquired even while the animal rises within the seller's courtyard. And the fish that gather and lift themselves because of the dead fish can likewise be acquired by the first fisherman. The second fisherman's taking would then be actual theft, not merely improper interference.
