For the first three days. The Jerusalem Talmud asks about one who declared his chametz ownerless on the thirteenth of Nisan and then left it until after the festival. Rabbi Yochanan prohibits benefit, while Reish Lakish permits. The Jerusalem Talmud first thought Rabbi Yochanan follows Rabbi Yosei, who holds that ownerless property remains with the owner until someone acquires it, while Reish Lakish follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that it leaves immediately. It ultimately says the dispute is instead whether to fear a deceptive stratagem.
Chavot Yair quotes the author of Avodat HaGershuni explaining the Jerusalem Talmud's initial comparison precisely this way: Rabbi Yosei says ownerless property does not leave the owner's hand until another acquires it, so nullification alone would not remove the chametz, while Rabbi Meir says the owner's declaration immediately removes it. Chavot Yair objects that the Babylonian Talmud and the codifiers distinguish only an owner's ability to retract during the first three days after declaring a field ownerless, not an ongoing proprietary title in all ownerless property. In my view his objection does not refute Avodat HaGershuni. The Babylonian distinction can be a rabbinic rule designed to prevent deception, while the Jerusalem Talmud's initial theory treats Rabbi Yosei's position as a Torah view that ownerless property remains legally tied to the owner until acquisition. Nor is there a basis to distinguish land from movables for the Torah theory. Tosafot's distinction between land and movables rests on the later rabbinic anti deception rationale. Under the initial theory that Rabbi Yosei holds as a matter of ownership that property does not leave until another acquires, the rule applies equally to all ownerless property. Accordingly, the explanation of Avodat HaGershuni is well founded. The Jerusalem Talmud initially linked the chametz dispute to the fundamental dispute whether ownerless designation itself removes ownership. It then concludes that Rabbi Yochanan's stringency can instead rest merely on concern for deception.
The practical authorities likewise do not clearly establish a special difference between land and movables in the three day ownerless rule. Tosafot at the end of Nedarim distinguishes them according to Rabbi Yosei only because the deception concern is greater with land. But other authorities do not adopt that distinction. Tosafot in Pesachim, when explaining how private nullification of chametz can work as ownerless designation, does not say that movables are different. They instead explain that chametz nullification is a context where there is no concern for deception because the owner genuinely wants the prohibition removed. Ran likewise raises the difficulty from Rabbi Yosei without relying on a land movable distinction. Beit Yosef in Orach Chaim also treats the ordinary three day rules of ownerless designation as potentially relevant to chametz, which is movable property. Sema's formulation likewise implies no categorical distinction. Therefore, although Tosafot gives a land based distinction under one explanation, the broader practical law does not establish that movables automatically leave ownership immediately while land alone remains retractable for three days. This supports the reading of Avodat HaGershuni.
