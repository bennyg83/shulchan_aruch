For all three days. See Sema, seif katan 13. He explains that so long as the person cannot retract by speech alone, fraudsters will fear that others may acquire the property before they reclaim it. At first glance this is very difficult. According to R. Yose, who holds that hefker is not complete until another person acquires, the rabbinic enactment can indeed help: without it a fraudster could retract verbally before anyone acquired, while after the enactment he must permit a full acquisition and therefore fears losing the field. But according to the Sages, whom we follow, even before the enactment complete acquisition by another person was already necessary to regain the property. If that risk deters fraudsters, why was any enactment needed? If Chazal feared they would still abandon property to escape tithes despite the risk, the enactment changes nothing, because acquisition works after the enactment just as before it. It therefore appears that during the first three days, if the original owner himself reclaims the entire field, that reclamation is ineffective for exempting the produce from tithes. Such conduct is transparently fraudulent, because he abandons the field and immediately reacquires it. He can obtain produce exempt from tithe only if the field itself remains ownerless and he merely gathers fruit from it as any other person might. Then he truly stands toward the field like any outsider. This explains Rosh in Nedarim, who says the fraudster fears that while he gathers from one side, another person may gather from the other. He cannot avoid tithes during the three days by simply reacquiring the whole field; he must leave the field itself ownerless, and that creates real exposure to others. Thus the enactment effectively deters sham abandonment.
