It is like a vow, and he is forbidden to retract. Rambam writes that ownerless designation is like a vow and one may not retract. Beit Yosef explains that this means the owner may not simply say, "I retract what I said." He can still reacquire the property from ownerless status like anyone else, but that is a new acquisition, not a cancellation of the original declaration. This raises a conceptual question. If the only reason he cannot retract is the prohibition of violating his word, perhaps ownerless designation itself does not immediately transfer ownership out of him. If it did, there would be no need to invoke the law of vows. On this theory, where the owner declares property ownerless and then dies before anyone acquires it, perhaps the property passes to his heirs. His personal vow does not bind them, just as heirs do not inherit their father's personal vow. The Gemara concerning one who declares his slave ownerless and then dies does not refute this, because the slave acquired himself from the ownerless declaration during the master's lifetime. Once the slave acquired freedom, the later death cannot restore ownership. The unresolved question concerns ordinary property declared ownerless but not yet acquired by anyone at the time of the owner's death.
Shabbat appears to show that ownerless designation does create an actual proprietary change. According to Beit Shammai, who require a person's utensils to rest on Shabbat, one can avoid the prohibition by declaring the lamp or vat ownerless. If the declaration merely created a personal vow against retraction while ownership remained, the utensils would still be his and the Shabbat obligation would remain. Rashba answers that passage by saying the ownerless status there is imposed through beit din. The court's power of confiscation certainly can remove ownership completely, so the passage does not prove that an ordinary private declaration itself does so. Yet another Shabbat passage says that regarding tzitzit, since it is within his power to declare the garment ownerless, the obligation can be removed. Rashi explains that the garment then leaves his ownership. That strongly suggests a real proprietary effect. One can perhaps answer that although the object remains in some sense "his" until another acquires, it is no longer legally in his possession because he is forbidden to retract. Property outside one's legal control can be treated like property no longer owned for certain mitzvah obligations. Thus tzitzit may fall away even if the proprietary title is not fully extinguished. This remains a significant question.
This theory also explains Rashi's view that intentional abandonment can count as "another person's transferring intent." Tosafot objects: once the owner declared the property ownerless, it has left his domain, so how can his intent later function as the intent of a transferor? If ownerless designation does not immediately erase the owner's title but merely places him under a binding prohibition against retraction, the later finder still acquires from the original owner's legal title. The owner's declaration therefore resembles a vow to the poor: even after the vow, the poor person who later takes the property acquires from the original owner. That can count as another person's transferring intent. This may also explain Rashi's distinctive approach to nullification of chametz. Tosafot explains nullification as ownerless designation derived from the verse excluding property of others. Rashi instead connects it to the positive command to remove chametz. If ownerless property remains technically connected to the original owner until another acquires, it may still count as "his chametz" for the special Torah rule that treats chametz as though in his possession. Therefore Rashi required the direct law of nullification under the command of removal rather than ordinary ownerless designation.

Beit Yosef in Orach Chaim cites Ran that an agent cannot nullify another's chametz if nullification works through ownerless designation, because one cannot make another person's property ownerless by agency. Beit Yosef explains this by saying ownerless designation has the law of a vow, and just as one cannot appoint an agent to make a vow binding upon the principal, one cannot appoint an agent to make the principal's property ownerless. He apparently derives the comparison between ownerless designation and vows from the opening of Nedarim, where the Gemara asks whether partial language effective for vows is also effective for ownerless designation. But the very fact that the Gemara leaves the matter unresolved suggests ownerless designation is not simply identical with a vow. If it were literally vow law, the rules of partial vow terminology should clearly apply. The Gemara itself asks whether ownerless designation resembles charity. That demonstrates that its relationship to vows is precisely what remains uncertain. It is therefore surprising that all the authorities write simply that ownerless designation is "like a vow" without clarifying the source. This requires study.
