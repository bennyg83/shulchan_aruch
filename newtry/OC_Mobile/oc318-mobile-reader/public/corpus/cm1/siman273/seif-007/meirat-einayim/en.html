He divided the ground as well. A.J. D.D. has written this article that is the author of the S.C., and here it is followed by the Rambam as a slave and mutual land:
Even if he declared it ownerless before one person. This opinion holds that at least one person is required, because that person hears the owner's declaration and can acquire the property for himself. Without anyone hearing it, the status of ownerless property does not attach. For this very reason the Sages said that ideally one should declare property ownerless before three people. Otherwise the owner could later deny the declaration and say, "I never abandoned it." Before three, each person can attempt to acquire it for himself and the other two can testify that they heard the abandonment, as the Mechaber concludes.
And some say even in private, etc. These authorities too agree that rabbinically three are required for the reason just explained. Their dispute concerns Torah law. They hold that one need not declare it ownerless even before one person, and a declaration made privately is sufficient. A practical consequence concerns one who lends or rents an animal to a gentile in the middle of the week for work, expecting it back before Shabbat, but the gentile does not return it. He may declare the animal ownerless privately so that he not violate the command that his animal rest on Shabbat. Even after the rabbinic enactment, such abandonment is effective, because we are witnesses that the owner truly prefers abandonment in order to avoid the prohibition. The Rosh writes this in the chapter Ein Bein HaMudar. See the Tur and Mechaber in Orach Chaim siman 246.
