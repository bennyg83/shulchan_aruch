One who spreads a trap in another person's field. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 1 of Hilchot Zechiyah, explains that one who spreads a trap in another's field without permission was not granted the rabbinic protection against taking what entered the trap, even for the sake of peaceful relations. He proves it from the rule that where the trap has an enclosed receptacle, one who takes from it is a robber. If the trap stood in another person's domain, its owner would not acquire, all the more so than a buyer's vessel in the seller's domain, where even though the seller consciously transfers ownership, the buyer may not acquire. Thus where one spreads a trap without permission, even if the trap has an enclosure, the owner of the land acquires the captured item if the land is a guarded courtyard, or if it is unguarded when he stands there and says, 'My field shall acquire for me.' This is the basis of the present seif.
Lechem Mishneh objects: just as a buyer's vessel in the seller's domain does not acquire for the buyer, we also rule that a seller's vessel in the buyer's domain prevents the buyer from acquiring. If Maggid Mishneh uses an a fortiori argument to deny acquisition to the trap owner, the same a fortiori argument should deny acquisition to the field owner. If the buyer's domain cannot acquire where the seller's vessel intervenes even though another person is consciously transferring ownership, then here, where there is no conscious transferor, all the more so should the landowner's domain fail because the trap, which is the trap owner's vessel, intervenes. Lechem Mishneh leaves this difficult.
In my opinion, an object is called another person's 'vessel' for this purpose only where he has permission to place it there. Tosafos in chapter HaSefinah, Bava Batra 85, ask why the Gemara is uncertain about a buyer's vessel in the seller's domain when it already says that a buyer's vessel in the public domain unquestionably does not acquire. They answer that in the public domain no one permits him to leave his vessel there, whereas in the seller's domain the Gemara is uncertain because perhaps the seller lends him the place so he can acquire, since the seller wants the sale. Tur, siman 200, cites Ramah that the case of the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain concerns a seller who permitted the buyer to place the vessel there. Since he did not explicitly say, 'Go and acquire,' it remains uncertain whether the permission was intended to support acquisition. Likewise, the case of a seller's vessel in the buyer's domain concerns a seller who brought his vessel into the buyer's property with the buyer's permission. The Gemara is uncertain whether the seller's vessel remains sufficiently independent to interrupt the buyer's domain or becomes subordinate to the domain. Here, by contrast, the trap was spread without the field owner's permission. It is not treated as a legally recognized vessel capable of interrupting the field owner's domain. The owner of the field therefore acquires. Maggid Mishneh's a fortiori argument is actually twofold: a buyer's vessel fails even though it was placed with permission and even though another person is consciously transferring ownership. All the more so should a trap spread without permission, where the trap does not count as an interrupting vessel at all and there is no conscious transferor, fail to acquire for the trap owner. But the field owner can acquire, because an unauthorized trap does not interrupt his domain. This is clear.
