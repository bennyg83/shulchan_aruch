The minors, whoever first takes possession of them acquires. This rule is derived from Kiddushin 23 concerning a convert who died. That case is understandable. The minor slaves became ownerless automatically, and a minor has no legal hand to acquire himself from automatic ownerless status. Whoever first takes possession of them therefore acquires. But where a master deliberately declares his slave ownerless, the matter appears different according to Rashi's approach, which Shach follows in siman 268, that hefker is considered another person's conscious transfer for certain acquisition rules. Where another person consciously transfers, a minor can acquire, as explained in siman 243, seif katan 15. Why then should a minor slave not acquire himself when his master deliberately makes him ownerless? Gittin 39, in the discussion that the slave committed a prohibition but not a monetary offense, and Rashi there, imply that even deliberate abandonment does not free a minor slave through his own acquisition. We must therefore say that even Rashi does not regard hefker generally as da'at acheret makneh for all laws. He uses that concept specifically for an unguarded courtyard where guarding by the person making the property available can suffice. Mordechai similarly indicates this regarding priestly gifts. Thus deliberate hefker does not generally supply a mature transferring mind that enables a minor to acquire himself.
