The borrower takes a hesset oath. Rambam writes in his Commentary to the Mishnah at the end of Shevuot that if Reuven claims money from Shimon and produces a document for that exact amount, while Shimon denies the entire debt, and Reuven himself admits that the document is early dated in the sense under discussion, Shimon takes hesset that he owes Reuven nothing. Rambam adds that we do not believe Reuven through a migo that he could have said the document was properly dated, because migo is used to exempt a person from liability, not to extract money from another. The Shach asks that Rambam himself rules an early dated document remains valid against the debtor personally and is invalid only against purchasers. If so, the creditor is holding a valid document as against the borrower, and a migo with a valid document should not be considered a migo to extract. Knesset HaGedolah cites Maharshal Yafeh as answering that Rambam in the Commentary must have held an early dated document invalid even against the borrower. He then expresses surprise, because Rambam in Hilchot Malveh explicitly rules that it remains valid from free property. There is no necessity for this answer. As I wrote in siman 82, seif katan 10, even where a document exists, a migo can remain extractive if the document itself lacks the type of property responsibility that gives it the status of something effectively collected. An early dated document cannot collect from encumbered property. It therefore resembles a document without property responsibility and lacks the special strength that can transform a migo into retention. This is supported by Gittin concerning Shemittah. Even according to Beit Shammai, who ordinarily treat a debt instrument awaiting collection as collected, a document without property responsibility remains subject to Shemittah. The "collected" conception applies only where land is actually encumbered through the instrument. There is also a deeper point. A document gives special force to a migo only where, even according to the borrower, it is an ordinary valid document and the dispute concerns later payment. Here the borrower says the document is an accommodation instrument or otherwise never represented a valid lien at all. The creditor says it is merely early dated. The parties are litigating whether there is any valid document. In such a case the creditor cannot bootstrap his migo from the disputed paper itself. A remaining question is why, if an early dated document known to be early is valid against the borrower, the borrower is ever believed to say that it is actually an accommodation instrument. The answer is that where the document's early dating is independently established, we do not add another defect merely because one defect exists. The document remains a valid free property instrument, and the borrower cannot simply assert a second defect. But where the only basis for calling it early dated is the creditor's own admission, while the borrower says the true defect is an accommodation arrangement, the document has already been removed by the creditor himself from its ordinary presumption of complete validity. The court must then choose between competing explanations of the visible defect. The creditor's migo cannot extract against the borrower's possession. This explains Rambam.
