The borrower takes a heset oath. See Shach, seif katan 9, and what he writes in explaining Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah, that the creditor is believed by a migo. At first glance this is difficult. If the document is valid, he does not need a migo, for he should be believed without one. It is also difficult that Shach initially says the case concerns a document written in order to borrow but under which no loan was made, whereas Rambam validates a document written for borrowing even before the loan, as explained above in siman 43. It therefore appears that when Shach says at the beginning, "written to borrow but he did not borrow," he means that the document was written for a contemplated loan and delivered directly to the lender. Rambam validates such a document only when it was first delivered to the borrower. If the witnesses delivered it directly to the lender, it is a trust document and invalid. At the end, where Shach says that the case concerns an actually antedated document that is nevertheless valid, he means that the witnesses delivered the document to the borrower, and the borrower gave it to the lender before any loan occurred and trusted him with it, while the witnesses did not know this. The lender claims that although the document was delivered to him before the loan, he later lent money on the strength of that document. In such a case the document is valid, as explained above in siman 43. The borrower claims that no loan was ever made and that the document remains merely a trust document. In this case the lender certainly requires a migo, because he admits that the document originally came into his possession without a loan. The mere fact that it is now in his possession is therefore no proof that he later made the loan. He is believed only where he has a migo.
