The borrower takes a heset oath, etc. The Shach asks that in the preceding siman the Rama cites an opinion, based on Ramban, that a migo may sometimes be used even to extract money when an authenticated document supports the claimant. If so, the Rama should have qualified the present ruling and said that according to that opinion the borrower takes heset only where the document is not authenticated. I think the answer is that Ramban's position is itself very strained, as I explained in the general rules of migo. The Rama cited it above only as an additional consideration in a case where the claimant already had a substantial supporting document and there were independent reasons for doubt. Here, by contrast, the document is certainly no more than a shard for the relevant purpose. Ramban's difficult position standing alone is not enough to create an additional practical doubt. Therefore the Rama properly states the Mechaber's rule without qualification.
The borrower takes heset. Rambam writes in his Commentary on the Mishnah to Sheviit, concerning the rule that antedated documents are invalid, that one example is a person who borrowed on a document, repaid it, and then borrowed again using the same document. The document is now antedated because it purports to create collection from the original date. Rambam then adds: if Reuven sues Shimon with such a document and Shimon says it was merely a trust document, while Reuven says, "No, it is antedated in the manner just explained," Shimon takes heset and is exempt. Reuven has no migo that he could have called it postdated, because a migo is not used to extract money. The Shach asks that in Mishneh Torah Rambam holds an antedated document remains valid against unencumbered property. Why then should Shimon swear and be exempt? He answers that the Commentary concerns the special case where the document had already been paid and its lien extinguished, making it completely invalid. This is forced. Rambam says explicitly that the document is antedated "in the manner stated above." In the earlier discussion, the essential invalidity is the false earlier date, not simply the extinguished lien, and the Shach himself elsewhere explains that the earlier case had delivery witnesses so that the extinguished lien alone would not settle the matter. More importantly, why did Rambam insert this additional practical law into his Mishnah commentary if it contributed nothing to explaining the Mishnah? The answer is that Rambam is resolving the Yerushalmi's question: how do we know that a document is antedated, since the signatories can no longer ordinarily invalidate their own signed testimony, and even if the date is demonstrably impossible perhaps the document was intentionally postdated from an earlier transaction? Rambam explains that the Mishnah can concern a case where the creditor himself admits the antedating. A litigant's own admission against his interest is stronger than witnesses. One might then say that if the creditor is our source for the defect, he should be believed by migo concerning the underlying debt. Rambam therefore adds that he is not, because the migo would be used to extract money. This shows that the "antedated" document in the commentary is the ordinary antedated document of the Mishnah, not a special wholly extinguished instrument. The better conclusion is that Rambam in his Commentary on the Mishnah followed the authorities who invalidate an antedated document completely, and later in Mishneh Torah revised his position, something that occurs in several areas. Bnei Yaakov likewise notes this possibility. The Shach's forced interpretation is unnecessary.
