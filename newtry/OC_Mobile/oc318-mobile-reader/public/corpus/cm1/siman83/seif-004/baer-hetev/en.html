The Sema writes that even the dissenting authorities who hold that a migo may be used to extract money, as cited by the Rama in siman 82, seif 12, agree here that the lender is not believed to say, "I had another note and lost it," by a migo that he could have remained silent and sued on this authenticated note. Since the lender admits that when he first came to beit din he came to collect on an invalid note, we do not employ a migo except to prove that the claim he asserted from the outset was true. Tosafot likewise write this, contrary to the Ir Shushan. The Shach writes that the Ir Shushan was correct and the Sema's words are astonishing. Tosafot gave that reasoning only according to Rav Yosef, but according to the conclusion of the Gemara that reason cannot stand; rather, the rule depends on the principle that a migo is not used to extract money. This is also evident from siman 146, seif 25, from Tosafot in Bava Batra 32b, from the Rosh there, and explicitly from the rulings of Maharam of Recanati, siman 248. The dissenting opinion in siman 82 necessarily holds that a migo is not lost merely because the claimant was compelled to lie initially. See there for additional proofs. The Shach concludes: As a matter of practical halachah, I cannot decide between these great authorities. In every form of the case it is therefore an unresolved question of law. The practical difference is that if the lender seized property, even before witnesses, it is not removed from him, as in every unresolved monetary question. Even if the note was not authenticated at the time, once he later authenticates it he is believed regarding what he had already claimed, since he is now in possession. Likewise, if a judge already ruled and collected the debt for the lender, what was done remains done. The Rambam writes in his commentary to the Mishnah at the end of Sheviit that if the borrower claims the note was a trust document and the lender says it was not a trust document but was merely predated, the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. The lender is not believed through a migo because a migo is not used to extract money. The Shach explains that this refers to a kind of predated note that is entirely invalid. But where it is merely genuinely predated and the Rambam holds that it may still collect from unencumbered property, then according to the lender's own statement the note remains valid against the borrower himself, and he is believed through a migo. This appears correct.
