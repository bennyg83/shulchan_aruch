He produced against him an authenticated note, etc. The borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. Sema writes that even the authorities who hold that a migo may be used to extract money, as Rama records in siman 82, seif 12, agree here that the creditor is not believed to say, “I had another note and lost it,” through the migo that he could have remained silent and collected with this authenticated note. This case is different, Sema explains, because the creditor admits that he originally came to beit din to collect with an invalid note. A migo is used only to establish the truth of the claim asserted from the outset. Tosafot write similarly. Sema therefore wonders at Ir Shushan, who explains that the borrower swears and is exempt because a migo may not be used to extract money, although the preceding siman, seif 12, records a dispute on that question and the present rule is derived from the Gemara. I say that Ir Shushan understood the matter correctly, while Sema’s words are difficult. Tosafot, in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak, say that there is no migo because the creditor was compelled to lie initially only in explaining Rav Yosef’s position. Once Rav Idi bar Avin ruled that the halachah follows Rabbah with respect to land and Rav Yosef with respect to money, that explanation cannot remain the basis of the ruling. The reason must instead be that a migo is not used to extract money. Were the alleged need to lie initially enough to destroy the migo, why would the halachah follow Rabbah regarding land, as all the poskim rule and as explained below in siman 146, seif 25? Tosafot state this expressly in Bava Batra 32b, s.v. Ve-hilcheta, in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak: the halachah follows Rabbah regarding land and Rav Yosef regarding money because a migo is accepted to retain property already held. Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 15 of Hilchot To’en, writes likewise. This is also explicit in Rosh there and in the chapter HaKotev regarding a woman who admits partial payment or reduction of her ketubah, and in the rulings of Maharam of Rothenburg cited by Maharam Recanati, siman 248. The dissenting authorities in siman 82 must likewise hold that being compelled to lie initially does not invalidate a migo. Those dissenters are Rivash, siman 336, in the name of later authorities who accept a migo even to extract money, namely Ramban and Rashba, whose view Maggid Mishneh cites at the end of chapter 14 of Hilchot Malveh. They explain the incident in the Gemara as one in which the note was not yet authenticated, though it could later be authenticated. Rabbah and Rav Yosef dispute whether the migo is effective while the note is presently unauthenticated. If it is already authenticated, however, they plainly hold that the migo is effective even to extract from the borrower, although the creditor was compelled to lie initially. This is explicit in Maggid Mishneh and in Ramban’s novellae to the chapter Chazakat HaBatim. Sema’s further objection, that the present law is stated in the Gemara, is therefore also no objection. The Gemara does not say that the note was authenticated, and the dissenting authorities interpret it as unauthenticated. Ir Shushan omitted their view in siman 82 because they certainly disagree with Tur and Shulchan Aruch here as well, whereas Mechaber and Rama rule here without qualification against them. Rama may have relied here on what he wrote earlier, and Darkei Moshe expressly states that Ramban and Rashba disagree with Tur and Rambam. Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch, who explain the case as involving an authenticated note, must hold that the reason is that a migo is not used to extract money. This is clear from Tosafot, Rosh, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Baal HaTerumot, gate 17, which gives precisely that reason and makes no mention of the creditor having been compelled to lie initially. The same is implied by Rambam’s Commentary to the Mishnah, cited below in seif katan 9. Nor can Sema be defended by saying that Tosafot retain both considerations, namely that ordinarily a migo may extract money, but not here because the creditor was also compelled to lie initially. If so, Sema should not have objected to Ir Shushan, for even on his own account this case requires the rule that a migo does not extract money. Ir Shushan’s words could therefore be understood in the same way. There are also difficulties for Sema from siman 82, seif 2, and siman 84, seif 4. Moreover, Tosafot and Hagahot Maimoniyot write that it is forced to say Rav Idi ruled from uncertainty. Rather, he ruled decisively for Rabbah regarding land and for Rav Yosef regarding money, and even an act already done in accordance with Rav Yosef regarding land would be reversed. Rabbeinu Yitzchak therefore explains that a migo is accepted to maintain possession. Since Rav Idi was not uncertain, the consideration that the creditor was compelled to lie initially cannot be a valid objection at all. Otherwise, there would be no reason to follow Rabbah regarding land. It cannot be said that Rav Idi was unsure whether that consideration was sound and therefore declined to remove the land, for Tosafot reject the view that his ruling was based on doubt. Rather, Rav Idi was certain that even though the creditor had to lie initially, the migo remained valid. He ruled for Rav Yosef regarding money for the separate reason that a migo is not used to extract money. Tosafot, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Baal HaTerumot further explain that the case of setarai, where a migo is used to extract, is different because the creditor has a fully valid note. According to Sema, that answer would be unnecessary, since in setarai the claimant was not compelled to lie initially. This confirms that the final explanation rests solely on the principle that a migo does not extract money. Rosh states this expressly in his conclusion. Accordingly, Ir Shushan’s words are correct, and Sema objected without justification. I suspect that Sema consulted only Beit Yosef’s abbreviated citation of Rabbeinu Yitzchak and did not examine Tosafot and Rosh themselves. Beit Yosef copied only the beginning of Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s explanation according to Rav Yosef, whereas in the final ruling Rabbeinu Yitzchak does not accept that reasoning, as I have explained.

The borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. I have already written that according to Ramban, Rashba, and other later authorities, the creditor is believed here through a migo when the note is authenticated, because they allow a migo to extract money. In their view, Rabbah and Rav Yosef dispute only a note that is presently unauthenticated but can later be authenticated, notwithstanding that the creditor now admits the note is invalid. Gedulei Terumah, page 99, raises two objections to their position. First, if the case concerns an unauthenticated note, then at the time of the admission there was no migo, so how can the Gemara conclude that the halachah follows Rabbah regarding land? Second, the chapter HaKotev appears to prove the opposite from the case of a woman who reduces her ketubah, where the Gemara asks how she can collect with a note that is mere pottery and answers that she says the ketubah was a trust arrangement between herself and her husband. Gedulei Terumah leaves their words requiring analysis. These objections are unfounded. The first should have been directed against Rabbah himself: why does Rabbah accept the migo if the note is not authenticated? The answer is that although it is not authenticated now, it can be authenticated later, and Rabbah holds that once it is authenticated the claimant is believed concerning the claim she already made through the migo then available. Ramban states this expressly in his novellae to Chazakat HaBatim. The proof from the woman who reduces her ketubah is likewise no proof. According to the final conclusion in Chazakat HaBatim, the Gemara initially assumes that her ketubah is not authenticated but can later be authenticated. It cannot say the ketubah is entirely genuine, because then one must explain how a ketubah of five hundred became one of a thousand zuz. The Gemara therefore asks how she can collect when the unauthenticated document is presently worthless. Alternatively, it initially assumes that she says the ketubah is forged and cannot later be authenticated, in which case all agree that she has no migo. It then establishes the case as one in which she says the document was a trust arrangement between herself and her husband. It prefers not to say that the witnesses erred, which would make them false witnesses, and instead preserves their integrity. Ramban states this explicitly. After explaining that Rabbah and Rav Yosef dispute an unauthenticated note that can later be authenticated, he writes that the question in HaKotev is either asked according to the halachic conclusion in the same type of case, or according to all opinions where she says her ketubah is forged and no witnesses are available to authenticate it. The Gemara therefore establishes that she says it was a trust arrangement and does not establish it as an authenticated ketubah whose amount she truly reduces, which would require assuming false witnesses. Gedulei Terumah evidently overlooked these words. The Gemara could also have established the case as an authenticated document that she calls forged and says was only for five hundred, in which case Ramban and Rashba would believe her through a migo. It chose the more common case of a trust arrangement, since a fully authenticated forged note is uncommon. Thus Gedulei Terumah’s further assertion that, according to Ramban and Rashba, a migo does not help a woman who reduces an authenticated ketubah is also incorrect and contradicts Ramban’s explicit words. As a matter of practical halachah, I cannot decide between these great authorities. It appears to me that every form of the case is a doubt in law. Consequently, if the creditor seized property, even before witnesses, it is not removed from him. Even if the note was not authenticated at the time, once he later authenticates it he is believed concerning his earlier claim, because he is now in possession. Likewise, if a judge already acted and awarded the debt to the creditor, the ruling stands. Similarly, below in siman 146, seif 25, concerning land, if the purchaser is in possession, we do not remove it from him even if the note was not authenticated at the time, provided he later authenticates it, in accordance with Ramban and Rashba. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 15 of Hilchot To’en, and Tur at the end of siman 146 write accordingly. Although Tur explains the Gemara’s case as involving an authenticated note, he appears to hold that an already authenticated note and one later authenticated have the same law; Rosh in Chazakat HaBatim also implies this. If a judge already placed the challenger in possession of the land, or the challenger seized it himself and it remains in his hands, we likewise do not remove it from him, even though the note had been authenticated. Tosafot write that an erroneous ruling is reversed only according to their interpretation that Rav Idi did not rule from uncertainty. That interpretation is not compelled. Although seizure does not generally help with land in a doubt of law, since we leave land with its original owner, as I explain at length in Tekufo Kohen, it may be that Rav Idi regarded Rabbah’s reasoning as stronger but could not decide conclusively. He therefore ruled for Rabbah regarding land and would not remove land from the purchaser contrary to Rabbah’s reasoning. Rashbam writes that Rav Idi was uncertain which opinion was correct. This must also be the position of Ramban and Rashba, who accept a migo to extract money; otherwise, why would Rav Idi follow Rav Yosef regarding money? Ramban expressly writes that the masters of the Gemara remained uncertain and ruled that no affirmative act should be taken, but that the existing possession should remain. Rambam writes in his Commentary to the Mishnah at the end of Sheviit that if the borrower claims the note was a trust arrangement and the creditor says it was not a trust arrangement but merely antedated, the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. The creditor is not believed through a migo because a migo is not used to extract money. Beit Yosef cites this above in siman 82 and identifies it with the incident involving Rabbah and Rav Yosef in Chazakat HaBatim. It appears that Rambam refers to the kinds of antedated notes he had just discussed, such as a note written for a loan that was never made, a paid note reused for a later loan, or a note whose lien was waived. Rambam treats such notes as entirely invalid, and the case then parallels Chazakat HaBatim. A literally antedated note, however, may still collect from the borrower’s unencumbered property according to Rambam, as stated above in siman 43, seif 7. On the creditor’s version, such a note remains valid against the borrower himself, and the creditor should therefore be believed through a migo. This appears correct to me.
