Seif 2. (The ruling favors the lender.) Regarding the Sages' statement, "A borrower is servant to the lender," it requires study whether this principle applies only to an actual loan and not to a deposit, unpaid wages, or ordinary credit extended in a sale. Since those are not literally loans, perhaps the element of subordination does not apply, and consequently the rule discussed in siman 83, where the reason given is only "a borrower is servant to the lender," would not apply. Without that principle, forced payment might constitute valid payment. This appears reasonable. Even if the debt was later formally converted into a loan and an ordinary promissory note was made, the original transaction may still differ, because the seller did not initially advance money as an act of benevolence toward the buyer but acted for his own commercial benefit. By contrast, an iska, one half of which is deemed a loan, appears to involve the principle of "a borrower is servant to the lender," because under the laws of heter iska the loan component must be distinct from the profit attributable to the deposit component. That loan resembles a benevolent loan. Credit given in an ordinary sale does not, especially where the seller is eager to dispose of the merchandise and benefits more than the buyer, as discussed in Yoreh Deah siman 227. What I wrote in siman 58 from the Gemara concerning "his money was freed" does not prove otherwise, for perhaps an oral debt there was an actual benevolent loan. Perhaps the case also involved highly marketable merchandise or a debt formally converted into a loan. Since the Sages did not enact an undifferentiated rule equating all these cases, there is room for the distinction above. If a buyer purchases a quantity of grain from a large heap and there were two separate sales on different terms, the seller has the upper hand to say which sale a later payment is applied to, especially where no particular grain in the heap was individually designated. The Urim VeTumim, citing the Radbaz, indicates that before the due date one cannot say that a payment was applied to that unmatured debt. This appears to concern a dispute over what the parties expressly stipulated, and all the more so, absent any stipulation, one cannot apply payment to a debt whose due date has not arrived. Perhaps, however, if they come to beit din only after the due date, the creditor can say, "I held the money until the due date and then applied it to that debt." Although it is ordinarily forbidden to retain another person's money without cause, one may perhaps do so to secure collection of one's debt where there is a genuine concern regarding the debtor's solvency. If the creditor is already in possession and the parties dispute what was expressly agreed, perhaps he may say that the debtor gave him the money even before the due date. Although this runs against a presumption, that presumption may not overcome possession, especially since a migo can overcome it, and here the creditor in possession has a migo. If one partner received payment and no other debt belongs to the partnership, he cannot redirect the payment to some unrelated personal debt when his partner sent it for a partnership debt. This appears reasonable. Finally, the rule in siman 83 based on "a borrower is servant" appears to be a rabbinic arrangement governing Jewish monetary law and should not necessarily apply against a non-Jew, particularly where retention would merely avoid a secular obligation and entail no desecration of Hashem's Name. Certainly this is so where under their law payment is applied only to the debt for which it was actually handed over.
