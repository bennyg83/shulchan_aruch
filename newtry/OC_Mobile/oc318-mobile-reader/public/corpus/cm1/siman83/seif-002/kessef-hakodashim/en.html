In the gloss. (And he accepted them in silence.) A buyer had a transaction with a seller for liquor. The buyer told a third person, "Receive the liquor from the seller into your house, on condition that I thereby satisfy the liquor I owe you." The third person accepted the liquor into his house, but afterward claimed, "The seller himself owes me that quantity of liquor, or at least owes me money, and I am now holding this liquor for myself rather than for you." It appears that this is governed by the rule at the end of siman 269 and siman 183. The Sages taught that where one person says to another, "Lift this lost object for me," the latter's silence and act of lifting show that he lifted it for his fellow. So too here, no later explanation should undo the legal effect of his silence. This is true even though, in the lost-object case, he might have felt compelled to remain silent because otherwise his fellow would have taken the object first for himself. Nevertheless the lifting acquires it for the first person. Here too, his receipt of the liquor ordinarily acquires it for the buyer. This requires careful study. If the buyer said nothing to the recipient, and the recipient merely told the seller, "I want to receive from you the liquor that you owe to so-and-so, who sold liquor to me," there is no conclusive indication that he intended to receive it for the other person. He can say, "Had I told you I was receiving it because of what you owe me personally, perhaps you would not have given it to me. A debtor may be more willing to pay the original owner than to pay one who claims through him." Such an explanation can therefore support the claim that he acquired it for himself. His intention may even have been apparent from the circumstances. This requires study. This also appears from the beginning of siman 83 in the name of Rashba. See the Urim there. Perhaps there is also a distinction between the creditor saying, "Acquire it for me," and the debtor saying to a third person, "Acquire it for the creditor." When the creditor says, "Acquire it for me," he clearly benefits from the acquisition, so his words are naturally understood as a specific request that the recipient act on his behalf. Therefore silence is treated as assent. Even where a person finds a valuable lost object and the other could have taken it himself, if he remains silent when told, "Acquire it for me," and then lifts it, the first speaker acquires it. The recipient cannot later say that he remained silent only so the other would not seize it first. The creditor's request was plainly intended as a binding instruction because it benefited him. By contrast, when the debtor says, "Acquire it for the creditor," his words may be only permissive, meaning, "If you wish to acquire it for him, you may." The debtor gains nothing from the third person's acquisition unless the creditor appointed that third person as agent, since otherwise the creditor does not bear the risk. Since the debtor's statement is not one of entreaty or request but merely grants permission, perhaps it resembles the case of the liquor above. This may be the reasoning of Rashba. My father-in-law suggested that perhaps the explanation, "I remained silent because otherwise the other person would have seized it," does indeed negate the inference from silence. This requires study.
