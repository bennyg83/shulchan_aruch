[Shach, subparagraph 2] He can no longer retain them for himself. Note: This is puzzling. If so, what does a migo that there never was such a matter accomplish? Even if we believe him that the sender owes him, he has already acquired the money for the others. Regarding the Shach’s main difficulty, it appears to me that where witnesses establish that the sender owes the agent, we believe the agent that from the outset he intended to acquire the money for himself, and this is the case discussed here. Likewise, if he has a migo by which we believe that the sender owes him, he is believed to say that he initially seized it for himself. Indeed, this is stronger than our case, because the migo also supports his assertion that he intended from the outset to acquire it for himself. But if the sender truly owes him nothing, he certainly intended to acquire it for the creditor to whom he was sent. Thus, the case below, where it is unknown whether the sender owes him, is one in which a migo that there never was such a matter allows him to claim both that the sender owes him and that he initially seized for himself. If, however, the money was delivered before witnesses, or if his only migo is that he returned it, we judge that the sender does not owe him and that he acquired the money for the creditor. The agent must therefore pay the creditor. Consider this carefully.
