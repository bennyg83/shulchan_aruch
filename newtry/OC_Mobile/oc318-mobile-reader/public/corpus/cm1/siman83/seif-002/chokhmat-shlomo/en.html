Seif 2: The law is with the lender. Note: At first glance this is difficult in light of the Rosh in the ninth chapter of Ketubot, in the sugya of Avimi. The Rosh writes there that without a migo the lender is not believed to say, “I accepted the payment toward another debt,” because a person is more likely voluntarily to pay a debt evidenced by a document than an oral debt. What does that prove? It may indeed be true that the borrower paid toward the documented debt, but the lender claims: “Although you paid intending to discharge the document, I received the money toward the oral debt,” and according to the rule here the law should be with him. Why then should he not be believed without a migo? At first glance this would show that the Rosh does not accept the rule stated here, which would mean that the Tur contradicts his father’s opinion without mentioning it. The matter can be explained. One who examines the Rosh there will see that earlier, regarding the case of the shepherd, he writes that without a migo one is not believed to say, “I seized it during his lifetime,” because any claim that property came into one’s possession without the owner’s consent is not a strong claim and is not believed without a migo. The Rosh’s words here can therefore be understood as follows. If the lender claims that the borrower gave him the money for the documented debt but that he himself applied it instead to the oral debt, he is in substance seizing the borrower’s money against the borrower’s will. The borrower did not give it for that purpose. Even though the lender has the legal power to apply the payment in that fashion, where the facts are disputed he is not believed without a migo because he claims to have taken it without the owner’s authorization. One might still ask why the lender should not be believed to say that the borrower himself gave the money for the oral debt. In that case he would be claiming possession with the owner’s consent and would seemingly not require a migo. To this the Rosh answers that such a claim is inherently weak, because a person is ordinarily more willing to pay first the debt evidenced by a document. Thus, if the lender says that he himself redirected the payment, he requires a migo because he took it contrary to the borrower’s designation; and if he says the borrower designated it to the oral debt, that is a weak factual claim. The Rosh’s words are therefore fully consistent with the rule here, and the law stated by the Tur is correct.
