In truth, he is correct that this is indeed the Rashba's view in a responsum: the borrower may plead, "I acquired it for myself immediately, and I never intended to acquire it for the person to whom it was sent." See the responsum of Maharam Sasson, siman 227, where he quotes this responsum at length. See there. But the Shach's view is that this applies even where the money was delivered before witnesses and the sender said, "Take this to so and so." This does not appear correct at all. Since we rule that "take" has the legal force of "acquire," it is as though the sender said, "Acquire it for so and so," and the recipient accepted it in silence. How can he then be believed to say that he first acquired it for himself? The rule is derived from the case of one who sees a found object, etc. Yet there the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 269 explicitly rule that if one says, "Acquire the found object for me," and the other takes it silently and later says, "I acquired it for myself," he is not believed, because silence is equivalent to admission. See the Bach there. What difference is there between the owner saying to him, "Acquire it for me," and Reuven saying, "Acquire it for Levi," where Shimon takes it silently? In either case he has acquired it for the designated person and is not believed to say, "I acquired it for myself." This is especially so because the Rashba derives his entire rule from the case where the person says, "I acquired it for myself first," and there it is explicit that if the instruction was "Acquire it for me," he is not believed. See Maharam Sasson, siman 227, who also questions the Rashba's responsum, although he did not note the point I made from the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. It therefore appears that since the Tur says he is "not believed," the ruling is based upon credibility. The Rashba maintains that when the transfer occurred before witnesses, so there is no migo, silence is treated as admission and no explanation afterward can overcome it. But the Rashba's case involved a transfer without witnesses, where the recipient has a migo that he could have denied the entire transaction. He is therefore believed by that migo to say that his intention was to acquire it for himself first. The presumption arising from silence is not so strong that we say a migo is ineffective against it. See Knesset HaGedolah, siman 125, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 5, who explicitly cites a responsum of the Geonim that if it was given before witnesses the recipient cannot acquire it for himself, because it had already been acquired for Yehudah. The matter is explicit as I wrote, and all the words of the Geonim are received tradition.
