The law follows the lender. If the debt backed by a guarantor has already matured while the debt without a guarantor is not yet due, the lender cannot say that an unspecified payment was applied to the unmatured debt. A person does not ordinarily leave a matured debt unpaid while paying one not yet due. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch therefore speak of a case where both debts had matured. Knesset HaGedolah infers that the same rule applies where neither debt has matured. I do not think the two situations are identical. If the borrower pays without specifying anything, then where both debts are still within term the lender may later apply the money to whichever debt he chooses, because the borrower did not designate the payment. If one debt is already due and the other is not, we apply the ordinary presumption that he paid the matured debt. The second clause is different. There the borrower expressly says, "Apply this money to debt A," while the lender says, "I will accept it only for debt B." If both debts are unmatured, the lender cannot force the borrower to prepay a different unmatured debt. The borrower can nevertheless force repayment of the debt he chooses, because repayment against a creditor's will is valid even before the due date, as explained in siman 74. A complication arises when payment is made with land and the two debts have different priority. Suppose the guaranteed debt is later in time and already due, while the earlier nonguaranteed debt is not yet due. The lender must initially accept the land for the matured guaranteed debt. But when the earlier debt later matures, can the lender retake that same land on account of the earlier priority, thereby leaving the guaranteed debt outstanding and collectible from the guarantor? In ordinary debt priority, a later creditor who first collects land must surrender it to the earlier creditor once the earlier debt matures. The Gemara in Arakhin concerning two successive valuations seems to say that when all the claims belong to consecrated property, a later consecrated claim that collects first keeps what it collected. Tosafot explains this because everything goes to consecrated property. Rambam says similarly that consecrated valuations are unlike ordinary debts and a later consecrated claim that collects first keeps its collection. This indicates that where all the claims belong to the same ultimate claimant, prior collection may remain effective. However, Tosafot also offers another explanation that the Arakhin case concerns movables or oral debts, where ordinary priority rules do not apply. Under that approach there is no proof that common ownership eliminates priority. Rambam's language suggests a different special law of consecrated property, based upon the power of consecration to displace liens and the rule that one who already paid according to his means need not pay again even if he later becomes wealthier. Therefore in ordinary private debts, if land subject to priority was initially collected on the later matured debt, there is strong reason that once the earlier debt matures the lender can retake that land for the earlier obligation, and the guaranteed later debt remains due. The exact result depends upon the ordinary rule for a later creditor who collected before an earlier creditor.
I will take it for myself. The Shach writes that even if the borrower gives the money to a messenger before witnesses and says, "Take this to so and so," or, "Give this to so and so," the messenger can still say, "From the beginning I intended to acquire it for myself," and retain it against his own claim. Although in siman 125 an agent cannot ordinarily do this after admitting that he originally accepted the money for the recipient, the present case differs because the messenger says he never intended to acquire for the recipient. Tumim objects from the law of a found object. If one says, "Acquire this lost object for me," and the lifter takes it silently, he cannot later say, "I lifted it for myself," because silence is treated as assent to the first person's request. If "deliver" is treated like "acquire," why should the messenger here be different? The answer appears already in the Shach in siman 269. The Gemara initially entertained that "give" could be legally equivalent to "acquire," yet even on that assumption it still distinguished the lifter's credibility. The formula "acquire for me" expressly directs the act of acquisition itself and creates a presumption that the lifter acted for the requester. By contrast, ordinary language such as "give" or "deliver," even where the law treats it as vesting rights in the recipient if the messenger actually intended to carry out the mission, does not remove the messenger's ability to say that in fact he never intended to acquire for the named recipient. Thus legal equivalence for transfer is not the same as evidentiary presumption regarding the messenger's subjective intent. The same applies here. If the borrower expressly said, "Acquire this for the creditor," and the messenger accepted silently, the messenger would not be believed to say he intended it for himself. But where the borrower merely said, "Take" or "deliver," the messenger can say that from the beginning he intended to retain it for his own claim.
