When the beit din attends to collection, etc. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 1 of Bava Kamma, siman 16, who rules that damages may be collected from encumbered property. The priority and method of collection follow the laws governing tort claims and the distinction between movable property, land, and property transferred to third parties.
They first collect from movable property, etc. The Sema explains that this is because a tortfeasor presumably prefers to pay movable property. His explanation is incorrect. The plain meaning of the Rambam, Mechaber, Tur, and many other authorities is that the beit din itself must first collect movable property when it is available. The rule is not dependent upon the preference of either party. The Rambam immediately continues that, under Talmudic law, if the tortfeasor dies leaving movable property and land, collection from the heirs is made from the land. If the tortfeasor himself could freely choose land despite available movable property, the heirs should not have weaker rights than he had. The structure of the ruling therefore shows that, during his lifetime, available movable property is collected first. It is true that Rabbeinu Tam, as understood by the Rosh, Mordechai, Tosafot, and other authorities, permits the tortfeasor to pay either land or movable property. Rabbeinu Tam interprets Rav Huna's statement, “either money or best-quality land,” as granting the payer a choice. The Rambam, however, understands it differently: The claimant's right is to money or movable property when such property exists, and otherwise to the best-quality land. As to money, we do not follow Rav Huna, for even bran or other low-grade movable property is legally equivalent to best-quality payment. This is explicit in the Baal HaTerumot and in the words of Rabbeinu Chananel, who derives from the Gemara that a creditor is not paid with land unless it is known that the debtor lacks money. Raavan and Ramah likewise indicate that where the tortfeasor has both movable property and land, the beit din compels payment from the movable property. The Nimukei Yosef also states expressly that this follows from the Rambam. The Bach argues that the Rosh required movable property first only for an ordinary creditor, not for damages, and that in tort claims the tortfeasor has the superior right of choice. In my opinion the Beit Yosef is correct. The Rosh's discussion of a creditor accommodates even Rabbeinu Tam's position, but once the Rif's view is accepted, Scripture's inclusion of “the equivalent of money” teaches that collection is fundamentally from movable property. It is therefore unreasonable to leave the choice entirely to the tortfeasor. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 14b further proves this. It initially understands “the equivalent of money” as property bearing responsibility, meaning land, and concludes that the baraita referring to collection from land concerns heirs. For the living tortfeasor, the beit din collects movable property. The Rambam adopts the Gemara's own term, writing, “When the beit din attends to collection,” precisely because his ruling is drawn from that passage. The initial possibility cannot mean that a tortfeasor lacking land would escape payment altogether; such a result is impossible and would allow anyone without land to damage freely. Rather, the Gemara's discussion concerns which class of property the beit din compels him to use when both are available. The Maggid Mishneh's comments in Hilchot Chovel, Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, and Hilchot Malveh also support this reading. In addition, an ordinary creditor may compel payment from movable property when it is available, as ruled above in siman 101. There is no reason to weaken an injured party, who in other respects has a stronger claim and collects from best-quality land. The special rule that a tortfeasor need not pay actual coins, even when he has them, follows from Scripture's inclusion of any property equivalent to money. It does not imply that he may force the injured party to accept land while movable property is available.
Even if he has money, he may satisfy the claim with bran. This means that even if he has coins, he need not pay coins and may give the least valuable movable property, unlike an ordinary creditor under siman 101. This follows the Rif, Rosh, and Rambam, who rule like Rav Pappa and Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua rather than Rabbeinu Tam. The Tur's abridgment of the Rosh also states that the law does not follow Rabbeinu Tam with respect to damages. The Rosh's proof is that Rav Kahana challenged Rav from the verse, “the carcass shall be his,” reasoning that if the tortfeasor can pay even bran, Scripture need not award the carcass specifically. Some textual and chronological details in the Rosh's formulation are difficult, for Rav Kahana was a student of Rav, not of Rava, and Rav Pappa and Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua were later than Rava. Nevertheless, the substantive conclusion is correct: The later authorities hold that any movable property is valid payment, even when coins are available. The Nimukei Yosef cites Ramah as limiting payment with bran to a case where the tortfeasor lacks coins. Maharam of Tiktin questions this, since the accepted view permits bran even when coins are available. It appears that Ramah may distinguish actual money from other movable property, but the consensus of the authorities is that “money” in this context includes coins, and even where coins are available the tortfeasor may pay with other movable property. This is also demonstrated by Rav Kahana's challenge, which would otherwise have an easy answer. Nevertheless, if the tortfeasor wishes to pay coins, the injured party cannot compel him instead to give particular movable property or land.
