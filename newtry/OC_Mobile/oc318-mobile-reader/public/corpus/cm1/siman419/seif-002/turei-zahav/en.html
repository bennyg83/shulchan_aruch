At the beginning of Bava Kamma the injured party, entitled to superior land, asks to receive a larger quantity of intermediate land. The tortfeasor answers: if you take according to your law, take at today's value; otherwise take it at the anticipated higher price. Rashi explains that the verse “he shall return” includes even bran, meaning that if the injured party wishes to take the tortfeasor's intermediate land he may do so at its future value, for it is still equivalent value. The Gemara initially assumes that without an expected rise in value the injured party could obviously compel acceptance of intermediate land, since his right is stronger even regarding superior land. Rav Acha bar Yaakov objects that allowing only the future price weakens the injured party's right regarding intermediate and inferior land. The discussion then applies a parallel analysis to a creditor and inferior land, rejecting future pricing because it would close the door before borrowers. The Rosh limits these objections to a case where the debtor reveals willingness to give the requested land if the claimant accepts it at the future value. If the debtor says the particular field is dear to him and he does not wish to give it at all, the court does not compel him, just as one who owns several superior fields may choose which to give. The Tur accordingly rules that where the tortfeasor owns two superior fields, the choice is his unless he discloses willingness to give one only at its anticipated higher price. I do not understand this. A person's willingness to sell a beloved item for an unusually high price does not show that it is not beloved. Bava Metzia itself says that household utensils are dear to their owner and are sold only for an excessive price. Here too the tortfeasor expressly conditions that he will give the field only at the higher value, which shows that he does not consent to give it under the ordinary law. There is a further difficulty. The Rosh says that if the tortfeasor simply refuses to give intermediate land and offers only superior land, he cannot be compelled. Yet Rashi states explicitly, before the Gemara's objection, that if no price increase were expected, the injured party could compel him to give intermediate land, because his right to superior land is even stronger. Thus, all agree that the claimant may choose a lower grade where it satisfies his claim. The Gemara's objection does not create that principle; it challenges only the debtor's attempt to impose the future price. The same is true of an ordinary creditor who asks for inferior land. He may say: had my money remained with me, I could have purchased inferior land at today's price; now that my money is in your hands, you cannot force me to accept only superior land against my will. The correct distinction is therefore this. Where the tortfeasor owns two fields both classified as superior and offers one while the injured party prefers the other, the Torah merely required “the best,” and both satisfy that requirement. The tortfeasor may choose, just as a debtor chooses which movable item to give. But where the injured party asks for intermediate land instead of superior land, or an ordinary creditor asks for inferior land instead of intermediate land, the debtor cannot refuse merely because he prefers to retain that grade. The claimant may take it at its present value. This appears logically correct, although it conflicts with the Rosh and the Shulchan Aruch. The Levush properly questioned the ruling. The Sema's attempt to extend the Rosh's distinction to movable property is also incorrect, because with movable property the rule is simply that any item may be tendered, without dependence on whether the debtor revealed willingness to give a particular article.
