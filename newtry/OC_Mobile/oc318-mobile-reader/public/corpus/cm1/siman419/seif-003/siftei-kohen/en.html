They collect for the injured party from the heirs' movable property. Rabbeinu Ephraim objected to the Rif that tort claims are uncommon. The Geonim enacted collection from heirs' movable property for creditors to prevent lenders from refusing to lend, and for a wife's ketubah to support marriage and procreation, but they did not enact it for damages. The Rosh answers that in later generations movable property has become equivalent to land for all purposes of security, like the Arabian camels and the ropes of Kimchonia from which a woman collects her ketubah. In my humble opinion, the Rosh's proof is not conclusive. The Gemara's rule concerning a ketubah may be unique because the woman relied upon those commercial assets when she married, since the household's principal business consisted of movable property. That reasoning does not necessarily apply to torts. Nevertheless, most authorities, including the Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber, rule that nowadays damages are collected from heirs' movable property, and Maharshal rules accordingly in chapter 1 of Bava Kamma, siman 39.
