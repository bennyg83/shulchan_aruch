We authenticate the document, etc. Tosafot and Rosh explain that because document authentication is rabbinic, Chazal were lenient enough to allow authentication outside the presence of the opposing party. Beit Yosef cites a responsum of Rashba giving another reason: the authentication witnesses are not testifying to the money written in the document but only to the handwriting of the signatories. Bnei Shmuel asks: According to Rashba's rationale, if the original document witnesses themselves testify to their own signatures, and we hold that in such a case they are effectively testifying to the substance of the debt written in the document, should authentication then require the opposing party's presence? He suggests that, according to Rashba, perhaps it does. That is strained. No major authority distinguishes the cases that way. I found that Rashba himself, in his novellae to Bava Kamma, combines both reasons: the witnesses signed in the document are already treated as though their testimony was examined in beit din, and the authentication witnesses are not testifying to the loan itself but only to the witnesses' handwriting. Why does Rashba need both? The answer follows the distinction explained above. If the original signatories themselves come and testify to their signatures, the explanation "they are only testifying to handwriting" is less applicable, because we hold such witnesses can be treated as testifying to the substance of the document. There we rely on the other reason: ordinary authentication is only rabbinic. But suppose the alleged signatories themselves are saying, "These are not our signatures," and independent witnesses come to authenticate the handwriting. According to what I explained above, that can create a Torah-level authentication problem. Then one can no longer rely on the simple statement that authentication is merely rabbinic. Rashba therefore adds the second rationale: the independent authenticating witnesses are not giving testimony about the underlying debt in the absence of the litigant. They are only identifying handwriting. The rule requiring testimony in the presence of the litigant is therefore less directly implicated. Thus Rashba's two reasons are complementary, not redundant. His wording itself supports the distinction developed above.
