Outside the litigant's presence. The Shach limits the leniency of validating outside the litigant's presence to ordinary witness signed documents, because either the validator is not testifying to the debt itself or signed witnesses are treated as though already examined in beit din and validation is only rabbinic. He says the borrower's own handwriting is different and requires Torah level validation, so it cannot be validated in his absence. Bach says similarly. In my view there is no such distinction. According to Rambam, witness signed documents are not biblically treated as already examined, yet Torah law still does not suspect forgery. The true basis is a Torah presumption that people do not ordinarily forge instruments and that we do not create an unsupported defect. That presumption can apply equally to the borrower's handwriting. The Sema in siman 110 also indicates that a deceased person's handwriting can be validated for his minor heirs through ordinary procedures. The Tur itself says that a testament written in the father's handwriting can be acted upon when beit din recognizes it, which would be impossible if the handwriting could never be validated outside the debtor's presence. The source cited by Beit Yosef concerning a testament that cannot be validated outside the party's presence is distinguishable. Such a testament may not have the ordinary status of a formal document and can depend on special factual proof. Therefore I conclude that a borrower's handwriting is not categorically more stringent than an ordinary document. The standard rules of rabbinic validation can apply, including validation outside his presence where otherwise permitted.
