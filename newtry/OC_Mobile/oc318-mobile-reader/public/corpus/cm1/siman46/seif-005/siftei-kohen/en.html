A deed may be authenticated even outside the presence of the opposing party. This appears limited to a deed signed by witnesses. Rashba explains that the authenticators testify only about the signatures, not about the debt, and Tosafot and the Rosh add that authentication is rabbinic because signed witnesses are treated by Torah law as having testified in beit din. Neither reason applies to the borrower’s own handwriting. There the testimony directly imposes liability upon him, and Rabbi Yochanan’s requirement that evidence be received in the owner’s presence applies. A handwritten obligation should therefore be authenticated only in his presence. This is also implied by the Sema and by Rav Sherira Gaon. Although the custom may be to authenticate even a borrower’s handwriting in his absence, that can be justified only as an accepted custom overriding the formal law. Where no such custom exists, one should not be lenient.
