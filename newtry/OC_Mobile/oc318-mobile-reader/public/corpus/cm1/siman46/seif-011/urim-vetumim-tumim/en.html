They prove this from the Gemara's question: if there is no other person, what should we do? The Gemara does not answer that the surviving witness should simply state, "I testify only to the handwriting." Maharibal asks that perhaps the case is one where no one else recognizes the surviving witness's signature. If he alone says, "This is my handwriting," he still needs a second person to join him, and there is no one. But the structure of the sugya answers this objection. The case is where two witnesses signed and one died. Rabbi is lenient and the Sages stringent. If no one besides the surviving witness recognizes his signature, then even according to Rabbi there is no authentication at all. The surviving signature is not independently established. Therefore there must be at least one other person who recognizes the living witness's handwriting. If so, why are the Sages stringent and why does the Gemara ask what can be done? The living witness could say, "I testify only to handwriting," and that other person could join him in identifying his signature. The same legal result as Rabbi's view would follow. The fact that the Gemara still needs a special remedy proves that the surviving witness cannot avoid the Sages' rule merely by verbally characterizing his testimony as handwriting testimony. There is, however, a possible practical question according to Rosh. Rosh holds that if the original witnesses no longer remember the transaction, the document can still be authenticated, but two witnesses are then needed for each signature because they are only testifying to handwriting. Suppose there is another person who joins the surviving witness in authenticating his own signature, and the surviving witness says he does not remember the underlying transaction. May he then join that other person in identifying the deceased witness's signature? There is logic to permit it. The surviving witness is no longer creating more than half the monetary force through his own word, because his own signature itself requires a second witness. He could therefore also testify about the deceased witness's handwriting. Rosh does not state this expressly, which gave me pause. Perhaps Rosh holds that ordinarily witnesses are presumed to remember the contents of the documents they sign, as explained above. If the surviving witness says, "I do not remember," he is believed only because he has a migo and thereby weakens the document. But here his claim of non-memory would improve the document's position, because if he remembered he could not authenticate the deceased signature in the same way, whereas by saying he forgot he creates a route to full authentication. A migo does not ordinarily support a claim used to strengthen the document against the opposing party. Therefore perhaps he is not believed to say he forgot in order to gain this procedural advantage against the presumption that signatories remember. Still, why should a witness not be believed concerning his own actual memory like any other factual testimony? This remains difficult. I then found Bach and Perishah state simply that if he does not remember, so that another person must join him concerning his own signature, he may indeed testify concerning the deceased witness's handwriting as well. Their view should be accepted over my hesitation. Accordingly, in that configuration the document can be authenticated.
