And the authentication judges, etc. Shach, seif katan 49, understands Rama to mean that all agree authentication judges who are relatives of the lender or borrower are invalid, even though some authorities validate relatives as authentication witnesses. Shach objects that there is no reason to distinguish, and that the proof from judges authenticating a document without reading it should apply equally to kinship of the judges. He also questions Rivash, who attributes to Rashba's novellae to Gittin the rule that authentication judges related to the parties are invalid. Shach says no such statement appears there and that the opposite seems indicated. This was written too hastily. Rashba states that witnesses who are relatives of the lender or borrower can be valid for authentication, deriving this from the rule that judges can sign the authentication even though they did not read the document itself. But Rashba then says that judges who are related to the litigants are certainly invalid in ordinary cases, while in a get there was room for greater leniency because Chazal were unusually lenient in get authentication, just as they permitted the agent's declaration and other special procedures. Thus Rashba's discussion itself distinguishes ordinary document authentication from the special rules of gittin. Rivash's attribution is therefore correct. Shach's question from judges authenticating without reading the document is also answerable. Where authentication witnesses are used, their names need not always be written into a standardized form that clearly preserves who they were. If relatives were categorically invalid and the judges could authenticate without even reading the parties' names, a serious practical failure could result. Chazal therefore had reason to be lenient with authentication witnesses. Judges are different. Their names are written in the authentication itself. Whether they are related to the parties can always be investigated later. The defect is therefore a matter capable of clarification and does not create the same systemic risk. There is also a practical reason for the distinction. People able to recognize a particular signature may be difficult to find. If only relatives recognize it, refusing their authentication testimony could cause the document to be lost entirely. Qualified judges, by contrast, are ordinarily available. There is no need to use relatives as judges when valid judges can be appointed. Accordingly, Chazal could be lenient with authentication witnesses while remaining strict with authentication judges. Rama's distinction is therefore well founded. This distinction also answers Shach's claim that the same proof from judges who do not read the document should validate related judges. The inability of judges to read the full document does not mean their identity is hidden. Their signatures are themselves part of the authentication record. The court reviewing the instrument can always inspect who the judges were and whether they were related to the parties. Authentication witnesses are different because the instrument does not always preserve their identities with the same clarity unless the court deliberately writes them. Thus the procedural safeguard differs. The leniency for authentication witnesses is therefore driven by necessity and traceability, not by a general rule that all kinship restrictions disappear in rabbinic authentication. This is exactly why Rama can validate relatives in one role while disqualifying them in the judicial role.
