Seif 10. One who signed a document and comes to testify concerning his handwriting in beit din. The Gemara in Ketubot 21 states that if one witness says, “This is my handwriting,” and the other says, “This is my handwriting,” they must join another witness with each signature. This is difficult according to Tosafot's first answer on 18a. Tosafot explain that witnesses are not believed through a migo to say they were coerced by monetary pressure because the alternative claim of coercion by threat to life is uncommon. This implies that absent that problem, a migo could be effective even against two witnesses. If so, why here, when each witness identifies only his own signature, are they not believed through the migo that each could instead identify the other witness's signature as well? Nor is this a migo to extract money, since with a document we do employ a migo even to collect. This requires study. There is another difficulty in the Gemara's question, “Is this not obvious?” Since the borrower claims with certainty that the document is invalid, while the witnesses authenticate the signatures and the only uncertainty is a legal one whether their testimony is considered testimony about the money in the document or merely about their handwriting, this is a doubt in law. If seizure is effective in a doubt in law, there should be a practical consequence here. This may therefore support the view that seizure does not help in an unresolved Talmudic question or other doubt in law. This too requires study. Seif 10. One who signed the document and comes to testify concerning his handwriting in beit din. Ketubot 21 asks whether Shmuel really holds this view and explains that Shmuel was concerned for an erroneous beit din: perhaps a court would think that although the halakha generally follows Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi against an individual colleague but not against several colleagues, in this matter it follows him even against several. Pnei Yehoshua asks why the Gemara must assume that a court would formulate such a general mistaken principle. His resolution is difficult in my view. Shmuel's concern is that the document will later be brought for collection and the judges will err. But at that point the judges will see that the borrower does not admit the document and instead claims forgery. How could they assume that he had admitted it before Shmuel? If they think he is now retracting and therefore treat him as one who has denied an earlier admission, what proves that? Even if they will not assume Shmuel erred, they could instead say that the borrower was not before Shmuel at all and that Shmuel authenticated the signatures merely because the authentication might become useful if the borrower later admitted writing the document. Once they hear that he does not admit it, they would have no reason to rely on that authentication. They might also say that Shmuel wrote the authentication because another witness could later appear concerning each signature. Why then must we suppose they think the halakha follows Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi even against several colleagues? Moreover, although we generally say one beit din does not critically reexamine another beit din, that itself rests on the rule that we do not suspect courts of legal error. Shmuel, however, is precisely concerned that courts can err. If so, the later court might make the opposite mistake and conclude that Shmuel's court erred. When we fear judicial error, one court may indeed reexamine another, as appears in Yevamot 106 and Bava Batra 138. It therefore appears that the Gemara means to distinguish Torah law from rabbinic law. In Torah law courts are presumed proficient and we do not fear legal error. In rabbinic law, however, courts are more prone to error because the rules are less familiar. Thus Shmuel's concern here is limited to a rabbinic matter. In ordinary Torah law the judges certainly know that the halakha follows Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi against one colleague but not against several. Only in a rabbinic rule might they mistakenly extend his authority even against several. There is therefore no contradiction between this passage and Bava Batra's rule that we do not fear an erroneous court. Bava Batra concerns Torah law, whereas here the issue is rabbinic. This also resolves a difficulty I raised elsewhere against Magen Avraham's defense of Rambam concerning the requirement to detail a vow because of concern for a mistaken court. I asked that we ordinarily do not fear such error. According to this distinction, the requirement to detail the vow is itself rabbinic, and in rabbinic matters we do fear judicial error. Another answer to Pnei Yehoshua is possible. The Gemara may avoid saying that the court will simply make an ordinary error, because generally we do not suspect courts of error. Here, however, there is a special reason for error because the matter is monetary. Although the halakha generally follows Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi against an individual but not against several, the superiority of the several is simply that they are the majority. In monetary law we do not follow a majority to extract money. Since Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi's position here would leave the money in the possessor's hands, a court might therefore think that in this monetary setting his view prevails even against several colleagues. That is a plausible error and does not contradict the general rule that courts are not presumed to err. Examine this carefully.
