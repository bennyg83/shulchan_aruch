Ink strokes, etc. The rule applies specifically to ink strokes or smearing, not to a full erasure. With a full erasure we fear that an earlier document together with its witnesses may have been written in the blank area between the original document and the authentication. The holder could have erased that earlier text and then written the present document in the margin. Most authorities say this. Sema and Shach ask why, even with mere ink strokes, we do not fear that a condition unfavorable to the creditor was originally written there and then removed. There is no difficulty. Why would the creditor choose such a clumsy method and leave a visible defect on the face of his document? Before bringing the document to beit din for authentication, he could simply cut away the area after the document and have the court write the authentication on the reverse or margin. If he instead erases an unfavorable condition in a visibly suspicious way, he exposes the forgery to everyone. There is no reason to assume such foolish conduct. Shach has difficulty with Rashbam, who appears to permit even a complete erasure. He explains Rashbam according to his own theory that we do not suspect a holder of deliberately creating a defect that could invalidate his instrument. I do not find that persuasive. The better explanation follows one answer in Tosafot, Bava Batra 163b. According to that approach, witnesses are not permitted to write a one-line document together with its witnesses while leaving a large blank margin above, because the holder could later cut away the original text and write a new document above the surviving signatures. If so, suppose there is only one blank line between the document and the authentication. Even if that line were fully erased, we need not fear that a separate one-line document with its witnesses had originally stood there. Proper witnesses would not have created such a dangerous form with a large usable margin. The same analysis extends to larger gaps if the ink smearing or erasure is arranged strategically. For two blank lines, one can smear the line nearest the authentication while leaving the upper line blank. Then if the holder presents the original document, the large blank margin makes it implausible that a one-line document with witnesses once stood in the erased line. If he cuts away the upper document and tries to use the remaining line, the surviving erasure itself can generate the concern that a prior one-line document had existed there. For three lines, one can stagger the smearing so that every possible cutting strategy leaves an arrangement inconsistent with a normal original one-line document. The precise physical details depend on how much blank margin witnesses are allowed to leave, but the principle is that the visible pattern of erased and blank areas can make any reconstruction of an earlier valid mini-document implausible. However, a closer reading of Rashbam makes an even simpler explanation possible. Rashbam says that if an authentication comes before us with two blank lines that were not smeared, it is invalid because the form was not executed according to Chazal. Why does he speak of two blank lines? Even one blank line can be problematic under the ordinary document rules. It appears Rashbam holds that, just as witnesses cannot physically place their signatures with absolute precision immediately beneath the text, authentication judges also require some ordinary small spacing. Therefore a single line may be tolerated structurally, while two blank lines create a genuine defect. Rashbam's whole discussion is according to Rav, who holds that from a one-line gap near an authentication we do not derive new rights in the same way as from the body of the document. Under Rav, if two lines are present, smearing one line can reduce the usable gap to the equivalent of one line and remove the danger. This also explains why Rashbam can allow ink treatment in configurations that would be more problematic according to R. Yochanan. According to R. Yochanan, we do derive law from an authentication in situations where Rav would not. Then the Tosafot forgery objection becomes stronger and simple smearing may not suffice. Thus Rashbam need not actually disagree with the practical law. He may be explaining Rav's position. Once we rule like R. Yochanan that the authentication can have greater evidentiary force, the stricter treatment of erasure applies and the authorities' formulation of "ink strokes" rather than full erasure becomes necessary. This also explains why Rashbam and Tosafot seem to disagree about the usefulness of smearing. Their positions track the underlying dispute about what can be derived from the authentication and how much weight the concluding lines carry. Accordingly, in practical halachah, mere ink strokes that visibly render the blank space unusable can be effective. A full erasure is more dangerous because it may conceal an earlier document and witnesses, and therefore it is not accepted absent stronger safeguards. The technical point about the width of the spaces is essential. A forger can profit only if, after cutting or rewriting, the remaining paper can still present a facially valid relationship between text, witnesses, and authentication. The halachic measurements of one line and two lines are designed precisely to determine when that is possible. Where a blank of one normal line remains, there are configurations in which the paper cannot be converted into a valid new document because the signatures would no longer stand in an acceptable place relative to the newly written text. Where two lines remain, there is enough room to create both substantive wording and a witness structure. That is why the law becomes much stricter. Rashbam's tolerance of smearing therefore must be understood against his assumptions about what can legally be learned from the neighboring lines. If the surviving structure cannot support a new enforceable claim, the possibility that something once stood in the erased area is irrelevant. A court does not invalidate a document because of every imaginable historical possibility. It invalidates only where the present physical form enables a plausible forgery with legal effect. This also answers the concern that the holder might erase existing witnesses and write a one-line document in their place. If that manipulation leaves two adjacent erased lines, the resulting paper itself becomes defective because the court can say an earlier two-line document may have existed there. The attempted forgery destroys its own evidentiary structure. Likewise, if the forger cuts diagonally to preserve a larger upper margin, the unusual cut itself is visible and creates suspicion. Document law assumes ordinary rectangular trimming and ordinary scribal practice, not elaborate physical reconstruction that advertises manipulation. Another possible arrangement is to leave the middle line unsmeared while smearing the lines above and below in an alternating pattern. Then any attempt to use the unsmeared space as a new document leaves an erased line in a position that triggers the very concern the forger is trying to avoid. The exact drawing is difficult to describe verbally, but the principle is straightforward: the court can arrange the ink strokes so every potential writing zone is blocked by a neighboring defect that invalidates a reconstructed instrument. This is why the Gemara's advice can work even for gaps larger than one line. Still, all of this fits Rav better than the final halachah. Under Rav, a one-line document with its witnesses in the relevant configuration does not gain the same force from the authentication, so certain forgery routes are harmless. Under R. Yochanan, whose view we follow, those routes can become effective. Therefore later authorities correctly restrict the valid cure to visible ink strokes and reject a full erasure. The difference between "smearing" and "erasure" is not cosmetic. Smearing announces that the space was intentionally rendered unusable. A full erasure conceals what was there and creates a blank surface on which a different history can be imagined. That is why smearing is safer. The court sees that no one attempted to hide prior writing. Instead, the judges or scribe deliberately destroyed the blank space to prevent insertion. This is analogous to drawing lines through unused portions of a modern legal instrument. The mark itself is protective evidence. A clean erasure does the opposite: it removes evidence. Therefore, unless the shape of the remaining document independently excludes every plausible forgery, a clean erasure is unacceptable. This distinction also answers Sema's original question about an erased detrimental condition. With visible smearing, there is no reason to suppose an unfavorable condition stood there, because the purpose of the smearing is obvious and public. With a clean erasure, concealment is the very concern. Thus the two forms should not be treated alike.
And we are not concerned, etc. Shach understands the phrase "beit din does not sign on ink smearing" to mean that the smearing appears before the written authentication formula, not immediately adjacent to the judges' signatures. In that configuration the judges plainly signed the authentication text, not the smearing. But if there is blank space between the authentication text and the judges' signatures and that space is smeared, Shach would invalidate, because perhaps the judges signed only upon the smearing. This is persuasive. There is also another way to explain why smearing before the authentication text is safe. If the judges did not intend to authenticate the document, they had no reason to preserve the smearing at all. They could simply cut it away and place the authentication on the side or reverse. By contrast, where the smearing lies between the authentication and their signatures, cutting it away would destroy the already written authentication and the scribe's work. The judges may therefore leave it there and sign beneath it. That creates a real ambiguity whether they signed the authentication or merely the smearing. Accordingly, smearing between the authentication text and the judges' signatures should indeed invalidate.
