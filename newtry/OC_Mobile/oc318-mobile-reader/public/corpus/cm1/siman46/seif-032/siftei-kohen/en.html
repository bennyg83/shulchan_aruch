Ink lines, and so forth. The Maggid Mishneh explains that these lines are the meaning of tiyuta. This is correct, because tiyuta cannot mean filling the entire blank area with ink. If it did, we would suspect that another document had originally been written there and authenticated, after which the present document was written above it and the old text covered with ink. The Ramban’s novellae and Tosafot, folio 163a, explain similarly. Rabbeinu Tam interprets tiyuta as dots or lines through which some of the blank remains visible, making it evident that no earlier writing was erased. If the area were entirely blotted with ink so that one could not tell whether anything had been erased, and the document were nevertheless valid, a person could blot out the entire document together with the witnesses below, write a new document in the upper margin, forge the witness signatures, and rely upon the authentication below. Hagahot Ashri states the same. In my humble opinion, it is strained to interpret tiyuta as mere lines. The term ordinarily implies an area filled with ink. Rashbam explicitly explains that one soils it with ink, and that the witnesses sign concerning the blotting, testifying that the area was inked and erased so the holder should not be suspected. The Nimukei Yosef writes similarly, as cited by the Beit Yosef above in siman 45, seif 7. The objection of Tosafot and the Maggid Mishneh is not difficult. We do not generally suspect that a person will erase both the document and its witnesses, because the judges who authenticated it may later expose the forgery by testifying that they never authenticated witnesses who signed on an erasure, only witnesses who signed on ordinary paper. The forger would then lose his document entirely and have no document bearing genuine witnesses. I explain this below in seif katan 89. This appears clear. The Rambam’s phrase “ink lines” need not have been adopted because of Tosafot’s objection. As I write below in seif katan 88, the Rambam does not suspect that the entire document and witnesses were erased. Rather, he invalidates because perhaps a term adverse to the holder had been written in the blank area. He therefore explains tiyuta as lines. According to my explanation that tiyuta may mean complete ink coverage, one can answer that people were not accustomed to write adverse terms after the witnesses’ signatures. Although a receipt written after the signatures is valid, as stated below in siman 65, seif katan 71, we do not suspect that a partial-payment receipt or other adverse term had been written there, because people do not ordinarily write after the signatures. Tosafot and the Maggid Mishneh themselves must accept this possibility. Otherwise, they could have proved from this concern alone that the passage speaks of lines.
We do not suspect that the beit din authenticated the ink lines. The Sema explains that this concern applies only to witnesses, not to authentication judges, because there is no reason to suspect the holder when an adverse term is not ordinarily written after the witnesses’ signatures. His words are difficult, because we are discussing visible lines, which themselves show that nothing had been written there. Perhaps there is a scribal error and these words belong in the preceding seif katan, where he cites Tosafot. The question there would be why we invalidate from concern that the document was erased rather than from concern that an adverse term had been written there, and he answers that people do not commonly write such terms after the witnesses’ signatures. Even there, however, his answer is not compelling. It appears that Tosafot indeed hold the document invalid for that reason. They could not prove the interpretation from it because perhaps the law does not invalidate for such a concern, given that adverse terms are not ordinarily written after signatures. They therefore argue that if even an indistinguishable erasure were valid, we would have to suspect that the entire document was erased and a new document written in the upper margin. It follows that an erasure not recognizable as such is invalid because an adverse term might have been there. Accordingly, even if it is known that the entire document was not erased and replaced, such as where the authentication states, “We, the beit din, authenticate the signatures of the witnesses signed on this document, which begins at the top of the parchment,” the authentication is still invalid because an adverse term may have occupied the erased space. This appears to be the view of Tosafot, the Maggid Mishneh, the Rambam, and the Mechaber, contrary to the Sema. Under the alternative explanation of the passage that I give above in seif katan 88, however, it is valid. See there.
Some say that we are concerned, and so forth. The Beit Yosef writes that this is the view of the Rif and Rosh because they omit the entire discussion concerning Rav’s statement. This proof is not decisive. They may hold that the Gemara’s discussion merely explains Rav’s reasoning. The Tur indeed writes without qualification that if the judges left a gap and filled it with tiyuta, the authentication is valid because a beit din does not sign concerning tiyuta. This indicates that the Rosh also accepts the ruling. Moreover, all editions of the Rif before us include the discussion that a beit din does not sign concerning tiyuta. Tosafot, in one answer, suggest that Rabbi Yochanan disagrees with all of Rav’s ruling. Hagahot Ashri similarly records that Rabbeinu Yitzchak was uncertain whether Rabbi Yochanan disagrees, but found it likely that he certainly does, holding that even a beit din may sign concerning tiyuta. Therefore, one blank or inked line is invalid, because the law follows Rabbi Yochanan. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 27 of Hilchot Malveh writes that according to the Rambam all agree that if lines were drawn it is valid. Nevertheless, the Halachot appear to indicate that tiyuta does not help according to Rabbi Yochanan, because we must suspect that the judges signed concerning the tiyuta, and the Rif omitted this discussion. The Rashba likewise writes that according to Rabbi Yochanan it is invalid even when tiyuta was made. The Maggid Mishneh concludes that the Rambam’s reasoning is convincing. For practical law, the Rambam’s ruling appears primary. First, why assume Rabbi Yochanan disputes Rav and create an unnecessarily broad dispute? Second, the Gemara can be explained differently from Rashbam. Rashbam explains, “A beit din does not sign on tiyuta,” to mean that judges do not ordinarily sign concerning ink blotting, although witnesses might sign concerning anything, even ignoramuses. The Rambam’s and Mechaber’s wording appears to follow this. But the Gemara’s phrase is not well suited to that reading. It should have said that a beit din does not act in that fashion, and the word “sign” is awkward. It is also extremely difficult to say that witnesses sign concerning lines, according to the Rambam and those who interpret tiyuta as lines. Why would they sign, and what testimony can they give about meaningless scratches? If there is no substantive matter there, the concepts of testimony and signature do not apply. Even according to my explanation above in seif katan 85 that tiyuta means full ink coverage, and Rashbam’s explanation that they testify so the holder will not be suspected, it is unclear why we assume their purpose is to protect him rather than the opposite. Furthermore, why does the Gemara ask that one could also make tiyuta between the witnesses and the authentication? If the meaning is that witnesses signed to show that the blank had been intentionally inked, tiyuta between the document and witnesses should plainly be invalid. Otherwise, whenever a condition or adverse term appeared at the end of a document before the signatures, the holder could blot it out. Rather, the Gemara should be explained as follows. “People may say that the witnesses signed concerning the tiyuta” means that because the witnesses’ signatures appear after the inked area, perhaps they also attested to an adverse term that had been written before them. Certainly we do not suspect an adverse term in tiyuta placed at the very end of the document after the witnesses’ signatures, because the holder could simply have cut it away and no one would know. The Gemara then asks that between the witnesses and the authentication we should likewise say that the beit din signed concerning the tiyuta, meaning perhaps some adverse statement had been written there and the judges signed concerning it, since it could not have been cut away without losing the authentication. The Gemara answers that a beit din does not sign concerning tiyuta. That is, the judges do not sign immediately after it. They first write that they have authenticated the document, and their signatures relate to that authentication, not to the tiyuta preceding it. This explanation also shows why the Rif and Rosh properly omitted the discussion. It creates no practical distinction, because the answer rests upon an obvious feature of an authentication. It also follows that if lines were made between the document and its witnesses, the document is valid. One should not object that if a two-line space between document and witnesses invalidates, lines should be drawn there. The same question could be asked why the space is not filled with relatives, unfit persons, or a repetition of the document’s concluding words. The answer is that these methods indeed work, but the rule addresses a case where nothing was done. Likewise here, if no lines were drawn and the space was blank or fully blotted with ink, the document is invalid, whereas when the judges blot the gap before the authentication it is valid. Even according to those who interpret tiyuta as lines, the answer “a beit din does not sign concerning tiyuta” can mean that the judges do not sign directly after the lines, but first state the authentication. The Rif and Rosh omitted the matter because the Gemara treats this as obvious. The Rambam’s ruling is therefore primary. I later saw that the Ramban’s novellae object to one who explains that a beit din does not sign concerning tiyuta because it states in the authentication what it is authenticating. He argues that a beit din need mention only the witnesses’ signatures, and a general statement, “This document was authenticated before us,” is valid. Judges may also sign without reading the document or knowing its contents, as taught in Shenei Dayanei Gezerot. I do not understand the objection. Even if they did not read the document, they write immediately before their signatures that this document was authenticated, and thus identify what they signed concerning. What was written before their signatures is the authentication. Witnesses, by contrast, have no statement immediately before their signatures other than the tiyuta itself, so one could say they signed concerning it. It follows that if tiyuta appears within the authentication after the authentication text but before the judges’ signatures, the authentication is invalid, because one could say the judges signed concerning the tiyuta. This is why the Gemara and authorities validate only a gap between the witnesses and the authentication, not a gap between the authentication and the judges.
