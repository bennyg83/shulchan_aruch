He forged and signed. Beit Yosef cites Meisharim that the same concern applies to a plotting witness. Tosafot leaves open whether a person later disqualified for some unrelated transgression, not involving theft or financial corruption, should be treated similarly. In my view such a person resembles a relative rather than a thief. One disqualified for robbery is suspected of lying for financial benefit, so if his signature is validated only after he became a robber we can suspect that he forged it now. A person wicked through another type of transgression is disqualified by the Torah's decree, "Do not place a wicked person as a witness," but he is not necessarily suspected of lying about unrelated monetary matters. The fact that a person is suspected in one domain does not automatically make him suspected in every other domain. Nimukei Yosef states that one who swears falsely about an obviously false statement can be disqualified as a witness by Torah law without thereby being treated like a robber who is suspected in matters of monetary temptation. Therefore if such a person signed while still qualified and only afterward became wicked through a nonfinancial offense, his earlier signature can be validated just as the signature of a person who later became related can be. His present statutory disqualification does not create a presumption that the old signature was forged.
