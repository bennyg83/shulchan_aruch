However, if she later admits it. The plain wording implies that even where the original kol was never formally established before beit din, and even if the court later investigates and finds no legally sufficient kol, nevertheless her later admission that she had already been betrothed, together with the earlier partial report, makes her prohibited by her own statement. The Bach understands it this way. But one who studies the underlying responsum of the Rosh will see that there was no prior kol before the later kiddushin. A man named Yaakov spread a report only after Rabbi Moshe had already betrothed her, claiming that he had betrothed her earlier. The Rosh writes that even a full kol arising after the later marriage is disregarded, and indeed the incident did not even involve a legally valid kol. Nor do I think the Rosh intended to make her legally prohibited by her own admission in the sense that the husband would be forbidden to remain with her. When he writes that even if she explicitly admitted receiving kiddushin from Yaakov, she could not thereby prohibit herself to the man who had already betrothed her, he continues that we would merely tell the husband that since according to her own words she regards herself as a married woman, it would be proper to divorce her without a ketubah. This sounds like advice and a pious course, not a strict prohibition upon him. In the actual case she did not even make a full admission. She had written a false get in Yaakov's name because she feared he would procure false witnesses, and she wished to have a document with which to counter their plot. That conduct did not amount to an admission that she had ever accepted kiddushin from him. This understanding of the Rosh is also supported by his rulings in Yevamot and Ketubot, where he follows Ri that even a woman who says to her husband, "I am defiled to you," is not automatically believed, because the Sages saw that later generations might use such claims to escape a husband. One of the Nedarim cases was especially ugly, involving a woman and an adulterer hiding together, yet she was still permitted to her husband under the stated reasoning. If so, a mere partial kol certainly should not make her forbidden. See Mahari's rulings, siman 222, cited below in siman 115:6, concerning circumstances that support her claim. According to the Rosh's approach, the phrase that she made herself a prohibited object means only a basis for proper conduct and advice, not a strict legal prohibition. The Tur further cites the Rosh that although a mere rumor does not require a get, if she nevertheless received a get because of such a rumor and later became betrothed to another man who died, she may not return to the first. People might assume the first get was valid and say that he is taking back his divorcee after she became betrothed to another. A public announcement would not fully cure the problem because some people may hear about the get and not the announcement. The Rama already cites this briefly above in siman 10:1. See also what I will write in siman 48:6. The Beit Yosef in siman 42 cites Rashba that one who spreads a report of kiddushin can be compelled either to substantiate his words or to give a get.
