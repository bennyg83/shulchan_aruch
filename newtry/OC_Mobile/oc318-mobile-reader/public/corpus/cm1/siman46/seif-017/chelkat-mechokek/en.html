Nevertheless, if she later admits it. The plain wording implies that this applies even if the original rumor had never been formally established in beit din, and even if now, when beit din seeks to investigate, it cannot verify a legally significant rumor. Nevertheless, since she admits that she had previously been betrothed, she has rendered herself a prohibited object in combination with the earlier rumor, even though that rumor was not complete. Bach writes similarly: since there was at least some initial rumor that she had been betrothed to another man, her own admission renders her prohibited to herself. But one who examines the responsum itself will see that there was no prior rumor before the later betrothal. Rather, the man named Yaakov spread a rumor only after R' Moshe had betrothed her, claiming that he had already betrothed her previously. The Rosh writes explicitly: "Moreover, even had it been a complete rumor, a rumor arising after marriage is disregarded, as stated in the final chapter of Gittin. So too here, since R' Moshe already betrothed her, we no longer consider a rumor that arose afterward." Indeed, there was not even a legally significant rumor after the marriage, as the Rosh writes immediately before: "There is no concern of prohibition because of the rumor he spread. First, this is not considered a rumor, as the Talmud says," etc. "If a man says, 'I betrothed so-and-so,' and she says, 'You did not betroth me,' that is not a rumor." Nor does it appear that the Rosh intended to render her legally prohibited to herself by the principle that a person can render herself a prohibited object, in the sense that others must prevent her from eating something prohibited to her. For the Rosh writes: "Even if she had expressly admitted that she accepted kiddushin from him, she has no power to prohibit herself to the man who already betrothed her, and there would be no prohibition if he had relations with her. We would only say that she rendered herself a prohibited object, since she says that she is a married woman, and we would not actively feed her a prohibition; he should divorce her without a kesubah." If the Rosh meant that she was legally forbidden, he should have said directly that she is prohibited by law, that although the husband himself would not violate a prohibition by relations, she is nevertheless forbidden to him from her side and he may not facilitate her prohibition, and therefore he must divorce her without a kesubah. It therefore appears that the Rosh means only advice: strictly speaking, even after a complete admission there is no prohibition if he has relations with her, but one would tell him that since according to her own words she is a prohibited woman, even if he is not obligated to believe her, why remain married to such a woman? It is better to divorce her without a kesubah. Since here there was not even a complete admission, she is not a prohibited object even according to her own account, because she explained why she acted as she did. The responsum explains that she had written a false bill of divorce in the name of the same Yaakov who claimed to have previously betrothed her, so that if Yaakov later produced false witnesses to the alleged kiddushin she could produce the false divorce and defeat his scheme. Writing that false divorce therefore was not an admission that she had accepted his kiddushin; rather, seeing that he was seeking false witnesses and planning harm, she devised her own improper stratagem to frustrate him and avoid embarrassment if false witnesses appeared. It appears possible to prove that this is the Rosh's intent, unlike the way Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Bach understood him. In his rulings in Yevamos, chapter 2, seif katan 8, and in Kesubos, chapter Af Al Pi, the Rosh agrees with Ri in Tosafos, s.v. "R' Yehuda said," Kesubos 27b-28a, that the incidents at the end of Nedarim 91a-b concern a wife who says to her husband, "I am impure to you," and nevertheless the Gemara rules that the wife remains permitted for the reason given there by Ri and Rosh: the Sages saw the deterioration of the generations, where women set their eyes upon other men and most women who make such claims lie, and therefore they deemed them permitted. There can hardly be a more disgraceful circumstance than one of those incidents, in which a woman and the adulterer were hiding together and the adulterer broke through the barrier where they had hidden and fled, and she admitted that she had become impure; nevertheless the Gemara says the woman is permitted. Although the Gemara gives a reason that if he had actually sinned the adulterer would have hidden inside the house so that the husband not notice him, this still certainly constitutes at least a rumor. All the more so, if there was merely a partial rumor, why should she become forbidden to her husband? See Piskei Mahari, siman 222, cited below in siman 115, seif 6, that where there are strong indications she is believed when she says, "I am impure to you." That applies specifically to indications like those incidents in Nedarim and follows Rashba's interpretation that the woman there is permitted to the alleged adulterer, unlike Ri. The Rosh, however, follows Ri. Necessarily, when the Rosh writes here that she "rendered herself a prohibited object," he means only that it is proper and advisable to separate, not that she becomes legally forbidden. Thus below, siman 115, seif 6, the ruling is that if the husband believes her words he must divorce her.
Tur cites a responsum of the Rosh: although a mere rumor does not require a bill of divorce, if in fact a bill of divorce was given because of such a rumor and the woman was afterward betrothed to another man who died, she may not return to the first man. People will assume that the first divorce was legally required and will say that he is taking back his divorced wife after she was betrothed to another. One cannot solve this by public announcement that the first divorce had been unnecessary, because some will hear of the divorce and not hear the announcement.
