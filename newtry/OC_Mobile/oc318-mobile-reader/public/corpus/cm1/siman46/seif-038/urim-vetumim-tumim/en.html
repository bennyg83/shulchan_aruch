That the borrower was a minor, etc. The rule rests on the presumption that witnesses do not sign unless the transaction was performed by an adult. In my novellae to Ketubot I questioned this as follows. We rule that a leap year may be declared until Nisan, and in Orach Chaim we rule that one born in Adar of a year that later becomes leap does not become thirteen until the second Adar. It is therefore possible that the witnesses allowed him to sign and make a kinyan in Adar because at that time they did not know the year would be declared leap. According to the calendar as it then stood he had already reached thirteen and was an adult. Beit din subsequently decided to add a second Adar. Retroactively, the month in which the sale was executed was Adar I, and he was still a minor. The witnesses could then truthfully say that he was a minor at the time of the sale without this reflecting any misconduct on their part. What could they have done, since the leap year had not yet been declared? I discussed this there at length. For us, who know the fixed calendar long in advance, I have abbreviated the discussion because it has no practical consequence.
He may retract. The Shach objects and writes that Rivash mentioned this only as a supporting possibility and not as a rule with any real legal foundation. See what I wrote above, where the Rif's language gives some support to Rivash. Still, as practical law I do not know how this can stand. We rule like Rambam that a document signed by one witness has legal effect. If that witness can later retract, how can beit din extract money today, when tomorrow the witness may appear and retract? This is difficult to understand. Perhaps Rivash holds that where one holds a document signed by one witness the debtor cannot claim payment, because the result would be a case in which one is obligated to swear but cannot swear and therefore pays. If the single witness himself says that the debt was paid, however, he is believed, since one witness can exempt from an oath. According to this, the witness has a migo to say that the debt was paid. Tosafos asked a parallel question concerning two witnesses and answered that we do not say a joint migo for two people. That answer does not apply to one witness. This requires study. Rama writes in Responsa, siman 110, concerning a receipt signed by two witnesses where the handwriting of the second witness was authenticated but that of the first was not. The second witness testified that when he signed, the first witness's signature was already present, and the scribe also testified that he wrote the receipt, that the kinyan concerning the receipt was performed before him, and that the witnesses signed it. Rama validated the receipt for three reasons. First, the handwriting of the scribe together with one witness can join for collection from unencumbered property when the scribe is before us and testifies that it is his handwriting, and the same should apply to proof of payment. The Shach objects that the scribe's handwriting helps only where the scribe testifies that he knows the underlying loan, as in a get where he knows the underlying divorce, not merely because he recognizes his handwriting. His objection is correct. It is possible that Rama also meant this, because in that case the scribe knew the underlying transaction was genuine. Second, Rama writes that since the scribe knew of the kinyan he joins with the authenticated witness. For purposes of collecting from unencumbered property we do not need specifically the witnesses who received the kinyan, and here proof of payment against a ketubah is equivalent to collection from unencumbered property. The Shach objects that there are major consequences for collection from purchasers, as is evident from Bava Metzia and below, siman 65, seif 18. I do not understand his objection. The entire Gemara there precedes Shmuel's rule that one who sells a debt document may later forgive the debt. We now rule like Shmuel, and therefore where the woman herself admits that a receipt is genuine we return it to the husband, because there is no concern of collusion. Since two witnesses here testify to payment, there is no greater forgiveness than this. Even if she sold the ketubah, she caused her own loss, for she always retained the power to forgive it. Testimony that a kinyan was made on a receipt is a complete waiver. There is therefore no reason to worry about the public notice normally required for collection from purchasers. Especially regarding a kinyan, the reason we require specifically the witnesses who received the kinyan before collecting from encumbered property is, as Rashba explains, that otherwise the creditor could produce multiple writings and collect repeatedly. That concern does not exist regarding proof of payment. The Shach's objection therefore has no force. Third, Rama writes that since the second witness says the first witness's signature was already present before he signed, even though he does not recognize the signature, we may reason like the rule of a reliable Samaritan witness: the second witness would not have signed beside him unless the first signature were genuine. This is regarded as testimony concerning the first signature and can combine with the scribe's testimony recognizing it. The Shach objects with a dilemma. If the second witness actually saw the first witness sign and merely cannot now recognize the handwriting, there is no need for this analogy, because seeing him sign is itself the strongest possible authentication, and the fact that his own signature appears beside it identifies the very signature he saw. If he did not see the first witness sign, but merely found a signature already on the paper when he came to sign, what does this prove? Perhaps the husband forged a first witness's signature and the second witness then innocently signed beside it. This is a strong objection, and Rama's third reason requires study.
