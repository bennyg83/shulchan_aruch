But one witness, etc. This requires study. We rule in siman 51 like the Rambam and his supporters that one witness in writing obligates an oath, making him equivalent to oral testimony. How then can he retract? The Rivash, siman 127, suggests only as a possibility that even the Rambam might permit a single written witness to retract, and he did not rely on that reason alone. He permitted the particular case because the Yerushalmi, Ramban, and Ramach hold that one written witness is entirely ineffective; because the woman had already remarried; because the witness had not signed at the husband's direction and the document had not been delivered to him, so it was testimony from writing rather than from the witnesses' mouths; and because the witness retracted with an explanation that he signed under threats and fear. Responsa authors often add a possible argument to other grounds even when it is not independently accepted. Since we rule in siman 51 that one written witness has the status of one oral witness, it is clear that he cannot retract when he wrote his testimony as a formal deed at the litigant's direction, so that the defect of testimony from writing does not apply. The Rama, responsum 110, describes a partial ketubah receipt. Rabbi Isaac of Prague certified that two witnesses authenticated the signature of Shemayah son of Meir, one signer of the receipt; Shemayah himself also identified his signature, remembered the receipt, and said he signed after the first witness. Mordechai son of Yehudah, the cantor, testified that he wrote the receipt, saw the woman accept a formal acquisition before the two signers, and saw them sign. The Rama offered three grounds for validation: the cantor as scribe joins Shemayah as witness; the cantor's oral recollection of the acquisition joins the signed witness; and Shemayah's recollection that the first witness signed before him, together with the cantor's recognition of that signature, authenticates both signatures. None of these grounds is satisfactory. A scribe's writing does not join a witness, as I wrote above in seif 14, seif katan 41; Rabbeinu Yerucham meant that the scribe appears and testifies orally. The second ground may create oral testimony, but it does not authenticate the receipt, a distinction affecting collection from purchasers. A found receipt is not returned even when the woman admits it, because of possible collusion against purchasers, as explained in siman 65, seif 18, seif katan 49. Even as against the woman herself, the ground fails for the reason explained below. The Rambam's rule that one who says, I made an acquisition from him concerning the loan, creates a full deed does not help, because the Rashba's responsum, siman 1073, says only that such testimony defeats denial of the underlying transaction, while the debtor may still claim payment. It therefore helps only against unencumbered property and adds nothing to the first argument. The third ground is also unclear. If Shemayah did not see the first witness sign but merely found the signature already present, the comparison to a gentile witness is inapt. In Gittin both signatures are recognized, and the question is only whether the gentile is reliable; because he is biblically competent, the Sages believed the Jewish witness's statement in a rabbinic matter. Here the first signature itself is unrecognized. If the first witness signed before Shemayah and Shemayah saw it, his present inability to recognize the handwriting is irrelevant. His certain recollection is the strongest recognition, effective even for biblical authentication and even if based on a unique identifying feature of the deed. However, Rabbi Isaac's written report is itself only testimony from another witness. Even if Rabbi Isaac's signature is recognized, his report is no better than his oral testimony before beit din that others testified before him, which does not authenticate a deed. Perhaps Rabbi Isaac was then the rabbi of Prague and acted under the local custom allowing a rabbi to authenticate alone. Even so, that custom may authorize only direct authentication by the rabbi, not his report of testimony given before him, especially where it concerns only one signature and therefore only half an authentication. The matter requires study.
