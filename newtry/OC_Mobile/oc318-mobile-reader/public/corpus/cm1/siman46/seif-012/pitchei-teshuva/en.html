Who became his son in law. See Baer Heitev, who explains that disqualification through kinship does not create suspicion of forgery. See Responsa Neot Deshe, siman 68, who writes that if a person signed a document before becoming financially interested and only later became interested, witnesses must have seen the document in his possession before he acquired the interest, just as in the case of a robber. As the Sema writes at the beginning of siman 37, it is clear that a financially interested witness is disqualified because we suspect him of lying. See what I write there in seif katan 1. This is also explicit in Bava Batra 159. The Gemara there first brings the case of one who became a robber, where others may testify to his signature, and establishes it where witnesses saw the signature beforehand. It then brings the case of one who signed a document before property later fell to him by inheritance, after which others may testify to his signature, and likewise establishes it where witnesses had seen the signature beforehand. Urim, seif katan 105, also cites this law of inheritance in the name of that Gemara. One cannot say that inheritance is different because once the property falls to him he is no longer merely an interested witness but is the litigant himself, and therefore he is suspected of forgery, whereas an interested witness would not be. This is incorrect. Since his essential testimony concerns another person and only indirectly produces a benefit for himself, he is like any other financially interested witness. It is no less so than the case in siman 66, seif 21, where a debt document is sold to the witnesses signed upon it, and they are described there only as interested witnesses. See there, and see what I wrote above in siman 33, seif 16, seif katan 9.
