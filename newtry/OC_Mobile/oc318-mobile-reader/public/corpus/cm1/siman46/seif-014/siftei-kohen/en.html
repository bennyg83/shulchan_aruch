Or the judge himself together with another, etc. The Beit Yosef explains that two witnesses are required to establish a judge's signature. Even if the judge himself identifies his handwriting, another person must join him, because whenever no other judge testifies to his signature, the judge is understood to testify only about his own handwriting. The Beit Yosef derives this from the Rosh. He apparently distinguishes the later case in seif 15, where two judges of the authentication panel each testify to his own signature and the deed is authenticated, because there both judges testify, whereas here there is only one judge. This distinction is not persuasive. The Tur writes, or the judge himself together with another, to teach that even then the witness and judge do not combine, because each testifies to a different matter. The Sema and Bach explain it this way. The Rosh's words cannot be explained that way because he states the rule in connection with Rabbi Yehudah in Shmuel's name, who holds that they do combine. It therefore appears that the Rosh first presents one view and then cites Rabbeinu Yonah's contrary conclusion, which he accepts. Accordingly, if one outside witness testifies to the signatures of the witnesses on the deed and one judge alone identifies his own signature on the authentication, they combine. A judge is not viewed as testifying merely to his handwriting, but to the deed's authentication. This is also plainly implied by the Sema and Bach, and by the Ran and the Mechaber below in seif 16, who write without qualification that one witness and a judge who testifies to his signature combine. They do not require the judge plus another witness, although in the next clause they expressly require two outside witnesses to identify a judge's signature. The Mechaber appears to have withdrawn from his statement in the Beit Yosef and to hold that when a judge himself identifies his signature, he never requires another person, because he testifies to the authentication of the deed, as though saying that the deed was authenticated before him. This is the correct ruling.
It is ineffective, even if the witness signed on the deed. This remains ineffective even where that witness also testifies to the second witness's signature, so that together with the judge the entire deed would seemingly be established. Three quarters of the necessary authentication would then rest upon the signed witness's testimony, which is invalid, as explained above in seif 11 and in the Ritva cited below in seif katan 45. If, however, there is one independent witness to the second signer's signature, that witness may combine with authentication of one judge's signature, such as where two persons testify to the judge's signature. The judicial authentication then has the force of two witnesses concerning the second signature and resembles the case above in seif 13. The judge himself alone also suffices to identify his own signature, as I wrote immediately above in seif katan 38, and in that manner he combines with the witness who testifies to the second signer's signature.
For what this one testifies to, etc. The Tur therefore concludes that there is no concern if the witnesses to the deed are related to the judges who authenticated it. The Rosh likewise reasons that since a deed is not authenticated through one witness and one judge, kinship between the judges and witnesses is irrelevant. The Beit Yosef cites this. I found, however, in a responsum of Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition, siman 48, also printed among the Rashba's responsa, siman 853, that according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak's proof that a witness and judge combine, if at a later time one recognizes the signature of one witness and the signature of one judge, that suffices. Maharam therefore writes that persons related to the witnesses should not sign the authentication endorsement. I proved below in seif katan 45, following the Ritva and the Ran in the Raah's name, that one judge and one witness do combine. Maharam's ruling is therefore primary. The practice is accordingly that judges do not authenticate the signatures of their relatives. Nevertheless, if it happened that the judges authenticated the signatures of relatives and the signatures of all three judges, or even of two judges, are themselves recognized, the authentication is valid after the fact. Examine this carefully.
Likewise, if the scribe's handwriting was authenticated, etc. The Sema cites the source as saying that the creditor may nevertheless collect from unencumbered property, and he also cites Rabbeinu Yerucham and Darkei Moshe. It appears to me that he was not precise. How can the creditor collect from unencumbered property merely because the scribe's handwriting is recognized? Who says that the scribe served as a witness? The Gemara in Gittin explicitly relates that Rabbi Abbahu recognized the text of a marriage document and the signature of one witness and considered validating it, but Rabbi Yirmiyah told him that the rule concerns a scribe who signed, not merely a scribe who wrote. This proves that a scribe's writing together with one witness is entirely invalid. The Rivash likewise writes that although we rule that the scribe's signature is effective, ordinary scribal writing is not. In the case before him, however, the copy expressly stated the gift in the scribe's signed attestation, which was superior to ordinary scribal writing and had the force of a scribe's signature. He adds that according to the Baal HaIttur and Rabbeinu Chananel, an expert scribe together with one witness, although not valid initially, is no worse than a get written entirely in the husband's handwriting, where the Mishnah says that the child is legitimate. It would therefore suffice in monetary law to collect from unencumbered property. We do not follow the Baal HaIttur and Rabbeinu Chananel on this point, as explained in Even HaEzer, end of siman 130. Rather, a document written by a scribe and signed by one witness is no get at all. Rabbeinu Yerucham himself states that if she remarries she must leave the second husband and the child is a mamzer. It is therefore certainly invalid even for collection from unencumbered property. The Rivash's additional inference from the Baal HaIttur and Rabbeinu Chananel is also not compelled. Their leniency regarding the child's status rests on Rashi's explanation that an expert scribe versed in gittin would not write a get without hearing the husband's instruction, a rationale irrelevant here. In any event, no authority says that a scribe's handwriting plus one witness permits collection from unencumbered property. Rabbeinu Yerucham's actual language in Meisharim is that the creditor may collect from unencumbered property according to the Yerushalmi's rule concerning one witness in writing and one orally. He plainly refers to a case where the scribe also testifies orally, as is clear from his discussion of collection of debts. This is the case governed below in siman 51, seif 1, of one witness in writing and one orally.
