That the witnesses sign before them. So writes Beit Yosef. Eir Shushan comments that although we said above that authentication is judicial procedure, and ordinarily a witness cannot become a judge, with rabbinic document authentication they were lenient. This is difficult. Even ordinarily such a case is permitted, for 'hearing cannot be greater than seeing.' Therefore, as explained above in siman 7, if a beit din sees an act occur before it by day, it may adjudicate on the basis of its own observation without other testimony. So too here: certainly when the judges see the witnesses sign before them by day, they may authenticate immediately, for that is like judging from what they themselves saw. Even if the witnesses did not sign before them, but the document comes before them by day and the judges recognize the signatures, their daytime observation of the signatures is like seeing the witnesses sign before them, and they can authenticate on the basis of that observation even if document authentication were Torah law. Yet Rambam and the Mechaber list first, 'the judges themselves recognize the signatures,' and second, 'the witnesses sign before them,' which would be an unnecessary progression from the stronger case to the weaker unless the second case refers even to where the signing occurred before them at night. At night they cannot adjudicate or formally receive testimony, and the principle that hearing is no greater than seeing cannot be invoked in the ordinary way. In Perishah I explained this, and the Tur and Mechaber nearby in seif 24 also state another leniency, that a witness who testifies may become a judge because document authentication is rabbinic. See Sema there, seif katan 53. If so, Eir Shushan may likewise have interpreted the case in this manner, which is why he needed the special leniency of rabbinic authentication.
That the witnesses signed on it come. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that there is a dispute in the Tosefta: if the witnesses say, 'This is our handwriting,' while others say, 'This is not their handwriting,' whether further authentication is required. The halacha was not resolved. So cite Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe.
And each says, 'This is my handwriting.' Each need not testify to his fellow's signature, because we rule that they are testifying to the money contained in the document. Their testimony is therefore as though each said, 'I saw this lender lend this borrower.' Rambam and the Mechaber conclude, 'and I am a witness to this matter.' This means that from seeing his signature on the document he recalls the loan. Otherwise each would also need to testify to the other's signature, as the Mechaber writes in seif 10. Nevertheless, they need not explicitly say that they remember it; ordinarily we assume they do. See Perishah.
The fourth method is that witnesses come and testify, etc. Even if the original signers are present, the signatures may be authenticated through testimony of other witnesses. So write Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, seif 10.
This testimony from their writing. Meaning, two witnesses write that they recognize the signatures, and the beit din accepts their testimony from the writing and authenticates the original signatures as though the authentication witnesses testified orally before it. This excludes ordinary testimony, where according to Rambam we require oral testimony unless the witnesses themselves signed a formal document, which is accepted because of the needs of society, as the Mechaber writes above in siman 28, seif 11.
Open possession, etc. This must come from two separate documents. From one document concerning one field, it is possible that the signature was forged and the purchaser simply happened to have been allowed to possess the land. See Perishah.
Ketubah documents. Here too the two women must have lived openly with their husbands for three years. So writes the Tur in the name of Ramah.
Even if both documents are authenticated. Although we do not suspect the authenticated documents themselves of being forged, there is concern that the person forged the present signature by copying from those authenticated signatures.
And some disagree. Their reason is that another person cannot accurately forge a signature letter for letter in the witnesses' handwriting. By contrast, where the comparison documents themselves are not authenticated, there is concern that all three documents were forged and that one person wrote all three purported signatures in his own forged hand. In Derishah I wrote another reason according to those versions of the Tur that read that authentication may be made from an authenticated document even if it is in the possession of the person seeking authentication: he is afraid to forge from a document that the beit din scrutinized and authenticated. That rationale fits specifically the Tur and those who explain the Gemara's concern as one that the present signature might be forged from the comparison signatures. Rambam's wording, however, can imply that the dissenting view understands the concern to be that the comparison documents themselves are forged, not that one is copied from another. I therefore gave the first rationale. See Derishah, where I explain that Rambam's position is not necessarily compelled to mean this.
From a document that was challenged. They hold that when a litigant challenged a document as forged, the beit din certainly investigated it carefully and would not have authenticated it unless the signatures were established with certainty. Therefore another signature may be authenticated from it, unlike an authenticated document that was never challenged. The Mechaber writes similarly in seif 37.
As below in seif 37. There the Mechaber himself cites both views. The Tur cites both views here, but in the law of seif 37 he writes without qualification that one may authenticate from an authenticated document even if it was never challenged, following his father the Rosh. The Mechaber here copied Rambam's language and therefore did not state the dissenting view in the same way. In Perishah I wrote that Rambam's position is not conclusive; perhaps he too permits authentication from an unchallenged document and merely adopts the Gemara's wording. The Gemara may have used the ordinary case, because they did not customarily authenticate a document unless it had first been challenged, as the Rosh explains.
Just as authentication is made from deeds of two fields, etc. There the comparison documents may be used even if they themselves have not been formally authenticated.
But not from a letter. The reason is that a person does not write a letter with precision; his handwriting varies according to the pen and urgency. This is also the reason of the view that authentication may not be made even from a book, because a person is precise primarily in his signature. See Darkei Moshe, seif 24.
