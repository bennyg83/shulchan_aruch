The fourth. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Netiv 2, part 5, writes that there is a dispute in the Tosefta where the witnesses say, 'This is our handwriting,' and others say, 'This is not their handwriting,' whether further authentication is required. The halacha was not decided. See the Shach, who writes that he saw this dispute in the Yerushalmi. Ritva explains the Yerushalmi as follows. If the witnesses themselves say, 'This is our handwriting,' and others say, 'This is not their handwriting,' it is two against two. Although according to the Rabbis each document witness testifies only to his own signature while the two outside witnesses testify concerning both signatures, the original witnesses are not treated as two witnesses against one, because they are testifying to the maneh recorded in the document and thus together count as two against the other two. But if the document witnesses say, 'This is not our handwriting,' and others say, 'It is their handwriting,' Rabbi Chiya teaches that the document witnesses neither raise nor lower the document's standing. The case is one where each document witness denies only his own signature, while the outside witnesses testify to both signatures, leaving two against one regarding each signature. Rabbi Yose says the Mishnah proves this: if there are other witnesses who testify that this is their handwriting, the document witnesses are not believed. The reason is that they cannot invoke the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted, since even if they said, 'This is not our handwriting,' it would not help once there are witnesses that it is their handwriting. Ritva concludes that if each document witness also testifies regarding the other's signature and says that neither signature is theirs, then it would indeed be two against two. The Shach cites this. At first glance, Rabbi Yose's proof from the Mishnah is difficult, because Tosafot write that we do not say a migo that depends upon two people, since perhaps the other person would not agree to make the same statement. The reason witnesses are believed to say, 'We were coerced' or 'we were minors,' through a migo that they could have denied their handwriting, is that if even one of them said, 'This is not my handwriting,' there would remain only a document with one witness and it would have no effect. If so, where there are other witnesses who authenticate the signature, it is obvious that one document witness who says, 'This is not my handwriting,' is not believed because he is one against two. But this does not prove the law when both document witnesses deny their signatures. The answer is that because each original witness denies only his own signature and says nothing about his fellow's signature, each is certainly only one against two, since two outside witnesses testify about each signature. The only possible argument would be that one who says, 'This is not my handwriting,' should be treated like two witnesses, just as one who says, 'This is my handwriting,' is treated like two for this purpose. Rabbi Yose therefore proves from the Mishnah that he is not treated like two. If he were, then even one original witness denying his own handwriting while two outsiders authenticate it would create two against two, and he would possess the relevant migo. It follows that one who says, 'This is not my handwriting,' counts only as one. Consequently, even if both original witnesses each deny only their own handwriting, each remains one against two. As I write in seif katan 11, even if both original witnesses testify regarding each other and each says that both signatures are not theirs, the original document witnesses still do not lower the document and two outside witnesses are believed more.
From their writing. There is a difficulty with Rama. Below, seif 36, he rules without qualification that where a witness became mute, his signature is authenticated by others and not by himself. Here, however, he writes that the witness may give this testimony through writing. See the Shach, who says it is strained to distinguish a mute person on the ground that he cannot speak, for above in siman 28, seif 11, we do not follow such a distinction. We rule that testimony from writing is invalid whether the person is mute or not. If so, even in a rabbinic matter there should be no distinction. This requires study. See what I write below in seif katan 19 resolving this difficulty.
The fifth. The Tur in siman 69 cites Rav Sherira Gaon that a borrower's handwriting may not be authenticated by comparison with two other documents or two ketubot. Since authentication of documents is rabbinic, the leniency applies only where the Sages expressly stated it. It appears that no distinction should be made between this authentication and other forms of authentication. The Bach explains Rav Sherira Gaon as follows: with witnesses' signatures the Sages were lenient because authentication is rabbinic, since signed testimony is regarded as though already investigated before beit din. The borrower's own handwriting, however, requires authentication by Torah law. The Bach therefore criticizes Baal HaTerumot and the Tur for disagreeing with Rav Sherira Gaon on the basis of reasoning alone without grasping the Gaon's view. The Shach in siman 69 responds that they certainly understood the Gaon's reasoning and nevertheless disagreed, because once the process is a valid authentication, there is no distinction between a witnessed document and the borrower's handwriting. Comparison with other documents is not merely a special rabbinic leniency but a complete form of authentication. The Shach therefore concludes that the borrower's handwriting requires authentication by Torah law, in accordance with the Bach, and that it may not be authenticated outside the borrower's presence. We already explained our own view regarding the Torah status of a borrower's handwriting in seif katan 5. But the Shach's assertion that comparison with other documents is complete authentication and not a leniency is not convincing. Rabbeinu Tam's responsum, cited by the Mordechai in chapter HaIshah Shenitarmelah, states that we require comparison with two documents because perhaps on two occasions the forger succeeded in matching their signatures. Strictly speaking, even that should not suffice. The Sages allowed it only to prevent a creditor from being unable to collect and thereby causing the door to be locked before borrowers. When the comparison documents are in the creditor's own possession, we suspect forgery, and even when they are in another person's possession Rabbeinu Tam says, 'we are ashamed,' but the Sages permitted reliance on old documents. By contrast, when the document is authenticated by the very witnesses whose signatures appear on it, even if the creditor holds a thousand documents we do not suspect forgery, because the witnesses recognize their own writing through close perception and can distinguish even a hairbreadth variation. Merely comparing signatures resembles those matters where comparison is not accepted. Rabbeinu Tam proves this from the requirement of two hairs and from the law of a person who knew testimony concerning land and later became blind. Even if he can identify the boundaries by measurement, the length of a cloak, or the weight of a piece of metal, the Amoraim's positions were rejected. This shows that essential testimony depends upon direct recognition, not on comparing one thing to another. The Sages nevertheless created a special authentication enactment because of possible loss to purchasers. From Rabbeinu Tam's sacred words it is clear how weak comparison authentication is. If a borrower's own handwriting requires Torah-level authentication, there is no proof from witnessed documents to the borrower's handwriting, because the former is rabbinic and the latter Torah law. Thus the Bach's explanation of Rav Sherira Gaon is well founded. However, I have difficulty with Rabbeinu Tam's proof that testimony depends on direct recognition from the fact that the Amoraim were rejected in the case of one who became blind. There they were rejected because testimony must be valid from beginning to end, and a blind person is disqualified as a witness. The disqualification may therefore be his blindness, not the fact that he relies on measurements rather than direct recognition. A blind person cannot testify even from information he heard and recognized by voice. It would follow that a person who is not blind could indeed testify on the basis of weight and measurements. If not, the Gemara would have rejected the testimony simply because it lacks direct recognition, whereas the actual challenge came from the baraita about one who was sighted and then became blind. This requires study. See what I write in siman 297. As to the practical law of authenticating a borrower's handwriting by comparison, however, Rav Sherira Gaon's view appears correct from Chullin 96a. Rava says: originally I thought identifying signs were stronger than visual recognition, because we return a lost object through signs but not through visual recognition. After hearing certain teachings I concluded that visual recognition is stronger than signs. Rav Yitzchak son of Rav Mesharshiya says: know this, because if two witnesses said, 'So-and-so, who has these identifying signs, killed a person,' we would not execute him, whereas if they said, 'We recognize him visually,' we would. The same applies here. To identify the borrower through distinguishing marks, meaning through comparison rather than direct recognition, is insufficient to impose monetary liability. Although we authenticate a witnessed document by comparison, which likewise relies on identifying features of the letters, that is because the Sages were lenient in rabbinic document authentication. The identifying signs there concern the witnesses' signatures, not direct identification of the borrower. Where the witnesses identify the borrower himself only through letter-comparison, it is like saying, 'So-and-so, who has these signs, borrowed one hundred from so-and-so,' and money cannot be extracted through such signs rather than direct recognition. This is precisely Rav Sherira Gaon's statement: the borrower's handwriting cannot be authenticated through comparison, for the Sages expressly permitted comparison with witnessed documents and no further. Tosafot in Chullin ask how money can be extracted through a promissory note found in the street and returned to a creditor because of identifying marks. They answer that at the time of return no money is being extracted; the lost document is merely being returned. So too, comparison is useful only with the document's witnesses, while direct identification of the borrower himself requires actual recognition. Therefore, in practice the principal ruling follows Rav Sherira Gaon: the borrower's handwriting should be authenticated only by direct visual recognition and not by comparison of letter forms. The Taz in siman 69 objects from Yoreh Deah siman 228, which implies that even in a Torah-level matter recognition of a signature suffices. There the case is one who swore not to perform a certain act without permission from a particular person. Once that person gave permission, the oath is released, and Ramban writes that permission need not be face to face; it may be by handwriting so long as he recognizes the person's signature. Although it is possible that Ramban meant direct recognition of the handwriting and not comparison, his language implies that any reliable recognition of the signature suffices, including comparison. According to what we have explained, however, there is no difficulty. The problem with comparison is that one does not extract money or impose capital liability through identifying signs. In a matter of prohibition, identifying signs are effective. We indeed permit a married woman on the basis of signs identifying a deceased husband, as Tosafot write in Chullin. Therefore comparison may suffice in the law of oaths even if it would not suffice to extract money from a borrower.
Even if the documents are authenticated. This applies specifically when the authentication itself was through comparison. If the comparison documents remain in the creditor's possession, we do not authenticate from them, as Rabbeinu Tam writes: concerning old documents already in another's possession we are ashamed but nevertheless rely on them because of the enactment, whereas a document held by the creditor himself is subject to a serious forgery concern. But when the prior documents were authenticated through direct recognition, which is full authentication, we have no concern even if they are in his own possession. Baal HaItur, however, holds that even with direct recognition, if another document or book written by those witnesses remains in the creditor's possession, we suspect that he forged it. See the Shach, who writes that the consensus of the poskim is not like Baal HaItur, and this is the ruling we follow.
