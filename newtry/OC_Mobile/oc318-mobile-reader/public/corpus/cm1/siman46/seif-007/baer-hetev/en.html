That they should come. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that the Tosefta contains a dispute where the signatories say, "This is our handwriting," while others say, "This is not their handwriting": whether additional validation is required. The halacha was not decided there. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this. The Shach notes that he found the same dispute in the Yerushalmi, Ketubot chapter 2, as Ritva explains it. The Yerushalmi further states that if the signatories say, "This is not our handwriting," and two other witnesses say that it is their handwriting, the original signatories neither add nor subtract from the evidence. Ritva explains that this refers to a case where each signatory testifies only concerning his own signature, while each of the contradicting witnesses testifies concerning both signatures. It is therefore two witnesses against one regarding each signature. This implies that if each original signatory also testifies concerning his colleague's signature, the case becomes two against two. Shach.
Witness. Meaning that by seeing his signature on the document he recalls the underlying loan. Otherwise each signatory would also have to testify concerning the other's signature, as the Mechaber writes in seif 10. They need not expressly say that they remember the loan; we assume ordinarily that they do. Thus the Sema, and the Shach agrees.
And they testify. Even if the signatories themselves are present, the document may nevertheless be authenticated through other witnesses. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. This is obvious, and even more is explained immediately above: if the signatories themselves say, "This is not our handwriting," they are not believed against proper authentication. Baal HaIttur writes that the authenticating witnesses need not recognize every letter; recognition of most of the signature is sufficient. Shach.
Their writing. The Shach writes that the Rivash expressed doubt regarding this law, and the Rosh and authorities following him appear to disagree. The Tur and Mechaber below in seif 36 rule regarding a person who became mute that he cannot testify through writing. This creates difficulty for Rama. Since he follows the Mechaber there without dissent, how can he rule here that the witnesses may testify from writing? It is strained to distinguish a mute as one who is physically unable to speak, because in siman 28, seif 11, we rule that there is no distinction: even a person who can speak cannot give testimony in writing, because the Torah requires testimony "from their mouths and not from their writing." If so, even in a rabbinic matter it is difficult to distinguish between a mute and another witness. The matter requires study.
From two. Ramah writes that the two comparison documents must be in the possession of two separate purchasers. The Tur disagrees. For practical halacha the Tur's view appears primary, and the other poskim likewise do not make such a distinction. Shach.
Fields. If both documents concern the same field, perhaps the signatures were forged and the purchaser happened to be allowed to eat the produce without protest. Sema.
Ketubot. The Sema writes that here too the two women must have lived continuously and peacefully with their husbands for three years, as the Tur writes in the name of Ramah. The Shach disagrees. Rambam and the Mechaber do not imply this, because they state the three-year requirement regarding fields but do not specify it regarding women. Moreover, the Gemara states the condition "provided they possessed them," after mentioning both two fields and two ketubot, while Rambam and the Mechaber deliberately place the requirement immediately after the two fields, implying that for a woman any established period of living with her husband is sufficient. This also appears to be the position of the other poskim, and it is primary.
Validated. We fear that the holder may have forged one signature by imitating another. This concern applies specifically where recognition is based only on comparing one signature to another. If witnesses recognize the signatures by direct visual familiarity, then even if the holder possesses another signature there is no concern that he forged it. So write the poskim. The Rosh derives from here that if a person holds two documents signed by the same two witnesses, those documents may be authenticated only through direct visual recognition, not merely by comparing the signatures. This is the practical ruling, contrary to Baal HaIttur. Shach.
A challenge. Meaning a claim that the document was forged. In that case the beit din certainly paid close attention and investigated thoroughly, and would not have validated it unless it became clear to them and established before beit din that these were indeed the witnesses' signatures. Sema.
That is validated. The Shach writes that the Mechaber also agrees with this. The phrase "against which a challenge was raised" means that the present judges do not recognize the prior validation itself and fear that perhaps the validation was forged. Therefore they may use it only if, because of the earlier challenge, the validation became established before beit din and is presumed reliable. The Shach says this is the primary interpretation, unlike what appears from the Beit Yosef, who understood that even if the present court recognizes the prior validation, one may not authenticate from it unless a challenge had previously been raised, lest the earlier beit din have been insufficiently careful. The Ir Shushan and Sema followed that reading, but the Shach rejects it and brings several proofs from the poskim, including Rambam's own wording.
Alone. The Shach writes that the Raavad holds that a document can never be authenticated from only one other document, even if that comparison document had been established after a challenge. Baal Halachot Gedolot likewise writes that the baraita's phrase "a document against which a challenge was raised" is merely concise wording. The Shach asks how they would explain the case of showing a person's handwriting on a shard. It is difficult to say this means displaying two shards. He therefore suggests that they agree where the validating signatures themselves are certainly known, but hold that even after a document survived a challenge, one may not rely on a single such document when the prior validation itself is not otherwise recognized. In the shard case, where the person signs before us, one known signature suffices for comparison. This appears primary. Thus, where the signature is certainly genuine, such as where he signs before us or where the prior validation is recognized, everyone agrees that one comparison document is sufficient. Even if someone disputes this, the Shach rules that the practical halacha follows this view. If the prior validation is not recognized, however, no authentication may be made from a single comparison document where no challenge was previously raised, according to all opinions. If a challenge was raised and the document was then established, Rambam allows authentication from that one document even though the prior validation is not independently recognized, while Raavad does not. Shach.
Or. In that case authentication may be made even though the comparison documents themselves were not formally validated. Sema.
From a letter. The Sema explains that a person does not write an ordinary letter with the same care as a formal signature, and his handwriting varies with the pen and writing conditions. This is also the reason for the opinion that even writing from a book cannot be used for comparison, because a person is careful principally with his signature. Sema.
