The document is authenticated. Throughout this siman it appears that even where the borrower admits writing the document but pleads payment through a migo of forgery, two witnesses are ordinarily needed to authenticate the signatures. One witness is insufficient. This is not contradicted by the rule that a migo does not prevail against one witness. The strength of the borrower's plea here is not merely an ordinary migo. So long as the document is not fully authenticated, the borrower is not expected to fear leaving it in the creditor's hands, because it lacks the ordinary presumption, 'If it was paid, why is your document in my hand?' Even if one authentication witness could create an oath, the borrower may still be unconcerned about a document that does not presently establish monetary liability. Moreover, the borrower can formulate his plea as, 'It was paid and the document is forged.' In that case the one witness does not testify with certainty that money is presently owed; he testifies only that the document is genuine, and payment may still have occurred. The rule that one witness creates an oath where two would create payment applies where two witnesses would create the monetary liability by their own testimony. If the two would require an additional presumption, such as the presumption that an unpaid document remains in the creditor's possession, one witness does not combine with that presumption to create a Torah oath. This differs from the classic case of Rabbi Abba, where the witness directly establishes the act producing liability. It likewise differs from a document containing a credibility clause, where one witness to the clause can create a Torah oath because the obligor undertook to pay according to the claimant's statement. Therefore a document authenticated by only one witness does not create the rule of one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, and the borrower retains his plea of forgery.
That the witnesses signed in it should come. Rabbi Yerucham records a dispute where the signers say, 'This is our handwriting,' while others say, 'It is not their handwriting.' Urim VeTumim asks what further authentication can accomplish, since two witnesses are like one hundred. There is no difficulty. Recognition of handwriting depends on visual identification. In a matter dependent upon recognition, we do not always apply the rigid rule that two equal one hundred; a larger number of qualified recognizers can matter. Therefore additional comparison and recognition can be relevant. The further objection that the signers could simply rewrite the document is also incorrect. They themselves say they already performed their mission and that the existing document is genuine. The question is whether the questioned writing can in fact be theirs, for example where it is the rapid script of a professional scribe. Beit din may therefore require them to write before the court and compare the writing. Nor is this a forbidden retraction from prior testimony; the issues are distinct.
The fifth method is that their handwriting is known from another source. The Shach discusses authenticating handwriting by comparison. Ketzot HaChoshen compares this to identification by signs and says money cannot be extracted merely through signs. Although handwriting comparison is indeed analogous to signs and is one of the leniencies of document authentication, the comparison is not exact. Ordinary intermediate signs may be only rabbinic, but exceptionally distinctive signs are Torah-level proof. Monetary law can rely on such distinctive identification. We also cannot fully equate monetary and capital cases. A person may not be executed merely because witnesses identify a same-named person by ordinary signs, while monetary documents routinely rely on a name and locality absent another known person with the same identity. Moreover, the concern that a claimant saw the sign and adapted his claim applies to signs supplied by the claimant, not equally to distinctive features testified to by independent witnesses. Therefore strong handwriting comparison may support monetary authentication. Ketzot HaChoshen's proof from identifying field boundaries likewise does not establish a special difference between land and movables; there the dispute is only which of two identified properties was sold, and precise measurements or boundaries can resolve that question without the concern that the sign happened coincidentally.
