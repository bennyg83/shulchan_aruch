That the witnesses should come, etc. The Shach, seif katan 12, cites Rabbeinu Yerucham that where witnesses say, "This is our handwriting," while other witnesses say, "This is not their handwriting," there is a dispute whether another authentication is required. The Shach says that the Yerushalmi treats this as two against two, even though each of the first witnesses testifies only concerning his own signature and not that of his colleague. Raah and Ritva indeed write that it is two against two. But Rabbeinu Yerucham cannot be understood that way. If the case were truly two against two, what would another authentication accomplish? The matter could never be clarified. Even if a thousand additional witnesses came and supported the first pair, two witnesses are like one hundred, as the Tur writes in Even HaEzer, siman 152. The Yerushalmi itself, and the Beit Yosef's fuller quotation of Rabbeinu Yerucham, indicate a different explanation. The Yerushalmi states: "They say, this is our handwriting, and others say, this is not their handwriting. It is treated like a document against which an objection was raised. Rabbi Assi says, like a document against which an objection was raised and which was authenticated in beit din. Rabbi Elazar says, like one that was not authenticated in beit din. Rabbi Elazar gives the reason: once it was established in beit din that they were signed upon the document, it is as though their testimony had been investigated in beit din." The meaning is as follows. If the signatories themselves say with certainty that the signatures are theirs, how can other people truly contradict them? A person's handwriting can change for various reasons, especially deliberately. If the signatories stand by their identification, they could, if necessary, write a replacement document, since the first document has been weakened and they have not yet completed their mission. The only concern is that once others claim that the signatures are not genuine, perhaps we should suspect that the signatories initially identified them too casually, not having contemplated the possibility of forgery. Should beit din therefore ask them to reexamine the signatures carefully? If they then remain certain, they are unquestionably believed; if they retract, the document has been weakened. Or do we say that their original testimony is already treated as testimony fully investigated in beit din, so they cannot retract and no further inquiry is permitted? This is the Yerushalmi's dispute. Rabbi Assi says that another beit din authentication is required, meaning that the signatories should testify again after careful examination and confirm that they were not mistaken. Rabbi Elazar says that no further authentication is needed. Once their handwriting was established in beit din by their original testimony, their testimony is treated as fully investigated and they may not retract. We disregard the later witnesses who say that the signatures are not genuine. This is exactly Rabbeinu Yerucham's meaning as quoted by the Beit Yosef: there is a dispute whether the document must be treated as one that has been challenged and requires a further authentication, or whether the first testimony is already treated as investigated and no further authentication is required. If the issue were two against two, being "investigated in beit din" would accomplish nothing, because the second pair also testified in beit din. Thus, according to all opinions, if the signatories reconsider carefully and remain certain that the signatures are theirs, there is no further concern and the document is authenticated. This also explains the Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 152, regarding a get. If two witnesses later challenge it as forged, once it has already been authenticated, there is nothing after the authentication, because we do not second guess a beit din authentication. At first sight this is difficult. If the authentication was based on two witnesses who identified the signatures, and two later witnesses contradict them, why is this not simply two against two, in which case even a woman who remarried might have to leave? According to the explanation above, the ordinary authentication is presumed to have been based on the signatories themselves, whose certainty concerning their own handwriting is not undermined in the same way by outsiders. If the record expressly shows that the authentication was based only on other witnesses, then Baal HaIttur may require further clarification, and the Tur's challenge against him is not decisive because there is still a possible clarification through the signatories themselves. The Yerushalmi further says: "They say, this is not our handwriting, and others say, this is their handwriting. Their testimony neither adds nor detracts. So too the Mishnah teaches: if there are witnesses that it is their handwriting, or if it is authenticated from another source, they are not believed." Raah and Ritva explain that each signatory denies only his own handwriting, while the outside witnesses identify both signatures; therefore each denial is only one witness against two. If each signatory testified concerning both signatures, it would be two against two. According to their approach, the Mishnah's rule that the signatories are not believed to say they were coerced once their handwriting is authenticated implies that they do not have a joint migo to say, "Neither this nor our colleague's signature is genuine," because that would be a migo requiring coordinated testimony from two people. Still, I do not understand how there can truly be two against two once the signatories previously testified that these are their signatures. Once they testified, they cannot retract, and their later statement that the signatures are not theirs should have no legal force at all. This is especially so according to those who hold that Torah law does not require authentication even where forgery is claimed. We would then have only one remaining witness contradicting two. I have not found this approach of Raah and Ritva adopted by the other poskim, and it requires study. Nor can the Gemara in Gittin 9 be explained by saying that the "objection" consists of the signatories themselves saying the document is forged, because then the phrase "it must be authenticated by its signatures" would be difficult to explain, especially outside the Land of Israel. The ordinary meaning is that others raise the objection.
And they may give this testimony from their writing. The Shach, seif katan 17, asks from the Mechaber's ruling below, seif 36, that one who became mute cannot testify concerning his handwriting. There are two answers. First, Rivash's leniency applies only where a witness says, "This is the handwriting of so and so," which is merely document authentication. But where the signatory himself says, "This is my handwriting," we rule that he is testifying concerning the one hundred stated in the document, meaning the substantive obligation. Such testimony requires full testimony and cannot be given from writing. See what I wrote above, seif katan 5, in the name of Rashba, who allowed authentication outside the litigant's presence precisely because the witnesses testify concerning handwriting and not concerning the debt itself. This is why the Rama did not place his ruling in the third method, where the signatories themselves appear and testify from writing. Rivash's leniency applies only to the fourth method, where other witnesses identify the signatures. Second, Rivash could rely on Rabbeinu Tam's general view, cited above in siman 28, that written testimony is valid. Because document authentication is rabbinic, Rabbeinu Tam's view may be relied upon there. This is so only for a person who is otherwise capable of oral testimony, under the principle that whatever is fit for mixing may be valid even without actual mixing. A mute person, who is not capable of oral testimony and is therefore unfit under all opinions for ordinary testimony in writing, is not granted this leniency even for authentication. Thus there is no contradiction. Even so, although the Shach's question can be answered, it remains difficult to rely upon Rivash's ruling. He gives no direct proof, but merely compares it to other leniencies in document authentication. We should not automatically compare one rabbinic institution to another. Where the Sages expressly stated a leniency, it applies; where they did not, we should not invent one. Indeed, there is some proof against Rivash from the Gemara's advice that if only one witness is available to authenticate the other signature, he should sign his own name on a shard and allow beit din to compare signatures, so that three quarters of the monetary result should not depend on his mouth alone. Why is all this necessary? He could simply testify from a written statement identifying his colleague's signature. Beit din could then authenticate the colleague's signature from that writing and his own signature by comparison, just as it does with the shard. It therefore appears that written authentication is not ordinarily relied upon, except perhaps under extreme necessity. This requires study.
From two documents. The Shach, seif katan 18, notes that the Tur cites Ramah that the two comparison documents must be in the hands of two different people, and the Shach sides with the Tur against Ramah. At first sight it is difficult to know Ramah's source. It appears, however, that Ramah relied upon the Yerushalmi, which says in the name of Rabbi Yose that according to the Sages authentication may be made from three documents belonging to three different people. The Shach, seif katan 21, strains to explain that this refers specifically to loan documents and that three are needed for that reason. This is very difficult. The Yerushalmi means that the authentication uses three documents in total, counting the document now being authenticated among them. That is the equivalent of the two comparison documents mentioned by the Bavli. The poskim therefore did not cite the Yerushalmi separately, because it states the same rule. Since the Yerushalmi says that the three documents must belong to three different people, it follows like Ramah that the two comparison documents cannot both belong to the same person, but must be in the hands of separate parties. Ramah thus has a clear proof from the Yerushalmi.
And even two authenticated documents, etc. The Shach asks: Rambam's concern, as quoted by the Mechaber, is that perhaps the person forged all of the comparison documents, not that he copied a genuine document. If the two documents are already authenticated, so that they cannot be forgeries, why must they be in the possession of someone else? It appears that the Rama inferred this from Rambam's wording. Rambam writes that one may compare a document to one that was challenged and then upheld by beit din, just as one may compare it to two marriage contracts or two deeds concerning fields. Why did Rambam formulate a comparison between these cases? The Rama understood that Rambam meant to teach that just as the two marriage contracts or land documents must emerge from another person's possession, as the Gemara explicitly requires, so too a document that was challenged and upheld by beit din must be in another person's possession. The reason can be explained as follows. Rambam holds that we fear that the suspect may have forged all the documents. If a person has occupied a field peacefully for three years, why is that not enough to show that the deed of sale relating to that field is genuine? Perhaps he truly bought the field and has a genuine deed, but because he could not reproduce the exact handwriting he created an additional forged deed concerning that same real transaction and hid the genuine one. The forged deed will resemble the false deed he now seeks to validate because both are his own work. The same concern can exist even with an authenticated document. Most authentication is performed by the original signatories identifying their signatures. The underlying transaction may indeed have been genuine and there may have been a genuine document, but the holder might have forged a second document resembling it. Because the signatories know that they did in fact sign a document for this person, and the forgery resembles their real signatures to some degree, they may not scrutinize it with full care and may identify it by assumption. Thus even a forged document could become improperly authenticated. The Rama therefore understands Rambam to require that the comparison document never have been in the suspected party's possession.
From a document that was challenged. The Shach asks: once the document has been authenticated, why does it matter that it was previously challenged? Do we suspect that beit din improperly authenticated it? It may be that this very point lies behind the dispute between Baal HaIttur and the Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 153, whether a later challenge after authentication requires further clarification. Baal HaIttur may follow Rashi's concern that authentication performed without a prior challenge may have been done somewhat casually, while the Tur follows the Rosh that authentication alone is sufficient. According to the explanation above, the requirement of a prior challenge is understandable. We assume that the original transaction was genuine and the signatories did once sign a real document, but that the holder lost it and forged another. Because the signatories know that the transaction itself was genuine, they may not scrutinize the replacement closely and may authenticate it. If, however, that very document was challenged as a forgery and then upheld after investigation, the prior challenge ensured that the matter was examined carefully. There is still a difficulty. Mordechai explains that we require two comparison documents concerning two fields or two marriage contracts because a forger may be able to imitate one sample, but cannot easily create a forgery matching two different genuine samples. Experience supports this. Why, then, does one authenticated document that was challenged suffice? Raah in Ketubot indeed holds that two are always needed, but this is not the implication of most authorities, including the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. It must be that where the comparison document was itself challenged and upheld, the holder of that document has a special interest in preventing anyone from creating another document resembling it. He fears that if a similar forgery later emerges, the old challenge may be revived and he may himself be suspected of collaborating with forgers. The holder therefore guards the document and does not allow the suspected person to use it as a model. This explains why a prior challenge is specifically required.
From that document alone. The Shach cites authorities who hold that even if the comparison document is authenticated, two comparison documents are still required. He asks how they understand the Gemara's advice that a person sign his name on a shard so that beit din may compare from it. The Shach answers that those authorities require two documents because they fear that even a document challenged and authenticated may still be a forgery, whereas a signature made before beit din on a shard is certainly genuine. That explanation is strained, because the Shach himself writes in the preceding discussion that once beit din authenticated a document, and certainly once it was challenged and then upheld, no one maintains that we continue to suspect forgery. The correct reason for requiring two documents is the one stated above: a forger can imitate one model but has difficulty matching two separate documents. The case of the shard is different. All these safeguards are needed only because of a concern that the comparison sample itself may be connected to a forgery. Where beit din knows directly that the signature on the shard is genuine, because the person signed before them for the very purpose of authentication, there is no reason to add special precautions. The essence of authentication is comparison of handwriting. In a case free of forgery concern, one genuine sample is sufficient. This also appears somewhat from the Shach himself. Therefore, as a matter of practical law we should follow Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch that where the comparison document was challenged and then authenticated, one such document is sufficient, because we do not accept Rabbeinu Tam's concern that the forger merely copied one genuine sample.
And some say even from a book. This is the Rosh's view. He proves it from Abaye's statement that the person should sign on a shard. Abaye did not advise him simply to write an alphabet or a sequence of letters, which would also provide a handwriting sample. The Shach rejects the proof, saying that Abaye could equally have advised him to sign at the top of a scroll rather than on a shard, so no conclusion can be drawn. In truth, Tosafos at the beginning of Perek Get Pashut appears to support the Shach's point. Yet Abaye's two statements need to be reconciled. In Ketubot he advises signing on a shard. In Perek Get Pashut he says that one who wishes to show his signature to beit din should not sign except at the top of a scroll. What is the difference? The concern with signing at the top of a scroll is that someone might turn the sheet around and create a tied document. For example, if there are two signatures, one could write a document lengthwise and turn it into a tied instrument. If there is only one genuine witness, one could add a second genuine witness to the loan and a disqualified signature to complete the required three, as discussed in Gittin 81. Money could then be collected from the borrower and purchasers on the strength of one actual witness, while that witness might assume that the second signature is also genuine, since document witnesses need not sign in each other's presence. In Bava Batra, Abaye is advising a private person who wants to show beit din his signature. Beit din did not direct him to do it and expects that ordinary witnesses may still come. He therefore need only protect himself from a foreseeable misuse, and signing at the top of the scroll suffices because his own handwritten obligation in a tied document would not have force according to Tosafos. In Ketubot, however, beit din itself is instructing him how to create a comparison sample. Beit din must ensure that no Jew can suffer harm through the advice it gives. It therefore does not even allow signing at the top of a scroll, because that could potentially be converted into a tied instrument in the manner described. Beit din instructs him to sign specifically on a shard, which cannot be made into a document. This resolves the difference.
