The witnesses signed upon it should come. Rabbeinu Yerucham records a dispute in the Tosefta where the signers say, ‘This is our handwriting,’ and others say, ‘It is not their handwriting.’ The Yerushalmi explains that this may be treated as two against two, because the signers testify to the debt embodied in the instrument, while the opposing witnesses testify regarding both signatures. Conversely, where the signers deny their own signatures and others identify them, each signer ordinarily speaks only about his own hand, so the two identifiers prevail over each individual signer. If each signer also testifies regarding the other signer’s handwriting, the case is two against two.
And I am a witness to this matter. This is Ramban’s language and follows his view that the witness must remember the underlying testimony. According to those who disagree, it is enough that he also identifies his colleague’s handwriting even if he does not remember the event. Even according to Ramban, he need not expressly say that he remembers; ordinarily we presume that he does. The Sema states this, and I prove it further below.
The fourth to come. Witnesses and witnesses, and even if the witnesses before us are able to follow Joseph’s house and D.C., and they are not even told by us:
He said that he had written the hand of those who had been sent before him, or that he had written an end to his name or his brother, or his father’s name, and that all the signs of his or her father had been given to him, and that he had been given to him
They may testify to this from their writing, but another beit din must receive that written testimony and sign, thereby authenticating the deed. These are the words of Rivash. See below, seif 29, seif katan 68.
From their writing. Rivash initially remained uncertain whether, since authentication is rabbinic and may be performed without the litigant’s presence, written testimony might also suffice. Elsewhere he reports that he ruled before his teacher, the Ran, that because the debtor’s permission is unnecessary and authentication may proceed despite his protest, witnesses may write and send their testimony to beit din. The Rosh and those who follow him appear to disagree. They rule that a mute person cannot testify in ordinary matters because the Torah requires testimony ‘from their mouths,’ not from writing, and that his signature may be authenticated only through others, not through his own written statement. The Rama below adopts that ruling. It is therefore difficult that he permits written testimony here. One cannot readily distinguish a mute witness from one capable of speech, since we rule that the exclusion of written testimony applies to both. This requires study.
From two deeds, etc. The Ramah writes that the two deeds must be held by two separate purchasers. The Tur disputes him. Although the Tur's wording is somewhat difficult, the correct ruling appears to follow the Tur, that the signatures may be authenticated from them even when both deeds are held by one purchaser. This is also implied by the responsum of Rabbeinu Tam cited by the Baal HaIttur, and by the Mordechai in the second chapter of Ketubot. The other authorities likewise make no such distinction. The Mechaber's unqualified language also indicates this view. The Gemara says that the deeds must emerge from the possession of another, but not from his own possession. The term another is parallel to his own and therefore implies that possession by a single other person is sufficient. Were two other persons required, the Gemara should have said that they emerge from the possession of others. See the Bach, who discusses the Ramah's language at length. I have written what appears correct to me.
Whose owners consumed their produce for three years. This means for three years after the deeds were signed, as stated by the Ritva in the second chapter of Ketubot.
From two ketubah deeds. The Tur cites the Ramah as requiring that a wife have resided peacefully with her husband for three years. This does not appear to follow the Rambam and the Mechaber, or the Semag, who mention three years of possession above regarding fields but do not mention it here regarding ketubot. Moreover, the Gemara states, after mentioning two field deeds and two ketubot, that their owners must have consumed the produce for three years. The Rambam and the Mechaber moved that requirement and placed it immediately after the two field deeds. This indicates that they understood three years to be required only for fields, because the chazakah of houses and fields is established through three years. A woman's residence with her husband, however, is sufficient for any appreciable period. The Gemara's phrase, whose owners consumed them for three years, likewise applies specifically to fields, for which consumption of produce is relevant. This also appears to be the view of the other authorities, and it is the correct ruling.
Ketubot. Specifically, one may authenticate from two deeds concerning fields or from two ketubot, because the holder's consumption of the produce or the woman's residence with her husband demonstrates that the documents are genuine. One may not authenticate from two other loan deeds or two receipts that were never authenticated, because there is nothing external that establishes these documents. Their owner may have forged them without the supposed obligors knowing that they should contest them. This is stated by the Tur in the Ramah's name, the Ran in the second chapter of Ketubot, the Baal HaIttur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and is also implied by the Gemara and the authorities. The Yerushalmi states in the name of Rabbi Yose bar Bun that there must be three deeds belonging to three persons. It appears to me that this refers to the rule that handwriting may be authenticated from another source, namely from three deeds belonging to three different people. It does not conflict with our Gemara, which permits authentication from two field deeds or two ketubot. In those cases, because the owners consumed the fields or the women lived with their husbands, two suffice. The Yerushalmi speaks of loan deeds, and therefore requires three, each held by a different person. It is surprising that the authorities did not cite this Yerushalmi. They may have interpreted it differently. Alternatively, they may hold that because our Gemara mentions only field deeds or ketubot whose owners possessed them for three years, loan deeds never suffice. This is not conclusive, however, because the Gemara needed to teach that two are sufficient, whereas there was no need to state that three deeds suffice, since three constitutes a chazakah in many contexts. The matter requires study.
Perhaps he forged everything. According to this reasoning, if the deeds he produces were authenticated in beit din, the signatures may be authenticated from them, even from a single deed. This is the Ran's conclusion. The Baal HaIttur and the Ritva similarly explain that the concern is that he may have forged all the signatures. The Ritva writes that Tosafot asked why documents in his possession differ. The answer is that perhaps he first forged several versions of his own handwriting, deliberately changing it so that it would not resemble his usual hand, and then produced several matching examples from two signatures of each witness. One might have thought that anyone capable of such manipulation can also imitate another person's handwriting. The Gemara answers that such skill is not common. A person may be able to alter his own hand and make several altered samples resemble one another, yet still be unable to imitate the handwriting of the witnesses. This is the correct explanation.
Even if the two deeds, etc. The phrase and some disagree is imprecise. According to the Rambam and the Mechaber, authenticated deeds are effective, as I wrote immediately above. The dissenting opinion is the Rambam's own view quoted by the Mechaber, as explained by the Ran, Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and the later authorities. The Rama should instead have written: If the deeds are authenticated, the signatures may be authenticated from them even if there are one hundred deeds; and some disagree, holding that even two authenticated deeds do not suffice, etc.
They may not be authenticated from them. Since the specimens are before him, we are concerned that he forged the signatures by copying from them. This is Rashi's view, and also that of Tosafot in Rabbeinu Tam's name, the Rosh, Mordechai, and Tur. They explain that this applies only where the witnesses' signatures are recognized through comparison of one signature to another. Where witnesses recognize the signatures by direct visual familiarity, there is no concern that the holder forged them merely because he has another specimen in his possession. The Tur states this, and their position is also mentioned by the Baal HaIttur and the Ran. The Rosh derives that one who holds two deeds signed by the same two witnesses may authenticate them only through direct recognition, not through comparison. Rabbeinu Yerucham states the same. According to the Rambam and those who follow him, even comparison is effective. The Baal HaIttur cites Rabbeinu Tam but disagrees, ruling that if it is known that a deed or book written by those witnesses is in the holder's home, and one document is authenticated, the second may not be authenticated even from another deed and even through witnesses who recognize the handwriting directly, because we say that he forged it. The consensus of the authorities is not so. Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai, Ran, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham clearly reject that extension, and certainly the Rambam, Ritva, and Mechaber permit authentication in such a case. This is the ruling we follow.
Some say that this applies even if no objection was raised against it. In my opinion, the Rambam, Rif, and Rashi also agree that when the earlier deed's authentication is itself recognized, it may be used even if no objection had been raised against that deed. It would be astonishing to say that formal authentication is ineffective because perhaps the beit din did not examine the matter carefully. A beit din does not sign an authentication unless it has investigated, for otherwise there would be no end to the matter. Rashi's requirement that an objection have been raised applies where the present judges do not recognize the authentication endorsement itself, since it might itself be forged. Rashi expressly says that without a prior objection we fear that the earlier deed itself was forged. Where an objection was raised and the deed was then upheld in beit din, we do not have that concern, because we know that a deed of this description was contested and judicially established. It is therefore presumed genuine, just as field deeds and ketubot are presumed genuine even when we do not recognize their witnesses' signatures, because we know that the parties actually possessed the fields or lived as husband and wife. The Ritva objects to Rashi that once a deed was upheld in beit din there can be no concern of forgery, for otherwise all authentication would be nullified. According to my explanation, however, Rashi's view is entirely clear. The Baal HaIttur also follows Rashi and infers from the baraita's wording that a prior objection is required. His words should likewise be understood as referring to a case where the authentication endorsement is not recognized. Rashi's interpretation is the principal reading of the Gemara. All our texts read: If an objection was raised against it, yes; if not, no, which supports Rav Asi's statement that a deed may be authenticated only from one that was contested and then upheld in beit din. According to the Rosh, the Gemara's wording must be forced, or he must have read: If it was upheld, yes; if not, no. Rashi's reading also explains why the Nehardeans' ruling is connected to Rav Asi. Just as Rav Asi permits authentication from a deed known to have been contested and upheld, even though its signatures are not presently recognized, because we know that such an authenticated deed genuinely existed, so too the Nehardeans permit authentication from field deeds and ketubot because we know that genuine documents of that sort ought to be in the holders' possession. The Rif and Rambam also require prior objection when the authentication endorsement is not recognized. Contrary to the Sema, the Rambam's view is not left open. His Commentary to the Mishnah explicitly says that a second method is to use a deed bearing the same witnesses that had previously been contested and thereafter upheld in beit din. The printed Rif likewise has Rav Asi's formulation that the deed must have been contested and thereafter upheld. They did not need to state the obvious case where the authentication endorsement itself is recognized. Their reasoning also implies a fortiori that if the endorsement is actually recognized, the deed may be used even without a prior objection. This is the correct ruling, unlike the Beit Yosef, Ir Shushan, and Sema, who require prior objection even when the authentication is recognized because they fear that the first beit din was careless. Although the Ramah cited by the Tur holds that view, it is not convincing. The Ritva and Rosh are correct that recognized authentication is effective, for otherwise all judicial authentication would be undermined. The Beit Yosef reached his conclusion because he understood Rashi and the Rambam to agree with the Ramah. Once it is established that they too permit reliance upon an actually recognized authentication even without prior objection, the rule follows as I have written. This may also be inferred from the Rambam's own wording. He says that a deed may be authenticated from one that was contested and upheld in beit din, from that deed alone, just as one authenticates from two field deeds or two ketubot. The comparison is otherwise unnecessary. His meaning is that just as one may rely on field deeds and ketubot even without presently recognizing their signatures, because the surrounding facts establish their genuineness, so one may rely on a deed that was contested and upheld even without presently recognizing its signatures, because it too is presumed genuine. This is clear.
They authenticate from that deed alone. The Raavad writes that a deed can never be authenticated from one other deed, even if that deed had been upheld, but only from two deeds, either where each was upheld in beit din or where the owners possessed each property for three peaceful years without objection. The Ran quotes this. The Ritva writes that the Nehardeans do not disagree with Rav Asi, for they concede that one other deed upheld in beit din suffices, as shown by the later rule that a witness may write his signature on a shard, cast it before beit din, and have it established there. Anything properly established in beit din is as though the witness displayed his signature there. Tosafot also take this view, and it is primary. The Raavad and Halachot Gedolot, however, understand that even Rav Asi and the baraita require two deeds, and that the Nehardeans merely teach that prior judicial establishment is unnecessary. The singular form used by Rav Asi and the baraita is merely concise language. It is difficult to explain, according to them, the case of displaying the signature on a shard. It is forced to say that the witness writes on two shards. It is more reasonable that they agree where the authentication endorsement is recognized, but hold that where it is not recognized, even a deed that had been contested and upheld cannot alone be relied upon. The baraita discussed that latter case, as I wrote above. It therefore resembles field deeds and ketubot, of which two are required. By contrast, where the witness signs before us on the shard, one genuine signature is certain and suffices. The Ran and Ritva may also have understood the Raavad this way. The correct conclusion is that where the signature is certainly genuine, such as where it was written before us or the authentication endorsement is recognized, one other deed suffices according to all opinions. This is also the plain implication of the Talmudic case of the shard and of the authorities and Tur and Mechaber below in seif 13. Even were there an authority who disagreed, the proper practical ruling is to authenticate from one deed in such a case. Where the authentication endorsement is not recognized, no one permits reliance upon the deed if it had not been contested. If it was contested and upheld, the Rambam permits reliance upon the single deed even though the endorsement is not recognized, as explained above, while the Raavad requires two.
But not from a letter. The Ritva's words cited by the Beit Yosef below in seif 16 imply that signatures may be authenticated even from a letter. He writes that one may also write, at the end of a scroll, the reversed alphabet or the alphabet itself, without concern, and that handwriting may then be established from it. The Yerushalmi likewise says that one may learn from a corrected book. The Ritva further explains the Yerushalmi's statement that one learns from a corrected book, such as where people say, These are Asi's books. This means that we have an established presumption, without separate testimony, that he wrote them. The Yerushalmi then asks whether the same applies to letters. The Ritva explains that the unresolved question concerns private letters that are presumed to have been written by these witnesses, whether that presumption alone suffices. This is also how his teacher explained it. This reading appears primary. According to the Rivash's explanation, the Yerushalmi need not have said, such as where they say these are Asi's books. It could simply have said that one learns from a corrected book and then raised the question of letters. Nor would the book's being corrected matter. Rather, the Yerushalmi means that one learns from a corrected book from which others studied and which they corrected, where those correctors testify that they studied from Asi's books. The book is thereby established as Asi's. When the Yerushalmi asks about letters, it implies that where it is certainly known that this particular letter was written by the witness, the signatures may indeed be authenticated from it.
Some say, etc. The Shulchan Aruch and Sema cite the Ran here, and the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe likewise attribute this opinion to him. This is surprising, because the Ran does not say so. On the contrary, he expressly writes that just as signatures may be authenticated from two field deeds or two ketubot, they may be authenticated from a book written by the witness, citing the Yerushalmi's statement that one learns from a corrected book, such as where they say, These are Asi's books. The Rosh and Mordechai do imply that signatures cannot be authenticated from a book, because one cannot select individual letters from ordinary writing and compare them. They also infer this from the Gemara's instruction to write one's signature on a shard, rather than to write the alphabet on a scroll. Their proof is not conclusive, for the Gemara could equally have suggested writing at the beginning of a scroll, as Tosafot and other authorities observe. It therefore appears correct that signatures may be authenticated from a book, in accordance with the Rivash, Ritva, and Ran cited above, as is evident from the Yerushalmi. The Beit Yosef writes that the Yerushalmi supports the Rosh because authentication is allowed only from Asi's books, which he understands to mean Asi's signed name, but not from the rest of the book, since people commonly sign differently from their ordinary handwriting. I do not understand this. The Yerushalmi implies precisely the opposite, that authentication is made from the book itself.
