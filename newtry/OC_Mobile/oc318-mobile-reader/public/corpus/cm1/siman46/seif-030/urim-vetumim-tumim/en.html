And some say it is invalid. Shach, seif katan 76, suggests there may be no real dispute. He says Rosh invalidates only where it is known with certainty that the relative or disqualified judge was actually one of the three judges making up the authentication. I do not understand this. In Rosh's responsum, klal 60, siman 4, the facts involved four people who strengthened the document, while the third was disqualified. Rosh writes that if their procedure was the local form of authentication, the authentication was invalid though the underlying document itself remained valid. There were four signers, so the case cannot be limited simply to exactly three judges. Shach elsewhere says that the invalidity of an authentication containing a related signer parallels the rule above, siman 45, concerning a formal document with a related or disqualified witness among its signers. Where the final signers are the ones from whom the document is ordinarily authenticated, including a disqualified person among them creates a direct risk that the document will be authenticated through relatives. In an authentication, however, we do not find a rule that only the final two judges' signatures matter. An authentication can be established from the handwriting of any two judges. Therefore, wherever the relative or disqualified judge appears among the authentication judges, there is a risk that the document will later be authenticated through his signature together with one valid judge. That would effectively authenticate the document through a relative. For this reason Rosh's concern applies regardless of where the disqualified judge signed. This explains Rama's broad wording. Rosh's actual responsum happened to involve the disqualified person signing later, but the reason is not limited to the final position. Rivash, who validates the authentication, follows his own broader approach regarding disqualified signers in documents generally. Above, siman 45, he follows Rambam and Mechaber in allowing greater leniency concerning where a related signer appears. The two positions are therefore consistent with their underlying theories. Accordingly, there is a genuine dispute. Rosh's view is stricter: because an authentication can later be established through any two judicial signatures, a related or disqualified judge can invalidate the authentication wherever he appears. Rivash follows the more lenient framework and does not automatically invalidate it. One can sharpen the distinction from siman 45. In an ordinary document, Chazal focused particularly on the final witnesses because common authentication practice looks to those signatures. Therefore the location of the disqualified witness can matter. In a beit din authentication, however, any two judges' signatures can later be used to establish the authenticity of the judicial certification. There is no privileged "last pair." That is why a disqualified judge creates concern wherever his signature appears among the judges. This difference explains both Rosh's strictness here and the narrower invalidity rules in ordinary witness-signed documents. Accordingly, Rama's formulation is deliberate, not careless.
