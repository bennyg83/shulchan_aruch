Documents are not validated at night. See Sema, who writes that if both parties consent, validation at night is permitted because it is as though they accepted a relative or disqualified judge. Tumim writes that this applies only to a document without warranty. If the document contains warranty and can be collected from purchasers, what effect can the litigants' consent have to the detriment of purchasers?
And if two judges validated it. See Taz, who writes that although Rivash holds that witnesses may give testimony from a writing, the beit din that rules on the basis of that writing must itself recognize the witnesses' handwriting. It does not suffice for other witnesses to testify that the writing is theirs, because that would be testimony from the mouth of another witness. See Sema, seif katan 7, who writes that if the document is in the debtor's own handwriting and the debtor admits it, the validation appears effective. At first glance this is difficult, because a document in the debtor's own handwriting requires validation by Torah law, and even testimony from writing should not suffice for such validation. It therefore appears that Sema speaks where the debtor instructed these witnesses to sign and validate the writing. Their statement then has the status of testimony embodied in a document, like any document written at the direction of the obligor.
Validation of documents is rabbinic. Shach at length challenges Rabbi Avigdor, who rules that when the husband claims a get is forged, validation is required by Torah law. The Gemara in Gittin states explicitly that even if the husband later comes and challenges it, we disregard his challenge. There is a scribal error in Shach in the passage beginning "and in this Rabbi Avigdor holds." It should read that there is no such implication in the Gemara and that the halachic authorities do not rule like him. In my humble opinion, Rabbi Avigdor can be defended according to Maharik, cited by Shach regarding witnesses who say, "We did not sign this document." Maharik writes that once the document came before beit din previously, and so forth, it has an established status. His words show that there is a distinction between a document that reached beit din before the challenge and one that did not. The same can be said regarding a husband's forgery claim. If the document was already established before beit din and thereby acquired a presumptive status of validity by Torah law, the husband can no longer remove it from that status. Rabbi Avigdor's precise language supports this. He writes that although validation of a get through the declaration "it was written and signed before me" is required only because of a possible later contradiction, when the husband has not yet come to challenge it, that present absence matters. Beit Shmuel, siman 142, writes similarly. With respect to a married woman and other prohibitory matters, one can likewise distinguish between a document that came before beit din before the person who contradicts it and a case where both appear together. I write this only to show that Rabbi Avigdor's position is not as difficult as it first appears. The principal law, however, follows Shach and the implication of the authorities: even where the obligor or husband claims forgery, validation is not required by Torah law. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 148, brings proof for Rabbi Avigdor from Rambam and Tur, who write that where the husband says the get is forged, it is no get at all. In my humble opinion this proof is not compelling. The rabbinic rule that ordinary documents need validation assumes at least that we know who the purported witnesses are, although their handwriting is not recognized. Rambam and Tur there expressly write, "and the witnesses were entirely unknown." One might object that in every get brought from overseas the witnesses are ordinarily unknown, yet the principal basis for believing the messenger's declaration is precisely Reish Lakish's rule that by Torah law validation is unnecessary. The messenger is not ordinarily questioned as to who the witnesses are. This seems to imply that even where the witnesses themselves are not known, validation is only rabbinic. It therefore appears that if someone brings a document or get and expressly says that he has no knowledge at all of where the witnesses come from, the writing is certainly not treated as a valid document or get without further proof. The principal reason that validation is unnecessary by Torah law is that forgery is a matter likely to be exposed and therefore a person is afraid to forge. If even in such a case no validation were required by Torah law, what fear of exposure would there be? A forger could invent any names he wished and say that the witnesses lived at the end of the world and that he does not know where they can be found. Rather, when he gives such an account, the forgery becomes apparent to beit din, under the principle that one who lies places his witnesses far away, and Torah law requires validation. Ordinarily, however, we do not suspect that the document was forged. The forger would fear that beit din might ask where the witnesses are. If he says he does not know, his falsehood becomes apparent; if he names their location, the matter is capable of verification. Rambam's language that the witnesses were entirely unknown therefore indicates a case where the woman herself has no information whatsoever about their location. See Pnei Yehoshua, Ketubot 108. His words can perhaps be read, with some difficulty, consistently with this explanation. Shach also writes that where the purported witnesses themselves say, "We did not sign it," validation is still not required by Torah law. Tumim asks why they should not be believed. The presumption that a person is not suspected of forgery does not prevent two witnesses from proving wrongdoing, just as every Jew has a presumption against violating Shabbat or worshiping idolatry, yet two witnesses are believed to establish that he did so. This is a strong question. It therefore appears that the rule that by Torah law no validation is required and the signatories are not believed to say, "We did not sign," applies only where each purported witness testifies solely about his own signature, saying, "This is not my handwriting," without testifying regarding the other person's signature. Each is then one witness against the apparent testimony of two signatories, and one does not overcome two. See what Shach writes in seif katan 23. If, however, each also testifies regarding the other, so that each says, "This is neither my handwriting nor my colleague's," then there are two witnesses establishing forgery. Although each separately speaks to one signature, together there are two witnesses to the forged nature of the document through a form of separate testimony. In monetary law separate testimony can be valid at least by rabbinic law, though Tumim in siman 30 writes that such separate testimony is not valid by Torah law. In that case the document may still require only rabbinic validation. See there. Shach further writes that where a document of betrothal is found, validation is required by Torah law because the document is not held by any person claiming under it, so the usual presumption that a claimant is afraid to rely upon a forgery does not apply. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this from Tosafot regarding one who finds a deed of acquisition. There is no difficulty. There, the lender claims the document and says that it is his, that he lent money on the strength of it. What difference is there whether he himself produces the writing or receives it from the finder and then claims upon it? Since he affirmatively says it is valid and seeks payment, he would fear relying on a forgery in a matter capable of being exposed. Although a deed of acquisition can theoretically be written whether or not the loan has yet been advanced, Rivash, siman 161, writes that one cannot say no loan will ever be made at all. That would transform the writing into a different kind of obligation document. Rather, the contemplated loan must ultimately be advanced, though the money of the loan may have the status described by Rivash. Moreover, the lender ordinarily must claim that the kinyan occurred in his presence, because otherwise the document is not necessarily considered one prepared for writing and delivery. He therefore fears verification. A found document of betrothal is different. If the husband is not before us and beit din itself seeks to treat the woman as betrothed solely because of the found writing, one can suspect that some dishonest person fabricated a betrothal document and threw it where it would be found in order to create a rumor and cast suspicion upon the woman's status. With a debt instrument written in the lender's name, there is ordinarily no reason for a stranger to forge it, because the lender himself would be afraid to sue upon a forged document.
