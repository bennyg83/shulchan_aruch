Seif 24. Two judges recognize the signatures of the witnesses and one does not recognize them. It appears to me that this rule applies specifically where the two judges themselves recognize the signatures. Once they sign the authentication, their signatures have at least partial effectiveness as testimony concerning the witnesses' signatures, according to Rivash and Rama. Therefore, if the third judge was not present with them, the first two have already acted in the capacity of witnesses, and their original judicial combination has been dissolved. They cannot later add the third signature and become judges again. Once their first signatures had some legal effect, they need a fresh judicial act. But if the two judges did not themselves recognize the signatures and other witnesses testified before them, then until the third judge also hears that testimony the signatures of the first two judges accomplish nothing, because otherwise their certification would be testimony from one witness based on another. Their judicial combination has therefore not yet been dissolved, because their signatures had no effect at all. This resembles the rule in Even HaEzer concerning a get: where the first delivery was entirely ineffective, a new delivery is not required merely because the first attempt occurred; but where the first delivery had some partial effect, a fresh valid delivery is required. The same distinction applies here. Where the first two judges' signatures initially had some effect, the third cannot later sign with them unless the first two execute a new certification as well. Where their signatures had no effect, the third may later join them. The same should apply to the following rule concerning testimony that one of the judges is a robber, since the teachings appear together. They concern a case in which the judges themselves recognized the witness signatures. Where they did not and merely heard testimony from others, their earlier signatures had no legal effect and the original combination was not dissolved. Accordingly, Shach's explanation of the phrase concerning signing is not correct when he speaks of testimony given before only two judges. In my view, if those judges themselves did not recognize the signatures and only heard testimony from others, then until the third judge hears it there is no valid authentication at all, since otherwise it is testimony from one witness based on another. It is therefore as though no testimony had yet been given, and the judges may later write and sign once the third has joined. This, I believe, is the meaning of Shiltei Giborim cited by Shach. Shach objects that it contradicts the Gemara and all the authorities, but according to my explanation there is no contradiction: Shiltei Giborim speaks where the judges did not recognize the signatures themselves and others testified before them. In that case everyone agrees they may sign later. This also yields a proof for Rivash's position that written testimony is effective in this context. It further suggests that the Shulchan Aruch's restriction against writing the certification stems from an increased concern for falsehood when a written certification is involved. This appears correct and clear as practical law.
