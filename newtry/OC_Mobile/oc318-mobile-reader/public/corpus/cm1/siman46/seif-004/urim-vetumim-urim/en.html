Documents are not validated at night. Validation is a judicial act. If they nevertheless validated it at night, the result depends on the law stated above regarding judges who transgressed and adjudicated at night, whether their judgment is valid. If the lender and borrower consented to validation at night, Sema and Shach hold that it is effective. See Tumim, where I wrote that this is correct with respect to a handwritten obligation, but regarding a document intended to be collected from encumbered property it requires study whether such consent is effective. Where the custom is to validate documents at night, it is effective, just as a custom of validation by a single judge is effective, Shach.
If they validated it with two, etc. Shach concludes that if others testified before the two that the signatures were those of the witnesses, or if the witnesses themselves testified to that effect, or if the borrower admitted before them that these were the witnesses' signatures, the validation is entirely ineffective, because this is testimony from one witness through another. Since they are only two, they do not constitute a beit din. But if the two themselves recognize the signatures, it is not a beit din validation. Nevertheless, according to what Rama writes below in seif 7, it constitutes valid testimony regarding validation. It is still not called a beit din validation, only validation by testimony, and there are many practical differences between the two, as explained above. This is Shach's conclusion, unlike Sema, and it is correct.
It appears to me that the reason, etc. This reason is strained, as all the commentators note and as Shach writes. Even an unquestionably expert single judge cannot validate documents alone. The entire reason is rather that since this became the accepted custom, it is as though the parties accepted him upon themselves, and as though from the outset they stipulated that the rabbi alone would be believed to validate. Any monetary stipulation is valid, and there is no need for forced explanations. Sema writes that one must not extend the custom beyond its limits. Only the rabbi may validate alone. A practice in which synagogue functionaries or scribes do so is certainly a mistaken custom, unless two people testify that the signatures were made in their presence, in which case it is testimony, as below in seif 7 and as explained above.
Validation of documents is rabbinic. See Tumim, where I discussed at length whether, when the borrower expressly claims that the document is forged, validation may then be required by Torah law. Many authorities dispute this, and numerous leniencies are based on the principle that validation is rabbinic, as Rama writes. If in a particular case validation were required by Torah law, those leniencies could not be used. I concluded that even when the borrower claims forgery, although Rambam and the Mechaber rule regarding a get that Torah law requires validation, that is because matters of forbidden relationships require fully clarified testimony. In monetary law, however, Torah law does not require validation even when the borrower claims forgery. The presumption that a person would not brazenly forge a document is a compelling inference sufficient to extract money, just as money may be extracted through certain forms of clear circumstantial knowledge even without direct sight, because witnesses were created only for cases of falsehood. See there. If the witnesses themselves say, "We never signed this document," I established from Tosafos, unlike Maharik, that even by Torah law monetary matters require validation, and certainly so in testimony concerning women. This also appears to be Shach's view, unlike Sema, whose language implies that Torah law requires validation in every case where the borrower claims forgery. See there.
