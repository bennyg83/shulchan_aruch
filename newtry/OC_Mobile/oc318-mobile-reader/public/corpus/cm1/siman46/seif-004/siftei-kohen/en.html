[" With two, it is not an authentication. This is true even after the fact, as stated below in seif 29. Sema, seif katan 7, writes that this applies only where the two judges themselves do not recognize the signatures of the witnesses to the document, but the signing witnesses acknowledge before them that these are their signatures. If the judges themselves recognize the signatures, however, it is as though they testify concerning the maneh stated in the document. This does not appear correct to me. Three are therefore required, for otherwise this would be testimony from the mouth of another witness, even though they recognize the signatures. This is also evident from the Gemara in the second chapter of Ketubot concerning three who sat to authenticate a document, two of whom recognized the witnesses’ signatures and one of whom did not, as explained in the Tur and Mechaber below in seif 24. [" It is not an authentication by a beit din. [" And it appears to me that the reason, etc. Namely, that a single expert judge has the status of three ordinary judges, etc. Bach and the other later authorities also followed the Rama in this reason. In my view, however, this reasoning is incorrect, because authentication is not analogous to adjudication. As I wrote above in siman 3, seif katan 8, only with regard to adjudication does one expert equal three ordinary judges, but not with regard to receiving an admission or authenticating a document. In authentication, therefore, a single judge would leave us with testimony from the mouth of another witness, and three are required in order to constitute a beit din, as Tosafot, the Rosh, and the other poskim write. See also above, siman 28, seifim 20 and 21. Ritva likewise writes in the second chapter of Ketubot that regarding a sage who is learned, discerning, and qualified to judge alone, his teacher maintained that the Sages specifically decreed that document authentication requires three, so that it not be testimony from the mouth of another witness. Thus, even the beit din of Rav Ashi could not authenticate with two, although Rav Ashi was certainly learned and discerning. His reasoning is convincing. [" Authentication of documents is rabbinic. Sema, seif katan 9, cites Hagahot Mordechai to Kiddushin, p. 665a, as saying that if the witnesses are alive and state that they did not sign this document, or if they are no longer alive and the litigant says the document is invalid, authentication is required even by Torah law. It is true that Hagahot Mordechai records this in the responsum of Rabbi Avigdor HaKohen. His position is that the statement that authentication is rabbinic applies only where there is no contradiction. He interprets the opening passage of Gittin to mean that, as a matter of Torah law, two witnesses should not be needed to authenticate ordinary documents where there is no contradiction, in accordance with Reish Lakish’s rule that witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din. The Sages nevertheless required two for ordinary documents even without contradiction, such as collection in the debtor’s absence or from orphans or purchasers. In the case of a get, because of concern for agunah, one witness suffices where there is no contradiction. Although the declaration ‘it was written and signed before me’ was instituted because of the possibility that the husband might later challenge the get, at present he has not come and there is no contradiction. But where the litigant is before us and says, ‘It is forged,’ authentication is required even by Torah law. Rabbi Avigdor sought proof from the institution of a date in a get, intended to prevent a man from shielding his sister’s daughter. If forged signatures required no Torah level authentication even against contradiction, the safeguard could be defeated by fabricating a get and witnesses. He therefore concluded that Reish Lakish’s rule does not apply where there is contradiction. Even if the woman would join the husband in acknowledging the forged get, once the circumstances demonstrate that it was produced for rescue, beit din itself pleads forgery and requires authentication, treating the case as one containing contradiction. He further argued that obvious fraud cannot be dismissed as too remote, because in Yevamot we are concerned that a woman might erase the date in a kiddushin document for purposes of rescue, despite the obviousness of the fraud. This concludes his position. In my view, however, his words are not compelling and his proof does not establish the point. To the contrary, once Torah law does not require authentication because we do not suspect a person of forging a document, that same reason explains why the Sages were not concerned that one would forge a get to shield his sister’s daughter. Erasing a date is also not comparable, because that is a common and easy act, whereas forging a document is not, as Rashi says at the beginning of Gittin, a person is not brazen enough to forge a document. Rashbam likewise writes in Get Pashut that Jews are not suspected of fabricating forged documents. Moreover, the concern about erasing a date does not prove a concern that one will imitate witnesses’ signatures, a fraud that can later be investigated. Even regarding the date itself, the Gemara states that we are not concerned that a fraudster will simply cut away the date. Thus one form of fraud cannot be equated with another, certainly not the far more serious act of drawing forged signatures, where the matter can be investigated. Rabbi Avigdor’s interpretation of the opening of Gittin as referring specifically to a present absence of contradiction is also difficult, because the Gemara explicitly states that even if the husband later comes and challenges the get, we disregard him. The same is stated at the end of the second chapter of Gittin, and the implication there is that she may marry on its basis initially despite the husband’s challenge. Rashba states this explicitly in his commentary to the beginning of Gittin, and it is also clear from the Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer, beginning of siman 142. This appears to be what Tosafot meant at the end of the second chapter of Ketubot when they wrote that Gittin proves that even where the litigant alleges forgery, Torah law treats the signatures as testimony already examined in beit din. All this therefore contradicts Rabbi Avigdor HaKohen. It is likewise forced to explain the Gemara’s statements that a witness may become a judge in authentication because authentication is rabbinic, and that an adult may testify regarding matters he saw as a minor, such as recognizing his father’s handwriting, because the Sages trusted him in rabbinic authentication, as applying only where the litigant is not presently alleging forgery. If that were the limitation, the Gemara should have stated it. Yet all the poskim, including the Tur and Mechaber in many places, state the leniencies of authentication without such qualification. Tosafot in Ketubot likewise write explicitly that by Torah law no authentication is required because signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony was examined in beit din, and that even a person established as a robber who produces a document does not appear to require Torah level authentication, for although suspected regarding money, he is afraid to forge a document lest beit din recognize the forgery. Thus, when the Sages instituted authentication against a claim of forgery, they did so even when the debtor himself says that he knows the document is forged. Although Tosafot ultimately conclude there that the Sages required authentication even against orphans, their premise remains that Torah law does not require authentication even when the debtor alleges forgery. Tosafot say the same in the second chapter of Ketubot, explaining that according to Rashi the Torah does not require authentication because signed witnesses are like testimony examined in beit din, and the Sages required authentication only when forgery is alleged, not for other claims such as payment. Rabbenu Tam agreed. Tosafot in Yesh Nochalin likewise say that the Sages required authentication only when one explicitly alleges forgery. Rashbam in Get Pashut, Ritva at the beginning of Bava Metzia, and Responsa Maimoniyot to Sefer Shoftim, siman 12, state the same. The Tur at the beginning of this siman also implies this: the Sages instituted authentication because until a document is authenticated, not only is the debtor believed if he says it is forged, but even if he merely says it was paid he is believed by a migo that he could have alleged forgery. This plainly treats even the forgery claim as subject to a rabbinic institution. This is the primary ruling. I later found that Maharik, shoresh 74, responded directly to Rabbi Avigdor HaKohen and rejected his position, writing that the assertion that every Talmudic statement that authentication is rabbinic concerns only a case where the defendant does not allege forgery was an error, and citing Rashbam and Tosafot in support. See there. As to Rabbi Avigdor’s separate position that if the witnesses themselves are before us and say, ‘We did not sign,’ authentication may be required by Torah law, his view at first appears more plausible, because the ordinary statements that authentication is rabbinic can be understood as addressing the usual case where the witnesses are not themselves contradicting their signatures. Yet the responsum cited by Maharik disputes him even on this point and proves from the Yerushalmi that Reish Lakish’s rule that signed witnesses are like testimony examined in beit din applies even where they later say, ‘We did not sign,’ at least once the document had first come before beit din. The Nimukei Yosef in Eizehu Neshekh and the Ran at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah, in the name of Ramban, likewise explain the Yerushalmi as a case where the handwriting is not independently established and the witnesses themselves deny their signatures. Responsa Maimoniyot to Sefer Shoftim, siman 12, also states that by Torah law even if they say, ‘We did not sign,’ they are not believed. Nevertheless, the practical ruling on this particular point requires further study. Where the witnesses themselves are not present denying their signatures, however, and only the litigant alleges forgery, it is clear to me that Torah law does not require authentication, contrary to Sema. On this basis, the Rama’s ruling in Even HaEzer, siman 42, seif 4, also requires study. He writes that if a document is found stating that a certain man betrothed a certain woman and witnesses are signed upon it, no concern for kiddushin exists until the document is authenticated, contrary to those who are stringent. This follows his Darkei Moshe, where he understood Maharik, shoresh 74, to have ruled that even though the witnesses said they never signed such a document and never saw the kiddushin, concern for kiddushin remains and a get is required, while Hagahot Mordechai and Rashba held that no get is required until proper authentication. The Rama therefore concluded that Maharik’s position was rejected because he had not seen those sources. But the responsum printed in Maharik, shoresh 74, is not Maharik’s own responsum at all. It was written by an earlier great authority who directly answered Rabbi Avigdor HaKohen and rejected the very responsum recorded in Hagahot Mordechai. I have already shown above that we do not follow Rabbi Avigdor’s position that a kiddushin document requires Torah level authentication. Thus, the Rama’s distinction between an authenticated and unauthenticated document is not correct. In my view, even an unauthenticated kiddushin document may create Torah level kiddushin, because Torah law does not require authentication. The specific case addressed by Rabbi Avigdor is nevertheless properly decided for a different reason, namely Rashba’s rule that a document written without authorization of the relevant party is invalid, as I explained above in siman 39, seif katan 8. Likewise, a kiddushin document merely found in the marketplace requires authentication, because when it is not in anyone’s possession we cannot apply the presumption that a particular holder would be afraid to forge it, since we do not know who the interested party is. This is analogous to the Yerushalmi at the end of Yevamot concerning a writing found stating that a certain person died, where even the authority who permits the wife to remarry does so only because of the special leniency of agunah, as with hearsay testimony. But where a husband produces a document signed by witnesses stating that he betrothed a particular woman, and it specifies that it was written with the woman’s authorization, even if the signatures cannot presently be authenticated and the woman claims forgery, the kiddushin takes effect by Torah law, for Torah law does not require authentication. It is true that rabbinic law requires authentication, so that until it is authenticated it does not have the full legal force of an authenticated document and in monetary matters we preserve the existing possession. Nevertheless, in a matter of kiddushin a Torah level doubt certainly remains even after the rabbinic enactment. Rashba also agrees with this. This appears clear to me, contrary to the Rama:"]
