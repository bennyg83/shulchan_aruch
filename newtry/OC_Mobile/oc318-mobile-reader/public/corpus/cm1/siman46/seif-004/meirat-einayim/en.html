Therefore documents are not authenticated at night. See above siman 5, seif katan 7, where I wrote that if both litigants come before a judge at night and ask him to adjudicate, he may do so because it is as though they accepted a relative or disqualified person. Authentication is different. The lender comes alone to authenticate his document, so that rationale does not apply. Although the borrower's consent is not required and the document may be authenticated by day against his will, as the Tur and Mechaber write in this siman, it appears to me that doing so at night without his consent is prohibited.
With two it is not an authentication. Because testimony before only two would be testimony through other witnesses, which is ineffective. But when there are three and they constitute a beit din, they authenticate the signatures, and a later beit din judging on the basis of that document is then treated as though it had heard the testimony from the original document witnesses themselves. This applies specifically where the judges do not themselves recognize the signatures and the signers merely acknowledge before them that these are their signatures. But where the judges themselves recognize the signatures, it is as though they testify to the debt contained in the document. If the instrument is in the borrower's own handwriting and he admits before two witnesses that it is his handwriting, it appears that their authentication is effective. See what I write immediately below.
Since the custom is that the rabbi, etc. Mahari in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 332, writes: a rabbi who heads a yeshiva and authenticated a document alone, if a litigant later challenges it, perhaps the authentication is ineffective. He brings proof. Nevertheless, there is a widespread custom that heads of yeshivot authenticate documents alone, and perhaps in such a case custom overrides the halacha. Beit Yosef comments: 'We have neither heard nor seen anyone follow such a practice.' Although Rama labors to provide a rationale for the custom, one should not extend it further. The current practice whereby scribes or cantors authenticate documents certainly remains subject to challenge by a litigant. This applies where he cannot otherwise authenticate the document witnesses. But if the instrument is in the borrower's handwriting and the borrower admits before the rabbi that it is his signature, that authentication is effective to the extent that if the borrower later claims, 'It never happened,' he must swear against it, as explained below in siman 81, seif 10. See Darkei Moshe there, where you will also find reason to question Rama's rationale from the incident of Rav Ashi.
For authentication of documents is rabbinic. Hagahot Mordechai to Kiddushin writes that if the witnesses say, 'We did not sign this document,' or if the witnesses are no longer alive and a litigant claims that it is invalid, authentication is required even by Torah law.
