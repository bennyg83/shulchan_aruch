The Sema writes that where the two judges themselves recognize the witnesses' signatures, that can suffice because it is as though they testify to the debt in the document. The Shach disagrees and says that even recognition of the signatures is only testimony about the handwriting, so three judges are required for validation. The passage in Ketubot concerning three who sit to validate a document, two of whom recognize the signatures, appears to support the Shach. Yet Rivash rules that witnesses may provide validation in writing, which supports the Sema in a certain form. The matter can be clarified as follows. Two people who merely testify that they recognize the signatures are ordinarily giving testimony from another testimony. What saves this from hearsay is that signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din. Thus witnesses who recognize their signatures may report the existence of that already established judicial testimony. But if the new written testimony itself was not created as a proper legal document, it does not itself acquire the status of examined testimony. Therefore if the later beit din merely hears other people identify those validation witnesses' signatures, that becomes testimony from hearsay. Rivash's permissive ruling works where beit din itself receives and recognizes the written validation testimony. It does not mean that an endless chain of people can validate the signatures of the validators. This also explains Rama's rulings that an informal written account that was not prepared as a proper document can nevertheless be used if it is itself properly received by beit din. Thus the Sema is correct where the beit din itself recognizes the original witnesses' signatures. The Shach is correct that a formal beit din of three is still needed for the act of document validation, even though the evidentiary recognition can come directly from the judges themselves. The distinction is between the evidentiary source and the judicial act of validation.
Validation of documents is rabbinic. Hagahot Mordechai writes that if the witnesses say, "We did not sign this document," or if they are unavailable and the litigant claims the document is invalid, validation is required by Torah law. The Shach argues at length that even where the litigant denies the signatures, Torah law ordinarily does not require validation. He therefore questions Rama in Even HaEzer, who rules that if one merely finds written that so and so betrothed so and so, with witnesses signed, no concern for kiddushin exists until the document is validated. In my view Rama's practical ruling can be maintained because his case concerns a writing found in the marketplace, not one produced by an identified interested party. The ordinary presumption that a person would not brazenly forge a document does not apply with equal force when we do not know who produced it. Nevertheless, Tosafot in Ketubot and Ramban imply that even a found deed of acquisition is not necessarily suspected of forgery on a Torah level. They cite Shmuel's rule that a found acquisition deed can be returned because the obligor already subjected himself, even though the instrument is not validated. This suggests that even a document found outside any person's possession may retain a Torah presumption against forgery. If so, Rama's requirement of validation remains difficult. Moreover, if a found document truly required Torah validation, one could not later validate it through lenient rabbinic methods such as comparison of signatures or validation outside the litigant's presence. Yet the sugyot do not clearly impose such a restriction. The matter therefore requires further study.
