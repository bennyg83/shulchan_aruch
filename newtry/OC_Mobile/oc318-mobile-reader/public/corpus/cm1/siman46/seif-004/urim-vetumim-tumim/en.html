Documents are not authenticated at night. Sema and Shach write that if both lender and borrower consent to authenticate at night, it is like accepting a relative or otherwise disqualified judge and can be effective, just as in other monetary adjudication. I am hesitant about this. Authentication can transform a document into one enforceable against purchasers. Before authentication, later purchasers can be given the defense that the instrument may be forged, as explained below in siman 106. The borrower and lender can waive procedural rights between themselves, but how can their agreement prejudice purchasers? If a borrower accepts a relative or disqualified person to adjudicate against him, that certainly does not allow the creditor to take property the borrower had already sold to third parties merely because of the borrower's private consent. The same should apply here. If nighttime authentication is intrinsically ineffective under the ordinary rules, the borrower's agreement should not make it effective against purchasers. Therefore, in my view, Sema and Shach's leniency applies only to an instrument enforceable against the borrower himself, such as the ordinary handwritten obligations used in Poland that collect only from free assets. But where the authenticated document would gain force against encumbered property or purchasers, consent of borrower and lender should not be enough.
Authentication of documents is rabbinic. Shach, seif katan 9, discusses at length whether, when the borrower expressly claims that the document is forged, authentication is still only rabbinic or becomes required by Torah law. Shach brings many proofs from Tosafot in Ketubot, Rashi, and Rashbam that even when forgery is expressly alleged, authentication remains rabbinic. He therefore challenges Rama in Even HaEzer 42, who rules that if a document is found stating that a certain man betrothed a certain woman and witnesses are signed upon it, so long as the document has not been authenticated we need not be concerned for the kiddushin. Shach argues: by Torah law, a signed document does not need authentication. If so, there should already be Torah-level kiddushin. However, Shach overlooked, as did Maharshal and several other authorities cited by later responsa, Rambam in Hilchot Gerushin 7 and the ruling of Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer 141:13. Rambam rules that if an agent brings a get in Eretz Yisrael without saying "It was written and signed before me," and the husband comes and challenges the get as forged, and the get is not authenticated, the woman is not divorced, she must leave if she remarried, and the child is a mamzer. This language indicates that when forgery is claimed, the get is invalid on a Torah level. If Shach were correct that authentication is still merely rabbinic, the child would at most be a rabbinic mamzer, and Rambam would not simply say the woman is not divorced. Beit Shmuel already raises this. At first glance, the opening sugya of Gittin is also difficult. Rava explains the rule of saying "It was written and signed before me" by concern that witnesses may not be available later to authenticate the get. The Gemara asks why two witnesses should not then be required, and answers that document authentication is rabbinic because Reish Lakish says signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony was already examined in beit din. Yet the entire purpose of the declaration is precisely to protect against a future challenge by the husband that the get is forged. Without a challenge, an Eretz Yisrael get needs no such declaration. If a husband's explicit forgery claim creates a Torah-level need for authentication, why does the Gemara call authentication rabbinic? The Gemara itself says that if the husband comes and challenges, the get can be invalidated. It later answers that the agent is careful because he knows the husband may challenge. This suggests that the rabbinic nature of authentication and the future challenge must somehow be combined. At first I thought the key was Maggid Mishneh's explanation of Rambam. Maggid Mishneh says the husband is believed in the get case because the woman remains in her prior presumption of being married. Even if authentication is ordinarily rabbinic, when the husband makes a definite forgery claim and the woman does not know whether the paper is genuine, we leave her in her original status. Accordingly, perhaps Rambam means only that where no one contradicts the husband, his definite claim joins the woman's prior marital status and creates a Torah-level obstacle. In ordinary monetary law, by contrast, the creditor does contradict the debtor and says the document is genuine. Then the case returns to the ordinary rule that authentication is only rabbinic. This would explain why the great authorities discussing Choshen Mishpat did not cite Rambam's get ruling. It would also fit Rashi in Gittin 5a. The Gemara establishes the case of an agent who brought a get from abroad but became unable to speak, and says the get must be authenticated by its signatures. Rashi explains that this requirement can apply even according to a view of Rabbah that does not share Rava's general concern about unavailable authentication witnesses. Why would authentication be necessary if the locale already knows how to write a get properly and Rava's concern is absent? According to the distinction above, because the agent can no longer verbally contradict a later husband's forgery challenge. Where the agent is able to speak, he would naturally contradict the husband and say, "He gave me this very get." Then, by Torah law, there is no reason to believe the husband's later challenge; ordinary authentication remains rabbinic. But once the agent becomes unable to testify, and the case is abroad where witnesses are difficult to find, a future challenge by the husband can become a Torah-level problem because no one stands opposite him with a definite claim. Therefore even Rabbah can require authentication in that special case. Rashi elsewhere says we do not ordinarily worry that the agent will die or disappear. But here the agent has already become unable to testify, so the concern is concrete. This is attractive. It would also vindicate Rama in Even HaEzer 42. Rama's case merely says that a written kiddushin document is found. It does not say the alleged husband is present and affirmatively says he betrothed her. If the woman says the document is forged and no man is present to contradict her, then there is no independently opposed claim. She remains in her presumption of being unmarried, and the unauthenticated document creates no Torah-level kiddushin. However, Rambam in Hilchot Gerushin 12:3 rules even more strongly. If the husband says the get is forged and the woman contradicts him, she is still not divorced for purposes of marrying another man, though she disqualifies herself from marrying a kohen because by her own admission she is divorced. The need for the kohen rationale shows that, from the law of the get itself, she is not regarded as divorced on a Torah level. If the get were effective by Torah law and merely rabbinically unverified, she would unquestionably be a divorced woman prohibited to a kohen. Here the woman directly contradicts the husband and says the get is genuine. Thus my first reading of Maggid Mishneh cannot be sustained. Maggid Mishneh's phrase that the woman does not contradict the husband must have been written merely as an additional point in one case, not as an essential requirement. Rambam evidently holds that an explicit forgery challenge can create a Torah-level need for verification even when the opposing party denies the claim. How then do we understand the Gemara's statement that authentication is rabbinic? One possibility is that the rabbinic rule rests on two presumptions together. First, a person is not ordinarily brazen enough to forge a signed document. Second, where a get is brought by an agent, the agent knows that if the husband later challenges it he will need to stand behind the delivery and therefore examines carefully before accepting the mission. The Gemara's phrase that by Torah law signed witnesses are treated as examined may operate only together with the ordinary presumption of authenticity. When no one actually challenges, these presumptions are enough to make authentication unnecessary by Torah law. When the husband comes and expressly screams "forged," one of the presumptions collapses. The paper then requires Torah-level verification unless some additional factor, such as the agent's carefulness or a special rabbinic enactment, restores reliability. This can explain why the Gemara initially worries that if the husband comes and challenges, the get will become invalid. An ordinary one-witness declaration is sufficient only for a rabbinic matter, not for a Torah-level dispute against the marital status. The Gemara answers that a get agent is especially careful because a man who gives a get before two witnesses checks the matter properly. Either the combination of presumptions removes the Torah-level concern entirely, or Chazal strengthened their enactment and trusted the agent even against the husband's challenge. We find elsewhere that Chazal can empower their procedural rules strongly enough to affect Torah-level consequences. This reading also explains the tension between Gittin and Bava Kamma. In Bava Kamma, even where the obligor stands and protests, we say document authentication is rabbinic and the document can be authenticated outside the debtor's presence. This suggests a distinction between ordinary monetary law and matters of marriage and divorce. In monetary law, testimony based on compelling knowledge can suffice even without direct observation because witnesses were created primarily to prevent falsehood. Where the court has a very strong presumption that a signed document is genuine, that can function like knowledge without direct sight. A false challenge does not necessarily create a Torah-level evidentiary void. By contrast, kiddushin and gerushin are matters of ervah. There, truth alone is not enough. Even where everyone knows with certainty what happened, Torah law requires formal witnessing for the creation or termination of the marital status. Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer 42:4 says that even if witnesses know with certainty that he gave her a ring, if they did not actually see the act in the legally required manner the kiddushin may fail because there is no matter of ervah with fewer than two witnesses. Thus the status is not determined only by whether the fact is probably true. The formal quality of the evidence matters. An unauthenticated written testimony directly challenged as forged may therefore lack the formal witness status needed in ervah even though, probabilistically, the forgery claim is weak. In monetary law, by contrast, the same strong presumption can suffice on a Torah level, leaving authentication only rabbinic. This would harmonize the apparently contradictory rulings of Tur and Rosh. They explain that even where the debtor stands and protests, civil document authentication remains rabbinic. Yet Tur reproduces Rambam's stringent get rulings in Even HaEzer without recording dissent. The difference is between monetary adjudication and matters of ervah. There is some proof for this distinction from the opening sugya of Gittin. The Gemara says that because signed witnesses are by Torah law treated as though their testimony was examined, Chazal were lenient for an agunah. It then asks: is this really a leniency? If the husband comes and challenges, the get will be invalidated. Tosafot and Rashba struggle to explain the question if authentication is always only rabbinic. They give narrow answers involving public suspicion. But Rambam and Tur can understand the Gemara more directly: once the husband actually comes and claims forgery, the get lacks the formal Torah-level evidentiary force required in a matter of ervah. How can one agent overcome that? The Gemara answers that the agent's special carefulness and the structure of get delivery provide the additional reliability needed. This indicates that explicit forgery can indeed trigger a Torah-level problem in ervah while ordinary monetary authentication remains rabbinic. According to this approach, Rama in Even HaEzer 42 is correct. A found kiddushin document that has never been authenticated does not by itself create a reliable matter-of-ervah testimony where its authenticity is challenged or uncertain. Still, I hesitate to rule leniently without reservation. Tosafot and Rashba at the beginning of Gittin sound as though even in this setting authentication remains rabbinic. Their language suggests the husband's challenge does not necessarily create a Torah-level invalidity. Therefore, in practical matters of kiddushin, one must take their position seriously. For monetary law, however, all agree that ordinary document authentication is rabbinic. There is a separate and important question: what if the very witnesses whose signatures appear on the document say, "These are not our signatures. They were forged"? Maharik, shoresh 74, argues at length that even then, by Torah law, they are not believed against the signed document. He brings various proofs from the Yerushalmi and Bavli. Most of his proofs can be rejected. His principal proof is from the Yerushalmi in Sheviit. R. Yochanan says witnesses can explain that a document is antedated. The Yerushalmi challenges from Reish Lakish's statement that signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony was already examined in beit din. It answers: there they said, "We did not sign"; here they say, "We signed concerning this point but not that point." Maharik infers that the Yerushalmi must be speaking where their handwriting is not independently authenticated. Otherwise the Gemara could simply distinguish that R. Yochanan's case is one where the signatures lack outside authentication. He therefore argues that even when they say, "We never signed this paper," they are not believed on a Torah level. Several later authorities, including Maharit and Maharashdam, attack this proof. In my view, before entering their objections one must recall the earlier dispute among Razah, Ramban, and Rashba about the Yerushalmi itself. Does the Yerushalmi allow witnesses to say the document is antedated even where their handwriting is independently authenticated, or only where it is not authenticated elsewhere? I discussed this at length above in siman 43. According to Ramban and those who follow him, the entire discussion of R. Yochanan necessarily concerns a document whose signatures are not independently established. The Yerushalmi challenges according to R. Meir, who holds witnesses cannot ordinarily invalidate their signed testimony through a later explanation. The answer distinguishes a common dating mistake from a total denial. On that reading, Maharik's inference collapses. Indeed, most authorities understand the Yerushalmi in that framework. Maharik relies on a reading associated with Razah, where the signatures are independently authenticated. One cannot simply transfer Razah's interpretation into Ramban's position and then prove a universal Torah rule. R. Avigdor HaKohen gives the correct explanation of the Yerushalmi's phrase, "We did not sign." It means not literally, "These signatures are forged," but rather, "We did not sign in a legally valid manner," such as saying they were under compulsion or some similar disqualifying circumstance. Maharik objects: who forces you to explain the words that way? In my view, the Yerushalmi's structure forces it. The Yerushalmi is not discussing abstract Torah law but practical law after the rabbinic enactment of authentication. It asks why witnesses are believed to say a document is antedated. If the comparison case simply meant, "We did not sign at all," and the signatures were not otherwise authenticated, everyone agrees they are believed under the rabbinic authentication system. Even R. Meir does not deny that where there is no independent authentication and the alleged signers say the signatures are not theirs at all, the paper lacks proper rabbinic authentication. R. Meir's strictness concerns using migo to weaken a signed paper through explanations like duress. Therefore the answer must mean: there the witnesses say, "We did not sign this in a valid testimonial manner," such as because of duress or another disqualification, and R. Meir does not believe them. Here, regarding the date, they can be believed because the date is an area in which ordinary mistake is possible. This is exactly the distinction explained above in siman 43. Accordingly, Maharik's proof from the Yerushalmi does not establish that witnesses who directly deny their signatures are ignored by Torah law. There is another indication from Tosafot in the second chapter of Ketubot. Tosafot explain R. Meir's view that even witnesses whose signatures are not independently authenticated do not have a migo to say, "We were under life-threatening duress," because one who admits a document was written need not authenticate it further. Rashi and Ri elsewhere give a different reason: ordinary authentication is rabbinic, so certain migo structures fail. At first glance Tosafot could imply that even when the witnesses themselves say their signatures are forged, by Torah law they are not believed. Otherwise why do they lack the migo of saying, "These signatures are not ours"? If their denial would create a Torah-level need for authentication, it seems like a strong migo. This appears to support Maharik and might seem to contradict Shach, who accepts that the debtor's forgery claim leaves authentication rabbinic but thinks the signatories themselves can create a Torah-level issue. On closer analysis, Tosafot can be read precisely in Shach's direction. Tosafot give two considerations together. One is that the witnesses may fear being contradicted if they claim their signatures were forged. Another is that ordinary document authentication is rabbinic. Why do Tosafot need both? If fear of contradiction alone defeats the migo, there is no need to mention the rabbinic nature of authentication. If rabbinic authentication alone defeats it, fear of contradiction is unnecessary. The answer depends on the nature of migo by two witnesses. Tosafot say elsewhere that ordinary migo by two people is weak because two people may not coordinate the same alternative story. Yet where the alternative is simply for both to remain silent, their silence naturally aligns. R. Shimshon in Sefer HaKeritut explains that this is why a migo of silence can operate even with two people. Shach, in his rules of migo, gives another approach. The problem of migo by two people arises where each needs the other's statement. But here, if each witness independently says, "My signature is forged," each one can undermine his own signature without needing the other to coordinate. Nimukei Yosef in Perek Chezkas HaBatim cites this in the name of Ri HaZaken. These two theories produce different alternatives. Under R. Shimshon's theory, the only reliable shared migo is silence. If both witnesses remain silent, then by Torah law signed testimony ordinarily needs no authentication at all because no one claims forgery. Only the rabbinic authentication requirement remains. Therefore silence is not a strong Torah-level alternative against the written testimony, and Tosafot can say the migo fails because authentication is rabbinic. Under Shach's theory, however, each witness can personally say, "My signature is forged." Once a witness himself makes that definite claim about his own signature, there is a real Torah-level challenge to that signature. This alternative does not require coordination. But it carries a different weakness: the witness may be afraid that other witnesses will come and prove that the signature is genuine. A single witness can be contradicted by two and then exposed as false. Thus Tosafot's first rationale, fear of contradiction, answers this alternative. Now both parts of Tosafot are necessary. If the alleged migo is silence, it fails because the underlying authentication need is only rabbinic. If the alleged migo is each witness claiming his own signature was forged, it is weakened because each fears independent contradiction. This reading is exact and elegant. It also shows that when the actual signatories themselves definitively say, "These are not our signatures," their statement can create a Torah-level need for authentication. That conclusion is also reasonable in substance. Two valid witnesses who say, "We never signed this paper," are not parties with a financial interest. Why should their testimony be disregarded? The presumption that people do not brazenly forge documents is no stronger than the general presumption that an ordinary Jew does not violate Shabbat or worship idolatry. If two witnesses testify that someone did such an act, their testimony is accepted despite the presumption. Why should this factual testimony about a forged signature be different? There is no reason. Accordingly, the great majority of authorities correctly conclude that when the supposed signatories themselves say the signatures are forged, their testimony is accepted on a Torah level and the paper requires genuine authentication. Maharik's position is exceptional. One must, however, distinguish between this and the rule discussed above concerning witnesses explaining that the date was mistaken. A witness saying, "This is not my signature at all," denies the very existence of his written testimony. A witness saying, "I signed, but I was mistaken about the date," seeks to limit or retract part of signed testimony already established. The latter is governed by the rules of migo, independent authentication, and inability to retract after testimony is fixed. The former is ordinary direct testimony about whether the signature is genuine. This distinction resolves many of the apparent contradictions among the Yerushalmi, Tosafot, Ramban, Rashba, Rashi, Ri, and the later authorities. Accordingly, the practical conclusions are: In ordinary monetary law, when the debtor alone claims forgery, document authentication remains rabbinic. In matters of kiddushin and gerushin, an explicit forgery challenge can create a more severe Torah-level problem because formal testimony is required in matters of ervah. When the very witnesses whose signatures appear on the paper say, "These are not our signatures," their direct testimony can create a Torah-level need for authentication even in monetary law. This is the view of the majority of authorities and is the sounder position.
