At night. Even after the fact, document validation performed at night is invalid, just as judgment at night is invalid under siman 7, seif 2. There is no distinction in this regard between monetary documents and a get, even in a case of agunah. So writes Responsa R. Eliyahu Mizrachi, siman 82, where he discusses the matter at length. Shach.
With two. The Sema explains that authentication by two is considered testimony transmitted from one set of witnesses through another, which is ineffective. If three act together, however, they constitute a beit din and can validate the signatures. A later beit din that adjudicates on the basis of the document is then regarded as though it heard the testimony directly from the original witnesses. This applies specifically where the three judges themselves do not recognize the witnesses' signatures and the signatories appear before them and admit that the signatures are theirs. But if the judges themselves recognize the signatures, the Sema says it is as though they testify directly to the amount written in the document. Likewise, if the borrower himself signed the document and acknowledges before two witnesses that it is his handwriting, the Sema writes that such validation appears effective. The Shach disputes the Sema's statement concerning judges who recognize the witnesses' signatures. They still were not present at the loan, so they do not testify to the amount itself but only to the handwriting. Therefore three are required even when the signatures are recognized, for otherwise the process remains equivalent to witness testimony transmitted through witnesses. The Gemara also indicates this. The Taz likewise disagrees with the Sema on this point.
That they practiced. The Sema writes that even though Rama struggled to give a reason for the custom, we should not extend the custom beyond its established bounds. The contemporary practice whereby scribes and cantors validate documents certainly permits a litigant to object. This concerns validation of witnesses' signatures. If, however, the document is in the borrower's own handwriting and the borrower admits before the official that it is his handwriting, such acknowledgment is effective at least to defeat a claim that the debt never existed, so that the borrower must swear as explained in siman 81, seif 10. Sema.
An expert. The Shach writes that this reason is incorrect. A single expert judge is equivalent to three only for adjudication, not for receiving an admission or validating a document, because those matters otherwise amount to testimony from witnesses transmitted through another and therefore require three judges constituting a beit din. Tosafot, the Rosh, Ritva, and other poskim write so, and Maharshal discusses it at length in the last chapter of HaGozel, siman 11. It appears, however, that in matters such as these established custom governs, as explained in siman 28, seif 23, even if the custom conflicts with the strict law. Terumat HaDeshen likewise writes so. The Bach writes that according to our custom a rabbi cannot validate a document at night. The Shach adds that perhaps where the custom is specifically to validate at night, here too we say that custom overrides the strict halacha.
Rabbinic. The Shach discusses at length and disputes Hagahot Mordechai and Rama regarding the leniencies of document validation stated here and in Even HaEzer, siman 42, seif 4. See also Beit Shmuel there. He writes that all leniencies based on the principle that validation of documents is rabbinic apply only to the handwriting of witnesses. They do not apply to a document in the borrower's own handwriting, whose authentication is required on a Torah level. Such a document also must, according to all opinions, be authenticated in the borrower's presence. This is explicit in the Sema, siman 35, seif katan 9, and is likewise indicated by Rav Sherira Gaon and other poskim. The Shach notes, however, that the practice appears to be lenient even in validating a borrower's handwriting outside his presence. Perhaps in this too established custom overrides the strict halacha, but where no such custom exists one should not be lenient. Even with witnesses' signatures, if they wrote without the litigant's authorization we do not say that their testimony has the status of testimony already examined by beit din. See siman 39, seif 3 in the gloss and seif 4, and what is written there in seif katan 11 in the name of the Shach.
