A beit din of two authenticated it. Even if they wrote before their signatures, 'We were sitting as a panel of three,' if they did not also write, 'and one is no longer present,' people may say that at first they intended to add a third merely as an extra precaution, but a third did not become available and they authenticated with only two. An observer might then conclude that two suffice to authenticate a document. So writes the Rosh.
Even if it says, 'in beit din.' Meaning, it says only, 'we were sitting as a beit din.' But if it says, 'we were sitting as a three-member beit din,' the Mechaber concludes that it is valid, because we do not suspect them to such an extent.
Language indicating that there were three. Rambam, cited by the Tur, writes that if the text itself indicates that there were three, such as, 'So-and-so said to us, and we said to him,' nothing further is required. It appears to me that even if that third person was not a recognized expert, and even if they did not state that he instructed them to authenticate, the wording proves that a third person was present. Since they previously wrote, 'we sat as a beit din,' or 'we sat as three,' an observer will not suspect them. The Gemara's phrase, 'and Rav Ashi said to us,' is not necessarily exact, as I explained in Perishah. This differs from Eir Shushan, who requires the third person to be a publicly recognized expert and to have instructed them to authenticate the document. That is not persuasive. Only Rashi, according to his own explanation, required an expert, because he explains that the text merely records that the matter occurred before an expert, meaning that the expert himself was not one of the judges authenticating it. In such a case the fact that it occurred before him reassures us that the authentication was proper, and therefore he must be an expert who would not err. But according to Rambam's explanation this is unnecessary. See also above siman 39 and my Perishah here.
Even if it says only, 'this document,' etc. Responsa Ramban, siman 91, implies that the signatures of all three are required. This refers back to the preceding law. Beit Yosef cites it.
