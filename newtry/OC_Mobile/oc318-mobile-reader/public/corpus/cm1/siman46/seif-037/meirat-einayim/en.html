Two witnesses are signed on the document and they died. This is the Gemara's wording, 'and they died.' According to the Rosh cited nearby in Rama's gloss, this is not literal; it means that the witnesses are not before us to authenticate their signatures. See Derishah.
They are believed, etc. Because the mouth that created the prohibition is the mouth that permits it, and they are believed through a migo.
Unless he claims that it was established, etc. Meaning, the lender says: 'Do not tear my document, because I will authenticate it.' Beit din does not make that claim for him, for even if he authenticates it there will be two witnesses against two. See Perishah.
From a document that was challenged, etc. The reason and dispute were explained above in this siman, seifim 17 and 18.
They do not tear it. Meaning, they also do not collect with it. But if he seized movable property, it is not removed from him, as Rama writes in the gloss, based on the Tur. See there for the differing opinions as to what type of seizure is effective.
All this applies where the witnesses have already died. Meaning, then the later witnesses are not testifying to disqualify living witnesses. The same applies if two witnesses say that the signers had been related through their wives at the time of signing, for now they are not testifying to disqualify the witnesses personally but only to invalidate the document. So writes Ran, cited by Darkei Moshe, seif 33.
The document too becomes invalid. The Tur writes this in the name of Tosafot: witnesses signed on a document are no stronger than if they were standing before us, in which case others could say, 'They are disqualified.' The Rosh, however, holds that once witnesses are signed on a document, it is as though their testimony has already been investigated in beit din, and later witnesses are no longer believed to invalidate the document. See Perishah and Derishah.
And the fact that they are not believed, etc. Hagahot Ashri writes that with witnesses to a receipt, the borrower retains what is in his possession and the receipt is valid, because money remains with its possessor. So cites Darkei Moshe, seif 33.
If he seized movable property, the reference is specifically to movable property, etc. Seizure does not apply to land. Such seizure of movable property is effective even if made before witnesses, because once the signers' handwriting is authenticated from another source, the case is two witnesses against two, and the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract.
And they say within the time of immediate speech, etc. If they say it after that interval, they would be retracting from prior testimony and would not be believed.
Or, 'we were disqualified as witnesses because of relationship and afterward became unrelated.' For example, they were related through marriage because of their wives, and the wives later died. They do not make themselves wicked by saying this, unlike saying, 'It was an accommodation document,' which does render them wicked and therefore they are not believed, as the Mechaber writes afterward. A relative is different. We say that the underlying event was true and that the loan really took place. Although they thereby caused collection from encumbered property unlawfully, this is not necessarily an act of wickedness, because the loan in fact preceded the later purchaser's acquisition and the purchaser should not have bought. Furthermore, the witnesses can explain that they assumed the lender would not rely upon them alone but would add valid witnesses, which according to the Geonim would make the document valid, as the Tur writes in siman 45, seifim 12 and 14.
And here there is only one witness. This does not mean that the remaining witness has the status of one witness who imposes a Torah oath, following Ramah and those who agree with him, cited by the Tur here. The Mechaber himself rules below in siman 82, seif 12, that in such a case it is as though there is no witness at all and only a hesset oath is required, following Rabbi Matityah and the Tur. If the remaining witness truly imposed a Torah oath, the Mechaber could not have written that the witness who says there was a condition is believed, because once he is believed, the other witness cannot impose an oath on the borrower. Rather, the Mechaber writes that the witness who says there was no condition is 'only like one witness' to exclude Ramah's view cited by the Tur in seif 36: when one witness says there was a condition and the other says there was none, if they testify concerning movable property the defendant takes a Torah oath based on the one who says there was no condition, because the Torah believed one witness to impose an oath, and wherever the Torah believed one witness he is treated like two, so the one saying there was a condition cannot stand against him. The Mechaber rejects that reasoning. Since the witness who says there was a condition is also believed, it is as though there is no document at all and the claimant sues orally. The defendant then takes a hesset oath and is exempt, as the Tur and Mechaber rule at the end of siman 82.
It was an accommodation document. An accommodation document should not be written and doing so is improper. By saying this, the witnesses make themselves wicked, and a person cannot render himself wicked by his own testimony. Hagahot Nimukei Yosef to chapter Chazakat HaBatim writes, however, that daily practice is to make an ordinary kinyan and trust the borrower regarding the money, even though he thereby undertakes an obligation. The prohibition concerns only writing the document itself. So writes Ritva, cited by Darkei Moshe, seif 34.
And afterward it became clear to them that it had been an accommodation document, etc. This is not a retraction from their prior testimony, but separate testimony about something they learned afterward. It is like witnesses testifying that a document they signed was later paid or waived.
If their handwriting is authenticated from another source, etc., they are not believed concerning anything, etc. This requires study. The Mechaber rules in siman 82, seif 12, that witnesses are believed to say, 'Our words were conditional,' even if their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere, because that does not retract the original testimony; the document remains valid if the condition is fulfilled. In Perishah and Derishah there I wrote that the Tur's wording also requires precision. He says that where their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere they are 'not believed concerning anything,' and afterward writes that the Rosh agreed to that view. Yet this contradicts what the Tur writes at the end of siman 29 and in siman 82. We must therefore say that 'they are not believed concerning anything' refers only to the matters listed in the Gemara: 'we were minors,' 'we were disqualified,' 'we were coerced,' 'it was an accommodation document,' or 'our words were subject to a protest,' and does not refer to 'our words were conditional,' which is not included with those expressions. The essential distinction is that saying there was a condition does not uproot the principal document, and for that reason the Gemara treats it as an unresolved question. The other claims uproot the document entirely, and therefore the Gemara plainly rules that they are not believed. The Mechaber's phrase that they are not believed in any matter that invalidates the document can likewise be understood to refer to claims that entirely uproot the document. Although the Mechaber writes in siman 29, seif 1, that a witness cannot later add a condition to oral testimony, one can distinguish: there he already testified orally before beit din that the debtor was liable, and had there been a condition he should have mentioned it at once rather than testify without qualification. Here the witnesses merely signed a document. The Mechaber can therefore follow the Rosh and Tur here. Rambam, however, holds that even 'our words were conditional' is not believed once the signatures are independently authenticated, as he writes in chapter 3 of Hilchot Edut. Beit Yosef in siman 29 writes that according to Rambam the two cases are alike and the witnesses may not later say there was a condition, whereas according to the Rosh they may in both cases. The Mechaber's distinction therefore remains somewhat forced. See Derishah.
That they should tell the holder of the document. The Rosh, klal 98, siman 4, writes regarding a mortgage document that did not specify the partition walls: had the partitions not been included, the document should have stated that explicitly. Even if the witnesses say afterward that the partitions were not included, they are not believed because they contradict what is implied by the document. So cites Darkei Moshe, seif 39.
Except to say, 'A protest was delivered before us.' The reason is that the Gemara says a protest is something that may be written. Ran explains that the sale document itself was properly writable, because if the sale is later voided because of the protest, the seller must still return the money he received, as recorded in that document. Therefore, according to this view, even if the witnesses' handwriting is authenticated elsewhere they are believed to say a protest was delivered, because they do not entirely uproot their testimony; the document still serves to recover the money. Ran adds another explanation, which I cited in Perishah. According to that approach, 'it may be written' means that the witnesses performed a mitzvah by signing the loan or acquisition document in order to save the borrower or seller from harm. Under that explanation they would not be believed once their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere. Even where witnesses say they signed under threat to life, the document was certainly permissible to write, yet they are still not believed once their signatures are independently authenticated. See Derishah and Perishah.
