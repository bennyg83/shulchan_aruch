His handwriting is authenticated from another source. Beit Yosef in siman 29 discusses a responsum of Rashba concerning one who wrote a mortgage of five hundred dinarim on his courtyard to his brother Shimon. Reuven and Shimon both died. Reuven's heirs claim that the mortgage was only conditional upon Reuven purchasing land for his wife, that he fulfilled the condition, and that the mortgage document therefore lost its force. Shimon's heirs deny any such condition, while Reuven's heirs have witnesses to it. Rashba ruled that even if the signatures are independently authenticated, those witnesses are believed, because they are not contradicting the witnesses to the document but clarifying the substance of the testimony. Beit Yosef writes that this does not contradict Rashba's earlier responsum, because here different witnesses testify concerning the condition. Gidulei Terumah, gate 21, asks: if the document witnesses themselves are not believed, that must mean that their signed document is treated as though they testified there was no condition, and they cannot retract and testify differently. If so, other witnesses who say there was a condition should likewise produce a two-against-two conflict. Tumim resolves Rashba by saying that once the matter is two against two, the land remains with its original owner, as in the law of a mortgage in chapter HaMekabel, where one party says it was for two years and the other says three. He cites Rashba, responsum 972. Yet that very responsum appears to prove the opposite. Rashba there discusses Reuven, who produced a deed showing that Shimon sold him a vineyard, while Shimon claims he sold it only on condition that if he repaid three hundred dinarim borrowed from Reuven by a certain date the deed would be returned and the sale canceled. Shimon brought witnesses to the condition. Arbitrators ruled that even if the document witnesses expressly testified there was no condition, the situation would be two against two and the property should remain with its original owner. Rashba rejected their comparison. Here, all agree that the purchaser lawfully entered the vineyard because the seller transferred it to him through money, deed, and possession. According to the seller, there was merely a condition that if he repaid the money by the specified time the sale would be void. Since the purchaser lawfully took possession, while the seller claims that he must withdraw because of a condition and brings witnesses, and the purchaser claims the sale remains effective and brings witnesses, the property remains with the party presently in possession. Land is not technically seized through mere physical possession in all contexts, but certainly their analogy was incorrect insofar as they ruled that the document itself must be returned. Since the signatures are independently authenticated, we neither tear the document nor return it merely because of the conflicting testimony. Rashba thus explicitly holds that where the uncertainty concerns a condition, we do not automatically restore the land to the first owner but may leave it with the purchaser. Perhaps one can distinguish a purchaser who entered under a complete sale from one who entered under a mortgage, where the land is always fundamentally presumed to belong to its original owner because the mortgagee holds only a security interest that will ultimately return to the owner. Gidulei Terumah writes that although the document witnesses themselves are not believed to say there was a condition, other witnesses are fully believed and the case is not two against two. That position is contradicted by the Rashba just cited, who treats even outside witnesses as creating two against two where the document's signatures are independently authenticated. Tumim further asks from gittin, where the condition is not written in the get, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 147, but is stated orally. How can such a condition ever be proved when most gittin have signatures authenticated from elsewhere? The get witnesses themselves are not believed, and other witnesses would create two against two, leaving the woman in doubt. It appears that Beit Yosef in siman 29 provides the answer. If a witness completed his testimony but, before mentioning the condition, the litigants interrupted him, and he afterward says that he intended to complete his words but was prevented and is now completing them, he is believed. A get in which a condition is deliberately not written is analogous to such an interruption. The oral statement of the condition is a permitted completion of the testimony rather than a contradiction of the original testimony.
The witnesses died. Rosh holds that even if the witnesses are alive but later became disqualified through robbery, the document is not thereby invalidated. In the second chapter of Ketubot he writes that we collect with the document because it is effectively two against two. The witnesses signed on the document stand as testimony to its validity. Although others now testify that these signatories were disqualified, it is as though two other witnesses testify they were qualified, because there is a presumption that a lender does not knowingly sign disqualified witnesses on his document. This presumption counts like witnesses. The case cannot mean merely that they are now disqualified, because then there would be no basis even to ask about two against two. Rosh adds that even if the later witnesses say the signatories are disqualified now as well, they are not believed to invalidate the document. Strictly speaking, even when the signatures are independently authenticated, one might have thought later witnesses should be believed because they do not deny the signatures but assert another disqualification. The reason they are not believed is that we ourselves testify, through the presumption, that an ordinary document was written and signed without disqualification. Thus later testimony that the witnesses are now disqualified does not invalidate the document once the signatures are independently authenticated. Even according to Rosh, however, the result is at least two against two. Therefore, regarding land, we leave it with its original owner. This explains the Gemara in chapter Merubah concerning whether an eid zomem becomes disqualified retroactively or only prospectively. Rava says prospectively because of loss to purchasers. The document still cannot extract land when the matter is two against two, so the land remains with its original owner. This resolves the Shach's difficulty. Rosh's approach also resolves Tosafot's difficulty in Ketubot 20a, s.v. 'Rav Nachman said.' Tosafot asks: according to Rav Nachman, why do we not collect through the document? Rav Nachman elsewhere holds that where two sets of witnesses contradict each other, each set may later testify independently despite the other's testimony disqualifying them. Here too, since the later witnesses do not say the loan never occurred but only that the signatories are disqualified, why not collect through the document? Tosafot answers that in the case of two conflicting sets we preserve each set's prior presumption of qualification. But where the later witnesses say the signatories were minors or otherwise disqualified, no such presumption helps. According to Rosh the matter is clearer: where they testify that the signatories are currently disqualified, the later witnesses are believed as to the personal status of those signatories. There is thus no presumption of personal qualification. The conflict remains only over the document's validity, and regarding the document there is no personal chazakah. It is like two witnesses saying, 'he borrowed,' and two saying, 'he did not borrow,' so we do not extract money from doubt. In the case of two witness groups contradicting each other, however, each group retains its presumption of qualification. Here the signatories themselves are certainly disqualified now, and the only uncertainty concerns the document.

He delivered a protest. See the Shach, who cites several authorities that even where the witnesses say there was a protest they are not believed, because we rule like Rav Nachman that witnesses who say, 'our words were subject to a protest,' are not believed. Tumim objects that at the beginning of this siman the Shach writes that where a borrower admits the document and says it was a trust document, he is believed, as is also stated below in siman 82, without hesitation. Yet Tosafot in Ketubot 19b, s.v. 'Rav Nachman said,' explicitly writes that according to the authorities who rule like Rav Nachman, even where the witnesses have a migo to say the document is forged they are not believed to say it was a trust document. This is no difficulty. Tosafot explains Rav Nachman's rule concerning a protest as follows: although the witnesses have a migo to say the document is forged, their oral statement cannot come and impair the document after they admit it was written and delivered according to law. This differs from cases where they say they were coerced or minors. There, they do not admit that there was a valid document at all, but overturn it fundamentally. The same reasoning would cause Rav Nachman to reject testimony that it was a trust document only if he considers a trust document to have been properly written and delivered as a document, with the problem arising merely from a collateral agreement. According to Rav Nachman, the reason for both trust document and protest is therefore the same: once they admit a valid document was written and delivered, their later oral words cannot impair it. We rule like Rav Nachman regarding a protest because the general discussion in chapter Chazakat HaBatim follows his position, as the poskim explain. But we do not follow his premise regarding a trust document. We rule that writing a trust document is itself wrongdoing and that such a document was never permitted to be written. Consequently, it is not a valid document at all. Once it was forbidden to write, the principle that an oral statement cannot impair a valid document does not apply, because there is no valid document. As Rama writes in siman 42, seif 1, and here in seif 10, a document written improperly is invalid. Therefore, concerning a trust document we follow the reason of Mar bar Rav Ashi that the witnesses are not believed only because it was forbidden to write it and they would thereby render themselves wicked. The litigant himself, however, is believed to say it was a trust document. Rav Nachman treated a trust document like a protest because in his view both were validly written documents that oral testimony could not later impair. We do not follow him on that point because multiple passages establish that a trust document may not be written and is void. It is therefore like testimony that the signatories were minors or otherwise disqualified, where the witnesses may be believed through the appropriate migo. Regarding a protest, by contrast, we do follow Rav Nachman, because the original document remains a full document and an oral statement cannot later impair it. Examine this carefully.
