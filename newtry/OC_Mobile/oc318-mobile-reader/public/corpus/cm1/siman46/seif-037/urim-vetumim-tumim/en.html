We do not tear it up. Shach, seif katan 101, says the same applies where two witnesses say the debt was paid and two contradict them. We do not collect on the document, but we also do not tear it up. He cites Rashba, responsum 972. That responsum actually gives little support and perhaps points somewhat the other way. Rashba discusses a purchaser of land where two sets of witnesses disagree whether the sale was conditional. He rules that we place the two against the two and leave the sale in its existing status. Perhaps here too we should say: set the two payment witnesses against the two who deny payment and leave the loan document in its prior force. There is, however, an obvious distinction. In Rashba's case the purchaser is physically in possession of the field. Here, even though the creditor possesses the document, the debtor possesses the money. For extraction of money, the debtor's possession is decisive. Indeed, Tosafot in Ketubot 20a explicitly establish a two-against-two case involving a receipt: one pair says payment occurred and the other pair contradicts them. We leave the money with the debtor even though the creditor holds a debt document. Thus the creditor does not collect. As to not tearing the document, no special proof is needed. Since the testimony is evenly divided, future clarification may still occur, so the paper remains rather than being destroyed.
But if the witnesses are alive, etc. The same applies where witnesses say that the signatories were relatives when they signed. Sema, citing Ran, appears to connect the rule with the fact that the signatories have now become unrelated. That cannot be the central reason. The entire dispute concerns their status at the time they signed. If they were relatives then, their testimony was disqualified then regardless of their present status. The real distinction is between a defect in the witnesses themselves and a collateral status such as kinship. If other witnesses say the signatories were related at the time, they are not attacking their moral reliability or declaring their testimony false. Kinship is a legal disqualification imposed even on completely truthful people. Their testimony is therefore not treated like direct testimony that the original witnesses were corrupt. The reference to their now being unrelated merely explains why the matter is no longer directly observable. The parties agree they are now unrelated and dispute whether the kinship already existed when the document was signed. By contrast, if witnesses say the signatories were thieves at the time and later repented, they are attacking a disqualification in the very persons of the witnesses at the moment of testimony. The fact that they later became fit does not change that. This distinction answers Kenesset HaGedolah's question why the Gemara did not simply use the case of witnesses who were thieves and later repented. That case is legally different because it concerns a defect in the witnesses themselves. Tosafot's language can sound broader, as though any disqualification that has since disappeared is treated alike, and Derishah seems to understand even thieves who repented like relatives who became unrelated. Nevertheless, the distinction I stated appears correct. There is proof from Tosafot's own question based on migo. Tosafot ask why the later witnesses are not believed through a migo that they could have said the document witnesses are still disqualified. They answer that a migo requiring two people to coordinate is not accepted. But where the alleged defect was theft followed by repentance, the later witnesses could simply omit the repentance and say only, "They were thieves." That is a migo of silence, which is much stronger because they need not coordinate any new story. If the signatories are not restored until beit din receives evidence of their repentance, withholding that final fact would leave them disqualified until now. Why then should the migo fail? The answer is that Tosafot's actual case is kinship that later ended. The current lack of kinship is an objective fact, not something the witnesses can conceal through silence. Their only alternative would be to invent some separate disqualification such as theft, and that requires coordinated testimony by two people. Thus Tosafot's own reasoning supports the distinction. Accordingly, where the witnesses say, "They were related at the time and later became unrelated," their testimony is not treated like an attack on the substantive integrity of the original testimony. Where they say, "They were thieves at the time and later repented," it does directly concern the witnesses' testimonial fitness at that moment and is governed differently.
Compelled by threat to life, etc. In such a case the witnesses do not make themselves wicked, because they were permitted to act to save their lives. Ordinarily nothing stands before preservation of life. I am uncertain, however, where they say the coercion occurred during a time of religious persecution, publicly, or with the specific intent to force transgression, in a situation where halachah requires one to surrender his life rather than violate the prohibition. Are the witnesses believed then? Does the principle that a person is related to himself and cannot make himself wicked still apply? Above, siman 34, I cited Rama in Even HaEzer 42 in the name of Rivash, who says forced apostates can remain valid witnesses. Although they violated the prohibition against desecrating Hashem's name, they acted under coercion and "the Torah exempts one under compulsion." On the other hand, Rashba, responsum 1236, discussing a man who says a woman committed adultery with him, does not allow us to explain the act as involuntary where the physical act itself reflects volition. Nor does he say gentiles forced him, because for prohibited sexual relations one must be killed rather than transgress. This creates difficulty. The principle that one cannot make himself wicked means his statement is not accepted to disqualify himself from testimony. If, according to Rivash, one who transgressed under coercion in such circumstances would not be disqualified, why should Rashba reject the statement on the ground that he makes himself wicked? Ketubot 19a appears to resolve the question. R. Meir initially says witnesses are not believed to say they signed falsely under threat to life, because witnesses told "sign falsely or be killed" should allow themselves to be killed rather than sign falsely, and Rashi explains that by saying otherwise they make themselves wicked. The Gemara rejects this because generally nothing stands before preservation of life. But suppose the case were one of the exceptional prohibitions for which one must surrender his life. Even then, if Rivash were correct that violation under coercion creates no testimonial disqualification at all, there would still be no self-disqualification and the witnesses should be believed. The sugya therefore supports Rashba: in a case where one was obligated to die rather than transgress, doing the prohibited act under coercion can still create at least a rabbinic disqualification. Perhaps such a person is disqualified only rabbinically and requires public proclamation. That may reconcile Rivash. His forced apostates may not have been formally proclaimed as disqualified, so their testimony could still be accepted in the particular setting. Rashba, however, can hold that a person is still not believed to bring himself into a status that would require beit din to proclaim him disqualified. A person cannot establish his own disqualification by saying he engaged in gambling or another rabbinically disqualifying act. Accordingly, where witnesses say they were forced under threat to life in a situation where halachah actually required martyrdom, they should not be believed to invalidate the document through that statement, because they thereby render themselves rabbinically disqualified.
Or disqualified as witnesses because of kinship. In the Mishnah, "disqualified witnesses" is explained by Rashi as relatives or gamblers. The commentators ask: if they say they were gamblers, they make themselves wicked and a person cannot disqualify himself. Raah cites R. Yosef ibn Migash as answering that because they say they have now repented, the principle of self-disqualification does not apply. Raah objects. Witnesses who say they signed under monetary coercion also describe a past defect from which they have now withdrawn, yet they are still not believed. Raah therefore explains that gambling is only a rabbinic disqualification based on failure to contribute to civil society, and in this respect the witnesses can be believed similarly to witnesses saying they were minors. This explanation is not universally accepted and has little explicit support among later poskim. In my view Rashi can be explained through Tosafot's doctrine of splitting a statement. Tosafot ask why, where witnesses say they were coerced for money, we do not split their statement and interpret "coerced" as coercion under threat to life. They answer that life-threatening coercion is uncommon. Rashi can use the same principle here. When the witnesses say, "We were disqualified because we gambled," we accept only the legally useful portion: they were disqualified. We do not accept the self-incriminating explanation that the disqualification came through wicked conduct. Instead, we can understand the accepted portion as a disqualification through kinship or another non-sinful ground. Thus we split the statement. They are not believed to brand themselves wicked, but the assertion that they lacked testimonial qualification can still be accepted through a non-self-incriminating interpretation. This resolves Rashi without relying on the disputed view that rabbinic gambling disqualification can be self-established. Still, the broader law of splitting testimony is itself disputed among major authorities, so practical application requires caution.
There was no condition. Shach cites Ramban and Ritva that the second witness means only, "I did not hear that there was a condition." If he says explicitly, "There was no condition," then the two witnesses contradict each other. One of them is necessarily false, so there is only one reliable witness for authentication. This can be answered according to our rule that separate witnesses can testify independently at different times. Suppose the second witness first says, "This is my handwriting," and only after more than the time of immediate retraction says, "There was no condition." At the moment he authenticated his signature, he had not yet contradicted the first witness. The authentication testimony was then valid. His later contradictory statement does not retroactively make the earlier authentication invalid. At most, from the later moment onward one of the two becomes disqualified through the contradiction. The original authentication therefore remains in the category of two witnesses who testified separately and only afterward entered a contradiction. There is some indication for this reading from Rosh. Where the witnesses no longer remember the substantive transaction, Rosh requires each to say, "This is my handwriting and this is my fellow's handwriting." The witness who says, "There was a condition," evidently does not fully remember the underlying transaction. The other witness must therefore also participate in authenticating both signatures. If the second immediately denied the condition, they would directly contradict one another at the very moment of authentication. The case is more coherent if the denial came only afterward. Then the authentication had already been completed before the contradiction arose. Accordingly, the ruling need not be limited to the formulation, "I did not hear of a condition."
And there is here only one witness. Tur cites Ramah that if one witness says there was no condition and the other says there was a condition, the defendant is obligated to take a Torah oath regarding movable property. We do not say that one witness cancels the other, because the Torah gave one witness the power to impose an oath, and wherever the Torah gave one witness credibility he is treated like two for that purpose. Rabbenu Mattityahu, however, obligates only a rabbinic oath of hesset, and Tur appears to favor that view. The commentators ask against Ramah that a supporting witness exempts from an oath, and it appears below, siman 94, that even Ramah accepts that rule. Shach therefore explains that the witness who says there was a condition does not necessarily testify that the condition was not fulfilled. His testimony is therefore uncertain as to whether the debt is presently due, and an uncertain witness cannot remove the definite Torah oath created by the other witness. According to Shach, if the second witness states clearly that the condition was never fulfilled, Ramah would concede there is no Torah oath. This explanation does not fit Ramah's language. Maharit, Even HaEzer part 1, siman 27, raises the stronger question from explicit Gemara passages in Yevamot and Sotah. When two witnesses arrive together and contradict one another, we say one stands against the other. Why should the witness who denies the condition be stronger? The answer is that the witness who says, "There was no condition," is not really giving a later independent statement about the condition. He says, "This is my signature," and the document itself is written without any condition. Had he remained silent about conditions and merely authenticated his signature, that authentication would itself amount to testimony that the instrument was executed as written, namely without a condition. A signed document is treated as testimony investigated in beit din from the time of signing. Thus the first witness's testimony against the existence of a condition is deemed to have entered beit din from the original date of the document. The other witness, who now says, "There was a condition," gives his contrary statement only now. Accordingly, the first witness is like a witness who entered beit din first and whose testimony acquired the status of two for the purpose of imposing an oath. The later witness cannot uproot that already-established oath obligation. This is Ramah's reasoning, and it is compelling. Rabbenu Mattityahu disagrees because he follows Raah's view concerning the rule that the first witness is treated like two. Raah limits that doctrine to matters of ervah, such as testimony that a woman's husband died or testimony concerning a sotah, where without the Torah's special one-witness credibility nothing less than two witnesses would suffice. In ordinary prohibitory matters, where one witness is generally believed, a later witness can still contradict the first even though he arrived later. Likewise, according to Rabbenu Mattityahu, a single witness who imposes a monetary oath is not treated like two in this procedural sense. The later witness can therefore neutralize him, leaving only a rabbinic hesset oath. Shach in Yoreh Deah 127 cites these two positions. In my view, they correspond precisely to Ramah and Rabbenu Mattityahu. Since Mechaber in Yoreh Deah, especially siman 39, follows Maharik against Raah and gives priority to the witness who came first in several such cases, the practical ruling here should follow Ramah. The wording here also points that way. Mechaber writes, "there remains only one witness," which treats the first witness as still effective to impose an oath. Had Mechaber followed Rabbenu Mattityahu, he should have said there is no effective witness testimony at all for a Torah oath. There is another proof. According to Mechaber's view that witnesses are not believed to say there was a condition once their handwriting is independently authenticated, their credibility to say "there was a condition" ordinarily depends on a migo that they could have denied the signature. If Rabbenu Mattityahu were correct that the condition statement completely removes the Torah oath, what migo would exist? Had the witness said, "This is not my handwriting," while the other witness authenticated his own signature, the surviving one-witness document would impose a Torah oath according to the Yerushalmi and the ordinary law of one authenticated signature. Yet by saying, "There was a condition," he would eliminate even that oath. That is not a valid migo because his actual claim gives him a stronger result than the alternative. It is difficult to answer that he could say both, "This is not my handwriting," and, "I know this debt was paid," because not every document admits such a payment defense, for example a debt still within its term. Accordingly, the view that the condition testimony requires a migo strongly supports Ramah's conclusion that the surviving one witness still imposes a Torah oath. Rabbenu Mattityahu can be sustained only if he holds, as some understand Tosafot in Ketubot 20a, that one written witness does not impose a Torah oath unless the other signature simply cannot be found for authentication, whereas if the second witness appears and says his signature is forged the document remains only a one-witness writing with lesser force. Or, more simply, Rabbenu Mattityahu may hold that saying there was a condition does not require a migo even when the signatures are independently authenticated. That would accord with the view Tur attributes to Rosh. Thus the underlying dispute concerning the need for migo and the force of a prior single witness fit together consistently. For practical law, the stronger reading of Mechaber and the surrounding sugyot supports Ramah.
We witnesses testify, etc. Shach discusses at length Baal HaTerumot's view that when the witnesses' handwriting is not independently authenticated and they therefore possess a migo, they can still say there was a condition even though the written instrument contains language of full and unconditional obligation. The explanation is that when signatures are not independently authenticated, the witnesses can say they did not scrutinize every clause of the text when they signed. This is similar to the rule concerning an antedated document: they may say, in a proper migo setting, "We signed regarding the transaction but not regarding this particular detail." Thus, even though the document appears unconditional, they can say the condition existed and they did not focus on the absence of that clause in the written text. Baal HaTerumot's language supports this. He speaks of a document written "without reservation" but does not say that the witnesses expressly wrote, "We witnesses testify that there was no condition." That difference is critical. If the document says explicitly, "We witnesses testify" to the absence of any condition, the witnesses have directly attested to that very point. They can no longer say they were not paying attention to it. This resembles a document containing an express credibility clause, where the witnesses cannot later say they did not acquire the relevant undertaking. Accordingly, Rama's added phrase, "and we witnesses testify," is deliberate. With that explicit formulation, even a migo does not allow them to say there was a condition. Ran asks why saying there was a condition differs from saying the document was an amana document. An amana document is one written and entrusted before the actual loan or obligation is completed. If the obligation has not yet arisen, the paper presently has no substance. A condition can likewise suspend the obligation until the condition is fulfilled. Why then are witnesses allowed to sign a conditional document but not an amana document? Ran answers that the distinction depends on whose act controls the condition. Where the condition is in the creditor's power, if the creditor wishes to fulfill it the debtor is then obligated to pay. The document therefore represents a genuine potential obligation and may be written. An amana document is different. Even if the creditor wishes to lend, the borrower can still refuse to take the money. The document may never correspond to any obligation at all. This implies that if the condition depends on the debtor, such as, "If the debtor travels to a particular place, he will become obligated," the case resembles amana. The debtor can simply choose never to trigger the obligation. In such a case the witnesses should not be believed to say there was such a condition, because the document was not yet a proper operative instrument. By contrast, if the condition is that the debt exists immediately but will be forgiven if the debtor later performs some act, the document is perfectly valid at the outset. The condition merely creates a later waiver. Baal HaTerumot explicitly gives a case of this kind and treats the witnesses as credible. Mahardbaz, part 1, siman 198, cites authorities who say that if the witnesses testify that the condition was one that had to be fulfilled within a fixed time, and they now say the time passed without fulfillment so the document is entirely void, they are no longer believed because they are completely uprooting the instrument. His formulation appears directed especially to the view that witnesses may say there was a condition even when their signatures are independently authenticated. Kenesset HaGedolah cites a manuscript responsum broadly in that direction. Some support this from Tosafot, who explain that a condition does not uproot the document because if the condition is fulfilled the instrument remains valid. They infer that if fulfillment is no longer possible and the document is therefore wholly destroyed, the witnesses should not be believed. In my view this proof is not decisive. Tosafot are explaining the question according to Rav Nachman, who held that witnesses are not believed even concerning moda'ah because it uproots the document. They therefore need to distinguish an ordinary condition by saying the document can still become operative if the condition is fulfilled. But practical halachah follows Mar bar Rav Ashi, who allows witnesses to testify to a moda'ah even though it can uproot the document entirely, because such a declaration was legally proper to write. Once we accept that principle, the fact that a condition now destroys the instrument completely should not by itself remove the witnesses' credibility. If it was a lawful condition to attach at the time, it resembles moda'ah. This is why the major codifiers do not generally distinguish between a condition still capable of fulfillment and one whose time has already passed. Baal HaTerumot itself seems to support this. He speaks of a debtor claiming that he fulfilled the condition and thereby nullified the document, yet still allows the witnesses to testify to the condition under the proper rules. It is strained to distinguish merely because the document had some temporary force before fulfillment. Mahardbaz may have been troubled by another argument. According to those who allow witnesses to say there was a condition even with independently authenticated signatures, why are witnesses who say, "We signed under threat to life," not believed through a migo that they could have said, "There was a condition that was never fulfilled," thereby voiding the document? One cannot answer merely that migo by two witnesses is ineffective, because a single witness can sometimes say there was a condition, and each witness would not necessarily need the other's coordinated statement. This may have led Mahardbaz to restrict condition testimony to a condition still capable of fulfillment. But the question is not compelling. As explained elsewhere in the rules of migo, a person prefers a claim with which the other relevant participant will agree rather than a claim the other may directly contradict. If one witness says, "There was a condition," the other witness may deny it. Creating a shared condition narrative is a classic migo involving two people and is not easy to coordinate. Moreover, if the debtor himself denies any condition, the witness cannot plausibly use that as his alternative claim. If the debtor agrees there was a condition, then the migo depends on both the debtor and witness and is again a multi-person migo. Tosafot similarly reject a migo of saying "paid" in a setting where the document itself and surrounding testimony point otherwise. Therefore Mahardbaz's proposed proof does not compel his limitation. Accordingly, even where the condition, if accepted, would now uproot the document entirely, the witnesses can still be believed under the rules governing condition testimony, provided the condition was one that could lawfully accompany the document when written. The fact that its fulfillment is no longer possible does not by itself convert it into an impermissible amana document.
An amana document, etc. Nimukei Yosef, citing Ritva, writes that people commonly make a kinyan and then trust the other party concerning whether the money will actually be advanced, and that the prohibition concerns creating an amana document. I do not fully understand this. If a kinyan creates the obligation whether or not the money is later advanced, as explained above in siman 39, then what aspect of amana remains? The obligor is already bound by the kinyan even if no later loan occurs. Nimukei Yosef himself writes in Bava Metzia that a kinyan obligates whether the creditor later lends or not. Perhaps Ritva follows the view cited above in siman 39 that the kinyan can be structured to take effect only if the future loan actually occurs. But according to the majority of authorities, and according to the Gemara's reason for returning a found shtar hakna'ah, we do not fear that it was merely written in contemplation of a loan that never occurred because the debtor already obligated himself through the kinyan regardless. On that mainstream view, I do not understand how the transaction is classified as amana. If the parties expressly conditioned that the obligation should arise only if the loan later occurs, then the document becomes antedated when it is written before the loan, as explained above. Accordingly, Ritva's formulation requires study.
They are not believed. According to Rambam's approach above in this siman, witnesses who testify regarding handwriting must remember the substance of the testimony itself. Here, even putting aside their statements that the document was an amana document or that the witnesses were disqualified, they clearly do not remember an actual loan. On the contrary, they say the written transaction is false and that no loan occurred. I explained above that ordinarily we presume the signatories remember. Therefore, once their disqualifying explanation is rejected because they are not believed to say it, their statement is treated as though they said nothing. We then revert to the ordinary presumption that they remember the transaction, and the document remains effective. According to Rosh's view above, where witnesses who do not remember can still participate in authentication if another person joins them, the case can be understood as one where such another witness joins them, or where each one identifies his own handwriting and that of his fellow. A further question arises where both witnesses say, "We were coerced for money." Although a person cannot make himself wicked, perhaps each is still believed regarding the other witness. Each one could have said, "That is not my fellow's handwriting." Through that migo, perhaps he should be believed to say the other signer was coerced for money. Then each witness would effectively testify concerning the other, as though two outsiders testified that the signatories acted under monetary coercion. At first glance, that would invalidate the document. Still, there is reason to hesitate. Every witness is presumed fit. How can one witness overturn the other's presumption of fitness? If the argument depends on migo, this is a migo against a strong presumption and should not be accepted. The Gemara's statement that "a person is related to himself" is also relevant. Why does the Gemara not simply say the witness is not believed because his claim is a migo against his own presumption of fitness? The answer is that if a person were believed concerning himself, his own admission could disqualify him even against one hundred witnesses. His self-admission would then undermine the very authentication testimony he is giving. Therefore the Gemara needs the rule that a person is related to himself and cannot make himself wicked. Raavad at the beginning of Makkot can be understood similarly. As explained elsewhere, we accept the factual portion of a person's self-incriminating statement but do not allow it to disqualify him from testimony. For example, where he says, "So-and-so had relations with me willingly," we may accept the underlying act against the other person while refusing to disqualify the speaker himself. Applying that here, even if we accept that the witnesses were under some coercion, the rule preventing self-disqualification preserves their competency as witnesses. But then how can the document remain valid if the admitted facts show they signed falsely? The answer is that the factual assertion itself is not believed merely because of migo where it stands against the strong presumption of proper witness conduct. Only the self-disqualification problem would otherwise have supplied the stronger route. Once the Torah blocks self-disqualification, the migo does not independently carry the claim. This also helps explain Tosafot's question why we do not split the statement. They suggest interpreting "we were coerced for money" as meaning that the speaker himself was coerced for money while his fellow was coerced under threat to life, thereby avoiding self-disqualification as to the fellow. That is easier to say than some other applications of splitting a statement because the witness's claim about his fellow is a separate factual assertion. Tosafot answer that document authentication is rabbinic and Chazal did not require such a strained division. According to Rami bar Chama, who applies a stronger distinction even when signatures are independently authenticated, the situation differs because there is no migo at all concerning the fellow's signature once the handwriting is established from elsewhere. Thus the whole analysis depends on the exact structure of authentication and migo. The practical conclusion is that the witnesses are not believed to invalidate the document through a claim of monetary coercion where that claim would undermine their own fitness and where the signatures stand with the ordinary presumptions in their favor.
They are not believed, etc. Shach, citing Beit Yosef, writes that although the original document witnesses themselves may be unable to say there was a condition once their handwriting is independently authenticated, other witnesses can still testify that a condition existed. Beit Yosef says this explicitly at the end of siman 29 concerning a responsum of Rashba. In that case one person had mortgaged a vineyard, and other witnesses said the mortgage was subject to a condition. Rashba ruled that they were believed even though the signatures on the mortgage document were independently authenticated, because the later witnesses were not contradicting the document witnesses but merely clarifying the matter. Beit Yosef explains that even though Rashba holds the original signatories themselves cannot say "there was a condition" once their signatures are authenticated from elsewhere, unrelated witnesses may do so. This language requires explanation. Either the original written testimony is understood as leaving open the possibility of an unstated condition, in which case why cannot the signatories themselves clarify what they meant? Or the ordinary meaning of the document is that there was no condition, in which case outside witnesses saying there was a condition are directly contradicting the document and the result should be two against two. Gidulei Terumah raises this difficulty and leaves it unresolved. One answer is that if the second group says the condition was made in the presence of the original document witnesses, then there is indeed a contradiction. The signatories wrote an unconditional document in circumstances where, had they known of the condition, they should have recorded it. Their written testimony therefore implies there was no condition. The later witnesses oppose that and create two against two. But if the later witnesses merely say a condition existed without saying it was made before the original signatories, we reconcile all testimony if possible. We say the condition may have been made afterward, outside the presence of the document witnesses. Then the original witnesses properly wrote an unconditional document based on what they knew, and the second group later clarifies an additional fact. There is no contradiction. This may be Rashba's meaning that the later witnesses "clarify" rather than contradict. There is, however, another and perhaps stronger explanation. Rashba's case involved a mortgage where the possessor had already entered the vineyard under the mortgage arrangement. That physical possession supports the authenticity of the original document and its witnesses. If later witnesses directly contradicted the entire substance of the mortgage, we would have strong grounds to preserve the original transaction because the possessor's conduct corroborates it. Rashba in responsum 972 similarly treats a land sale differently where the purchaser already possesses the property. Thus Rashba says: if the later witnesses were contradicting the main transaction itself, the original document would remain especially strong. But they are not denying the mortgage. They say only that a condition qualified it. That is a narrower dispute over the legal terms. Even if we regard the original witnesses as implicitly saying there was no condition, we now have two against two on that detail, while the land remains in the possession of the original owner or his heirs according to the ordinary rules governing a mortgage dispute. Thus the later witnesses can still matter without overthrowing the main document. Beit Yosef may have intended this when he says the difference is that "other witnesses testify so." On either reading, the practical rule is sound: other witnesses can testify that a condition existed even where the signatories themselves could no longer make that claim because their signatures are independently authenticated. If their testimony directly contradicts the original witnesses, the case becomes two against two and is resolved through the ordinary rules of possession and burden of proof. If it can be reconciled by saying the condition was made outside the original witnesses' presence, we prefer that reconciliation. This raises an important question concerning gittin. Even HaEzer 147 rules that one does not generally write the condition into the get itself; the condition can be stated orally. Most gittin have independently recognizable witness signatures. If the original signatories are never believed to say there was a condition once their signatures are independently established, and outside witnesses create only two against two, how can oral conditions accompanying gittin work in practice? We do not find that every conditional get requires a separate pair of witnesses beyond the signatories. The answer must be one of two things. First, the condition in a get is generally imposed at delivery, not in the writing. The signatories testify only that the get was properly written and signed. They do not testify that no later delivery condition existed. They can therefore testify orally about the condition of delivery without contradicting their signatures. Responsa of Mabit, part 1, simanim 221 and 231, supports this distinction. Second, even where the condition existed earlier, if the parties expressly agreed that the condition would remain oral and would not be written into the instrument, then the omission does not contradict the witnesses' testimony. A witness is unable to add a condition only where the ordinary legal form would have required him to write it. If the very agreement was that the condition remain oral, failure to write it proves nothing. This distinction resolves the well-known contradiction between the rulings here and those in siman 82. Here, and similarly in siman 29, the witnesses seek to say there was a condition that should have been included in the written instrument. Their silence in the document contradicts that claim, so once the signatures are independently authenticated they cannot retract. In siman 82, the condition concerns a separate arrangement, such as a condition governing delivery or a later waiver. The document itself was properly written exactly as intended, and the oral condition does not contradict its text. This is far better than distinguishing generally between oral and written testimony, because the Talmudic law of conditions itself is discussed specifically in the context of documents. The same principle applies to oral testimony before beit din. If witnesses gave full oral testimony without mentioning a condition that was part of the event they were describing, they cannot later add it after their testimony was completed. Their prior silence is equivalent to testimony that no such qualification existed. Thus the entire body of law is consistent.
Except to say, "A moda'ah was delivered before us." Shach cites authorities who hold that even regarding a moda'ah, if the witnesses' handwriting is independently authenticated they are no longer believed. That is indeed an early view. In my opinion, however, Mechaber correctly rules more leniently. Bava Batra 48b challenges Rabbah bar bar Chanah, who signed both a moda'ah and the later deed, from Rav Nachman's statement that witnesses who say, "It was a moda'ah document," are not believed. Why does the Gemara rely specifically on Rav Nachman? Rabbah bar bar Chanah's signature was surely well known to the sages. If independently authenticated handwriting itself destroyed the witnesses' credibility regarding moda'ah according to all opinions, the Gemara could have challenged him directly without invoking Rav Nachman. This strongly suggests that according to Mar bar Rav Ashi, witnesses can be believed regarding moda'ah even where their signatures are independently authenticated. I am surprised that Shach, in this very seif, cites authorities who treat Rav Nachman's rule as practical, while elsewhere he accepts that a debtor who admits the document but says it was an amana document can be believed through the appropriate migo. Tosafot explicitly say that according to Rav Nachman, even such a migo does not necessarily help regarding amana. The broader pattern of the poskim therefore indicates that Rav Nachman's strict position was not accepted as the final halachah in all these cases. One might still ask: if the Gemara initially challenges from Rav Nachman, perhaps the halachah indeed follows him, otherwise Rabbah bar bar Chanah and Rav Huna could simply follow Mar bar Rav Ashi. The answer lies in Ran's explanation of Mar bar Rav Ashi. Mar bar Rav Ashi says witnesses are believed regarding moda'ah because it was "given to be written." Ran explains that the witnesses could themselves have written a formal moda'ah document on that very day. Since such writing was legally proper, their later oral clarification receives credibility. But this rationale becomes available only after the Gemara has established that a written moda'ah is itself valid. At the initial stage of the sugya, the Gemara is entertaining the possibility that even a written moda'ah would not be effective. If so, the statement "it was given to be written" is not yet available. Therefore the Gemara can properly challenge Rabbah bar bar Chanah from Rav Nachman at that stage. Once it answers that the restriction concerns an oral statement but a written moda'ah is valid, Mar bar Rav Ashi's rationale becomes operative. This is why the Gemara then cites his rule as practical. Thus the sequence is exact. The final halachah can follow Mar bar Rav Ashi even though Rav Nachman's statement was used in the initial question.
