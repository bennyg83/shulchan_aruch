And they died. Whether this wording is precise, so that the rule applies only if they died, or whether it also applies when they are alive but overseas, is explained in the gloss, Sema.
They are believed. If they say this within the time required for an immediate retraction, they have a migo that they could simply have refrained from validating. Although this is a migo involving two people, the form "had they wished they could have remained silent" is accepted, Shach. See what I wrote above in Tumim, seif katan 4, and at greater length below in siman 82 in the general rules of migo.
Unless the defendant claims, etc. Specifically if he himself raises the claim. If he does not, we do not raise it for him, and we tear up the document, because even if validation were later found it would be a case of two against two, Rashba and Sema. Shach writes in the name of Ra'ah that with property of orphans we do not tear it up, since perhaps when they grow older they will search for validation. This is correct.
We do not tear it up, etc. The same applies if two witnesses say that the document was paid and two say it was not paid. We neither collect nor tear it up. See Tumim.
For the witnesses had already died, etc. According to this opinion, once they died they can no longer be disqualified. We must therefore explain the rule above in siman 34, seif 12, where a witness signed on a document died and witnesses testified that he had taken interest, not as disqualifying the witness, but only as weakening the document so that we do not collect with it and do not tear it up, as here. Shach, seif katan 102, discusses at length that according to most poskim even after his death the witness may be disqualified, and he is correct.
They are still alive, etc. The same applies if witnesses say that the signing witnesses had been relatives but later became unrelated. The testimony does not directly disqualify the witnesses, only the document, and therefore it becomes two against two. We neither tear it up nor collect with it. See Tumim regarding witnesses who say that the signers had committed robbery and later repented, whether that too falls within this rule. It appears that it does not.
Some disagree and validate the document. Meaning that they neither tear it up nor collect with it. Shach disagrees with Rosh on this point and rules against him, and Shach is correct.
But if he seized movable property, etc. Seizure does not apply to land because land remains in the presumptive possession of its owner. If he seized movable property, however, even if he seized it in the presence of witnesses, or beit din or the opposing party delivered it into his possession, he acquires it. We do not call this an erroneous seizure. Likewise, if they dispute a receipt, with witnesses signed on the receipt and two others testifying that those signers were disqualified, the lender loses, Sema and Shach. See what I wrote in the general rules of seizure.
Within the time of immediate retraction, etc. Afterward, once testimony has been given, it cannot be retracted.
Because of danger to life. Then they are not wicked, because transgression is permitted to save life. But if it occurred in a time of persecution, or publicly where the gentile intended specifically to force violation of Torah law, where one must die rather than transgress, I concluded in Tumim that they are not believed. They are rabbinically disqualified and cannot render themselves wicked.
Because of kinship, etc. Although they acted improperly by signing a document when they should not have, that alone does not disqualify them. They may have thought they were being added only for honor and that valid witnesses would also sign, or that the fact of their kinship was publicly known so no one would extract money based on their signatures, Sema and Taz.
And they later became unrelated, etc. See Tumim regarding Rashi's view, if they say that they had been disqualified because of a transgression, whether we divide their statement and believe only that they had been disqualified, but attribute the disqualification to kinship rather than transgression. This requires study.
And we did not see that the condition was fulfilled. If the condition depends on an affirmative act, such as, "If you go to a certain place I will owe you one hundred," the lender must prove that he went. If the condition is framed as inaction, such as, "If you do not go I will owe you," the borrower must prove that he did go and thereby nullified the obligation, Shach. So too below in siman 241, seif 10.
One witness says there was no condition. Meaning, "I did not hear of any condition," but he does not definitely contradict the second witness by saying that there certainly was no condition. See Tumim, where I wrote that he may even directly contradict him, but only where the witness who says there was no condition did not say so within the time of immediate retraction after testifying that this was his handwriting. See there.
There is only one witness here. Sema holds that there is not even a Torah oath, only hesset, because one witness stands against the other and it is as though there are no witnesses at all. According to him, if the claimant himself has no definite claim and relies only on the witness, there is not even hesset, since that witness's words are neutralized by the other. Shach appears to agree. In Tumim I wrote that this does not appear correct. The plain meaning of the Mechaber is that one witness remains to impose an oath, and this appears correct as law, especially where there is no possible claim of payment and therefore no migo to exempt him from the oath. See there.
If it is written in the document, etc. Shach is surprised because Baal HaTerumos holds that where the witnesses' handwriting is not independently established, they are believed even in such a case. See Tumim, where I distinguished between a document that merely says in general, "without any reservation," and one that says explicitly, "We witnesses testify, etc." In the latter wording they are never believed to contradict it, because that would be a migo against witnesses.
If it is written in the document, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that where their handwriting is not independently established, which is Rama's case, the document must state explicitly, "We witnesses testify, etc." If it merely says, "without any reservation or condition," that does not suffice, because they may still say that they did not pay attention to that formula, as with an antedated document. I also concluded that if they say the condition was limited to a certain period and that period has already passed so that the condition can no longer be fulfilled, they are nevertheless believed. Likewise if they say the condition was that if the borrower did a certain act the document would be void, and he did it, they are believed. But if they say the condition was that only if the borrower did a certain act would the obligation take effect, and otherwise there would be no obligation, they are not believed, for that resembles an entrusted document, concerning which the law expressly says they are not believed.
Regarding the substance of the sale, etc. Even if their handwriting is independently established they may explain their own words in this manner, even by giving a strained interpretation, so long as the explanations are not contradictory, Shach.
Or that it was an entrusted document. Since witnesses are forbidden to write such a document, if they say they did so they render themselves wicked and disqualified for testimony, and therefore they are not believed. If a kinyan was made merely on trust that he would later lend him the money but no document was written, Sema writes in the name of Nimukei Yosef that this is permitted. See Tumim, where I wrote that this ruling is difficult to understand as practical law.
They are not believed. See Tumim for my explanation according to Rambam, who requires the witnesses to remember the loan when they testify to their handwriting. If they do not remember it, according to Rosh, who allows them to say, "This is my handwriting and this is my colleague's handwriting," then if both say, "We signed under coercion," each has a migo because he could have refrained from testifying to his colleague's handwriting. See there.
They must compensate the borrower for the loss, etc. See what I wrote above in siman 29. This applies specifically to an entrusted document prepared without the borrower's knowledge, or where they were coerced because of money. But if the witnesses themselves were disqualified and nevertheless say the loan was genuine, or where the entrusted document was made with the borrower's knowledge, they are exempt from payment, because the borrower consented to what was done and it is not considered actionable causation. This is obvious.
And afterward it became clarified to them, etc. That is new testimony unrelated to the document itself, and they may testify to it just as they may testify that the debt was later paid or waived. Shach concludes that even if their handwriting is independently established, they are believed to say this. Knesset HaGedolah says likewise, unlike Bach. This is also somewhat indicated by the Gemara. The Gemara asks regarding Rav Huna in the name of Rav, who said that one is not believed to say a document was entrusted: if the witnesses say so and their handwriting is independently established, it is obvious that they are not believed. But perhaps they could have said that they themselves did not initially know of the entrusted nature of the document, in which case one might have thought they should be believed, and Rav teaches that they are still not believed, as Bach understands. The fact that the Gemara does not say this shows that in such a case they truly are believed.
They are not believed, etc. Other witnesses, however, are believed. Shach writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Yeruham and Beit Yosef. See Tumim. I concluded that this is specifically where the other witnesses merely testify that there was a condition, because that need not contradict the signing witnesses, who may simply have been unaware of it. But if they testify that the signing witnesses themselves knew of the condition, there is direct contradiction, and it is two against two. The document is not torn up, etc. See there at length.
In any matter. This appears to include even a condition, and so the Mechaber writes above in siman 29 that witnesses cannot add a condition to prior oral testimony. Yet below in siman 82, seif 12, it appears that with a condition they are believed even where their handwriting is independently established. Sema distinguishes between oral testimony and written testimony and says that "in any matter" here is not literal, because with a condition they are indeed believed, as with a prior notice. This is strained, as Sema himself notes, and Shach likewise. See Tumim, where I distinguished between different kinds of conditions. I further wrote, and this is the primary resolution, that if the condition was made at the time the document was written and the witnesses had authority to include it, they are not believed later to add it, certainly not after oral testimony. But if the condition was made after the writing, or if they lacked authority to write it into the document, they are believed. This resolves all the sources. See there for a compelling proof.
To invalidate the document. The same applies to a mortgage document that did not mention dividing walls, where the witnesses later say those walls were excluded. Rosh rules in a responsum that had that been so it would have been written in the document, and therefore the witnesses are not believed to invalidate the document, Darchei Moshe and Sema.
Except to say, "A prior notice was delivered before us." The Gemara explains that such a statement is one that may be written. Ran explains that this is because if the sale is later nullified the buyer must be refunded his purchase money. See what I wrote below in the general rules of migo, because if so, what migo do the witnesses have to say, "This is not our handwriting," when that would also cause the buyer to lose the money he paid? Sema. According to this explanation, if the buyer has not yet paid and there is only the sale itself, they would not be believed. See Tumim, where I concluded that the main explanation is Ran's second one: because such a prior notice is itself permitted to be written as a document, as stated in the Gemara, the witnesses may also testify orally about it in every case. See there.
