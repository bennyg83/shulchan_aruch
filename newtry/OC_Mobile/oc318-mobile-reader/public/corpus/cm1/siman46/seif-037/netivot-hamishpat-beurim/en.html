And they tear the document. Although the witnesses are believed only through a migo that they could have remained silent and left the document unauthenticated, and an unauthenticated document is not ordinarily torn, here the result is different. Even if the creditor later authenticates it through other evidence, the case will stand as two witnesses against two witnesses and the document will have force only for limited possession questions. Therefore, while no independent authentication exists, the court may tear it. If the signers themselves say, 'This is our handwriting, but we were coerced,' perhaps the court should not tear it, because once their signatures are independently authenticated they can no longer retract. Some early authorities nevertheless treat their present statement as substantive testimony and permit tearing. The matter depends on the basis of their credibility.
And some disagree and validate the document. The Shach distinguishes between witnesses who are present and can be personally disqualified and witnesses who are absent or dead. Where later testimony says the signers were robbers, testimony about the witnesses themselves ordinarily must be received in their presence because they are the direct litigants concerning their own disqualification. If they are absent, one may be unable to disqualify their personal status and therefore the document can remain usable according to the validating opinion. Hazamah is different. Saying, 'You were with us elsewhere,' is not ordinary testimony about a separate transgression of the witnesses. Without the Torah's special rule it would merely contradict their account of the underlying loan, just as saying the lender or borrower was elsewhere would. The Torah made the latter witnesses believed regarding the transaction. Therefore, to use hazamah simply to defeat the loan recorded in the document, the primary litigant is the creditor, not the signers, and their personal presence is not required. But to punish or personally disqualify the witnesses on account of hazamah, they do become the relevant parties and must be present. This explains why hazamah can nullify a document's testimony even where the witnesses are absent or dead, while ordinary testimony that the signers were robbers may depend more directly on personal disqualification. In monetary law the Sages sometimes accepted testimony outside a party's presence, such as where witnesses are leaving the country. If such testimony is accepted to establish that the signers were disqualified, one cannot both accept it for the loan and refuse its necessary consequence for their future testimony. But the special rule of hazamah is itself a Torah novelty, and one does not extend its personal punitive consequences beyond the conditions the Torah provided.
There was a condition in the matter. The Shach distinguishes a condition requiring an affirmative act from one fulfilled by refraining from an act. That distinction concerns a future condition. With a condition referring to a past event, the claimant must always establish that the condition was satisfied. Proof is the rule concerning one who claims, 'I found an open entrance,' and thereby seeks to defeat the ketubah. Ran asks why a special presumption is needed, since the ketubah was itself conditional on her having been a virgin and she should have to establish fulfillment of that condition. Even though the husband alleges an affirmative breach, the burden still rests on the claimant. The reason is that a future condition is external to an obligation that already came into existence; it resembles a later duty resting on the recipient. A past condition determines whether the obligation ever began at all. Therefore the party claiming under the instrument must show that the past condition was satisfied.
He is believed, and there is only one witness. The Shach's explanation is strained. The witness who says, 'This is my handwriting and there was a condition,' is not affirmatively believed in the condition merely because of a migo. Rather, his statement prevents his own signature from functioning as an unqualified authentication. The other witness who says there was no condition can therefore create at most the ordinary effect of one witness. Urim VeTumim says that the witness denying a condition should be stronger because once his signature is authenticated his original testimony is treated as judicially examined. That analogy to ritual testimony is not convincing. One witness has Torah credibility both to create and to remove an oath; there is no general rule that the first one to speak becomes like two witnesses in monetary oath law. The better explanation is that the witness asserting the condition has no independent power to prove the condition once his original signature is established. If he is not believed to retract, he is simply treated as absent for that issue. What remains is one witness saying there was no condition, and therefore an oath can arise. Under Mechaber's view in siman 82, however, witnesses can be believed concerning a condition even without an ordinary migo in certain circumstances. On that view the two are one witness against one witness, so the Torah oath falls away and only hesset remains. This is the principal practical law. Ketzot HaChoshen asks that the borrower himself is believed to claim a condition only through a migo of forgery, and therefore if a single witness supports the document he should be one who must swear and cannot swear. There is no difficulty. With a document signed by only one witness, the borrower does not need any migo to answer, 'I was obligated only conditionally.' If one witness orally said, 'You owe one hundred unconditionally,' the borrower could directly swear and contradict him by saying the obligation was conditional. There is no difference between denying the whole debt and denying the unconditional character of the debt. The migo discussion belongs to a document signed by two witnesses, where their signatures themselves imply an unconditional obligation unless properly explained.
An accommodation document. Urim VeTumim asks on Nimukei Yosef, cited by Sema, who distinguishes taking a kinyan and trusting the other party regarding whether money will later be advanced. If the obligation was made effective whether or not money was later lent, then it is not an accommodation document at all. If it was conditioned on a future loan, he asks whether the document is antedated. There is no difficulty where the kinyan was made 'from now, if he later lends.' Such a conditional kinyan can take effect from now when the future condition is satisfied and does not create an antedated instrument. The novelty of Nimukei Yosef is different. With a deed held as an accommodation, there is a specific prohibition in maintaining an instrument that can wrongfully be used even though the underlying event never occurred. With a kinyan alone, there may be no prohibition under 'do not let wrongdoing dwell in your tent,' even if the witnesses do not know of a privately stated condition and theoretical loss could result. The special prohibition is attached to maintaining the wrongful document itself. If witnesses know of the condition, they can state it when collection is attempted; if others merely know a kinyan occurred but not its condition, that possibility does not itself make the private kinyan an accommodation document.
They are not believed in any respect. Sema and Shach understand this to include a later claim that there had been a condition. They ask from simanim 82 and 29. Urim VeTumim first suggests that witnesses can be believed if the condition arose only after the signing, because that does not contradict what their signatures said. That point is correct but does not solve ordinary preexisting conditions. His second answer, that they are believed where there was an express condition not to write the condition into the deed, is untenable. If otherwise they cannot retract, adding a condition that the condition itself should not be written does not create new credibility. The real distinction is between ordinary monetary deeds and a get. In an ordinary debt document, the witnesses' signed text itself testifies to the legal act. In an acquisition document their signatures attest the kinyan; in a non-acquisition debt instrument they attest the delivery or the legal facts that make the borrower obligated. To say afterward, 'There was a condition,' therefore contradicts the legal act to which their signatures already testified. It is a retraction. In a get, by contrast, the signed text is essentially the husband's formulation. The signature witnesses testify that the get was written at his instruction, not that delivery occurred and divorce was completed. The wife's possession creates the separate presumption that it was delivered for divorce. Therefore the witnesses can later testify that delivery was conditional, or even that delivery was for safekeeping, without retracting their signed testimony. They are contradicting only the external presumption arising from possession, not what their signatures themselves established. There is, however, one important monetary exception. If the creditor instructed the witnesses to write the condition in the deed or to write a separate contemporaneous document recording it, then the condition itself was authorized as part of the documentary record. Such a writing is like a receipt that the creditor authorized. The witnesses may therefore produce it and explain that the original deed was conditional, because they had power from the outset to create the qualifying document, and this is not treated as a later retraction. This is the proper understanding of authorities who allow testimony concerning a written condition even where the original signatures are independently authenticated. Where other witnesses testify to a condition, they are believed only if they do not directly contradict the original signers, such as where the condition was made outside the original signers' presence. If they directly contradict them, the case is two against two. In a sale the purchaser in possession retains the land; in a mortgage the mortgagor's possession controls according to the ordinary rules.
