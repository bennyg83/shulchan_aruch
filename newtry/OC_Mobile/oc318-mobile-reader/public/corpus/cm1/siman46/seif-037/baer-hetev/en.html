But. Meaning that the witnesses made this statement within toch k'dei dibbur of their original testimony, as explained below. Shach.
And they tear it. Seizure even in the presence of witnesses does not preserve the document holder's claim. Shach.
Claimed. The Sema explains that the lender says, "Do not tear my document, because I will authenticate it." Beit din itself does not advance this argument for him, because even if he later authenticates it there will merely be two witnesses against two. The Shach cites Ritva in the name of the Raah: since witnesses may arrive today or tomorrow to authenticate it, producing a two-against-two situation, and the document may then assist its holder regarding property already seized, we should not tear it merely because of present uncertainty. Ritva writes that this is persuasive regarding a document belonging to orphans. But if the lender himself is alive, the court need not preserve it when it is presently invalid. The Shach concludes that perhaps Rashba too agrees that a document of orphans should not be torn.
That already. Meaning that in this case the later witnesses do not come to disqualify the original witnesses personally. The same applies if two witnesses say that the signatories were related through their wives at the time they signed. They are testifying now only to invalidate the document, not to the witnesses' present personal status. Ran. Sema.
The document. The Shach explains that under this view the document is neither torn nor used for collection. He further writes that the first opinion is primary: once the witnesses are disqualified, the document is likewise disqualified. He brings proofs from several poskim and from the Mechaber in siman 34, seif 12.
Movable property. Seizure of movable property is effective even in the presence of witnesses, but seizure of land is ineffective. I have discussed this at length in my work Tekfo Kohen. Shach.
Disqualified. The Sema explains why the witnesses do not render themselves wicked by saying the document was antedated, unlike witnesses who say, "The document was issued on trust." Here they say the underlying loan truly took place. Although their antedating may enable improper collection from encumbered property, the Sema says this is not considered a wicked act because the loan actually preceded the purchaser's acquisition, and the purchaser should not have bought the property. The Taz finds this very difficult, because even where the underlying facts are true it is wicked to join with invalid testimony, as stated at the beginning of siman 37. See the Taz's own resolution.
That it was fulfilled. This implies that if the condition required only abstaining from an act and the borrower claims that he violated the condition, the document remains in force. Ritva writes that whenever a condition requires an affirmative act, the party whose rights depend on the condition must prove that he performed it. But where the condition requires merely refraining from an act, the person is presumed not to have acted, and the opposing party must prove that he affirmatively violated and thereby uprooted the condition. Ran writes likewise, and so does the Mechaber below in siman 241, seif 10. See also Even HaEzer at the end of siman 38 in Rama's gloss and Responsa Maharit, simanim 75 and 109. Shach.
One. The Shach writes that the defendant takes a shevuat heset and is exempt, as explained at the end of siman 82. The rule that a supporting witness exempts from an oath, discussed in simanim 75 and 87, applies where the witness definitely testifies that the defendant is exempt from the money. Here, the witness who says there was a condition does not know that the condition was actually fulfilled. The other witness who says there was no condition therefore certainly obligates the defendant in an oath. With respect to the oath this testimony has the force of two, because the Torah granted credibility to one witness for that purpose, and the opposing single witness is no longer believed to contradict him. See Yoreh Deah, siman 127, seif katan 14. Shach.
And they testify. The Shach asks why the witnesses should not be believed through a migo. He suggests that because the document explicitly appears to have been executed without any condition, their statement that there was a condition resembles saying that the document was issued on trust. The practical law nevertheless requires study. See there.
They are believed. This is so even when their handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere, as the Tur implies. The following rule, that they may also explain their original words, likewise applies even where their handwriting can be independently authenticated. Nimukei Yosef says so explicitly, and Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 64, writes that they are believed even if their explanation is somewhat strained. See there and Responsa Maharashach relating to part 2, siman 1. Shach.
On trust. Such a document was not permitted to be written, so the witnesses would be rendering themselves wicked by saying they signed it. Nimukei Yosef writes, however, that everyday practice is to make an ordinary kinyan and then trust the other party concerning whether the money was actually advanced, even though one has thereby obligated himself. The prohibition concerns only creating a formal document. Sema.
To pay. See above, siman 29, seif 2, and at the end of siman 32 and beginning of siman 38, and what the Shach writes there.
In any. The Sema writes that this requires study. In siman 82, seif 12, the Mechaber rules that witnesses are believed to say, "Our words were subject to a condition," even where their handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere, because this is not considered retracting testimony: the document remains valid if the condition is fulfilled. Therefore, the statement here that they are not believed "in any matter" can refer only to the cases mentioned in the Gemara, namely that they were minors, disqualified, under compulsion, or that the document was issued on trust or after a prior protest. It cannot include the claim that there was a condition, because such a claim does not uproot the document itself. By contrast, the other claims seek to invalidate the document entirely. Although the Mechaber in siman 29, seif 1, writes that a witness cannot later add a condition to his testimony, that case concerns oral testimony already given before beit din. If there was a condition, he should have mentioned it rather than testify without qualification. Signing a document is different. See what is written in siman 25, seif 5, seif katan 3, where the Sema's words there appear inconsistent with what he writes here. The Shach likewise writes that this distinction is unconvincing and cannot explain the Tur, because in siman 29 the Tur says that the Rosh disagrees with Rambam even though the Rosh discusses documentary testimony. The Tur itself poses no difficulty because it can cite differing opinions, but the Mechaber remains difficult and requires further study. See Responsa Maharam Alashkar at the end of siman 34. The Shach further notes that the Mechaber's wording implies that where their handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere, the witnesses are not believed even to say that at the time they signed they did not know the document was issued on trust but learned this only afterward. Nimukei Yosef, however, implies that they are always believed to say they learned the matter only after signing, even when their handwriting can be independently authenticated. They then resemble witnesses who testify that a document was later waived or paid, and may say, "We signed only concerning the original transaction." Ritva writes similarly, and this is primary. Perhaps the Mechaber also agrees, and his statement concerning independent authentication refers only to the earlier cases and not to information discovered afterward. Shach.
Prior protest. The Ran explains that with a prior protest the sale document itself was permitted to be written. If the sale is later voided because of the protest, the seller must return the purchase money that he received, as stated in the document. Therefore the witnesses are believed, according to this view, even when their handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere, because their testimony does not completely contradict the document. The document still has legal effect to prove the amount that must be refunded. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that according to Rashi, the Ran's own view is that where the handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere they are not believed even regarding a prior protest. Tosafot, the Rosh, and many other poskim agree. Perhaps had the Mechaber seen these authorities he would not have ruled as he did, since they were not cited in the Beit Yosef. See the Shach's discussion.
