[" They are believed. Because the mouth that created the prohibition is the mouth that removed it, and if they wished they could have remained silent. This is not comparable to what is stated below in seif katan 101 that we do not ordinarily apply migo to two witnesses. [" And we tear the document. Seizure in the presence of witnesses does not help:"]
[" Unless he claims, etc. Ran writes in the name of Rashba that if the holder asks for time to authenticate the document, we grant it, and if he succeeds in authenticating it we do not tear the document. But if he does not make such a request, we tear it and do not concern ourselves that tomorrow or on some later day he may find witnesses and authenticate it, because even if he does, the case will then be two witnesses against two witnesses. Ritva writes that Rashi’s position is that the document is torn, and Baal HaItur writes the same in the name of Raam, as does his teacher Rashba. His teacher Raah, however, held that since today or tomorrow witnesses may come and authenticate the document, creating two against two, and the document would then help for purposes of seizure, we should not tear it because of doubt. He compares this to the statement in Shevuot that we do not tear a document lest there be a judge who rules like Rabbi Eliezer, even though one should not initially rule that way. Ritva says that Raah’s position is persuasive with respect to a document belonging to orphans, as in that case, but where the creditor himself is alive we do not preserve his document, because if he had authentication available he should already have produced it, and authentication is not readily found; even if it is later authenticated, it will help only for seizure. Since the document is presently invalid, we do not delay. This is the primary ruling. Raah must therefore have understood Rav Nachman’s statement, ‘place two against two,’ not as Rashi did regarding tearing the document, but as Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri in the name of Rashba explain it regarding a receipt. Examine this carefully. Nevertheless, it appears to me possible that Rashba too agrees that a document belonging to orphans is not torn:"]

[" And some disagree, etc. The first view is primary. Once the witnesses are disqualified, the document they signed is likewise disqualified. This is the view of Ran, and Bach rules accordingly. There is no sound basis to distinguish and preserve the document on its prior presumption of validity. The Gemara at the beginning of Merubah and at the beginning of Zeh Borer states that where witnesses are disqualified because of robbery, the document is invalid even retroactively, including for the period before testimony was given concerning their robbery, according to Abaye, whose rule we follow that disqualification operates retroactively. According to the Rosh’s opposing view here, one would have to say that those passages concern witnesses being disqualified as robbers in their own presence, while here the witnesses are alive but absent. It is not reasonable to distinguish on that basis. Indeed, Tosafot appear to go further: even where the witnesses have died, if later witnesses testify that they had been robbers, the document is invalid. This is consistent with Ran’s initial formulation that when the later witnesses say the signers ‘were disqualified witnesses,’ he interprets this as meaning that they had been relatives through marriage and later became unrelated. But if the later witnesses say, ‘They were robbers,’ their testimony is accepted even after the signers died. Tosafot’s statement that ‘they were disqualified’ cannot mean that they are still disqualified, because then the later witnesses would certainly be believed; rather, it means that at the time of signing they were disqualified by family relationship and afterward became valid. Tosafot do not intend to distinguish between dead and living witnesses. Proof is that they immediately ask why the later witnesses are not believed by a migo that they could have said the signers are still disqualified, and Rabbenu Yitzchak answers that migo does not apply to two witnesses because one does not know the mind of the other. If Tosafot meant to distinguish death from life, this question would not arise, because the baraita expressly says that the signers had died. Rather, even after death, had the later witnesses said that the signers were robbers and therefore remained disqualified, they would be believed. Tosafot say this explicitly at the end of Bava Kamma 72. Rivash, siman 266, likewise cites Ramban as explaining the case of two witnesses who signed and then died, and later witnesses say they were disqualified by relationship, that if the claim had been that they were gamblers, we would not simply say there are two against two, because can the later witnesses not disqualify deceased signers for robbery? Rivash appears to agree. Ritva in the second chapter of Ketubot likewise rejects an explanation that because the signers died and their persons can no longer be disqualified, the later testimony counts only as testimony concerning the money. He says the correct rule is as we have stated, and that his teacher explained it in the name of the great master. Beit Yosef above in siman 34, new material 18, likewise cites Responsa Rashba that if a witness signed on a document died and later witnesses testified that he had lent at fixed interest, the document is invalid. The Mechaber codifies this in siman 34, seif 12. Thus, witnesses need not be disqualified only in their own presence; even after death they may be shown to have been robbers, and all documents bearing their signatures are invalid. This is the primary ruling:"]

[" Or there was a condition in the matter, and we did not see that the condition was fulfilled, etc. This appears to mean that the witnesses say there was a condition requiring the holder of the document to perform a certain act, and they did not see him perform it, while the borrower or seller says that he did not perform it. The document is then ineffective until its holder proves that he performed the required act. Rashi likewise explains: ‘Our words were conditional, he sold it to him on condition that he do such and such, and we did not see that he fulfilled the condition; this one says, He did not fulfill it for me, and therefore it is not a sale.’ This implies that if the condition required mere abstention from an act, and the borrower claims that the holder violated it, the document remains in force unless the violation is proved. Ritva likewise writes that when the witnesses say ‘fulfill your condition,’ they are believed to say their original transaction was conditional when their handwriting is not authenticated from another source. If the party claims that he fulfilled the condition, the burden of proof is upon him where the condition required an affirmative act. Ritva writes similarly in Kiddushin, explaining that every affirmative condition, whether in divorce or kiddushin, places the burden on the party upon whom the condition rests to prove that he fulfilled it. If a man says, ‘You are betrothed to me on condition that I give you two hundred zuz,’ or, ‘on condition that you give me,’ the presumption is that neither he nor she gave until proof of payment is produced. By contrast, where the condition is one of abstention, the presumption is that the forbidden act was not performed, and the party who claims that it was performed and the condition thereby nullified bears the burden of proof. Ran in Mi SheAchazo and the Mechaber below in siman 241, seif 10, write likewise. See also the gloss at the end of Even HaEzer, siman 31, and Responsa Maharit, simanim 75 and 109:"]
[" And even one witness, etc. Ritva explains that two witnesses signed the document. One says, ‘This is my handwriting, but there was a condition and we did not see that it was fulfilled,’ while the other says only, ‘This is my handwriting, and I did not hear of any condition.’ If the first witness instead said, ‘There was a condition and it was fulfilled,’ the document would remain valid even according to his own statement. Likewise, the second witness must not be understood as saying positively, ‘There certainly was no condition,’ thereby directly contradicting the first, because then one of them is necessarily lying and there would merely be one witness against one witness, which has no force. The Rama cites this in Bedek HaBayit and likewise cites Ramban, who explains that they do not contradict one another, because one heard the condition and the other did not. If they directly contradicted one another, the document could not be authenticated through their testimony at all, because one of them would be disqualified, as we say regarding two groups of witnesses who contradict one another. This was explained above in siman 31. See there:"]
[" And there is only one witness here. Meaning, only one effective witness remains regarding the document, which is insufficient. The defendant swears a heset oath and is exempt, as explained at the end of siman 82. This follows Rabbi Matityah Gaon and the Tur, contrary to Ramah, Beit Yosef, Bach, and other later authorities who explain the dispute between Ramah and the Tur as depending on whether a supporting witness exempts from an oath. They say Ramah holds that a supporting witness does not exempt, while Rabbi Matityah and the Tur hold that he does. This does not appear correct to me. Their words do not naturally bear that reading. Moreover, had this been the issue, the Tur would not have needed to quote Ramah at length, disagree with him, and then cite Rabbi Matityah Gaon. Since Ramah allegedly held that a supporting witness does not exempt while the Tur plainly rules below in simanim 75 and 87 that a supporting witness does exempt, the Tur’s disagreement here would follow automatically. He also should have noted that his father, the Rosh, disagrees with Ramah, for the Rosh rules in the first chapter of Bava Metzia that a supporting witness exempts from an oath. Further, below in siman 75, seif 17, the Tur himself cites Ramah in a way that proves Ramah also holds that a supporting witness exempts, as I write there in seif katan 42. Rather, Ramah’s reasoning here is that the witness who says there was a condition does not know that the condition was not fulfilled. The witness who says there was no condition therefore certainly obligates the defendant to swear, because according to his testimony money is definitely owed. One witness is believed by the Torah to impose an oath like two witnesses are believed to impose monetary liability. The other witness cannot then contradict him for purposes of exemption, because even according to the second witness’s own statement that there was a condition, it is not certain that no money is owed. A supporting witness exempts from an oath only where he testifies with certainty that the defendant is not monetarily liable. When Ramah writes that the same law applies if the witness says ‘it was paid,’ he means that the document was subject to payment, for example the creditor acknowledged before them that he was obligated to tear the document, but the witness does not know that actual money was paid, or similar circumstances. The Tur disagrees even here: once one witness says there was a condition, even apart from the doctrine that a supporting witness exempts, the testimony of the witness who says there was no condition is itself neutralized. The Tur therefore cites Rabbi Matityah Gaon. This is the primary ruling. Examine this carefully. See also what I wrote in Siftei Kohen to Yoreh Deah, siman 127, end of seif katan 13:"]
[" And if it is written in the document, etc. The Rama derived this from the Tur, and Shulchan Aruch and Sema mark the Tur as its source. In truth, however, it is not compelled by the Tur. The Tur may refer specifically to the case just discussed, where their handwriting is authenticated from another source. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 21, beginning of part 3, expressly writes regarding this ruling that it applies where their handwriting is independently authenticated, and Beit Yosef cites him below in siman 82, seif 18. Yet Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 2, end of part 2, appears to discuss even where their handwriting is not independently authenticated. He first writes that the great authorities agreed that witnesses are believed to say, ‘Our words were conditional,’ only when their handwriting is not authenticated from another source, and afterward says that if the document itself states that it was made without any condition or reservation, the witnesses are not believed in any circumstance because once they have testified they cannot retract. If so, Rabbenu Yerucham and Baal HaTerumot would disagree, as Responsa Mahari ibn Lev, book 3, end of siman 63, indeed writes. At first glance Baal HaTerumot would appear primary, because the witnesses should be believed by migo. One cannot say that because the document expressly says there was no condition their later statement resembles saying the document was an amanah, thereby making themselves wicked, for with a condition the obligation at least takes effect in part and this is not comparable to an amanah, as Ran writes. Accordingly the Rama’s ruling would require study because Baal HaTerumot explicitly disagrees. It is also possible, however, that Baal HaTerumot and Rabbenu Yerucham do not disagree. Baal HaTerumot may discuss a document that says only ‘without any reservation,’ in which case, if their handwriting is not independently authenticated, the witnesses may explain that they did not intend this phrase to exclude a condition. Rabbenu Yerucham may discuss a document that explicitly says ‘without any condition.’ If so, the difficulty against the Rama is even stronger, because the Rama writes, ‘If it is written in the document, We the witnesses testify that there was no reservation in the document,’ etc., and in such a case everyone would agree that when their handwriting is not independently authenticated they are believed to explain their statement. One may answer, however, that Baal HaTerumot addresses only whether the creditor must swear. That is the specific issue there: he writes that the creditor need not swear and then qualifies this by saying the witnesses’ handwriting is independently authenticated. His meaning may be that if it is not independently authenticated, the creditor at least must swear. There is evidence for this, because immediately afterward Baal HaTerumot brings the dispute whether witnesses who say their words were conditional are believed only when their handwriting is not independently authenticated or even when it is, and he does not decide the matter. How, then, could he have just stated without qualification that his ruling applies only when the handwriting is independently authenticated? Rather, his qualification concerns the oath. According to this, Baal HaTerumot does not disagree with Rabbenu Yerucham, contrary to Mahari ibn Lev, and the Rama’s words are reconciled. One should not object that the witnesses have a migo, because where the document explicitly states that it was made without a condition, their claim that there was a condition resembles an amanah statement. Nevertheless, the practical ruling still requires study:"]

[" And if their handwriting is authenticated from another source, etc. The Tur and Mechaber appear to imply that where their handwriting is independently authenticated they are not believed even to say that when they signed they did not know the document was an amanah and only afterward learned that fact. Nimukei Yosef in Chezkat HaBatim, however, in the name of Ritva, implies that they are always believed to say they learned the matter only after signing, even when their handwriting is independently authenticated, because they are then like witnesses who testify that a document has been paid or forgiven. They may say, ‘For this we signed, but for this later fact we did not sign.’ Ritva says this explicitly in the second chapter of Ketubot: all the foregoing rules concern witnesses who say that at the time they signed their words were an amanah, conditional, or subject to a protest. But if they say they did not know this when they signed, that they signed lawfully, and only afterward learned that an admission to that effect had been made before them, they are always believed, even when their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere, because they do not uproot their original testimony at all. It is as though they say the creditor later waived the debt or later sold the right. This is the primary rule. It is possible that the Tur and Mechaber agree, and that their phrase ‘if their handwriting is authenticated’ refers only back to the preceding claims, not to the case where the matter was learned afterward. The Tur’s statement regarding later discovery comes from the Rosh in the second chapter of Ketubot. Rosh does not distinguish there between whether the handwriting is authenticated elsewhere or not. Ir Shushan and Sema, seif katan 103, likewise explain that the witnesses’ later discovery is not a retraction from testimony but new testimony about a fact that became known after signing, like testimony that the document was later paid or forgiven. If so, they plainly remain believed even when their signatures are independently authenticated. This is contrary to Bach, who writes that the Tur intentionally teaches that where their handwriting is independently authenticated they are not believed even to say that they did not know the truth at the time of signing. Bach’s attempted proof from Tosafot in Ketubot 19 does not establish this. Nor is his claim persuasive that the Tur’s rule in siman 43, seif 8, in the name of Rabbenu Yerucham, that witnesses whose handwriting is independently authenticated are not believed to say they erred, derives from here. Ritva here does not discuss an initial mistake in dating or drafting. He discusses witnesses who correctly signed a document without knowing it was an amanah, and only later heard an admission that it was an amanah. That is entirely unlike siman 43, where they say they were mistaken from the outset regarding the regnal year. Thus the present rule of Ritva is undisputed, and the Tur may agree, as I have written. I later found Responsa Maharam Alshakar, end of siman 34, citing Ritva’s ruling without dissent, that where the witnesses say they learned the fact only afterward they are believed even when their handwriting is independently authenticated. I also found that the Mechaber himself, in Bedek HaBayit, cites Ritva’s statement without qualification as practical law. This is therefore the primary ruling:"]
