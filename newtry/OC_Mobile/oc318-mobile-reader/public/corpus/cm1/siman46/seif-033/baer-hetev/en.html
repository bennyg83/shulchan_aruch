The Sema asks why the Mechaber did not say that the document is invalid even without this concern, since perhaps there had originally been another document with witnesses written in the erased area, which was erased and replaced by the present document and witnesses, while the original authentication was left in place to validate the forgery. He answers that the case can involve paper rather than parchment, where erasing and rewriting could not be concealed. Even so, we fear that the original document was written higher up, at some distance from the authentication, with the intervening area filled by ink lines, which can easily be removed and rewritten. The Shach rejects this. An ordinary ruling about a document must apply in all cases. Rather, the Gemara itself raises the same difficulty, and one must say that we do not fear that a person will erase the entire document and its witnesses, because the judges who signed the authentication might later establish that they never authenticated witnesses whose signatures appeared over an erasure, thereby causing him to lose his document entirely. We therefore fear only that he cut away the original upper portion, leaving the genuine body of the document in his possession. If the forgery is discovered, he can still produce the genuine document bearing the witnesses' signatures. For this reason the Rambam carefully writes that he 'cut the body of the document,' meaning that it remains in his possession. According to this, if the authentication expressly states that the beit din wrote the authentication immediately adjacent to the document, it is valid. This is why the Tur cites this ruling in the name of the Rambam, and this appears primary.
