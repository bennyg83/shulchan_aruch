Sema and Shach ask why Rambam gives this particular concern rather than the simpler possibility that the holder had one document, erased it, and wrote another document of his choosing on the same parchment. In that case there should be no dispute. Sema answers that the case involves parchment that cannot bear erasure and rewriting, but Shach correctly finds that strained. Shach further says that Tosafot's question against the sugya should also trouble Rambam. Tosafot ask: even if we prohibit leaving scratched space between the witnesses and the authentication, the holder can still erase the original document and write another one. Shach wishes to answer that a creditor will not destroy his valid document through erasure because he fears the manipulation will become known and he will lose the instrument, whereas merely cutting the document does not create that same risk. This is difficult. In the case of witnesses, the Gemara itself fears that one may erase and rewrite even though the original witnesses could later say, "We never signed this document." Those witnesses are expected to remember the substance of their signed document, and according to Rambam, if they do not remember, the paper can lose its force. Why then should a creditor be more afraid of authentication judges, who do not even need to have read the underlying document before authenticating it? One can distinguish that an original loan document may have been written for the borrower even when the lender was absent. The borrower could erase it and rewrite a document making someone else liable to him without losing anything of his own. By contrast, an authentication necessarily belongs to the creditor who came to validate his instrument, so he has more reason to fear destroying his own valid proof. Still, this is forced. Moreover, perhaps the first document was already paid and accompanied by a receipt, so it had become worthless. The holder could erase it and write a different document with a different amount and date without risking a valuable right. Another possibility is that the authentication formula is generic, merely stating that "this document was authenticated." The paper may originally have been a debt owed by the current holder to someone else, authenticated in that other creditor's hands, then repaid and returned. The current holder could erase the original text and write a new debt owed to himself. Thus the simple erasure concern remains. If we instead say that Rambam's novelty applies even where the authentication itself identifies the lender, borrower, date, and amount, the question becomes easier. In such a case ordinary erasure and rewriting of the substantive terms would not profit the holder. Nor need we fear that the original document contained a condition favorable to the debtor and he erased it. Rambam follows Rashba's view above, siman 44, that an erasure in a document does not automatically create a concern that some omitted condition once stood there. Rambam therefore teaches a different concern: the holder may cut away the original document, collect with it, and later use the portion bearing the authentication to create another document on the scratched area and collect a second time. Still, this explanation is somewhat strained. A stronger explanation answers Tosafot's question directly. The Gemara says that where a document is written on erased parchment and the witnesses on ordinary parchment, even if they write, "We witnesses are signed on ordinary parchment and the document is on erased parchment," the document is invalid, because the holder might erase and erase again. The Gemara asks why beit din cannot erase a sample and compare, since parchment erased once differs from parchment erased twice, and answers that we fear an erroneous court. We find a general principle that in Torah-level matters we sometimes fear judicial error, but in rabbinic matters we are more lenient. Where the document is on erased parchment and the witnesses on ordinary parchment, the concern is a full Torah-level forgery: the holder may preserve the genuine witness signatures and rewrite the debt above them. We therefore fear even judicial error in comparing the erasures. But where both document and witnesses are on erased parchment and the authentication is on ordinary parchment, any further concern involving alteration of the witness signatures themselves is only part of the rabbinic law of document authentication, because authentication itself is rabbinic and people are not ordinarily brazen enough to forge signatures. In such a case beit din may erase the authentication handwriting and compare the parchment, because we do not extend the fear of judicial error so far in a merely rabbinic authentication issue. Accordingly, the remaining danger is not ordinary double erasure. The real danger is that the blank scratched area between the document and authentication may originally have contained a complete valid document. The holder can cut away the original document and later write a second document in the scratched space. Since this new writing uses an area erased only once, comparison of first and second erasures will not expose it. This is why Rambam identifies precisely that concern. It also explains why Tur cites the rule specifically in Rambam's name even though the Gemara itself discusses the general law. Rambam's formulation teaches that the disqualifying concern is this particular one. A practical consequence follows. If the authentication is written on the reverse of the document and itself states that the document and its witnesses were on erased parchment, while the suspicious scratched area cannot be used to create a second document linked to that authentication, the concern disappears and the authentication can be valid. Thus Rambam's wording adds a real limitation and is not merely a repetition of the Gemara.
And some validate it. These are the authorities mentioned above who hold that one may not leave scratched space between the witnesses and the authentication. Shach, Taz, Bach, and others ask what reason exists to say R. Yochanan disagrees with Rav on this point, and they find it strained to say that judges would sign upon a scraped or smeared area. A different explanation is available. The dispute of Rav and R. Yochanan reflects two competing document safeguards. If Chazal permit leaving space between the witnesses and the authentication and protecting that space by ink scratches, they must correspondingly invalidate a document and witnesses written on erased parchment where the authentication is on ordinary parchment, because of the forgery concern explained by Rambam. But if Chazal instead require judges to place the authentication immediately adjacent to the witnesses, leaving no usable space even if scratched, then the latter concern can be removed and a document and witnesses on erased parchment with an authentication on ordinary parchment can be validated. Rav holds that judges cannot always sign with exact precision immediately after the witnesses. As Tosafot and Rashbam explain, some normal spacing is unavoidable, just as witnesses themselves cannot always sign perfectly adjacent to the text. Therefore Chazal allowed protective scratching and, as the corresponding safeguard, invalidated the erased-document and ordinary-parchment-authentication combination. R. Yochanan holds that because scribes commonly reuse erased parchment, Chazal were concerned not to invalidate such documents unnecessarily. He therefore requires judges to sign without leaving usable space, even if that space would be scratched, and in exchange validates the erased-document and ordinary-parchment-authentication form. The two rules are thus interconnected. This also explains Rashbam's apparent view that according to Rav we do not derive law from a one-line document adjoining an authentication, while according to R. Yochanan we do. One might otherwise ask whether they disagree on two independent matters. According to the present analysis they do not. Once R. Yochanan permits reliance on that compact document-authentication structure, he must reject the scratching solution because Tosafot's forgery concern becomes serious. The two rulings flow from one another. Derishah cites Maharshal as answering Rambam and Tur through Tosafot's suggestion that a forger is afraid the letters of the earlier writing will remain recognizable after erasure. At first glance this is difficult. Why should that answer apply according to R. Yochanan but not Rav? And we ourselves rule that when a document is on ordinary parchment and the witnesses on erased parchment, the witnesses must write the explanatory clause, because otherwise the holder might erase and alter. If both document and witnesses are on erased parchment, we still consider the possibility of repeated erasure. The distinction is this. Tosafot explain that scribes commonly erase prewritten document forms. Therefore visible remnants of a scribe's letters in an erased document are not suspicious; that is exactly how reused parchment comes to exist. But witness signatures are different. Their handwriting is generally coarser and visibly distinct from professional scribal writing. If the holder erases both the document and the witness signatures, remnants of those heavier witness letters can reveal that a completed signed document once stood there. Accordingly, in the authentication case, where the feared manipulation involves erasing an entire prior document together with its witnesses, the forger can be deterred because the erased witness lettering may remain visible. By contrast, where the concern involves only alteration of the document text while the witnesses remain, remnants of ordinary scribal writing prove nothing, since scribes routinely erase old forms. This explains why Tosafot's fear-of-visible-letters rationale can work in one case but not the other. It also explains why Tosafot did not prove their conclusion simply from the baraita invalidating a document on erased parchment with witnesses on ordinary parchment. That case concerns the scribe's writing, whose traces are expected. Their stronger proof comes from a scenario involving repeated erasure of witness writing. There remains a deeper conceptual difficulty. We rule like Rabbenu Yonah that even a document capable of forgery can retain some documentary status, at least to prevent the debtor from claiming the transaction never occurred. If so, why should an authentication separated by improper space be wholly ineffective? Even if the holder can alter it, the underlying fact that the original document was authenticated may still be true. The answer may be that Chazal deliberately invalidated alterable forms unless the prescribed safeguard is actually used. In an ordinary document, even when the form is alterable, some limited evidentiary effect remains because otherwise the debtor could always deny the transaction entirely, and the filling-with-relatives safeguard gives the document a practical route to valid use. In an authentication, however, if we said the authentication remained effective even without the required scratching, courts and scribes would stop applying the protective measure altogether. The rabbinic enactment would become meaningless. Therefore Chazal made actual compliance with the safeguard a condition of relying on the authentication. This point remains somewhat difficult and depends on broader questions concerning one who admits having written a document and the nature of an alterable instrument, discussed elsewhere. For present purposes, however, the dispute can be coherently explained as above: Rav and R. Yochanan choose different paired safeguards, and the halachah follows R. Yochanan. There is also a useful practical consequence of this pairing of rules. If one adopts Rav's framework and permits a scratched intervening area, then the authentication itself must be treated more cautiously because the holder possesses greater physical room for manipulation. The legal system compensates for that by refusing to validate certain combinations of erased parchment and ordinary-parchment authentication. If one adopts R. Yochanan's stricter spacing rule, the physical opportunity for such manipulation is removed at the outset. Chazal can therefore be more permissive regarding the parchment materials themselves. Thus the dispute is not an arbitrary disagreement over drafting style. It reflects two different methods of securing the same goal: preventing the holder from constructing a second enforceable document from the authenticated sheet. This also explains why the halachah can choose R. Yochanan without creating any contradiction with the general anti-forgery principles elsewhere. We are not becoming more lenient overall. We are relocating the safeguard from the parchment-type rule to the spacing rule. Accordingly, where the judges have complied with R. Yochanan's stricter requirement and no usable intervening space remains, the very concern that drove Rambam's invalidation can disappear.
