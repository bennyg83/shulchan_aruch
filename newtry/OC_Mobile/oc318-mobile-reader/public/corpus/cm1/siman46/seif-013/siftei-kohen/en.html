Before witnesses. The Sema writes that in Perishah he explained that the Tur erred by not writing this requirement. The Bach says that the Tur abbreviated because the matter was understood. It appears to me that when the Tur says that the witness should write his signature on a shard and cast it before beit din, he means that the entire act, both writing and casting, is to occur before beit din. This is why he writes before beit din rather than merely cast it in beit din. The Rambam may also agree that if the witness writes his signature before beit din, no additional witnesses are required, because the judges themselves see it. Alternatively, the Rambam may hold that he should sign outside beit din before witnesses specifically, because if he signs in beit din for the purpose of enabling them to recognize his handwriting, that itself resembles direct testimony in beit din regarding his signature. The matter requires study.
On a shard. The Ran explains that if the witness wrote on a scroll, another person might find it and misuse it. A shard presents no such concern because writing on it can be altered, and a deed written on such material cannot collect even from unencumbered property. The Ritva and Tosafot state the same. Tosafot further imply that this explanation follows Rabbi Meir, because according to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that witnesses to delivery effect the transaction, the finder could write a gift deed above the signature and make it effective, as I wrote above in siman 42, seif katan 2. The Rambam, Mechaber, Rosh, Tur, and other authorities do not appear to understand it this way, because they rule like Rabbi Eliezer and nevertheless instruct the witness to sign on a shard. Nor can one say that they hold a gift deed written on a shard invalid, since below, at the beginning of siman 191, they rule that it is valid. Rather, they follow the Ritva's explanation in the name of Rabbi Yechiel: because beit din throws the shard outside, people do not imagine that it bears significant writing and do not pick it up. A scroll found on the ground, however, is likely to be picked up and examined, after which the finder may write whatever he wishes above the signature. A further explanation is that because it is highly unusual to execute a deed on a shard, the signer will remember that he signed such a shard before beit din and the facts will become clear. He will also be believed through a migo that he could say that he paid, or that the document contained a condition and was altered. Even if the document purports to waive ordinary defenses, he can say that the waiver was forged, since the shard is material that can be altered. A scroll is more dangerous, because the finder might write a waiver of defenses above the signature, eliminating the signer's migo even if he remembers the circumstances. The signer may also forget why he signed. This is, in my opinion, a clear explanation.
