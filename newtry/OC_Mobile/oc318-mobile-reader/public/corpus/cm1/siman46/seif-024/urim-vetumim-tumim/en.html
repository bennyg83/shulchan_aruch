Rather, the signing. The language clearly implies that signing before the witnesses gave their authentication testimony is forbidden. That is why the Gemara says that once two judges have signed, the witnesses can no longer testify before the third judge and have him add his signature. At the moment the first two signed, the authentication said that three judges sat together, while the third still did not know the witness signatures. Shach, seif katan 59, goes too far in saying that even initially this is permitted because we do not fear the appearance of falsehood. He relies on Ran's explanation that the first signatures are false only because there had not yet been a proper three-judge sitting, and on Riaz, who permits judges to sign before receiving the testimony provided they do not release the document from their possession. That cannot be the practical halachah, especially according to Mechaber. Above, siman 39, Mechaber rules that witnesses should not draft and sign a document before the kinyan merely because the transaction will occur later, due to the appearance of falsehood. All the more so should a beit din avoid signing a judicial authentication before it has received the testimony. Ran's own wording supports this. He says that when the first two judges signed, the third was not yet fit to join them because he did not recognize the witnesses' signatures. If, as Shach says, all three judges could sign before any testimony was received, why would this third judge be unfit merely because he had not yet heard the evidence? The same logic should allow him to sign in anticipation of later testimony. One could force a distinction between a case where all three lack knowledge and expect later testimony and a case where two already know while one does not, but that is strained. The better explanation is that we distinguish between the written authentication formula and the judges' signatures. The scribe may prepare the text of the authentication before the judges sign, because writing a draft is no more serious than witnesses preparing ordinary document wording where the law does not fear the appearance of falsehood. But the judges' signatures themselves certify that the court already sat and completed the authentication. Signing before that occurs is a false judicial act. Thus the Gemara can permit advance writing of the formula under the lenient view while still prohibiting advance judicial signatures. Riaz indeed states a broader position: even after writing and signing, the third judge can later receive testimony and sign, provided the document has not yet left their hands. He links this with Rabbenu Tam's broader rule that we do not fear the appearance of falsehood. That is a coherent dissenting approach, but it is not Mechaber's ruling. Accordingly, the practical law is that the authentication text may be prepared in advance where ordinary drafting rules allow, but the judges should not sign until the required testimony or recognition has actually been completed. There is a further proof that judicial signatures themselves are treated more strictly than mere preparatory drafting. The entire formula of an authentication is written in the language of completed judicial action: "We sat as three, the witnesses came before us, and their signatures were authenticated before us." Before testimony has actually been received, the judges cannot truthfully sign beneath such words. A scribe may write the formula in anticipation, because drafts are not yet testimony or adjudication. But once judges affix their signatures, they adopt the formula as a completed judicial statement. This is precisely why the Gemara distinguishes the point of signing. If the third judge had not yet heard the evidence, the first two cannot truthfully certify that a full panel had completed the authentication. Later testimony before the third does not retroactively make the earlier signatures truthful. That is the force of Ran's expression that the third was "not fit to join" them at that time. The panel must exist as a qualified panel at the moment the judicial signatures are affixed. One can also understand why a later joinder differs from ordinary testimony where several witnesses sign at different times. Witnesses do not create a court through their signatures. Each witness contributes his own testimony. Judges, by contrast, certify that a beit din acted collectively. The truth of that certification depends on the collective state of the panel when they sign. Accordingly, one judge cannot later be added to transform two earlier judicial signatures into a statement that three judges had already sat together. This is the core problem, beyond the general doctrine of appearing false. Riaz's dissent must therefore be read as adopting a fundamentally broader notion of judicial completion. So long as the document remains in the court's possession and no one has relied upon it, he allows the later procedural steps to complete the authentication before release. Under that theory, the earlier signatures are provisional until the instrument leaves their hands. But Mechaber does not follow that view. For Mechaber, judicial signature itself is already a certification, and therefore it must be truthful at that moment. This also answers why later receipt of evidence before the third cannot cure the defect if the first two already signed. The problem is not merely missing information. The problem is that the earlier judicial statement was false when made. A later truthful event cannot convert an earlier false certification into a proper one. The judges must execute a new authentication after the proper three-judge proceeding has occurred.
