They testify before him, and he signs with them. Although the other two served as witnesses, and we rule in siman 7, seif 5, that a witness may not become a judge, the Sages were lenient regarding rabbinic authentication of documents. The Sema adds that a witness signed on the document may serve as a judge to receive testimony concerning the second witness signed with him, citing a responsum of the Rashba, siman 969, and Darkei Moshe 12. Above, in siman 7, section 6, the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite Rabbeinu Yerucham, who writes that a witness signed on a document may not adjudicate that document. Baal HaIttur writes the same in the name of the Rif. The Rashba, however, wrote in a responsum that there is room to validate even a signed witness as a judge. This appears to present a dispute. In truth they do not disagree. Rabbeinu Yerucham means that the witness may not adjudicate the underlying loan, while the Rashba means only that he may serve as a judge for authentication of the document, in accordance with the Gemara’s rule that a witness may become a judge for rabbinic document authentication. Regarding the substance of the document, the Rashba certainly agrees. The Beit Yosef himself cites in siman 7, section 9, a responsum of the Rashba that a witness signed on a will may not sit as a judge concerning that will. It appears in the printed Rashba responsa, siman 1130. This is clear.
Once they have signed, testimony may not be given before him. Neither they nor other persons may testify before the third judge, as I write below in seif katan 59 in the name of the Ran, cited by the Beit Yosef. Baal HaIttur, folio 34, beginning of column 4, likewise writes that once the two have signed, even others may not testify before them, because the three were not sitting together as one court. Although we are not concerned with an appearance of falsehood, here the authentication is actually false, for when they signed and purportedly authenticated the document there was no beit din of three, and the authentication was therefore made unlawfully. I saw in Shiltei HaGibborim, second chapter of Ketubot, folio 476b, that after stating the rule that the two may not testify before the third after writing and signing, he writes in his own name that even after they wrote and signed they may testify before the third, who may then sign with them, following Beit Hillel. He attributes this to Rabbeinu Chananel and adds that judges may write and sign the authentication even before testimony is given, provided it does not leave their possession until testimony is given before them. His final point is correct, because we are not concerned with the appearance of falsehood and all three were assembled when they signed. See siman 39, seif 13, seif katan 35. His first point, however, does not appear correct. It contradicts all the authorities and the plain meaning of the Gemara. I also do not know his source for attributing it to Rabbeinu Chananel. Perhaps he means that all three must sign again together, and that we should not say their earlier signatures permanently prevent them from signing because they are interested parties. This would accord with Rabbeinu Chananel’s view discussed below in seif 26, seif katan 65, rejecting Rashi’s explanation that they are interested because they already signed. This requires study.
It is permitted to write, and so forth. At first glance, the phrase “writing is not the essential act, but signing is” might mean that it is forbidden to write and sign an authentication before the document is authenticated because signing creates an appearance of falsehood. That is not correct. I proved above, siman 39, seif 13, seif katan 35, that even regarding a signature we are not concerned with an appearance of falsehood. I likewise cited nearby, seif katan 58, the Shiltei HaGibborim’s ruling that judges may write and sign the authentication before testimony is given before them. When the Gemara and authorities state that once two judges signed, testimony may not be given before the third so that he may sign, the reason is that the three were not together at the time of signing, and the authentication was therefore made unlawfully. This is explicit in the Ran. He writes that once the first two signed, neither they nor others may testify before the third because their initial signatures were false, since they did not know the signatures. Once they signed without the third joining them, he can no longer join, for the original assembly ended. At the time of signing they were not an assembly of three constituting a beit din, since the third was not fit to join them because he did not recognize the witnesses’ handwriting. It therefore appears that the Rambam’s and Mechaber’s statement that it is permitted to write because writing is not essential but signing is, excludes the case where two receive testimony and sign, and only afterward testimony is given before the third so he may sign. That is ineffective even if testimony is later repeated before all three together, because the first signatures were false and the three were not assembled when they signed. Once the two signed without the third joining, he can no longer join, as the Ran writes. If they merely wrote but did not sign, however, it is permitted even though only two were present at the writing, provided testimony is afterward given before all three together, because writing is not essential and signing is. Although this explanation is somewhat strained in the wording of the Rambam and Mechaber, it is compelled by the Gemara and is the primary practical ruling.
Some disagree. It appears to me that the first opinion is primary in the Gemara. This is the view of the Rif and Rambam, the Rashba in his novellae to Kol HaGet, the Semag, positive commandment 109, folio 190c, Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot Edut, Baal HaIttur, folio 34d, and apparently the Ran. The Ritva rules this way, and it is also Rabbeinu Tam’s view. The Rosh’s explanation that the Gemara’s words “and this is not so” reject only the objection raised against Rav Bibi from Rav Papi is extremely strained. In Kol HaGet, where the anonymous Gemara itself raises the objection, it should not have said “and this is not so,” but rather, “Here the case is different because of Rav Nachman,” and so forth. Furthermore, the essential distinction that documents differ from authentication is absent from the text, and the other answers offered by Tosafot are likewise strained. The primary explanation is that “and this is not so” rejects Rav Papi’s ruling itself. The Gemara in the second chapter of Ketubot nevertheless objects from Rav Papi because, as Tosafot explain there in Rabbeinu Tam’s name, the Gemara sometimes raises a difficulty from a position that is not accepted as law. The Rashba, responsum 1230, cites several such passages. The Ritva likewise writes that although Rav Papi’s ruling was rejected in HaKotev and Kol HaGet, the Gemara cites it here only to show that Rav’s statement appears directly opposed to Rava’s. The answer establishes that Rav does not disagree with Rava. This pattern is common in the Gemara. Some explain that Rav Papi was rejected only after the fact, while others limit the rejection to matters that are not acts of beit din, such as blank document forms, and retain his concern initially regarding judicial acts. These explanations are incorrect. When the Gemara says Rav Papi’s ruling is not accepted, it means it is rejected entirely. Moreover, the case in HaKotev is both prospective and a matter of beit din, yet the concern is not applied. Although Tosafot in Kol HaGet note versions that omit the words “Rav Papi” and distinguish the case in HaKotev because they write that she swore as the beit din ruled, the primary explanation remains as stated.
