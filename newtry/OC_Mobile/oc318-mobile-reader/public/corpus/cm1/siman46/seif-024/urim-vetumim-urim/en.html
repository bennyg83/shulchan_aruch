They testify in his presence, etc. In validation of documents, a witness may become a judge. Therefore, testimony before the third judge is considered testimony before a beit din even though he is alone, because the witnesses themselves are also regarded as judges, Sema. Likewise, a witness signed on the document may serve as a judge to receive testimony validating the signature of the second witness who signed with him, Rashba. Shach explains that this does not mean judging the underlying loan, only receiving validation of the second witness's handwriting, and this is explicit in the responsum. One must explain why, although we established above that judges of validation must be unrelated to the witnesses of the document, the witness himself may here be a validating judge. This case is preferable, because in the former there is concern that a mishap may result, since people may come to validate through a witness and judge in a prohibited combination, as I wrote above in Urim and Tumim. Here, however, it is visibly obvious that he is the very witness. Nor do we say that three quarters of the money is established through him, because he does not testify to the second signature, but merely serves as a judge receiving testimony from others, together with two additional judges.
After they signed, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that although we are generally unconcerned with an appearance of falsehood regarding the wording of the validation, because that wording is like testimony of witnesses, the judges' signatures themselves are more serious and an appearance of falsehood is a concern. Even if two judges know the matter, all three must know it, since they write that they sat as a panel of three.
Because the writing is not primary, etc. Writing is like a document prepared by witnesses, and we rule that an appearance of falsehood does not invalidate it. Signing, however, is a formal act of beit din, and a beit din certainly may not act falsely. Shach holds that the judges may even sign before receiving the testimony. His words do not appear correct, as I wrote in Tumim.
Some disagree. This is Rosh and Tur. They hold that with an act of beit din, even writing the validation before signing is prohibited because an appearance of falsehood is a concern. This dissent also refers back to the first case, where two know and the third does not. Before they wrote the validation, the two may testify before the third. Once they wrote it, even before signing, they may no longer testify. So states the Tur. Shach rules like the first opinion because most poskim disagree with Rosh, and his ruling is correct.
