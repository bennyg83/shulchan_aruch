But two from the marketplace, etc. The phrase cannot mean that the two outsiders also testify concerning the document witness, because then the second document witness would still have only one person supporting him, namely the judge. Rather, the clause refers back to the earlier structure where one outsider already testifies to both document witnesses. If two additional outsiders testify concerning the judge's signature, then each document signature is supported by two and the judge's signature is also supported by two. Beit Yosef accordingly corrected the text of Ran, where Ran cites Raah as saying that two outsiders testify concerning "the witness and the judge." Beit Yosef reads it as "the judge alone," relying on the previous statement that another outsider already identifies the document witnesses. Shach objects that Beit Yosef and those following him overlooked Ritva. Ritva explains that even if the judge alone identifies the second document witness, that can suffice because document authentication is rabbinic. According to him, the real problem in joining a document witness with a judge is not that only one person establishes the second signature. Rather, the problem is that the document witness and judge testify to different types of facts. If two outsiders testify jointly concerning a document witness and a judge, their testimony is of one type and can combine. Shach would therefore validate the form more broadly. This would be persuasive had Ran cited Ritva. But Ran cites Raah. Now that Raah's commentary to Ketubot is available, he explicitly follows Rashi and Tosafot: a witness and a judge do not combine because, as to the second document witness, only the judge testifies and that is merely one witness. Thus Beit Yosef's correction is compelling. Maharit, Even HaEzer siman 69, also quotes Ran in precisely the corrected form: two outsiders testify regarding the judge, not regarding both witness and judge. Ran himself later says that the Yerushalmi, which allows a document to be authenticated through the handwriting of one witness and one judge, disagrees with the Bavli and follows Rami bar Chama. If Ritva's explanation were correct and two outsiders could simply testify concerning one witness and one judge, Ran would not need to treat the Yerushalmi as conflicting so sharply. The language of the Yerushalmi itself would fit the broader form. This confirms that Ran follows the Tosafot-Raah understanding. For practical halachah, Shach nevertheless wishes to rule like Ritva. He argues from another issue: according to Ritva, judges authenticating a document should not be relatives of the document witnesses, and that is indeed common practice. Since the custom follows Ritva there, perhaps we should follow him here as well. I do not understand this proof. According to Tosafot and Raah, if a document witness identifies his own signature and another witness identifies the second signature while one authentication judge identifies his own signature, the structure can sometimes produce most or all of the monetary force through related people. If the authentication judge is related to the document witness, the money may effectively be extracted through a combination of relatives. Thus even under their view there are strong reasons to avoid kinship between authentication judges and document witnesses. For example, if the document witness says, "This is my signature," and another outsider testifies to the second document witness, while the judge identifies his own authentication signature, one can end up with a valid combination where related participants supply the decisive evidence. The concern therefore does not prove Ritva's broader theory. Responsa of Maharam and Rashba, siman 853, also discuss a case where one document witness and one authentication judge can combine in a certain form and then warn that the authentication judges should not be relatives of the document witnesses, lest such a combination arise. At first glance that responsum seems to support Ritva. But its words can be explained consistently with Tosafot. The combination works where the document witness and judge are joined by an additional independent witness so that every signature has proper support. In that setting, kinship can still cause most of the monetary force to issue through relatives. Thus the practical warning proves nothing about the underlying one-witness sufficiency. There is also a difficulty from Rosh. Rosh concludes that because a document cannot be authenticated through one witness and one judge, we need not worry if the authentication judges are relatives of the document witnesses. That seems to contradict the concern just described. To reconcile Rosh, it appears he follows a third theory: authentication cannot be split. Either the document is authenticated through witnesses or through judges, but not half through one route and half through the other. Maharit, Even HaEzer siman 9, says similarly, and Semag appears to imply it. Under that view, even if a document witness does not remember and would otherwise need a second person concerning his signature, he cannot combine with a judge to authenticate the other half. The entire authentication must belong to one procedural category. Then there is indeed no realistic route through which kinship between document witnesses and authentication judges would create a valid partial combination. Rosh can therefore allow such kinship. This also explains why Rosh may reject a witness-and-judge combination even in some cases where each signature individually has enough numerical support. The defect is structural: there is no half-authentication. This third approach helps explain Baal HaItur as well. At the beginning of his authentication laws he lists several routes, including a case where a document witness appears and a judge appears. Later he rules like Rava that a document witness and judge do not combine. The passages appear contradictory. One can explain the earlier route as a case where the document witness himself remembers the underlying debt and therefore is not functioning as part of authentication at all. He gives substantive testimony to the debt. The only remaining authentication issue concerns the judge, and the document is not being authenticated "half by witnesses and half by judges." By contrast, where both the document witness and judge are merely being authenticated as signatures, Baal HaItur follows Rava and rejects the split structure. This is a reasonable defense of his language. The practical conclusions are therefore as follows. The majority of authorities, including Tosafot, Raah, Ran, and Rivash, do not accept Ritva's broad leniency that one judge alone can effectively count as enough for a second document signature merely because authentication is rabbinic. Rosh appears even stricter and may reject split authentication altogether. Ritva and those who follow him are therefore a minority. The halachah should follow Shulchan Aruch's stricter ruling. Nevertheless, authentication judges should not be relatives of the document witnesses, because even under the stricter approaches there are forms in which kinship can create a problematic evidentiary structure. There remains a separate question. Suppose we wish to authenticate a document through the signatures of authentication judges themselves, and one pair of witnesses testifies to the handwriting of one judge while another pair testifies to the handwriting of a second judge. Is that valid? According to Rif's doctrine of half a matter, this may be a problem. The first pair's testimony establishing one judge's signature accomplishes nothing by itself because one judicial signature does not authenticate the document. Only after the second pair establishes another judge does the evidence become useful. That resembles half a matter. By contrast, according to Tosafot, where each pair cannot reasonably know the other handwriting, testimony is not considered half a matter merely because it covers only one component. The witnesses are testifying to everything they could know. Thus under Tosafot the structure is easier to validate. According to Rif, one might argue that authentication is rabbinic and a mere disclosure of facts, so Chazal were lenient and did not apply the full half-matter restriction. We find similar leniency in accepting a child's testimony concerning handwriting when joined with an adult. Still, I do not know of a clear early source extending the leniency this far. Accordingly, under Rif's approach the matter requires study. This analysis also clarifies why the dispute cannot be reduced simply to whether document authentication is rabbinic. Everyone agrees it is rabbinic. The disagreement is how far that rabbinic leniency extends. Ritva uses the rabbinic character of authentication to tolerate a structure where one judge's testimony, standing alone regarding one portion of the document, receives enhanced weight. Tosafot, Raah, Ran, and Rivash do not go so far. They accept leniencies against remote forgery concerns, but still require the basic numerical structure of two witnesses on each signature unless another recognized legal mechanism supplies the missing force. This is why, according to their approach, if a judge alone attests to the handwriting of one document witness, that does not become equivalent to two ordinary witnesses merely because the matter is rabbinic. The leniency does not erase the structure of testimony. Rosh's approach is stricter still. His rule that authentication cannot be divided means that even where each half seems independently supported, we do not create one completed authentication by stitching together fundamentally different procedural routes. A document is either being authenticated through direct recognition of the original witnesses' signatures or through recognition of the court's authentication signatures. Mixing the two may leave each component incomplete as a unified legal act. This provides a strong conceptual basis for his leniency regarding kinship between document witnesses and authentication judges. If the two categories can never combine into one authentication unit, then the kinship cannot create a case where the money is extracted through one related evidentiary chain. By contrast, according to Ritva, because witness and judge components can combine more flexibly, kinship creates a much more immediate concern. This is why the practice of avoiding kinship does not prove Ritva is correct. It can be adopted as a safeguard under multiple theories. The same is true of the responsum of Maharam and Rashba. Their warning that authentication judges should not be relatives of document witnesses does not necessarily imply that a judge alone is enough for one side of the authentication. It may simply reflect cases where an additional outsider joins and the combined structure becomes valid. Once that happens, kinship can become consequential. There is also support for the stricter view from the language of the Bavli. Rava's rejection of Rami bar Chama is stated broadly: a witness and a judge do not combine. The Gemara does not qualify the rule by saying, "unless another outsider joins this one," or, "unless the matter is rabbinic." That broad formulation naturally favors Tosafot and Raah. Ritva's qualification is ingenious but less direct. This further supports Shulchan Aruch's stricter practical ruling. As to Baal HaItur, the distinction suggested above is especially important. When the original document witness appears and remembers the actual debt, he is not engaged in authentication at all. He is testifying substantively that the loan recorded in the document is true. The judge's role then relates only to the separate judicial authentication. There is therefore no divided authentication. But when both the document witness and judge are being used merely as handwriting identifiers, then one half comes through witness authentication and one half through judicial authentication. That is the structure Rava rejects. Thus Baal HaItur's apparently contradictory rulings can stand together. Finally, the question of two separate pairs authenticating two judicial signatures deserves practical caution. Suppose pair A says, "This is Judge One's handwriting," and pair B says, "This is Judge Two's handwriting." Under Rif's half-matter doctrine, pair A alone has established nothing legally operative. One judge's signature is not a court authentication. Only after pair B supplies the second judge does the result become useful. That looks like each pair testified to only half of the legally necessary fact. Tosafot's broader understanding of half a matter is more lenient. Where each pair testified to everything available to them, their testimony can combine. A witness cannot be expected to know handwriting he has never seen. Under that approach, the two pairs can authenticate the two judges separately. Whether the rabbinic nature of document authentication is enough to follow this leniency even according to Rif is uncertain. We do find Chazal accepting forms of authentication that would be insufficient in ordinary Torah testimony, such as a minor joining an adult in limited circumstances. But one should not create a new leniency merely by analogy where the early authorities did not state it. Therefore I leave this final case requiring study. For the settled law, however, the conclusion is firm: we do not follow Ritva's broad one-judge leniency against the larger group of authorities; a witness-and-judge combination requires the complete supporting structure recognized by Shulchan Aruch; and authentication judges should not be relatives of the document witnesses.
