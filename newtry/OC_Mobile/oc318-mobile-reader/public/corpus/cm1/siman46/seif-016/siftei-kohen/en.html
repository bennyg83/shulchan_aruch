Because the Mechaber and the other later authorities did not properly understand the words of the Ran or the law here, I am compelled to elaborate somewhat. The Ran writes in the second chapter of Ketubot: “That to which the witness testifies,” and so forth. The witnesses to the document testify concerning the maneh recorded in the document, in accordance with the Sages, while the judges testify that the document was authenticated before them. They therefore cannot combine. The Raah writes that a witness and a judge can combine where one outside witness testifies to the signatures of both document witnesses, and one judge testifies to his own signature. Through the witness we have half of the authentication, and through the judge we have the other half. The same law applies where the judge does not testify to his own signature, but two outside witnesses testify concerning the document witness and the judge, for then all the testimony is of one kind.” The Beit Yosef cites this in section 15. The Mechaber wrote in Bedek HaBayit: “This is difficult, for concerning the second document witness there is only the testimony of the judge alone. It therefore appears that the words ‘the witness and’ should be deleted, and the text should read: ‘Rather, two persons from the marketplace testify concerning the judge.’ This is like what I wrote nearby in the name of the Rivash.” His ruling here in the Shulchan Aruch follows that emendation. The Sema, seif katan 45, and the Bach likewise write that the Ran contains a scribal error. In my humble opinion, they did not reach the depth of the matter. It is evident that there is no scribal error and the Ran’s words are clear. According to their emendation, why did the Ran state a separate case at such length? He should have combined everything and written initially that a witness and judge can combine where one outside witness testifies to both document witnesses and one judge testifies to his own signature, or where two witnesses testify to the judge’s signature. Moreover, why did he change the stated rationale? In the first case he wrote that the witness supplies half the authentication and the judge supplies the other half, while in the latter case he wrote that all the testimony is of one kind. Rather, it is clear that the Ran, in the name of the Raah, maintains that it is sufficient when two outside witnesses testify only to one document witness and one judge. This is also compelled by the Gemara. The proposed combination of a witness and judge was rejected only because the judge does not testify to that concerning which the witness testifies. Were that not so, the combination would have been acceptable even though the second document witness was supported only by the judge’s testimony. Thus, when the Gemara concludes that they do not combine, that applies where the document witness testifies to his own signature and the judge testifies to his own signature, so that one testifies concerning the money in the document and the other concerning the signatures, and it is improper to combine them. Where two outside witnesses testify to the document witness and the judge, however, all the testimony is of one kind and it is effective. The Ran was therefore precise. In the first case there is half of the authentication through the witness and half through the judge. In the second he writes that all the testimony is of one kind, meaning that although there is not a complete second half of authentication, since the second document witness has not been fully authenticated, it is nevertheless sufficient because all the testimony is of one kind. I also found that the Ritva in the second chapter of Ketubot explains the Gemara in this manner, and his words explicitly support my position. He cites Rashi’s explanation that the document witness testifies concerning the money in the document, while the judge testifies to his handwriting. Tosafot object that there is insufficient authentication, since no one besides the judge testifies to the second witness’s handwriting and two are required. If the witness also testifies to his fellow’s handwriting, then, even though Rashi did not explain it this way, two additional witnesses are still needed, lest three quarters of the money be extracted through one witness. Tosafot therefore explain that this itself is Rava’s objection. Shmuel’s case is where the witness testifies to his own handwriting and his fellow’s, and the judge likewise testifies to the writing of both witnesses. There are then two witnesses to each signature. Rava objects that the testimony is not the same, because the witness testifies concerning the money in the document while the judge testifies concerning the writing. The witness therefore does not need the judge for his own handwriting, whereas the judge needs the witness for the second signature, and three quarters of the money emerge through one witness. The Ritva finds the Gemara’s wording strained under that explanation and asks how Shmuel and Rami bar Chama, both great scholars, could have erred concerning the explicit rule that two outside witnesses are required. He therefore explains that they did not insist upon full authentication in this case. The requirement of two outside witnesses applies when an authentication is first being made. A document already bearing an authentication is not commonly forged, because one who forges generally forges either witnesses or judges, not both. Since one document witness’s handwriting and one judge’s handwriting have been authenticated, that suffices for rabbinic authentication of documents. Rami bar Chama therefore praised the teaching because two outside witnesses were not needed for the other signature. Rava answered that even though complete authentication is not required for that reason, the witness and judge still cannot combine because their testimony differs. It follows that where two outside witnesses testify concerning the witness and the judge, the combination is effective even according to Rava, since all the testimony is of one kind. The responsum of Maharam of Rothenburg, Prague edition, siman 48, also printed among the responsa of the Rashba, siman 853, states that a witness and judge combine. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak’s proof that the judges’ signatures on an authentication must be recognized, their signatures are useful because if later testimony is given concerning one document witness and one judge, or concerning two judges, that suffices. Maharam therefore also appears to explain the matter as the Ritva did. Even after Rava concludes that an ordinary witness and judge do not combine, they do combine in this configuration. This is clearly the view of the Ran in the name of the Raah, contrary to the Mechaber, Sema, and Bach. This also appears to me the primary rule in practice. It is true that the Rivash, siman 382, concerning a judge signed on a chalitzah document and a judge signed on an authentication, and the Rosh and Tur cited above in seif 14, seif katan 40, appear to hold that a witness and judge never combine. The Gemara could also be explained differently to resolve Tosafot’s objection, namely that a witness and judge never combine, while Rami bar Chama thought the incomplete testimony sufficient and Rava objected that each does not testify to what the other testifies. Had the judge also testified concerning the money in the document, it would have been authenticated; had the witness testified that the document was authenticated before him, it would resemble two judges testifying to their signatures, as the Rosh and Tur write in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. Rabbi Eliezer ibn Chaim likewise explains this in his novellae to Ketubot, and perhaps this was Rabbeinu Yonah’s basis. Nevertheless, the explanation of the Ritva, Ran, and Raah is primary. First, it fits the language of the Gemara. Second, otherwise how could Rav Yehudah in the name of Shmuel have thought they combine when there was not complete testimony, and why did Rami bar Chama praise such a defective statement? If one says he thought it effective despite the deficiency, then Rava added nothing by objecting that the testimony differs. Rather, Rami bar Chama praised the teaching that complete testimony is unnecessary and authentication through one witness and one judge suffices. Rava answered that even if complete testimony is unnecessary, they cannot combine when the matters to which they testify differ. There is no basis to question this authentication because it is incomplete. We find many places where the Sages were lenient concerning authentication of documents because it is rabbinic. Signatures may be authenticated from two deeds of sale or two ketubot even though we do not know independently that those documents are genuine, and authentication may rest upon convincing circumstantial evidence. All the more so here, where there is a strong presumption that one does not forge both witnesses and judges. Since one witness and one judge are certainly genuine, we would otherwise have to assume that he forged the second witness and the remaining judges. The Sages did not suspect such a combined forgery and left the law at its Torah basis. The Mordechai in the second chapter of Ketubot similarly writes regarding recording one’s testimony in a document when one remembers it independently: “There is no basis for hesitation, for authentication of documents is rabbinic and a slight rationale suffices for leniency.” That applies all the more here, where the rationale is strong. This is also the primary reading of the Gemara. Furthermore, according to what I wrote above in seif 14, seif katan 40, that judges customarily do not authenticate the signatures of their relatives, the practice necessarily rests upon the rule that a witness and judge combine in this configuration. Accordingly, this is the established law.
