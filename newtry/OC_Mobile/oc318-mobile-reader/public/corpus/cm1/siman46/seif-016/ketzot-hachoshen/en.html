And one judge. Ran cites Raah that one witness and one judge can combine in validation where the witness supplies half of the validation by testifying to the signatures and the judge supplies the other half by testifying to his own signature on the prior validation. One might fear that perhaps the other judges' signatures on the original validation were forged, so that this judge's signature alone proves nothing. Raah answers that because validation of documents is rabbinic, we do not introduce an unsupported forgery concern merely to weaken the signature already validated. He brings support from the Jerusalem Talmud, which allows one signed witness to create an oath when the other signature cannot be validated. Yet this seems difficult from the law of a document written on one line and two further witnesses on a lower line. There we fear that perhaps the upper document was cut off and a new document inserted between the first set and the lower signatures. Because of that concern, the lower signatures cannot be used for validation. Why there do we weaken even signatures that are themselves established merely because other parts may be forged? A distinction is required. Perhaps there the physical form of the document itself creates a visible irregularity and concrete suspicion of alteration, while Raah speaks of a normal validation document with no present defect. In an ordinary document the rabbinic system does not generate a hypothetical forgery concern. Where the writing itself appears structurally suspicious, the Sages did require stronger proof.
