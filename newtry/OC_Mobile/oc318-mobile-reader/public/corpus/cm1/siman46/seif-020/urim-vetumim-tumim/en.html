Provided that they write, etc. Shach objects to Rama's explanation because the second opinion follows Rashba, who validates relatives as authentication witnesses. If so, why would writing their names in the authentication matter? They would be valid in any event. Rama's intent is different. If Mechaber merely repeated Rashba's view here, that would duplicate the immediately preceding seif. Rama therefore understands the two opinions as corresponding to Ritva's two answers. Ritva asks how judges can sign an authentication without reading the underlying document. Perhaps the authentication witnesses are relatives of the lender or borrower. One answer is that the judges must at least know the names of lender and borrower, so they can ensure the authentication witnesses are not related to them. The second answer is that the authentication itself can identify the names of the authentication witnesses. Rama understands these as two practical approaches even according to the stricter view that related authentication witnesses are invalid. According to the first, merely recording the witnesses' names is not enough. Perhaps later no one will remember or investigate the kinship, and there is also some indignity in a beit din authenticating through relatives. The judges should therefore know the parties' names and avoid the problem before accepting the testimony. According to the second, Chazal did not require such advance scrutiny. If the authentication records exactly who testified, any later kinship issue can be checked. Since authentication is rabbinic, that documentation is sufficient. This is the dispute Rama is explaining. One might ask: if we fear the authentication witnesses were relatives, why are postdated documents valid? Perhaps the authentication witnesses were related at the time they testified and only later became unrelated, so the document now appears valid. The answer is that relatives are not suspected of deliberately giving false substantive testimony. Their invalidity is a Torah disqualification even where the event is true. In the original loan document, the lender himself is careful not to use disqualified witnesses, as the Gemara says concerning minors and other invalid signers. Authentication is different. The loan has already occurred and the creditor now needs someone who recognizes the signatures. If only relatives are available, he may be tempted to use them because otherwise he has no way to authenticate. That is why we are more concerned about kinship in authentication. Still, this creates a further question concerning a postdated authentication itself. Even if the authentication names the witnesses, perhaps they were related when they testified and became unrelated by the date written in the authentication. It is difficult to dismiss this merely as an uncommon event, because Chazal sometimes were concerned even with uncommon possibilities in antedated and postdated documents. The matter therefore requires further study.
