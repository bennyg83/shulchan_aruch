He does not sign, etc. See Urim, where I quoted Sema and the extended discussion in Perishah and Derishah. They distinguish between a defect discovered before the authentication judges sign and one discovered afterward, saying that before signing no new receipt of testimony is needed, whereas after signing the testimony must be received again. I do not understand this distinction. If the third judge does not regain fitness until witnesses testify in beit din that he repented or otherwise became qualified, then any authentication testimony he received before that point was received while he was disqualified. Whether the judges had already signed or not should make no difference. After he becomes qualified, the authentication testimony must be received again. According to Rashba above, siman 25, the other two judges must be with him when the testimony is received so that the receipt occurs in a sitting of three. According to Rosh, who disagrees there, the newly qualified judge can receive the testimony alone because the other two already received it. But either way, if he was genuinely disqualified at the original receipt, a new receipt is required. One might compare him to a gentile who saw handwriting and later converted, or a minor who saw and later matured, who can testify once qualified. That analogy does not help. Those people now testify to a fact they presently know. Here the judge's entire knowledge derives from a judicial receipt of testimony that occurred when he was not fit to receive testimony at all. A minor who purported to receive authentication testimony does not later become able to validate a document merely because he matured. Thus fresh receipt is necessary whenever the earlier receipt occurred in disqualification. The better reading of the Gemara is that it does not discuss receipt of testimony at all. The case is simply three judges who sat to authenticate because they themselves recognized the witness signatures. If a challenge arises against one judge and, before they sign, witnesses establish that he has repented and is now qualified, the three can still sign as a proper sitting. But if the challenge is resolved only after the other two have already signed, the first authentication is defective. At the time those signatures were placed, the third judge was not qualified to join the panel. They must now convene anew and prepare a new authentication. This explains the distinction between before and after signing without creating an artificial rule about whether testimony must be received again. If the original authentication did depend on testimony received while the judge was disqualified, then of course that testimony must also be received anew. A further question arises according to Rosh. Rosh holds that even in writing we sometimes fear an authentication formula that falsely says, "We sat as three," where at the time of writing the third judge was not fit. If so, even where the third judge becomes qualified before the signatures, why is the already written formula not false? The answer may be that Rosh follows the view that repentance or removal of the disqualification restores the judge retroactively, at least for this limited purpose, while the formal joinder is nevertheless not completed until the qualification is clarified. Then the earlier drafting is not truly false, because he was in fact fit retroactively, but the signatures cannot be placed until the qualification is established. This aligns with Ritva's alternative explanation cited by Shach. According to the stricter view that a sinner does not regain fitness until beit din is informed, Rashba's requirement of fresh receipt would remain necessary. There is also an important implication from Rif. Rif says that if one of the three judges was disqualified at the time the other two signed, the authentication is void because they were effectively only two judges sitting together. This suggests that the disqualified judge does not automatically invalidate the other two as though one relative or disqualified witness destroys an entire witnessing group. If the problem were total invalidation through a disqualified member, saying "they were only two" would be inaccurate. The two valid judges would also be disqualified from that proceeding. Responsa of Mahari Ashkenazi likewise indicate that judges do not necessarily follow the exact rule of a witnessing group where one is found related or disqualified. Raavad, however, in Hilchot Sanhedrin, appears to say that where a disqualified judge is actually counted in the panel, the whole panel can be invalidated analogously to witnesses. Later authorities also cite a stringent view concerning chalitzah panels and other judicial bodies. The evidence is therefore divided. Rif's language strongly supports the more lenient theory: remove the disqualified judge and ask whether enough valid judges remain. If three valid judges remain apart from him, the proceeding can stand. According to Raavad, the whole sitting may be tainted. This practical question depends on the dispute and requires further study. The dispute about when a challenged judge regains fitness also affects the precise moment at which the authentication must be reconstructed. If the judge was truly disqualified at the time the witnesses testified, then his participation in receiving that testimony was void. A later clarification cannot convert an invalid reception of testimony into a valid one. The remaining judges may remember everything that was said, but the qualified panel must receive the testimony again according to the procedural view that requires three judges at reception. If, however, the judge had already repented or otherwise become legally fit before the original testimony, and the later court testimony merely clarified that earlier fact, then the underlying reception may have been valid in substance. The only defect was that his qualification had not yet been judicially established. This is why some authorities distinguish sharply between objective fitness and judicial recognition of fitness. The Gemara's difference between challenge before signing and challenge after signing fits that distinction well. Before signing, once his earlier fitness is clarified, the panel can now truthfully complete the authentication. After signing, the written certification was already issued while his status was under an unresolved challenge. The authentication cannot simply be left standing as though no defect had existed. A new formal act is needed. This also explains why the words "before they signed" are central. If the whole issue were only testimony receipt, signing would be irrelevant. The decisive event would be the moment evidence was heard. The Gemara instead treats signature as the point after which the prior proceeding cannot simply be regularized. That supports the reading that the case involves judges who themselves recognized the signatures rather than a prior witness hearing. The signing transformed their recognition into a completed judicial certification. Once that happened under a defective panel, the paper itself became defective. The relation to "one relative or disqualified person invalidates the whole group" is therefore secondary. The immediate issue is whether the remaining judges' work survives after removing the invalid participant. Rif's language, "it is as though only two sat," strongly suggests yes: the valid judges are not themselves tainted, they are merely numerically insufficient. This has an important practical consequence. If there were four judges and one later proved invalid, Rif's reasoning would permit the authentication to stand through the remaining three, provided all three had actually participated. Under Raavad's stricter analogy to witnesses, the invalid participant could destroy the entire joint proceeding. The two approaches therefore produce different outcomes in panels larger than the minimum. This is not merely theoretical. Authentication panels were sometimes enlarged for honor or local custom. The question whether one invalid judge destroys the whole certification can therefore matter directly. Because the early authorities divide, one should not claim a universal rule without qualification.
