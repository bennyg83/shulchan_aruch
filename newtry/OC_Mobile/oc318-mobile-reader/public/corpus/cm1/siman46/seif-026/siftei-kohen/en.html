And two others came. The two judges themselves are likewise believed, even according to Rashi, because before they sign they are not considered interested parties. The text mentions others because of the concluding case, where even testimony from others is ineffective.
That he repented. If, however, they testify that he never committed the theft, there are two witnesses against two witnesses and he remains disqualified because of doubt. This is the Tur’s ruling. The reason is that when money would be extracted, we leave it in its present possession. The Rambam and Tur rule this way above, siman 34, seif 28.
He may not sign with them, and so forth. According to Rashi, only the two judges who already signed may not testify on his behalf, because they are interested witnesses. It is disgraceful for them to have signed judicially together with an unfit person. Other witnesses, however, may testify concerning him, after which he may sign. I am surprised that the Mechaber and Rama do not mention Rashi’s view at all. Apparently the Beit Yosef did not find anyone who agreed with Rashi. In my humble opinion, Rashi’s explanation is primary in the Gemara and is supported by many authorities. Rabbeinu Chananel’s interpretation, cited by Tosafot, is subject to serious objections, and the Ramban already refuted it in Milchamot in the second chapter of Ketubot. The explanations of the Rif and Rambam, although they do not follow Rabbeinu Chananel in validating the man in a case of two against two, are also difficult. First, the plain wording of the Gemara refers to the same judges who come to testify: “Three who sat to authenticate a document and an objection was raised against one of them, before they signed they may testify concerning him and he signs; after they signed they may not testify concerning him and he signs.” Second, a novel concept as remote as the cancellation of their original joinder should have been stated explicitly, yet the central rationale is absent from the text. Third, if after signing they can receive the testimony anew and form a new court, the Gemara should not have said without qualification that after they sign they may not testify and he may not sign. Fourth, it should not have said they may not testify concerning him, since their testimony is useful before the new panel. It should have said, “They may testify concerning him, but he may not sign with them.” Moreover, when the Gemara asks that an objection concerning family lineage is merely a matter of clarifying facts, so that he is fit retroactively and their joinder was never canceled, none of this is reflected in the Gemara’s wording. When it answers that they testify that he repented, the same objection returns, since once they establish that he repented he should be retroactively fit from the time of repentance. According to the Rif, Rabbeinu Chananel, and Rambam, one must say that he becomes fit only when testimony of his repentance is given in beit din. That is astonishing. The Ran notes that the Rif’s and Rabbeinu Chananel’s words imply that a robber is not restored until testimony of repentance is given in beit din, and that documents he signed between repentance and that testimony are invalid because he was then legally unfit. The Ran objects that testimony does not create his fitness. Once witnesses testify that he had repented when he signed, it is revealed retroactively that he was fit to join, just as in an objection concerning family lineage. He therefore prefers Rashi’s explanation that after the judges signed, they cannot testify because they are interested in avoiding the disgrace of having sat in judgment with an unfit person. The Ritva offers two answers. First, he says the man is not restored until testimony is given in beit din. That answer has already been rejected. Second, he says that even if repentance restores him retroactively, because his fitness was not clarified until now the original joinder is properly canceled. This too is unsatisfactory, for the same should then apply to an objection concerning family lineage. Rashi’s explanation is therefore primary, and the plain language of the Gemara supports it. Tosafot object to Rashi from the rule that when two groups of witnesses contradict one another, we ordinarily retain the person’s presumption of fitness. This is not difficult. The Rosh and Ran already distinguish the cases and reject the position Tosafot cite in Rabbeinu Tam’s name. Like the Rif, they hold that two against two leaves the person doubtfully unfit for purposes of extracting money through his testimony. The Ramban proves this at length in Milchamot, explaining that we do not rely on the person’s presumption to extract money, but leave the money in its possessor’s hands. Baal HaIttur, at the end of folio 35, also rules like the Rif rather than Rabbeinu Chananel. The Rambam rules this in chapter 12 of Hilchot Edut, law 3, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch do so above, siman 34, seif 28. Tosafot further object to Rashi’s explanation of the Gemara’s question, “It is merely a matter of clarifying facts.” They ask how it will ever be clarified, since if two witnesses say he is a slave, any number of witnesses contradicting them count only as two against two. This too is not difficult. Regarding a fact visible and knowable to all, we do not apply the rule that two equal one hundred. In Yevamot, at the beginning of HaIshah Rabbah, Rav says that if a woman remarried on the testimony of two witnesses that her husband died and the husband later appears, she need not leave. The scholars of the West mocked this: “The man stands before us and you say she does not leave.” Tosafot explain that even though two witnesses had said he died and normally two equal one hundred, when the matter is visible and known to all the rule does not apply. Maharik, shoresh 171, similarly writes that where the testimony is not formal testimony but only clarification of a fact, reason dictates following the greater number of knowledgeable persons, as stated in Yevamot and Sotah. The same applies here. Nor need one object that perhaps the investigation will establish his disqualification. The Ritva answers that regarding defects of lineage all families retain a presumption of fitness. There is also another answer. The Gemara’s question assumes an objection concerning family lineage whose truth can and must be clarified publicly. Otherwise the case would again be merely two witnesses against two. Since the judges undertake to prove their statement by an independently verifiable investigation, they are not interested witnesses. Rashi’s wording explicitly supports this. He explains that the matter must be clarified, that it is of a kind people will investigate until the truth is known, and that the fitness does not depend solely upon these judges’ statement. They merely disclose the facts. Thus, because the case concerns a family defect that can be investigated, the judges must substantiate their words and are not treated as interested parties. The Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham, although they ultimately cite the Rif’s explanation, also first cite Rashi. Baal HaIttur, folio 34d, initially cites the explanation of the earlier authorities that the judges are interested, and therefore they may not testify but others may. The Ramban in Milchamot, although agreeing with the Rif that two against two creates doubt and rejecting Baal HaMaor and Rabbeinu Chananel, also appears to agree with Rashi that the reason here is the judges’ interest. He argues against Rabbeinu Chananel that even if the first objectors are not believed to disqualify the judge, once the judges have signed they are no more fit to testify that he was always qualified than to testify that he repented, because in either case they appear interested. Shiltei HaGibborim, in the second chapter of Ketubot, folio 477a, likewise writes in the name of Riaz that after the judges sign, the other two may not testify that the third repented because they are interested in preventing their signatures from becoming void. I have already shown that the Ran agrees. This is therefore the primary explanation.
