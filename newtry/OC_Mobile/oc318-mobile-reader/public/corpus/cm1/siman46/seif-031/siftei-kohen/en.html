It appears that the beit din must write that the authentication is located on the reverse, or else begin the authentication at least one line below the top edge of the reverse side. Otherwise an authentication written on the reverse is invalid. We must suspect that the judges authenticated an unrelated document written below in the ordinary orientation, after which someone cut away that document and wrote a different document on the reverse. If the authentication begins one line below the top edge, this concern does not arise. Were the lower document cut away, there would then have been a one line gap between its witnesses and the authentication, which would invalidate it, and qualified judges would not have made such an authentication.

This excludes Hagahot Mordechai at the end of Bava Batra, which writes that one may wrap another sheet around the document and record an identifying mark upon it. The Maggid Mishneh likewise cites this in the name of a Gaon, but says that no identifying mark can be made that cannot be forged. The Sema cites this, and the Rama’s gloss and Darkei Moshe appear to follow it. In my view, the Gaon’s ruling is correct. Heaven forbid that the words of a Gaon be rejected without proof. It is well known that one does not dispute a Gaon absent clear proof from the Gemara, especially here, where Baal HaIttur and Hagahot Mordechai cite the Gaon as practical law, and Rabbeinu Yerucham at the end of Netiv 2 also cites Baal HaIttur in the Gaon’s name as law. The Maggid Mishneh writes that the Rambam’s ruling is primary, but in my humble opinion even the Rambam agrees with the Gaon. The Rambam discusses a document that has blank space on its reverse, in which case it is plainly preferable to write the authentication there. The Gaon discusses a case with no blank space anywhere on the sheet, such as where both front and reverse are fully written. In that situation another sheet is wrapped around it and the authentication includes a distinctive identifying mark. A distinctive mark can be made as the Maggid Mishneh himself describes. The judges write some words in their own handwriting upon the body of the original document and state in the authentication that the witnesses signed on the document bearing those particular words have been authenticated. If someone forges it, the handwriting can be compared and will not match. Other identifying marks could be forged because both the document and authentication remain in his possession. Understood this way, the Gaon’s ruling is well reasoned and primary.
