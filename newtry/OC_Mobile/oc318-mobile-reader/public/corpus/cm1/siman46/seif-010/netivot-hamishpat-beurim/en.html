Since this is so. The Shach explains Rambam to mean that when the witnesses are present but no longer remember the matter and therefore cannot testify orally, reliance on the document becomes testimony from writing. At first glance this is difficult, because Rambam generally invalidates testimony from writing even where the witness could speak, and validates ordinary deeds only through a rabbinic enactment. The correct understanding is that the rabbinic enactment validating documents was made because witnesses may die or travel overseas. It therefore covers the very situations for which the enactment was necessary, such as death or absence. But when the witnesses are presently before us, Rambam requires that they remain capable of oral testimony. The enactment was not made to replace oral testimony where the witnesses are available yet personally unable to testify about the matter. Even according to those who derive documentary testimony from Scripture, the same structure can be understood. Written testimony does not become judicial testimony merely when written. Even Rabbeinu Tam, who validates written testimony for a witness capable of oral testimony, requires the writing to reach beit din; until then the witness may retract, because testimony outside beit din is not complete testimony. Accordingly, Torah law may treat an ordinary deed as completing its testimonial status only when it reaches beit din. The rabbinic rule that a signed document is treated as though its testimony was already investigated from the time of signing was necessary precisely because the signers may die before production. Thus, if the witnesses are alive and before us when the deed first reaches beit din, they must still be capable of giving oral testimony. If they died or traveled, the rabbinic enactment preserves the deed. This is the Shach's intent.
And we do not remember. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that it is enough for the witnesses to remember that they signed, even if they no longer remember the underlying loan, because a signed document can itself create liability. The conclusion is correct, although that reason is incomplete. A document written without delivery does not necessarily create the debt, and here the writing was ordinarily made on condition that a loan would actually occur. The proper reason is that the essential testimony of ordinary deed witnesses is that the document was written at the instruction of the obligor. A loan document can be written for the borrower even when the lender is not present, and the later delivery is established by the fact that the document is now in the creditor's hands. Therefore, if the witnesses remember that they signed at the borrower's instruction, they have remembered the very testimony for which their signatures stand. They need not remember the physical loan itself. An acquisition deed is different where the document's date is used to establish an actual kinyan and its priority. There the witnesses must remember the kinyan and its date. Ketzot HaChoshen further suggests that if they remember the transaction but no longer recognize the person, joining their testimony with outside witnesses or a presumption should fail as partial testimony. That is incorrect. Partial testimony is a defect where, at the time of seeing, the witnesses failed to observe a necessary part they could have observed. It is not a defect merely because at the time of testimony their evidence must combine with other evidence. If the signers remember that a particular named obligor instructed them to sign but other witnesses are needed to establish the present person's identity, the two bodies of proof can combine.
That he remember the testimony, except with one witness. The Shach questions Rama's requirement that a single signed witness remember his testimony. According to what I explained above, there is no difficulty. By Torah law the written testimony becomes judicial testimony only when the document reaches beit din. The rule that a fully signed document is treated as though investigated from the time of signing is a rabbinic enactment. Sema writes that with one witness we do not say that his testimony was already investigated at the time of signing. Therefore, when such a document reaches beit din, the single witness must still remember the matter and be capable of oral testimony. If he died, the instrument may still be preserved through the rabbinic enactment made for that situation. If the document had already once been independently authenticated in beit din, then his testimony was already judicially completed at that time, and his later forgetfulness does not invalidate it. As for the method of authentication where each witness says, 'This is my handwriting,' there remains a technical leniency: even though each does not know whether the other truly signed, their authentication is accepted under the special leniencies of document authentication.
