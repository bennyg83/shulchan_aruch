[" Since this is so, etc. Meaning, when we do not remember, etc. This also appears in Halachot Gedolot, Hilchot Edut, p. 114c, in the name of Mar Rav Yehudai. It appears there that Mar Rav Yehudai holds that even if the document is authenticated from another source, this does not help. The later Rabbis disagree there and say: if this witness’s signature had already been established in beit din before the present litigation, he does not have the power to undermine his testimony; if his signature had not previously been established, he is believed. Rambam follows the later Rabbis. A responsum printed at the end of Maharik, shoresh 74, argues that the words of Halachot Gedolot and Rambam are not persuasive, because the Mishnah in the second chapter of Ketubot teaches: if one witness says, ‘This is my handwriting,’ and the other says, ‘This is my handwriting,’ according to Rabbi each requires another witness to join him, while the Sages say no additional witness is needed. The Gemara explains that according to Rabbi they testify about their handwriting, while according to the Sages they testify about the maneh stated in the document. Rashi explains that they are saying, ‘We saw the loan and signed,’ and therefore the two suffice. Rav Yehudah said in the name of Shmuel that the halachah follows the Sages. Thus, whenever a witness says, ‘This is my handwriting,’ he has testified that he saw the loan or transaction and can no longer say that he did not see it. This concludes that responsum. Its argument does not appear correct to me. Certainly, when a witness testifies without qualification, he thereby testifies to the underlying loan. This is why Rambam writes that he is forbidden to testify, etc., meaning that if he simply testifies that this is his handwriting, his testimony will be effective. Beit din plainly need not interrogate him whether he remembers the underlying testimony, because if he did not remember it he should not be testifying. But if he immediately states, within toch kedei dibbur, ‘This is my handwriting, but I never knew this testimony,’ then the document has not been authenticated. The Yerushalmi is also better explained according to Rambam. All agree that if he cannot remember the testimony even with the aid of the document, his testimony is ineffective, even together with another witness, for the reason I explain below. Their dispute concerns a person who does not remember it independently but does remember it through the document: Rav Huna follows Rabbi, and Rabbi Yochanan follows the Sages. Raavad challenges Rambam and writes that some do not rule this way and that judges equal in authority to Rambam hold that if the witnesses say, ‘We have no recollection at all,’ this is not a contradiction; indeed, even if they say, ‘This is our handwriting, but there was never a loan, it was merely an amanah document,’ they are not believed. See Kesef Mishneh. In my view this raises no difficulty. There they are not believed because by their statement they make themselves wicked, as explained below in seif 37, unlike the present case. I have likewise seen this in Migdal Oz. I also see a reason for Rambam’s position: when the witnesses remember none of the testimony, it is as though they testify from their writing, while we rule above in siman 28, seif 11, that witnesses may not send their testimony in writing for precisely this reason. Even according to the view that permits written testimony there when the witnesses are not deaf, that is because they remain fit to testify orally. Likewise, testimony contained in a valid document is effective because by Torah law witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din, as I proved there in seif 12. But when the witnesses themselves stand before us and do not remember the testimony, they are like deaf persons in this respect, because they are wholly unable to testify orally. It appears to me that this is Rambam’s intention when he says, ‘the document is not authenticated, and they are like deaf persons,’ meaning like deaf persons who may not testify by writing because the Torah requires testimony from their mouths and not from their writing, as stated in the chapter Mi SheAchazo and codified by Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot Edut, and explained above in siman 35, seif 11. I see a further proof for Rambam from Yevamot 33b: if they remember, let them come and testify; if they do not, perhaps at times they will see it in their writing and come to testify, while the Torah says, ‘from their mouths,’ and not from their writing. Tosafot and the Rosh there, and Tosafot in the second chapter of Ketubot, challenge this from several passages in Bava Kamma, Get Pashut, Ketubot, and Mi SheAchazo. According to Rambam all can be reconciled, because when the witnesses themselves are before us and do not remember their testimony, the situation is worse. Rambam does hold that in kiddushin even remembering by means of the document is insufficient, and that the ability to rely on this in monetary law is a specific leniency. See Rambam there. The explanations of Kesef Mishneh and Beit Yosef at the end of this siman do not appear correct to me. Bach also argues at length that since we rule that documentary testimony is effective by Torah law, contrary to Rambam in that separate issue, we should likewise reject Rambam here, and he cites Raavad. I do not agree. Even though we reject Rambam regarding the general Torah status of documentary testimony, Rambam’s reasoning here remains convincing for the reasons I have written. In particular, Halachot Gedolot in the name of Mar Rav Yehudai and the later Rabbis all agree with Rambam on this point. The plain language of Rabbi Yochanan, who says, ‘even though he does not remember it independently,’ also implies that he must at least remember it through the document; if he does not remember it at all, his statement has no force. This is precisely as I have explained. See also Ritva in the second chapter of Ketubot, who discusses the matter at length; I have written what appears correct to me:"]
[" And we do not require, etc. Rather, only with one witness, etc. It does not appear correct to distinguish between one witness and two witnesses, for we rule below in siman 51 that one witness in writing has legal force just as two do. Hagahot Mordechai at the beginning of Ketubot writes that although the Gemara concludes that a witness must remember the matter himself, this applies only to one witness, and cites Rabbenu Yitzchak in the second chapter of Ketubot. But that follows Rabbenu Yitzchak’s first explanation in Tosafot there. Tosafot ask: even if the witness remembers nothing, why should that matter? Let him bring his writing to beit din. They initially answer that a single witness in a document is not considered a valid document; only a document with two witnesses is, like the ‘book of purchase.’ As for the statement in Get Pashut that one witness in writing and one orally do not combine, which implies that two written witnesses do combine even in two separate documents, that passage may be discussing a case where the oral witness does not testify that he saw the loan, but that he saw delivery of the document from lender to borrower, which is equivalent to two witnesses signed on the document according to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that delivery witnesses effect the transaction. Tosafot also cite the Yerushalmi as explaining ‘one witness in a document’ to mean that two witnesses signed, but only one signature could be authenticated. If so, this proof shows that according to our ruling below in siman 51 that two separate documents combine even when no witness saw delivery, one written witness has the same legal standing as two for this purpose, and we do not follow Rabbenu Yitzchak’s first explanation. Rather, we follow Tosafot’s later explanation: one witness can have force in writing, and if there are two documents, each signed by one witness, they together constitute a complete document. Why then can the witness not simply bring his writing to beit din even if he remembers nothing? Because a writing has the status of a document only when made with the debtor’s authorization, indicating his obligation. Here the witness wrote his testimony without the debtor’s authorization, so it is not a document. The Rosh in Get Pashut likewise cites the Yerushalmi and explains that Rabbenu Yitzchak understood its reason to be that one written witness has no force, but that the Bavli disagrees with that principle. From the Bavli we learn that one witness in writing obligates an oath. Halachot Gedolot likewise states that if two witnesses signed a document and one was found related or disqualified, an oath is imposed on the basis of the valid witness. This is our ruling below in siman 51, seifim 2 and 3. Thus, one witness is legally comparable to two in this respect. Just as two witnesses whose document was written at the debtor’s instruction need not presently remember the testimony, according to Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur, so too one witness need not remember it for purposes of imposing an oath, and two separate documents need not be remembered for purposes of combining them. This appears clear and primary to me. The Rama, who writes without qualification that with one witness we require that he remember his testimony, requires study:"]
