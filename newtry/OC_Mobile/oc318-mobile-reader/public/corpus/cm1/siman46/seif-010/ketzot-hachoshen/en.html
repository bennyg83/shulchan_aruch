That they remember their testimony. Rambam requires the signed witnesses, when validating their own signatures, to remember the underlying matter. In my view they need not remember the physical transfer of loan money. It is enough that they remember having signed at the borrower's command. A debtor can become obligated through a document even where no money was lent, as explained in siman 40. Thus remembering the borrower's instruction to sign is remembering the legally operative event. The requirement excludes a witness who merely recognizes the handwriting as his own but remembers nothing whatsoever about the transaction or the circumstances of signing. However, Several Geonim and early authorities disagree with Rambam and hold that recognizing one's own signature is sufficient. A Tosefta strongly supports them: witnesses who say, "This is our handwriting, but we do not recognize the man or woman involved," can nevertheless validate the document. The distinction sometimes offered between monetary and prohibition matters is not sufficiently clear. Therefore the dissenting view has substantial force. The discussion also yields another point. If witnesses themselves say, "We never signed this," but others establish the signatures through ordinary comparison, that validation can still be effective on a rabbinic level. This is proof that ordinary document validation does not become Torah level merely because the signed witnesses deny the signatures. Otherwise the weak method of comparison could not suffice.
Only with one witness. Ketubot teaches that a person may write his testimony and later testify from it only if he remembers the matter. Tosafot initially explains that one witness written alone does not have full document status. Even if others later identify the handwriting, they would be testifying from the mouth of that witness because his statement was never legally treated as already examined in beit din. Therefore the witness must personally remember the event. If two witnesses are written in separate documents, however, their testimony can later combine, because each signed writing can have the legal character of examined testimony when properly executed. The apparent contradiction is resolved by distinguishing a solitary informal written note from a formal document made with the obligor's authorization. A note written by one witness merely to preserve memory is not a deed and does not become examined testimony. If beit din itself recognizes his handwriting, they can use it only within the special rules concerning the witness's own remembered testimony. If other people merely identify the signature, that would be hearsay. A formal one witness document prepared with the obligor's knowledge can have stronger status, but according to the first Tosafot explanation still does not fully become a two witness deed until combined with another. Rama's ruling therefore focuses on the ordinary case of one witness recording his own testimony, where memory remains necessary.
