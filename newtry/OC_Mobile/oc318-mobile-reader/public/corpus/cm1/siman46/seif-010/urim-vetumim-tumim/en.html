But we never knew this testimony, etc. It is clear, as Shach writes, that even if there are two witnesses identifying his handwriting and that of his fellow, according to Rambam, if he does not remember the testimony the document is not validated through him. Do not rely on responsum Darkei Noam, siman 5, which says that where another witness joins him in identifying the handwriting he need not remember the underlying testimony. That follows Rosh's approach and not Rambam's. Rambam himself explains that where other witnesses identify the signatures, we need not ask whether the original witnesses remember because they are not believed to weaken the document. This implies that where they do have a migo, such as where they themselves participate in authenticating the signatures and could simply have remained silent, they are believed to say they do not remember at all. Therefore Bach, Lechem Mishneh, and Shach correctly explain Rambam: even if another witness joins him, if the signatory says he does not remember, he is believed because he has a migo of silence, and the document is treated like mere parchment. Even according to Rabbi, who holds that the document witnesses are testifying about their handwriting, if a witness says, "This is my handwriting and this is my fellow's handwriting," but does not remember the transaction, the document fails where he has that migo. This also explains Ketubot 21. The Gemara asks what practical difference exists if Rabbi is uncertain whether document witnesses testify about their signatures or about the underlying debt, and answers with the case where one witness died. Why does it not give the simpler case where the witnesses do not remember the transaction? If Rabbi certainly held they testify only about handwriting, one might have thought the document valid; if he was uncertain, it should fail because one cannot say they testify to the debt without memory. The answer is that even according to Rabbi, where they have a migo and affirmatively say they do not remember, they are believed and the document is invalid. So that case would not distinguish the positions. The Yerushalmi cited by Tosafot and Rosh, however, requires explanation according to Rambam. It connects the dispute whether a witness can refresh his memory from the document or must remember independently with the dispute between Rabbi and the Sages. Rosh's explanation cannot be used literally according to Rambam, because if the witness does not remember at all, the document is invalid even if many others join him. The proper reading for Rambam is this. Rambam himself says that allowing a witness to refresh his memory from the document is a leniency in monetary law. Strictly speaking, testimony refreshed only from writing should not suffice. But because witnesses frequently remember only when they see the document, Chazal were lenient so documents would not routinely become worthless. R. Yochanan holds that once Chazal were lenient, they treated memory refreshed from the document like actual memory. The witness is therefore regarded as testifying to the debt itself and needs no additional witness to join him. Rav Huna is more limited. He agrees that some leniency exists, but only enough so that a witness who remembers from the document may join with another witness and be treated as testifying to handwriting. Chazal did not go so far as to equate such refreshed memory fully with independent recollection. Thus R. Yochanan corresponds to the Sages and Rav Huna to Rabbi. Where the witness independently remembers, everyone agrees one witness suffices in the manner explained by Rosh. We rule like R. Yochanan: refreshed memory is treated fully as memory and no additional joining witness is required. Ritva asks against Rambam from Bava Batra, where Rava denied that he had signed before R. Elazar, then was shown the signature and admitted it. If the document was forged, Rava could not have remembered the underlying testimony, so according to Rambam it would in any event be invalid. There is no difficulty. The signature may have been independently recognizable. The rabbis were certainly expert in one another's handwriting and Rava's own signature could have been known from elsewhere. Alternatively, the underlying oral loan may have been true, while someone forged a document in order to collect from encumbered property. Rava could therefore remember the actual transaction even though the document itself was forged.
And we do not require asking them, etc. This language suggests that where the signatures are not independently authenticated, beit din should ask the witnesses whether they remember the underlying testimony. It implies that even where the signatures are authenticated from elsewhere, were the witnesses legally able to weaken the document, we would ask them. It is strained to say that whenever witnesses come to testify we automatically presume they remember, based on Rambam's statement that one who does not remember should not testify, while only witnesses who are absent from beit din create concern that they may have forgotten. The phrase used earlier, "the witnesses shall come and say, this is my signature and I am a witness to the matter," also sounds as though they must actually state that they remember the testimony. Shach interprets it only as excluding a case where they say they do not remember, but the wording "and I am a witness" sounds stronger. Nevertheless, as a matter of halachah Shach's practical conclusion is compelling. After the fact, if beit din failed to ask and the witnesses simply identified their signatures, we presume they remembered. Otherwise, what would be gained by independent signature authentication? If the default were that witnesses do not remember, then even where their signatures are authenticated from elsewhere and they have no migo, why should the document stand? The mere possibility that they forgot would remain. Likewise Raavad's objection concerning witnesses who say the document was a trust document depends on this point. If their statement is rejected because they make themselves wicked, why not still say they admitted they do not remember? The answer is that once their disqualifying explanation is disregarded, they are treated as though they were silent, and silence carries the presumption that they do remember. This explains Rambam's statement: "Any witness who does not remember should not testify." If Rambam already held that unless the witness expressly says "I remember," his testimony is invalid, the warning would add nothing. And if the warning merely meant "Do not falsely claim to remember," that is obvious because false testimony is forbidden. Rather, Rambam warns the witness not to testify where he actually does not remember, lest beit din neglect to ask him, he depart, and the judges rely on the ordinary presumption that a witness who testified remembered the event. Accordingly, the proper rule is: initially, wherever clarification is possible, beit din should ask whether the witness remembers, so the matter is fully established; after the fact, if they did not ask and the witness gave the testimony without saying he forgot, we rely on the presumption that he remembered. This resolves the wording and the practical law.
Except with one witness, etc. Sema explains that with a single written witness, even if the signature is independently authenticated, the witness must appear before beit din, state the testimony orally, and remember it, because otherwise it is testimony "from their writing" rather than "from their mouths." This is difficult. Below, siman 51, Mechaber rules that according to Rambam a single witness in writing can create the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore must pay when the defendant claims payment. According to Rosh, at minimum, such a written witness obligates the defendant to take an oath denying him. Rama never suggests that the writing is ignored entirely until the witness appears and gives oral testimony. The correct explanation is as stated above. If a one-witness document comes before beit din, we can act upon it and presume the witness remembers. Rama's point is only that if the witness himself appears and says he does not remember, then even though his handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere the document loses force according to this view. Shach asks where Rama obtained such a rule. Tosafot, on the statement "a person may write his testimony in a document provided he remembers it," ask why memory is necessary. Let him simply submit the writing to beit din. One answer says the case is a single witness and a one-witness document has no force. Another answer says he was not instructed to write, or the writing lacks formal document form. Since below, siman 51, we rule that a one-witness writing does have legal force, the first answer is rejected and the second becomes primary. Shach therefore argues that Rama's special rule has no source. This question is strong. It is even stronger according to Rambam, whose view is the first opinion cited by Mechaber. Rambam understands the law simply: because the writing remains under the witness's control and he could destroy it, it is as though the signature were not independently authenticated, and if he says he does not remember he is believed and the writing is impaired. That logic does not create a distinction between one witness and two. Rif's responsum cited by Rosh in the first chapter of Makkot appears, at first glance, to support Rama. Rif discusses a document signed by a relative and an unrelated witness where the unrelated witness did not know of the kinship. He says the unrelated witness's testimony is valid if he remembers the event. Why require memory? Since the related witness does not invalidate him, should the unrelated signature not remain a one-witness document? But below in siman 51 I explained Rif differently, so no proof follows. The best explanation of Rama is that he follows his own broader approach. He generally adopts Rambam's position that, in strict theory, a signatory must remember the testimony. Where the signatures are independently authenticated, the witnesses ordinarily cannot weaken the document by saying they forgot because their written testimony is already fixed. But Rama also accepts Rivash's view, discussed later, that a signed witness can sometimes retract or explain his written testimony in ways that weaken the ordinary "once testified, cannot retract" principle. If so, a single witness who appears and says, "I do not remember," still has enough credibility to impair his own writing. Therefore the one-witness document fails. This is so only when the witness is before us and affirmatively says he does not remember. If he is absent, or has died, the writing remains valid under the ordinary presumption that he remembered when he signed. Just as with two witnesses, we do not distinguish without a source. Accordingly, Rama's law is best understood narrowly: a one-witness document can operate ordinarily; if the witness appears and expressly says he does not remember, his statement can invalidate its force under Rama's approach; if he is absent or dead, we preserve the writing just as we do with a two-witness document.
