For this is merely a disclosure of the matter, etc. There is room to ask about a challenge based on slave status. Suppose witnesses establish only after the judges signed that the challenged judge had been freed before the signing. Is the authentication valid? According to the authorities who say repentance does not restore a disqualified person until beit din is informed, the case of emancipation is easy. A slave becomes free from the moment of valid emancipation, not when a later court hears proof of it. If he was freed before signing, the authentication is valid. But according to the alternative view, even repentance legally restores fitness retroactively and the problem is only that his fitness was not yet known when the judges joined, perhaps the same concern should apply to emancipation. If no one knew he had been freed until after signing, perhaps the joinder should be treated as defective. In my view emancipation is different because it has public notice. Once a slave is freed, the change ordinarily becomes known. The earlier stigma or challenge is thereby weakened from the outset. Repentance can occur privately and has no comparable publicity. This distinction is strongly supported by Rif. Rif says that a challenge of theft differs from a challenge of defective lineage. If the present clarification shows that the person was in fact qualified from the outset, a lineage challenge is merely disclosure of an existing fact. But if the challenge was theft and he later repented, the earlier defect is different. At first glance, why distinguish theft from lineage? If witnesses were proven false concerning the theft, then the judge was always valid and the case is exactly like a false lineage challenge. If he really was a thief and only later repented, then likewise if he really was a slave and only later was freed, he was invalid before the change and valid only afterward. There would be no categorical difference. The distinction makes sense only if the lineage case includes a person who had already been freed before the relevant judicial act, even though that fact was clarified only later. Because emancipation is a matter with public notice, the joinder is not treated as defective merely because the proof came later. By contrast, repentance may have occurred earlier but remained unknown. In that situation the joinder can still be canceled because the qualification had no public manifestation. This reading also resolves the Gemara's own question. If the challenge is theft and there are two against two, the judge remains disqualified. If the challenge is lineage and later evidence establishes he was already freed, the matter is merely clarification and the authentication survives. The Gemara would not be teaching the obvious proposition that if accusers were proven false, the judge was always valid. Nor would it need to teach that a slave becomes valid only after emancipation. Rather, it is teaching the effect of later public clarification of a status that had already changed before the signing. Accordingly, the distinction between repentance and emancipation is coherent and supports the view that lack of publicity, rather than the mere lateness of proof, is what can cancel the joinder in the repentance case. The notion of "mere disclosure" also explains why a family-status challenge is treated differently from an accusation of theft. A theft challenge asserts a morally disqualifying act. Even if the accused later repented before signing, that repentance may be private and unknown. The other judges who sat with him had no reliable basis to regard him as restored. Their panel therefore lacked an established qualified member at the time of action. A lineage or slave-status challenge can be different because the later proof often shows that the supposed defect never applied at the relevant time. For example, witnesses may establish that emancipation occurred earlier. The later testimony is not creating his freedom. It is revealing an already existing public legal status. That is the exact sense in which Rif calls it "mere disclosure." This is why it is wrong to read Rif as contrasting only "true theft" with "false lineage accusation." If the theft accusation is proven false, that too is mere disclosure that he was always valid. There would be no conceptual difference. Rif's contrast must therefore concern a case where both forms of challenge had some initial basis, but the family-status clarification reveals an earlier public status change, while the theft case depends on private repentance. The publicity of emancipation supplies the missing distinction. It also explains why the challenge may be treated as having been neutralized from the beginning. Once a free person moves openly in the community as free, people ordinarily know or can know his status. The later formal proof merely confirms what the legal world already had reason to recognize. Repentance does not function that way. A person can repent privately with no public manifestation. Until the change is established, a court cannot safely build a proceeding on an assumption unknown to everyone. This interpretation gives coherent force to every phrase in Rif and to the Gemara's distinction.
