Because this is merely clarification of the matter. With repentance, the person's fitness does not arise for judicial purposes until testimony establishing repentance reaches beit din. Here, by contrast, later proof merely reveals retroactively that he was always fit. Sema. Tosafot ask how such a matter can ever be absolutely clarified, since two witnesses are no stronger than the original two. Shach answers that where the underlying fact is public and broadly known, the principle that two witnesses are like one hundred does not function in the same way. If witnesses later establish that the person had been freed from slavery, I conclude in Tumim that the authentication is likewise valid. I further prefer Ritva's explanation: even repentance can restore substantive fitness from the time repentance actually occurred, but if the beit din did not know of that fitness until after the signatures were made, the formal joinder can still fail. That defect does not apply where later testimony merely proves the person had already been free, because his fitness was objectively complete. Shach also finds Ritva's answer more plausible. If the witnesses accusing him of theft are themselves later discredited or disqualified, he is plainly fit retroactively and the authentication was never impaired.
