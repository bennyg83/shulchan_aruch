Urim VeTumim writes that if one original signer later became only rabbinically disqualified as a robber while the other remained valid, the document should still stand because the valid witness would not have joined an obvious wrongdoer. I do not recognize this proof. In gittin the rule that one qualified witness would not sign with an invalid Samaritan rests on the fact that get witnesses do not sign apart from one another and the qualified witness thereby effectively vouches for the other. Ordinary monetary witnesses may sign separately. The creditor could therefore have added the rabbinically disqualified signer later after seeing that one valid signature alone did not give him a full document. Likewise, one witness who says he saw the document before the other signer became disqualified cannot simply combine with the signed witness to authenticate the whole. Every signature ordinarily requires two authenticating witnesses or an equivalent legally complete method.
But if it emerges from the witnesses' possession. This ruling derives from Rif's discussion reconciling the rule that one may rely on testimony in a deed with the rule, 'from their mouths and not from their writing.' Rif distinguishes a writing that remains in the witnesses' own hands, which they can suppress, from one delivered to the litigant, which they can no longer retract from and which therefore functions as testimony. Ramban, Raavad, and Ran objected that this would invalidate many documents held by witnesses who no longer remember the transaction, and they also noted that a kiddushin document may already have been delivered before later being entrusted to the witnesses. They therefore explain that the essential distinction is whether the document is of a type meant to be delivered to the litigant and function as legal proof. In my view Rif can be explained coherently without rejecting those authorities. A writing has the status of testimony in a document only when a person who sees it can thereby know that the legal event actually occurred. An acquisition deed works even while in the witnesses' hands, because it may create the obligation from the kinyan itself and need not reach the creditor. Anyone seeing such a document can understand that an obligation exists. A non-acquisition loan document, however, does not establish anything until delivered to the lender; while still in the witnesses' hands, a viewer cannot know whether the borrower ever completed the loan. Therefore the writing is not yet documentary testimony at all. Only when it reaches the creditor does the writing become evidence of a completed transaction. Likewise, a kiddushin document written in the husband's formulation says only, 'I hereby betroth you,' and it creates kiddushin only upon delivery. While in the witnesses' hands, it proves nothing about whether she was ever betrothed. This is why Rif says that so long as the witnesses can suppress the writing, it has not yet become testimony concerning the actual legal act. The phrase that they could 'suppress the document' therefore does not mean merely that they possess a migo to deny it. It means that until delivery the writing has not yet become a public legal proof of any completed event. Once the lender has it, anyone seeing it in his possession can understand that the borrower became obligated, and the signed writing assumes full documentary character. This also explains why the Shulchan Aruch placed this law in the discussion of a witness who became related before delivery rather than merely in the earlier section about memory. A non-acquisition deed whose signer became related before delivery is like a document that had no completing delivery witnesses while the signer was still qualified. According to Ran, signed witnesses function like delivery witnesses when the instrument is delivered, because its emergence in the recipient's possession establishes delivery. If a signer is disqualified at that moment, the document lacks the necessary qualified testimonial force. Urim VeTumim explains Rif differently, saying that the requirement of remembered testimony returns whenever the document remains with the witnesses because they have a migo to suppress it. The wording of Rif does not support that; had that been the whole point, he could have said simply that they are believed through migo. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise uses Rif to answer Tosafot's question concerning a messenger who says, 'I am the second witness.' That explanation fails because when the messenger now delivers the get to the woman, the document becomes completed at that very moment. Torah law never required the signers to authenticate their own signatures before delivering the deed. Moreover the get had already left the signers' possession before the messenger later became the delivery agent. Therefore the special difficulty raised by Tosafot remains, and Ketzot HaChoshen's use of Rif is not correct.
