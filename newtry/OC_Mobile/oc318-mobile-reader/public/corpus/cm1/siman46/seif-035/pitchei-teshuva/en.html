Who became his son in law. See Baer Heitev, who explains that disqualification through kinship does not create suspicion of forgery. See Responsa Neot Deshe, siman 68, who writes that if a person signed a document before becoming financially interested and only later became interested, witnesses must have seen the document beforehand, just as with one who later became a robber. The Sema at the beginning of siman 37 explains that the disqualification of an interested witness is because we suspect him of falsehood. See what I write there in seif katan 1. Bava Batra 159 explicitly establishes both the case of one who later became a robber and the case of one who later inherited the property as requiring that witnesses saw the signature beforehand. Urim, seif katan 105, also cites the inheritance rule in the name of that Gemara. One cannot distinguish by saying that an heir becomes the actual litigant and is therefore suspected of forgery, whereas a merely interested witness is not, because when his primary testimony concerns another person and only produces an indirect benefit to himself, he has the same status as any interested witness. This is no less than the case in siman 66, seif 21, where a debt document is sold to the witnesses signed upon it, and they are described merely as interested witnesses. See there, and see what I wrote above in siman 33, seif 16, seif katan 9.
