And he became his son in law. The Shach writes that the same law applies where both witnesses became relatives. Tosafos in Bava Batra 159 writes that from here one may learn that if two witnesses testified and afterward became relatives, other witnesses may authenticate their handwriting. Their intent must be specifically to two witnesses, for with one witness there is nothing new to learn, since that rule is explicit in the Gemara. Yet if there were a reason to distinguish the case of two witnesses such that a special derivation were required, from where do Tosafos derive it? It appears possible that Tosafos follows Rambam, who holds that relatives through marriage are disqualified only rabbinically. In Gittin 10b the Gemara says that a Jewish witness would not have signed before a Samaritan unless the Samaritan were reliable. Tosafos asks that according to this reasoning the entire document would be authenticated through one Jewish witness, and answers that there is no concern because the Samaritan is truthful and is valid by Torah law. See Responsa Rama, siman 110, who derives from this that since document authentication is rabbinic, one may rely in such circumstances upon one valid witness. Accordingly, when a son in law signed on a document and there is concern that he antedated it improperly, another valid witness signed together with him, and that valid witness presumably would not have participated in wrongdoing. The ordinary presumption is that the document was executed properly and at its true time. Ordinarily one cannot rely on a single witness for this, but where the son in law is valid by Torah law and is disqualified only rabbinically, for a rabbinic matter one may rely upon the one valid witness to remove the defect. Thus there would initially be no basis for the Gemara's concern if only one of the two witnesses had become a son in law. We must therefore say that when the Gemara says "his son in law" it uses abbreviated language and refers to a case where both witnesses had become disqualified. Then there is room to suspect that perhaps they antedated the document, and Tosafos can properly derive that where both later became relatives, others may authenticate their signatures. Examine carefully. On this basis there is room for doubt where a person disqualified rabbinically as a robber signed a document together with a valid witness. Perhaps we need not suspect forgery or antedating, because the one valid witness would not have participated in it, and in a rabbinic matter we rely upon one witness, as Rama wrote. This requires study, for I have not found it discussed by the authorities. Certainly one may be lenient where one witness testifies that he saw the document before the signer became disqualified rabbinically as a robber, for then there are effectively two witnesses, the testifying witness together with the signer whose signature is on the document. It is possible that even where the signer became disqualified by Torah law as a robber, one outside witness would suffice in this manner.
Even if he did not see it. See what I wrote concerning the responsum of Maharam cited by Mordechai in Perek Zeh Borer, where he equates the law of a son in law with that of a robber. The Beit Yosef already expressed surprise at this in siman 33, and I discussed it at length in my novellae. It requires great study. One should not ask against Rambam, who holds that at the time of authentication the witnesses must remember the testimony, except that where their handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere they are not believed to say that they do not remember, as I explained above. Here, once the witness has become a son in law, it is as though we know that he no longer has legally usable recollection, for what difference does his memory make when his present testimony has no legal force? Why then should outside authentication help? The answer is that, as I explained above regarding witnesses who died and similar cases, the Sages validated the document as an enactment for orderly commercial dealings. The same enactment applies here, so that a document should not become invalid merely because the witness later married into the borrower's family. Otherwise no one would accept a document, since tomorrow its witnesses might become related to the borrower and thereby destroy it.
That is specifically when it has left the possession of the witness, etc. The source of this rule is the Rif at the end of Perek Arbaah Achin. The Gemara says that sometimes witnesses look at a writing and testify from it, which would be testimony from their writing rather than from their mouths. The Rif asks from the Mishnah that a person may say, "This is my father's handwriting." If the original witnesses themselves must remember the substance of the testimony, how can other people authenticate their signatures? He answers that the requirement of memory applies where the document is still in the witnesses' possession. Since they could suppress it and not deliver it, until it leaves their possession it is as though they have not yet testified. Therefore, if they remember the testimony their later reliance upon the writing may be effective, but if they do not remember, it is not. Once the document has left their possession and has been delivered so that they can no longer retract, it is regarded as though their testimony was already investigated in beit din, and it is considered testimony from their mouths. We may therefore authenticate such documents without regard to whether the witnesses presently remember the underlying event. This is the Rif's position in summary. The Beit Yosef built the present ruling upon it, reasoning that where the document has not yet left the witness's possession, his testimony is considered to occur now, when he is already the borrower's son in law. Ramban and Nimukei Yosef objected that according to the Rif many ordinary documents would be destroyed whenever the witnesses kept them long enough to forget the underlying transaction before delivering them. They therefore explain that a document that is complete and fit to be delivered is regarded as though it had already been delivered. Only a document of kiddushin that is not itself fit to be handed over as an evidentiary document remains in the category of testimony not yet investigated. The Beit Yosef rejected their distinction because the Rif says broadly that this is why documents may be authenticated through the witnesses' handwriting without concern whether the witnesses remember, which sounds as though he makes no distinction among kinds of documents. See what I wrote in siman 28. Yet the Beit Yosef did not answer Ramban's fundamental question that his reading would invalidate very many documents. Moreover, if a document once left the witnesses' possession and was delivered to a party, it certainly became like testimony investigated in beit din. If it later returned to the witnesses, it should not become invalid because they no longer remember. This is obvious. If so, why did the Sages not institute dating a document of kiddushin? When such a document leaves the witnesses' hands and is delivered to the husband for delivery to the woman, it should already be regarded as investigated testimony. Why should it matter if afterward it returns to the witnesses and they forget? It is very strained to explain, as Ritva does, that the Gemara speaks not of the actual document by which the woman becomes betrothed, but of a separate evidentiary document recording that she was betrothed through one of the methods of kiddushin. The simple reading of the Gemara, and the understanding of the commentators, is that it concerns the very document of kiddushin by which she becomes betrothed, and no date was instituted in that document. There is an additional difficulty from Rabbeinu Yerucham. He rules like the Rif that once a document has left the witnesses' possession no present memory is required, yet he also rules, in the case of a burned loan document where the original witnesses testify to its contents, that they must remember those contents independently, as I wrote above in siman 41, which is Rambam's position. These rulings appear inconsistent. It therefore appears that the master's words should be understood through the disciple, and that the Rif and Rambam are fundamentally of one position. As I explained above at length, the strict law is that witnesses must remember the substance written in the document, so that their testimony remains in the category of something capable of being orally expressed. Where their handwriting is authenticated from another source, however, we presume that they do in fact remember, and they are not believed to weaken the document by saying that they do not remember, because they lack any migo. Where the witnesses died, when it is certain that they cannot presently remember, the Sages nevertheless validated authentication as an enactment. According to this, if a document is presently in the witnesses' possession, even if it earlier had been in the party's possession, if the witnesses say that they do not remember, they are believed, because they have a migo: they could destroy or suppress the document. Their assertion of lack of memory therefore defeats it even if the handwriting can be authenticated elsewhere. This is the Rif's intent. In ordinary documents not in the witnesses' possession, authentication from another source is effective, and we disregard their claim that they do not remember, because we presume that they remember and they have no migo. But where the document remains in their possession, they have a migo to destroy or suppress it, so they are believed that they do not remember, and the document is then mere paper. This is why the Gemara says regarding a document of kiddushin that one should not leave it in the witnesses' hands. If it remains with them, they can credibly say that they do not remember, for they have a migo. If they then testify only from the writing, it is testimony from writing and not from the mouth. The Gemara therefore says that the document should not remain with them. If they remember, they can testify orally even without the writing; if they do not remember, reliance upon the writing is invalid, and their claim of nonmemory is believed because of the migo. By contrast, when the document is not in their possession, they have no migo, and since they already gave their testimony the document is treated as investigated in beit din, so they are not believed to say that they no longer remember. A careful reader of the Rif will see that his text differed slightly from ours. Our text says, "if they do not remember, sometimes they see the writing and testify," which sounds as though we suspect that they will read the writing and falsely say that they remember. The Rif does not accept that reading, for if they say they remember, they are believed. His text therefore reads, in effect, "if they see it in writing," meaning that they explicitly admit that they do not remember and are relying only upon the writing. That is invalid because Scripture requires testimony from the mouth. Everything now fits. A document in the witnesses' possession is not treated as so fully investigated that they lose credibility to say they do not remember, because they have a migo, as Rabbeinu Yerucham explains in the case of the burned document. But where the document is not in their possession and their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere, they cannot say that they do not remember; we therefore authenticate ordinary documents by presuming memory, as Rambam explains. The Rif and Rambam are one position. This also explains the law that when a person writes his testimony on a document and keeps it in his own possession, he is believed to say that he does not remember, which is why the Gemara requires that he actually remember it. Ramban's objection that this would destroy many documents is also answered. Once the witnesses delivered a document to a party, they ordinarily cannot later weaken it by saying that they do not remember, even if it later returns to them, because the original delivery removed their power. The fact that they now possess it does not retroactively restore a migo regarding testimony already completed. Beit din also does not have to interrogate every witness at the moment of ordinary delivery whether he remembers; we rely upon the presumption that he remembers. A document of kiddushin is different because consequences affecting capital law may arise from it, and Scripture requires careful judicial investigation. If the witnesses state at the time of delivery that they do not remember, their statement is credited. The Rif's words are compressed, but when carefully examined they align with Rambam and Rabbeinu Yerucham, and there is no real dispute among them. This also explains why the Gemara says that one should not leave a document of kiddushin with the witnesses because they may later forget, rather than saying simply that if the witnesses die the kiddushin will become void retroactively and the relationship will have been illicit. Under the explanation above, the force of the document and testimony begins at writing and signature, when the witnesses remember. If they later die while it remains in their possession, the document can still be valid through the rabbinic enactment permitting authentication of the handwriting, just as in other cases of dead witnesses. Similarly, in many places the Gemara says according to Abaye that the lien begins from the date of writing, without distinguishing whether the witnesses delivered the document that same day or only later. According to the Beit Yosef's literal reading of the Rif, if they delivered it only the next day the document should be antedated and invalid, because only then would it become investigated testimony and only then would its legal force begin. According to my explanation there is no difficulty. The testimony and document take effect immediately upon writing and signature, since at that time they remember, and the lien therefore begins then. Likewise, if the witnesses die while the document remains with them, the document is valid once their signatures are authenticated, because its force began at writing. Their later inability to remember does not destroy it, because the Sages enacted reliance upon authentication where the witnesses cannot testify. This also explains why the Rif chose specifically the Mishnah that a person is believed to say, "This is my father's handwriting," when asking how authentication can help if the witnesses themselves must remember, rather than asking from the many earlier Mishnayot that plainly allow authentication of documents and gittin. On that particular Mishnah the Rif had cited the rule that document authentication is rabbinic and that the Sages believed a person in a rabbinic matter. In other places one might have answered that strict Torah law indeed would not permit such authentication, but that in monetary matters the Sages enacted it for orderly commerce, as many authorities explain Rambam's broader theory of documents. But the Mishnah about identifying a father's handwriting is explained as a rabbinic authentication because by Torah law the document is already valid without authentication. The Rif therefore asks: according to his thesis, even Torah law should not validate it when the original witnesses do not remember. His answer is that once the document is no longer in the witnesses' possession, Torah law treats it as valid because we presume that the witnesses remember, and "this is my father's handwriting" can refer to a case such as where the witness went overseas and the like. In sum, whether one follows Ramban and Nimukei Yosef's formulation or the explanation I have given of the Rif, wherever the Sages validate a document that remained in the witnesses' possession and they later died, as an enactment for commercial order, the same applies where a witness later became the borrower's son in law. If we were to invalidate such a document merely because a witness later became related, the same problem would arise even for a document already in another person's possession, since the entire issue under Rambam is present legal memory and the son in law's memory cannot help. The Sages therefore upheld the document for the same reason of commercial stability. This is clear and correct in the Rif.
