Or I wrote it in order to borrow, etc. See Urim, where Beit Yosef is explained as speaking of a borrower who claims: "It fell from me and you found it." At first glance, why is he believed to say it fell from him through a migo that he could have claimed the document was forged? Rambam rules in Hilchot Gerushin 12, and Shulchan Aruch rules likewise in Even HaEzer 152:10, that if a get is not independently authenticated but the husband merely says, "It fell from me and you found it," he is not believed. Kesef Mishneh explains that although he has a migo, it does not help. Apparently such a claim stands against a strong presumption, and we do not say migo against a strong presumption. Why then should the monetary document here be different? I found in Maggid Mishneh that he distinguishes between gittin and monetary law. Concerning a get, he says we do not suspect such a fall in matters of prohibition the same way we do in monetary cases. His reason is not obvious. Why should we be more lenient in a prohibition involving a married woman than in money? Perhaps his idea is that most people are careful with their gittin and do not lose them, and in prohibitory law we follow a majority, whereas in monetary law we do not follow a majority to extract money. But that is difficult, because if the majority is strong enough to create the presumption, migo should not overcome it there either. A more plausible distinction is this. In a get, because the wife's release from marriage depends entirely upon the instrument, the husband is especially careful to guard it so that such a mishap will not occur through him. That creates a particularly strong presumption against loss. In ordinary monetary documents people are not so extraordinarily careful. At worst money is lost. The presumption is therefore weaker, and the borrower can be believed through migo. Still, I do not know what source establishes such a distinction, and it requires study. The Gemara in Shnayim Ochazin also seems to point against the broad leniency. There, when borrower and lender each hold a disputed document, the borrower claims, "It is yours, but I repaid you." The Gemara does not formulate the borrower's claim as, "I wrote it in order to borrow but never borrowed, and it fell from me." That suggests such a fall claim is not always available. Ritva in Shitah Mekubetzet, however, writes that the borrower could indeed have said, "I wrote it in order to borrow but never borrowed," and that the Gemara chose the repayment case because it wished to establish the dispute in a shtar aknayata, where the lien can arise from the kinyan itself. From this there is no proof against our case. Ritva's point may simply be that in an ordinary non-kinyan document the borrower could say no loan occurred and the lender somehow obtained the paper. But in a shtar aknayata he cannot say, "I wrote it in order to borrow and never borrowed," even if he claims the paper fell from him. If we do not fear repayment, a found shtar aknayata is returned to the creditor even without the debtor's admission. How then could the debtor defeat it merely by adding a fall claim under a migo? Accordingly, the rule here must be limited to documents whose legal force does not already arise independently from a kinyan. In an ordinary document, the borrower can sometimes say, "I wrote it to borrow, never borrowed, and it fell from me," through the appropriate migo. In a true shtar aknayata, that claim does not work because the kinyan already created the substantive obligation.
