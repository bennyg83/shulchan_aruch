I did not borrow. Beit Yosef explains that the borrower claims, 'It fell from me and you found it.' Urim VeTumim asks from the get case in Even HaEzer, where a husband who authenticates the get is not believed to say it fell from him and the woman found it. The distinction is that with a loan document, the reason we generally do not suspect falling is that a person carefully guards an instrument whose loss can harm him. An unverified debt document is different. The borrower may not guard it carefully because even if it reaches the lender he can still claim forgery, and heirs and purchasers likewise receive the forgery defense from beit din. Therefore falling remains plausible. With a get, by contrast, the document can permit remarriage without ordinary authentication in the same way, so the husband has far greater reason to guard it lest the woman find it and use it outside his presence. Maggid Mishneh distinguishes more broadly between gittin and monetary documents, but the same point can be explained through the different practical consequences of a fall. Even within get cases, falling from the woman after divorce is more readily suspected because she no longer has the same reason to guard it. Pnei Yehoshua's presumption that what lies under a person's hand belongs to him can also support the distinction: a debt document is physically the borrower's paper in a sense, while the creditor's claim concerns the lien evidenced by it, so possession alone does not resolve that underlying lien in the same way.
