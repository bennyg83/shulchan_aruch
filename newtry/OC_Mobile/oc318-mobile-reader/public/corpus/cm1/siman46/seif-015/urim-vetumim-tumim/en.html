Each one testifies concerning his own handwriting, etc. Sema asks from seif 14, which appears to require that when a judge testifies to his handwriting another witness must join him. Sema answers that the earlier case taught a greater novelty: even when another joins him, a document witness and a judge do not combine. Shach correctly observes that this does not fit Rosh. Rosh writes the requirement of an additional witness directly in explaining Shmuel's statement that a witness and a judge combine. There is no special novelty there. Shach says Beit Yosef seems to distinguish between one judge testifying to his handwriting and two judges each doing so, but he finds that distinction implausible. In my view it is quite plausible. Taz notes that Rabbenu Yonah explains why two judges may each testify to their own signatures by saying they are testifying to the authentication of the document. Taz asks: this is not comparable to a document witness, who can be viewed as testifying to the debt itself and therefore may not need another person on his signature. A judge's signature is only a signature on the authentication. If Rabbenu Yonah truly meant to equate the cases, Rosh should distinguish between a judge who remembers the actual authentication proceeding and one who merely recognizes his handwriting. When he remembers the full authentication, perhaps he should count like a document witness who remembers the debt and need no additional person. When he does not, two witnesses should be required. Rosh makes no such distinction. The explanation is different. Ran and Ritva ask why, when a judge identifies his signature, we do not fear that the other judges' signatures were forged. If so, there would be no valid three-judge authentication at all. They conclude that we do not entertain such a remote forgery concern. Even if the authentication clause does not explicitly say "we were three," we rely on the presumption that a beit din authentication was performed with three proper judges. The reason is that a beit din does not ordinarily proceed to authentication without the required quorum. Once we accept this, Rabbenu Yonah's words become clear. When one judge says, "This is my signature on the authentication," he is not merely identifying one isolated mark. He is effectively testifying that a proper three-judge authentication took place. If his own signature is genuine, we presume the others alongside it are the actual judges who sat with him, rather than forged substitutions for other judges. We do not say perhaps other judges sat and the current neighboring signatures are counterfeit. Document authentication is rabbinic, and Chazal were lenient regarding such remote possibilities. Rama in responsum 110 goes even further: if a witness says that before he signed, another witness was already signed on the instrument, this can count as testimony that the earlier signature is genuine. Again the basis is that, in authentication matters, a strong contextual presumption can suffice. Accordingly, when two judges each identify their own signatures, each necessarily testifies, in substance, that this was a proper authentication proceeding involving the judges who signed beside him. The first judge's statement therefore also supports the second judge's signature, and vice versa. Each says, in effect: "This is my signature on a valid beit din authentication, which required three actual judges." Thus each one is not only identifying his own handwriting but also confirming the validity of the entire authentication panel. There are then two witnesses on each relevant judicial signature. This is why two judges can suffice without an outside witness joining either one. By contrast, when only one judge appears, his statement cannot by itself supply the required two-witness authentication of his own signature. However strong the presumption about the existence of the other judges, his own signature still requires another witness. Therefore Rosh and Tur correctly require an additional person to join him. This distinction between one judge and two judges is both logical and necessary. It also explains Rabbenu Yonah's phrase that they testify "to the authentication of the document." He does not mean a judge is literally like a document witness testifying to the underlying loan. Rather, because a valid authentication necessarily involves a full beit din, each judge's identification of his own signature implicitly confirms the participation of the others. Thus, when two judges testify, their statements overlap and complete one another. When only one judge testifies, that overlap does not exist. This also fits Mechaber below, seif 16. There, if one outsider recognizes the signatures of the document witnesses and a judge testifies regarding his own signature, they can combine in a particular structure because both are ultimately addressing the document's authentication. The law does not treat "this is the handwriting of the witnesses" and "this is my signature on the authentication" as wholly unrelated categories. Both concern the same ultimate fact: whether the document is properly authenticated. Accordingly, the distinction drawn here is sound. This also explains why Rosh did not distinguish between a judge who actively remembers the entire authentication and one who merely recognizes his signature. The legal force does not come from his personal recollection of the hearing as though he were retelling an oral event. It comes from the institutional presumption attached to a valid court authentication. Once he identifies his own judicial signature, he thereby affirms that he participated in a proper authentication proceeding. That proceeding could not have existed with fewer than three judges. We therefore treat his statement as linked to the whole panel. This is stronger when two judges appear. Each one's identification of his own signature independently confirms that the other signer was part of the same valid panel. The two statements overlap on the existence of the proper authentication. Therefore no separate outsider is needed. By contrast, one judge alone cannot create a complete authentication of himself and the panel. His testimony still needs another witness to establish his signature. This also makes sense of Rabbenu Yonah's comparison to a document witness. The comparison is not exact in substance but exact in legal function. A document witness who says, "This is my signature," can, in the proper setting, be understood as affirming the debt recorded in the document. A judge who says, "This is my signature," can, in the proper setting, be understood as affirming the valid judicial authentication recorded beside it. The first speaks through the underlying debt; the second through the underlying court act. Thus the phrase "they testify to the authentication of the document" is precise. The consequence follows below in seif 16. If an outsider identifies the signatures of the original document witnesses and a judge identifies his own signature on the authentication, both testimonies ultimately converge upon one issue: that this document stands properly authenticated. We should not treat them as unrelated forms of testimony merely because one reaches that conclusion through the original signatures and the other through the judicial signatures. Accordingly, the distinction between one judge and two judges is not arbitrary. With one judge, there is not enough overlap. With two judges, each one's testimony reinforces the other's participation in a proper panel and the authentication stands.
