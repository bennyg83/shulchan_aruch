(Seif 33: Perhaps the authentication was distant, etc.) The Sema asks why we do not suspect that a different document had originally appeared above, that the holder erased it, and wrote the present one. The answer is that the witness signatures now appearing on the erasure cannot themselves be forged. If he had copied signatures that were originally on clean paper and then rewrote them over an erasure, what would he gain? One might say that he wished to avoid the invalidity of a document on an erasure with witnesses on clean paper, or to avoid arousing suspicion of forgery. But having both the document and its witnesses on an erasure creates still greater suspicion, even though such a format is technically valid, and witnesses are presumed not to act improperly. From this proof one might have thought the document could be authenticated from the judicial endorsement; the ruling therefore teaches that it is invalid for another reason. One should not ask why, even under that other concern, he cut away the upper portion rather than erase it and write whatever he wished. Perhaps he wished to retain a genuine document in his possession.
