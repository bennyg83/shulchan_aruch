And some validate it. This is Rosh's position. He infers from the rule that witnesses may testify before the third judge and then he signs that receipt of authentication testimony does not necessarily require three judges sitting together at the moment of testimony. The dissenters explain that the two witnesses who testify can themselves become judges, so there are effectively three. Shach, seif katan 62, presses this against Rosh and cites commentators who say the two testifying witnesses become judges. In my view, Rosh distinguishes between two concepts. We say in rabbinic matters that a witness can later become a judge. For example, after giving testimony he may join the panel, and if he saw the matter at night he may sometimes judge it by day under the special rules of document authentication. But we do not necessarily say that at the very moment he is giving testimony he simultaneously serves as both witness and judge. That greater fusion appears in get law, where Chazal made exceptional leniencies concerning the agent and authentication, but it need not apply to ordinary civil document authentication. Indeed, if testifying witnesses automatically counted as judges at the same moment, why would document authentication ever require three judges? If two witnesses come and testify concerning the signatures, add one judge and the required panel of three is already present. Yet we never find ordinary document authentication structured routinely with only one independent judge. By contrast, with a get the Gemara explicitly allows two because the agent is a witness and, under that special rule, a witness can also function as judge. This supports Rosh. One should not answer that ordinary authentication simply requires three by uniform enactment to avoid distinguishing cases. That is possible, but it proves that the conceptual identity of witness and judge is not being applied automatically. Rosh therefore holds that the testimony may be received by one judge in the relevant context and later incorporated into a proper three-judge authentication, rather than requiring all three present at the moment of testimony. Raah's comments also indicate discomfort with the claim that the same people are simultaneously witnesses and judges. There is a further apparent proof for the first opinion from Rivash's view that authentication testimony can be given through writing. If written testimony counts like oral testimony, why not say that when two judges sign statements identifying the witness signatures, their written statements themselves supply the testimony, and the third judge can immediately join them as a panel? The answer is that even if written authentication testimony is accepted, we do not extend that leniency so far as to make the writers simultaneously the judges receiving their own written testimony. We should not multiply rabbinic leniencies beyond their source. This supports the distinction drawn above. As for Maharsha, he writes in Ketubot that although a court that recognizes the signatures need not hear testimony, if the judges saw the signatures only at night the authentication is invalid because we cannot say that the rabbinic leniency of document authentication cures that defect. This requires explanation. The reason a witness who saw at night cannot ordinarily become a judge is that the nighttime seeing had the status of testimony rather than judicial seeing. But in rabbinic document authentication we do allow a witness to become a judge. Why then should nighttime recognition matter? Razah and Ramban indeed allow greater leniency. Perhaps Maharsha means that, even in authentication, some act of formal testimony is still required where the knowledge arose in a manner not fit for immediate adjudication. Seeing by day, when the judges could have judged immediately, can count like testimony already received. Seeing at night cannot. Therefore, if three judges recognized the signatures at night, two should formally testify before the third by day, then the roles can rotate as needed so that each has proper testimony before acting as judge. The rabbinic rule that a witness may later become a judge solves the role problem, but it does not erase the need for some formal act of testimony. This would explain Maharsha's statement that the general leniency of document authentication does not remove the problem. Still, he appears to be largely alone in this strict formulation, and I have not found broad support for it. The distinction also explains the phrase "a witness becomes a judge" found in other rabbinic settings. That phrase means that after a person has supplied testimony, he may later sit in judgment in a matter where Torah law would ordinarily bar the same person from occupying both roles. It does not necessarily mean that the same legal act counts simultaneously as testimony and adjudication. Rosh reads the authentication sugya with that temporal distinction in mind. Two people who are now speaking as witnesses do not automatically increase the number of judges receiving their testimony. Only after the testimony has been received can one ask whether they may subsequently function as judges. This is why one still needs a proper judicial framework for accepting the testimony. The special get procedure is different. There the agent's declaration is itself a rabbinically created substitute for ordinary authentication, and the two people before whom he delivers the get are allowed to serve within that uniquely constructed system. Chazal designed the mechanism as a unified process. One cannot infer from that special get enactment that ordinary civil authentication always permits witnesses and judges to merge in the same moment. This also gives force to Rosh's uniform three-judge requirement. If witnesses automatically became judges while testifying, the number of independent judges required would vary constantly depending on how many witnesses appeared. Sometimes one judge would suffice, sometimes two, sometimes three. Yet the halachah speaks consistently of authentication by a beit din of three. That uniformity indicates that the three-judge framework is institutional and is not ordinarily reduced merely because the people giving testimony are themselves eligible to judge later. Accordingly, the first opinion should not be dismissed as formalism. It preserves a coherent distinction between the act of giving authentication testimony and the act of judicially receiving and certifying it. The leniency that a witness may later become a judge remains intact. What is rejected is only simultaneous identity of the two roles within one act. This also explains the case of judges who themselves recognize the document witnesses' handwriting. There no testimony is being given at all. The judges are acting from their own judicial knowledge, and three are required because that is the institutional form of document authentication. The fact that two of them could have instead testified orally does not reduce the required court to one judge. The halachah therefore preserves a consistent structure: three judges authenticate, whether their knowledge comes from their own recognition or from testimony properly received. Rosh's view is thus not an isolated stringency but part of a coherent institutional model of authentication.
