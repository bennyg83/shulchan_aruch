He signed while healthy and afterward became mute, and so forth. This is the Tur’s wording, implying that one who has already become mute cannot sign a document. Its rationale appears to follow Rabbeinu Tam’s position cited above in siman 28, seif 15, that written testimony may be sent to beit din, and that only a mute, who is not fit to testify orally, is excluded. According to him there is no distinction between written testimony and a document. A document is valid because its witnesses are fit to testify orally. One who is not fit to testify may not even sign a document. According to the other authorities, however, including the Mechaber and Rama above in siman 28, seif 11, even a person who is not mute may not send written testimony to beit din. The Gemara’s reference to a mute as disqualified because testimony must be “from their mouths and not from their writing” is only an example, not an exclusive rule. Yet an ordinary signed document remains valid because it is not included in that exclusion. This makes the Mechaber’s and Rama’s unqualified adoption of the Tur’s ruling here difficult. According to their approach, the rule of “from their mouths and not from their writing” does not apply to a document, so even one who signed after becoming mute should be valid. According to the Rambam’s and Mechaber’s explanation there, that testimony in a document is accepted by rabbinic enactment so as not to close the door before borrowers, one can answer that the Sages did not validate a mute witness. Since the parties can execute the document before other healthy witnesses, they left the law at its Torah basis. But I wrote there that the primary view is that of Rashi, Tosafot, the Ramban, and the other authorities, that documentary testimony is valid by Torah law. Either once the witnesses wrote and delivered the document to the borrower the rule of “from their mouths and not from their writing” no longer applies, as the Rif writes in the fourth chapter of Yevamot, or because they wrote and signed at the borrower’s instruction, it is considered testimony from the borrower’s writing rather than from the witnesses’ writing, as Baal HaMaor and other authorities explain. Those rationales apply equally to a mute witness. Perhaps a strained distinction can be made, but the matter requires study.
[It is not possible to testify to his writings as a reference to what I have written above Article 12
Concerning his handwriting, meaning through his signature or a nod of his head. These are the Sema’s words. It appears to me that he was not precise. Why should the signature not be authenticated by comparison with another signature of his? Even the signature of an apostate or robber is authenticated by comparison, as the Rama writes above in seif 34. The Ritva’s reason, cited by the Beit Yosef in section 6, is that this is not regarded as testimony. The signature establishes itself. This is also evident from the Gemara and from seif 13 above, and the same applies here. The Rosh and Tur mean to exclude only his own written statement that this is his signature, or his confirmation by nodding, because that is prohibited by the rule, “from their mouths and not from their writing.” Authentication by comparison with his signature, however, is equivalent to authentication through others. Indeed, his signature can be used for comparison only if he signs before witnesses or before a beit din, whose members are believed. Where authentication through his signature is impossible, a written declaration by him does not help even though he can write. For example, if he signed while young, later aged, and his signature changed, or if comparison is impossible for some other reason, his written statement that the earlier signature is his is ineffective. This appears correct to me. Perhaps the Sema also means that he personally signs and thereby testifies that the earlier writing is his, not that the signatures are compared, but that reading is strained.
