Healthy and later mute. The Shach infers that if a witness is mute he cannot sign a document, apparently because a witness who cannot testify orally is excluded by "from their mouths and not from their writing." Yet according to the authorities who reject written testimony even from a healthy person while still accepting ordinary signed documents, the distinction between speech and writing cannot itself explain why a mute person is barred from signing a formal instrument. Nor does it explain why a mute witness cannot validate his own prior signature when written validation testimony is otherwise accepted rabbinically. Some later authorities attempted to answer that when a signed witness validates his own signature he is really testifying to the debt in the instrument, and a mute cannot give that substantive testimony. I do not find this persuasive, because the entire validation is rabbinic and written testimony is accepted for such rabbinic proof. The true explanation is that a mute person has a bodily testimonial disqualification derived from, "if he does not tell," excluding one unable to make oral declaration. This resembles a blind person or one who cannot hear. Even in a matter where actual oral declaration is not technically necessary, a person who lacks the bodily capacity required by the Torah is disqualified as a witness. The Gemara in Gittin makes this precise. It asks why a mute person cannot testify if he can communicate in writing, and answers that in testimony the Torah requires "from their mouths and not from their writing." The point is not merely that this particular testimony happens to be written. Rather, because his writing is not legally treated as oral declaration in testimony, he remains one "who cannot tell," and is therefore bodily disqualified. This explains why he cannot sign a document or perform other forms of testimony even where ordinary written documents are otherwise valid. It also explains why testimony seen while mute and given after recovery is beginning in disqualification. Thus the Tur's phrase, "he signed while healthy and later became mute," is exact. A person already mute at the time of signing was bodily disqualified at the beginning. As for the special messenger declaration in gittin, some authorities nevertheless allow a mute person to provide it in writing because that rule is a rabbinic enactment and other ordinarily disqualified persons can sometimes be accepted there. This is a separate leniency and does not undermine the general rule.
