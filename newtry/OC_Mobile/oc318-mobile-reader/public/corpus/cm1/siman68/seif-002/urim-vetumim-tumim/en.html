At first glance this is difficult, because the Gemara says that where the names are not clearly gentile, everyone agrees there is a concern of internal falsity. The difficulty is stronger because above, regarding a gift document, Rama rules that the document is invalid even when delivered before Jewish witnesses and does not distinguish recognizable gentile names. That follows the first Tanna, who decrees even recognizable names because of unrecognizable ones. Why then is the present evidentiary document treated more leniently? Rashi appears to understand Rava's Persian document similarly: ordinary gentile witnesses signed it, Jewish delivery witnesses were present, and no distinction is made based on recognizable names. The answer is that internal falsity is fundamentally a concern that the document itself is halachically worthless while appearing facially valid. In a gift document signed by gentiles, even if the gentiles tell the truth, the paper itself may be incapable of effecting the acquisition. The recipient is trying to acquire through an instrument that, under Torah law, is mere paper. That is the classic case of internal falsity. A loan-evidence document is different. A recognized gentile court document is accepted because those courts are presumed not to falsify their official records. If ordinary gentile witnesses sign and Jewish delivery witnesses testify that the transaction truly occurred, the Jewish witnesses establish the factual truthfulness that the recognized court would otherwise supply. Two Jewish witnesses are at least as good as an official foreign-court authentication for proving that the gentile signatories did not fabricate. Once their truthfulness is established, the document returns to the category of a valid evidentiary instrument, not mere pottery. This also answers Maharsha's question on Tosafot. Tosafot say the Sages invalidate a document signed by ordinary gentiles even with Jewish delivery witnesses because they fear people will later validate similar papers without delivery witnesses, relying on the rule that recognized foreign-court documents are valid. R. Akiva does not make that decree. Maharsha asks why Tosafot did not explain that the dispute is whether R. Elazar's doctrine of delivery witnesses applies to ordinary monetary documents. The answer is that even if delivery witnesses are not the constitutive witnesses of the document, they still testify that the underlying transaction is truthful. That factual testimony removes the only defect separating ordinary gentile signatories from reliable foreign-court certification. Thus the issue is rabbinic decree, not whether delivery witnesses formally "effect" the document. This also explains why, according to many authorities who do not rule like R. Elazar generally in monetary documents, the Gemara can still ask that the document should collect from purchasers. The Jewish delivery witnesses establish that the foreign signatories did not lie; the instrument can then receive the same evidentiary status as a recognized foreign-court document. Rama's stricter rule for gift documents remains consistent because there the defect is not merely possible lying. Even truthful gentile signatures may be incapable of creating the halachic acquisition, so the paper remains intrinsically defective. The distinction is therefore clear.
And some say this applies only where the witnesses' names are unmistakably gentile, and so forth. Shach wonders why Rama omitted Rosh's opinion that even unmistakably gentile names are decreed because of names that are not unmistakably gentile, following the first Tanna against R. Shimon. The difficulty is stronger because above, regarding a gift document, Rama rules that even delivery before Jewish witnesses does not validate it and does not distinguish recognizable names, and in Even HaEzer siman 130 Mechaber likewise rules stringently.
Therefore, the matter requires clarification. Rosh writes in the first chapter of Gittin that a gift instrument made in a gentile court, where royal law does not itself create the transfer, is mere paper even if delivered before Jewish witnesses. He explains that if such documents were accepted, the donor might later deliver them before gentiles, thinking that just as gentile signatures can sometimes be relied upon, so can gentile oral witnesses. Rosh adds that official gentile judges may be reliable in writing but not necessarily in oral testimony, and even unmistakably gentile names are decreed because of ordinary names. Several points in Rosh need explanation. Why did he choose the concern that the donor may later use gentiles as delivery witnesses? The simpler concern is that a later beit din might see the document elsewhere, assume the signatories are Jewish, presume it was properly delivered before valid witnesses, and rule on a worthless instrument. Further, if a gentile-signed gift document is intrinsically incapable of effecting a halachic transfer, why does it matter whether the gentiles would lie orally? Even truthful oral testimony should not be stronger than the invalid written instrument if they are not valid halachic witnesses. The answer depends on the underlying theory of gentile testimony. Rashi and several authorities indicate that gentiles are not inherently disqualified from all factual testimony by Torah law where their truthfulness is established. Their principal defect in gittin and certain formal documents is that they are not part of the legal institution that effects the act. In a gift deed where the signatures themselves create the acquisition, gentiles cannot serve as the constitutive halachic agents or witnesses through whom the written kinyan operates. The paper is therefore intrinsically defective even if the facts stated are true. Delivery witnesses are different. They do not themselves create the recipient's ownership through some independent gentile legal capacity. The recipient acquires through the donor's delivery, while the witnesses attest to that delivery. If reliable gentiles could serve as factual witnesses in such a setting, truthful oral testimony might have force. Rosh therefore needed to say that even recognized gentile officials are not trusted in oral testimony in the same way as in their formal written records. Otherwise the concern of later delivery before them could actually produce a valid factual proof. This also explains Rashi's emphasis on the possibility that a person will mistakenly deliver before gentiles. Where the gentile names are unmistakable, local people know they are not Jewish and are less likely to make that error. R. Shimon can therefore be lenient in that setting. There is a second possible concern, emphasized by Rashba: a later court may mistake the signatories for Jews and presume valid delivery. Unmistakable gentile names may not remove that concern universally because names vary by country. A name obviously gentile in one region may also be borne by Jews elsewhere. Thus different authorities weigh the two concerns differently. In a document written by ordinary gentiles rather than a recognized court, everyone is stricter. There is no official presumption of truthfulness at all. Even unmistakably gentile names do not help, because the underlying facts may be false and no normal chazakah of proper delivery can be built upon the paper. A recognized foreign-court document is different. Once we know the court does not falsify its records, the factual transaction is established. If the only remaining concern is that someone might later use gentiles as oral delivery witnesses, unmistakably gentile names can remove that confusion. This is why Riaz, cited by Shiltei HaGibborim, can rule that a get executed in a recognized gentile court with unmistakably gentile names is valid under the appropriate delivery-witness framework, following R. Shimon. Rosh, however, extends the decree more broadly and follows the first Tanna. Now return to Rama. Above, in the gift-document case, Rama rules strictly even with recognizable gentile names because a gift deed is a constitutive acquisition document. If its halachic creation is defective, the paper is intrinsically mere pottery. There Chazal were concerned enough to decree even recognizable names because of unrecognizable ones. Here, however, we are discussing an evidentiary loan document. If the foreign witnesses are reliable and Jewish delivery witnesses establish the truth of the transaction, the document is not intrinsically worthless. The concern is only that the gentile evidence may be false or later misunderstood. That is a much weaker concern. Therefore Rama can omit Rosh's stringency here and accept the view that unmistakably gentile names suffice in an evidentiary document, while remaining stringent in gift documents. This also explains Tosafot's distinction between the Sages and R. Akiva. The dispute is not fundamentally whether R. Elazar's rule of delivery witnesses applies to monetary documents. Even if delivery witnesses are not the constitutive witnesses, two valid Jewish witnesses can still establish that the foreign signatories stated the truth. Once truthfulness is established, the evidentiary document can be treated like one certified by a recognized foreign court. The Sages nevertheless fear that people will later rely on similar foreign documents without Jewish delivery witnesses. R. Akiva does not extend the decree so far. Rosh's position is connected to his broader understanding that official gentile written testimony may enjoy a reliability not granted to their oral testimony. According to the mainstream halachah, however, gentiles are generally invalid as witnesses by Torah law, and foreign-court documents stand through a specific rabbinic recognition of the court record. On that framework, the difference between constitutive gift documents and evidentiary loan documents is especially strong.
And after clarifying Rosh's position, Rama's rulings are consistent: in gift documents, even recognizable gentile names do not cure the intrinsic defect; in evidentiary documents supported by valid Jewish delivery testimony, unmistakable names can remove the remaining concern.
