An instrument whose witnesses are Cutheans. The difficulty is obvious, because above in seif 1 the Mechaber ruled concerning instruments of gifts and acknowledgments that even if they were delivered in the presence of Jewish witnesses they are invalid, because they are internally defective. The Tumim raises this question as well. It appears that the Mechaber holds that the internal defect here is only a rabbinic disqualification, arising from a decree lest one come to rely on such witnesses in another instrument without witnesses to delivery, as the Sema writes in seif katan 20. It is therefore no worse than an antedated instrument, from which, according to the Mechaber in siman 43, one may collect from unencumbered property. Although an instrument susceptible to forgery is not considered an instrument even with respect to a claim of payment, this necessarily reflects a distinction between an antedated instrument and one susceptible to forgery. An instrument susceptible to forgery can itself produce a defect and loss to the debtor even with respect to unencumbered property, and is therefore invalid even against such property. An antedated instrument, by contrast, is invalid only because of the decree concerning collection from encumbered property, and remains valid regarding a claim of payment. Thus, the instrument discussed here, which is invalid only because of a decree concerning another instrument, as the Sema writes in seif katan 20, is no worse than an antedated instrument and is valid against unencumbered property. A gift instrument is different. Since the instrument is at least formally invalid because it is internally defective, land cannot be acquired through it, for any instrument bearing a legal disqualification is unfit to effect acquisition. There is another reason as well. Where the instrument requires authentication, even when collecting from heirs or in the debtor's absence, there is no concern that people will come to rely upon witnesses whose names are not recognizably non-Jewish under the mistaken impression that they are Jews. Once the authenticating witnesses recognize the signatures, they will also recognize the witnesses themselves and know that they are non-Jews. The Pnei Yehoshua raises this point regarding gittin brought from overseas, and the Rashba similarly asks how it is possible for witnesses to stand before them and not recognize whether they are non-Jews. This differs from a get, through which a woman may marry in Eretz Yisrael without authentication, and even overseas when the husband himself delivers the get to her. Likewise, with a gift instrument there is a concern that the donor may admit the instrument that he wrote, the beit din will thereby establish the recipient's ownership, and a defect may later arise with respect to a creditor of the donor after the beit din has already established the recipient's possession and issued a ruling that the field belongs to him, when in truth it does not belong to him because he did not acquire it through a valid instrument. Alternatively, because of the donor's error, the recipient might occupy the land for three years and thereby create further complications. In such cases there is proper concern that one may come to rely upon these witnesses. The “instruments of acknowledgment” mentioned in seif 1 as invalid even with witnesses to delivery must therefore concern instruments creating an obligation through the mechanism of odaita, where the acquisition through written acknowledgment is void without valid witnesses to delivery and can thus generate a defect. This is clear as well from the fact that seif 1 applies even regarding a claim that the event never occurred. If the signatures are disregarded, the presence of witnesses to delivery alone would prevent such a claim of jesting. It must therefore refer to an odaita that creates an obligation.
Distinctively non-Jewish names. See Tumim, who cites the Rosh regarding a gift instrument and writes that it is invalid even when delivered in the presence of Jewish witnesses, because we are concerned that one may deliver it in the presence of the non-Jewish signatories themselves, mistakenly reasoning that just as they are believed when they sign, so too they are believed to witness delivery. This is incorrect, because the reason they are trusted not to sign falsely is that they will not compromise their professional standing, whereas in oral testimony they may compromise themselves and lie. The Tumim asks why the Rosh needed this reason. Even if they testify truthfully, a gift requires acquisition through an instrument, and just as their signatures do not effect acquisition, delivery before them should not effect it either. He answers that the Rosh holds that non-Jews are legally capable of testimony, but just as they are invalid for a get because they are not participants in the law of writing, so too they are not valid witnesses to delivery. Likewise, because a non-Jew does not acquire by means of a Jewish legal instrument, they are not participants in the laws of such instruments. Therefore, if the donor himself delivers the instrument before a non-Jewish court and the officials are otherwise valid witnesses, the acquisition is effective. I do not understand this. What difference is there between witnesses to signature and witnesses to delivery? Witnesses to delivery likewise operate through the legal force of an instrument, and non-Jews are not participants in the laws of Jewish instruments. It therefore appears that the principal reason why a gift instrument executed by non-Jews is ineffective, even according to those who hold that non-Jews are legally capable of testimony, is as follows. Even testimony in writing by Jews is ordinarily invalid as testimony from writing. A valid instrument is an exception derived from the verse, “and I took the deed of purchase,” and the document must be written in the formal mode of a legal instrument analogous to that deed of purchase, as Tosafot explain in Ketubot. Tosafot in Bava Batra further write that the derivation of “and I took the deed of purchase” is stated specifically regarding Jews. According to the Rosh, therefore, the reason an instrument signed by non-Jews is invalid is that the law of “and I took the deed of purchase” was written regarding Jews. Consequently, testimony written in a non-Jewish instrument has no legal substance. When the non-Jews are oral witnesses to delivery, however, their testimony is full oral testimony and is effective like the testimony of Jewish witnesses to delivery. The Rosh holds that a non-Jew can acquire through an instrument, and the written instrument is invalid only because the signatures constitute written testimony. When there are witnesses to delivery, that defect does not exist. The Rosh therefore needed to add that in oral testimony non-Jews may compromise themselves and lie.
