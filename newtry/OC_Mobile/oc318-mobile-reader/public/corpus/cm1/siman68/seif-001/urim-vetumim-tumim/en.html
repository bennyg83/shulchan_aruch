A document written in any language, etc. The Gemara and all the authorities make clear that documents may be valid in every language and script, whether Hebrew or another language. Tosafos in Gittin 10b initially appears to imply that a dispositive document, such as "my field is given to you," would be invalid if written and signed by non Jews even where their names are unmistakably non Jewish. But that cannot be the law. The Gemara later states that even a get may be valid in any script and language, and Maharam of Lublin already raised this difficulty. The correct explanation of Tosafos is different. The Gemara discusses a Persian document delivered before Jewish witnesses and asks that a valid document must be written on material that cannot be altered and must return to its subject in the final line. Rosh and Maharsha ask why the same objections were not raised against the Mishnah concerning documents executed in non Jewish courts. The answer is that according to Tosafos, such court documents are invalid by Torah law because the signatories are not valid witnesses; the Sages nevertheless validated them where we know the officials tell the truth. Once their entire validity is rabbinic, it is unnecessary to pile on additional technical invalidities such as failure to return to the subject in the final line or use of alterable material. The later ruling concerning a Persian private document, however, is presented as ordinary law rather than a special enactment, so the Gemara properly demands all formal requirements. This is also the meaning of the Ittur, who says that the Gemara's objection is directed to Rav Papa's case rather than to the Mishnah of governmental courts. Rashba challenges him, but according to Tosafos's approach the distinction is sound. Thus when Tosafos says that one cannot explain the gift document as involving non Jewish writing and signatures, the concern is not the nationality of the writers as such. Rather, non Jewish court practice commonly omitted the formal safeguards against alteration and the proper conclusion. A court evidentiary document can be validated by rabbinic enactment despite such formal defects, but a gift document that itself effects the acquisition cannot at the initial stage be treated as a complete dispositive instrument if it is written in a form susceptible to alteration. The delivery witnesses do not cure that defect. This is the intent of Tosafos, and many erred in understanding it otherwise.
Who are not known to accept bribes, etc. Rif and Rambam follow their view that the validity of governmental court documents rests upon the presumption that the officials do not lie. If they are known to accept bribes, that presumption disappears. According to the many authorities who instead rely upon dina d'malchuta dina where the king has decreed that such documents have legal force, the matter is different. If the sovereign orders that instruments certified by this official are binding, then whether the official in fact lies or accepts bribes does not alter the civil effect of the royal enactment. The king has power over property and can decree that such certification governs. Therefore, in countries where official court or notarial instruments are given binding force by law, we rely upon them even if some officials are known to accept bribes, because their force derives from dina d'malchuta rather than merely from a personal presumption of honesty.
Then they collect, etc. Rambam cites his teachers that this applies only to sale documents, while a loan document executed in a non Jewish court is invalid even if the transfer of the money was witnessed. Rambam disagrees and holds that it collects from unencumbered property. Raavad approves the teachers' view: since such a document cannot collect from encumbered property and has no publicity, the borrower may say that he repaid it, just as with a handwritten note. The Mechaber follows Rambam because Ittur, Rashba, and Ramban also appear to support him. The Shach correctly observes that those authorities do not prove the point, because they hold that such documents collect even from encumbered property; under their view the borrower plainly cannot claim repayment. Their position therefore does not resolve Raavad's argument for a document collecting only from unencumbered property. Nevertheless, Rambam's position appears supported by the Gemara in Gittin 11, where Rava says that a Persian document delivered before Jewish witnesses collects from unencumbered property because it lacks publicity. That must mean the borrower cannot say, "I repaid." One cannot reduce the rule merely to preventing a claim that the loan never occurred, because the Jewish delivery witnesses already prove the transaction. Nor can the Gemara's later objection about failure to return to the matter of the document be understood if the document only prevents denial of the transaction; a witnessed oral loan already does that. The ruling must mean that the document has enough force to prevent a claim of repayment even though it lacks sufficient publicity to bind purchasers. It is difficult to reconcile this with Raavad. Perhaps he distinguishes between a private Persian document deliberately delivered before Jewish witnesses and a governmental court document. In the former case, the debtor's effort to arrange delivery before valid witnesses shows that the paper was intended to remain as proof until payment; otherwise the witnesses alone would have sufficed. The borrower therefore knows that he must not pay without obtaining the document. A court document, by contrast, may have been created only to prevent denial of the original loan, and the borrower may not understand that it remains necessary after payment. This distinction is forced, but I see no better reconciliation. Practical law therefore appears to follow Rambam that the borrower cannot claim repayment. The Yerushalmi supplies some support for Rambam's teachers. It discusses a document from Beit She'an whose witnesses were non Jews. Rabbi Yochanan invalidated it while Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi validated it so that lenders would not be discouraged. The case must concern a governmental court document; otherwise the Mishnah explicitly invalidates it, and the concern of discouraging lending would make little sense. Since the halachah generally follows Rabbi Yochanan, one could infer that he rejected such loan documents. Still, the Bavli remains the stronger basis for Rambam's ruling.
From unencumbered property. This appears to refer even to sale documents. A sale before Jewish witnesses has publicity and may bind purchasers, but a document from a non Jewish court has no comparable Jewish publicity and therefore does not collect from encumbered property. One might ask that if the signatories have non Jewish names that are not distinctive, the document should be considered internally forged because later readers may mistakenly treat them as Jewish witnesses and collect from purchasers. But since the document bears the formal certification of the non Jewish court, the signatories' status is apparent from its context. More fundamentally, internal forgery concerns arise where the document is invalid in substance and may masquerade as valid. Here it is valid for its proper purpose, namely collection from unencumbered property. The fact that one must limit its collection does not render it internally forged.
And similarly documents of admission, etc. Commentators ask why instruments of admission, waiver, and compromise should not be valid in governmental courts, since such documents serve only as evidence. Rambam appears to follow Tosafos's basic premise that even court documents are invalid by Torah law because non Jews are not valid witnesses, and their validity is only a rabbinic enactment. The Gemara's question from gift documents then required him to define the scope of the enactment. He did not accept the strained explanation that the Sages validated one type of instrument but inexplicably not another. Rather, the Gemara's phrase that a gift document is "mere pottery" means that such an instrument contains numerous legal questions that foreign officials are not competent to evaluate according to Torah law. In a gift there may be conditions, coercion, asmachta, a hidden gift, a defective order of act and condition, and many other distinctions. Their official certification may record accurately what occurred and yet not establish the legal effect under Torah law. The same concern applies to documents of compromise, waiver, and admission. Statements may be conditioned, spoken only to appease another, made in jest, or subject to other legal qualifications. The non Jewish officials may record the words truthfully but their record does not establish the halachic legal effect. Rambam therefore groups such instruments with dispositive gifts.
And conversely, if it is invalid according to our law, etc. The Sema cites the Tur that dina d'malchuta can validate their formal instruments but not create modes of acquisition unknown to Torah law. He connects this to Ramban, but there is no real dispute here. Ramban speaks of substantive acquisitions, such as failure to transfer movable property agav land or failure to use the words required to transfer documents. The king's law does not ordinarily transform such ineffective Torah acquisitions into halachic acquisitions. Otherwise, one would uproot the civil law of the Torah entirely. The Rosh's responsum, which the Rama presents as a dissenting view, concerns something else: the interpretation of language customarily used in local instruments. In matters of ordinary drafting, we follow established commercial usage under the general rule that customary language has binding meaning. This is stronger still where royal law has fixed the accepted formulation. But that does not mean that a mode of acquisition that Torah law does not recognize becomes effective merely because the local legal system recognizes it. The Tur therefore could quote Ramban without noting any contradiction from the Rosh. Maharik, in the relevant responsum, likewise discusses interpretive rules such as construing an ambiguity against the holder of the document. The Rosh discusses customary language. They are not necessarily disputing the same issue. Indeed, the Rosh's own discussion of the Gemara's requirement that a document be written on material that cannot be forged shows that he did not assume royal law cures every substantive halachic defect. If he had, the Gemara's question from foreign court documents would disappear. There may, however, be a different dispute. Maharik cites a proof that dina d'malchuta does not override the Torah requirements that a document be resistant to alteration and properly concluded. This follows Rashba, Ramban, and Rosh, who understand the Gemara's objection as applying even to governmental court documents. Rambam, by contrast, as Maggid Mishneh explains, apparently validates such official documents even when their form would ordinarily be susceptible to alteration and lacking the standard final return. The Rama and Ittur also appear to accept this. Under that view, where the Sages or royal law validate the official instrument, certain formal document rules can be displaced. But even Rambam does not mean that every substantive Torah acquisition is replaced by royal civil law. That would abolish Torah monetary law, Heaven forbid. The special validation concerns the form and evidentiary force of official instruments, not the entire law of acquisition.
