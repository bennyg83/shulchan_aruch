(Shulchan Aruch, seif 1) Concerning instruments of acknowledgment stating that so-and-so acknowledged before the gentile court that he owed another: some explain that the obligor may say that he spoke jokingly before the court, and that even his instruction to record the statement was part of the joke. This explanation is unsatisfactory, for whenever the gentile official is legally qualified to testify, his testimony should be treated like that of a Jew. In my opinion, the reason these instruments are ineffective is that foreign law and Jewish law differ in many respects. The officials may write that a statement constituted an acknowledgment of liability even though, according to our law, the acknowledgment has no legal effect. The same applies to waivers and compromises: they may regard particular words as a waiver when Jewish law does not. Accordingly, if the instrument records the actual words, “So-and-so said to so-and-so,” the officials are believed as to the words, and we adjudicate them as though spoken before us. This explains why the Tur and Mechaber formulate the rule as an instrument stating that a person “acknowledged before us that he owes him,” language that otherwise seems redundant. The Sema says this excludes a record that he acknowledged having been paid. In my opinion, however, the point is that where the officials merely certify their own legal conclusion that he acknowledged, rather than recording his precise words, perhaps no valid acknowledgment occurred and they only construed the words as such. The same applies to a waiver or compromise. Even a certificate that he acknowledged receipt is therefore ineffective, because they may have inferred that conclusion from his words. Delivery before Jewish witnesses does not cure it, for the document is internally defective when the parties know that the officials write according to their own legal understanding. A loan document that expressly records the transfer of money is different, because they are believed as to the occurrence of that act. Even there, the Rambam requires it to state that they saw the money delivered, lest they have erred about some other matter that, in their view, created the debt. This appears to be the correct explanation of the Rambam.
