The Gemara asks: the Mishnah states this generally, whether sale or gift. A sale can work because the buyer already gave money, but how can a gift be acquired by a document signed by gentiles? Such a document should be mere pottery. This is difficult according to Sefer HaChochmah, cited by Mordechai, who explains the validity of gentile court documents by saying their judges will not damage their own reputation through falsehood. Moshe and Aharon are disqualified as relatives even though completely trustworthy, which shows that Torah law does not require only Jewish witnesses in every monetary context. Any persons publicly established as trustworthy can, in this view, serve as witnesses. Rashi likewise seems to accept the basic principle. He explains that gentile signed gittin are invalid because gentiles are not participants in the law of divorce, while in civil law they were commanded. Why then does the Gemara call a gentile gift document mere pottery? The answer follows Rabbeinu Tam. Tosafot in Bava Batra and Kiddushin says that land is acquired by deed through the verse in Jeremiah, "I took the deed of acquisition," a Jewish law of documentary transfer. Gentiles do not acquire land through deeds. Their acquisitions are primarily through money. Therefore a gentile court document can be perfectly valid as evidence but still fail as the actual mode of acquisition for a gift. This is exactly like a get signed by trustworthy gentiles. Their truthfulness does not help because they are not participants in the specific legal mechanism. Thus gentile judges can serve evidentiary functions in monetary law, yet their document cannot constitute the special Jewish mode of acquiring a gift through a deed.
