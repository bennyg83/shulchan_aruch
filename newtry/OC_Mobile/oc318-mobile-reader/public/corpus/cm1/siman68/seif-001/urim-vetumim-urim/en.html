And they know how to read it, etc. See above, siman 45, whether it is valid after the fact when they did not know how to read it and relied on others. See there, and see Tumim.
All documents whose signatories are non Jews. This means documents whose very substance effects the acquisition, such as where one writes, "My field is given to you," and title is acquired by delivery of the document, or in a sale where he writes, "My field is sold to you," and the document itself effects the transfer. For this, a non Jewish court is ineffective, because they have no legal capacity to effect the transfer and are neither valid witnesses nor parties to a halachic document.
Except documents of buying and selling. This refers to a case where he paid money and acquired the field through the money, while the document serves only as evidence, and likewise to a loan. Even such a document is invalid by Torah law, since they are not valid witnesses, but the Sages validated it for the orderly functioning of society. So write Tosafot. See Tumim, seif katan 1.
Provided that it was made in a recognized court, etc. According to the Rambam and the Mechaber, the procedure for documents issued by a non Jewish court is as follows: ordinary non Jewish witnesses sign the document, and afterward they come before the judge who heads the court. He investigates whether the facts are true and authenticates the legal effect of their document. Therefore one must establish both that the witnesses to the document and the judge are presumed trustworthy, for these are two distinct matters, the witnesses on the document on their own and the judge who authenticates it on his own.
And then collection is made from unencumbered property. Even in a sale, although we rule that one who sells his field in the presence of witnesses creates a lien collectible from encumbered property, this is inferior, because in this view there is no publicity at all from such courts. See Tumim.
From unencumbered property. Nevertheless, the debtor cannot claim that he paid. Since it has the legal status of a document, he would not leave it in the lender's possession after payment. It is not comparable to a handwritten acknowledgment. See Tumim, where I brought proof for this from the Gemara.
And a register, etc. Where the practice is that the officials record in their ledger everything brought before them, that record also has the force of a document, because once this is the established practice they are precise in what they record. It appears that in such a case, according to all opinions, one cannot collect from encumbered property, for even among Jews, when a scribe writes a copy of a document, until the document is delivered to the party it has no status of a document for purposes of encumbered property.
For ordinarily recognized courts do not accept bribes. The Sema writes that according to this view, witnesses sign a document only in the presence of the court official. Accordingly, one need not know that the signatories themselves are presumed trustworthy, for if the matter were not true, the court official, who is presumed trustworthy, would not permit falsehood to be recorded. It would follow that where the custom is as the Mechaber describes, that the witnesses sign first and afterward come to the court to authenticate the matter, one must also establish the witnesses as trustworthy. In truth, his words are not compelling. Although it is true that according to the Rosh the procedure of such courts is as the Sema describes, there is no practical difference. Even if it was done as the Mechaber describes, the court official does not authenticate and certify the force of their testimony unless he himself knows that the underlying matter is true and correct. Thus they testify truthfully.
Nevertheless, for false testimony, etc. See Tumim. It appears that if their documents are validated by virtue of the law of the kingdom, because the king commanded that documents be made specifically according to their legal system, then even if those officials accept bribes the document may be upheld, because this is the king's command and the king has authority over property.
Such as, "My field is given to you." The same applies to a sale where a person sells a field because of its poor quality and the principal acquisition is through the document, "My field is sold to you." Here too it is ineffective. Sema, and this is clear.
He admitted before us that he owes him. This admission requires valid witnesses. Since these are not valid witnesses, it is as though he admitted privately between himself and the other party, which is ineffective even if both presently agree that the admission occurred. Therefore, where witnesses are not required for the admission, as explained below in siman 81, a document made in their court is valid according to all opinions.
Or documents of waivers. The Mechaber omitted this, because waiver requires neither an act of acquisition nor witnesses, and witnesses are needed only to prevent false denial by the party who waived. Since these officials are presumed not to lie, the document should suffice. The later authorities labored greatly to reconcile the contrary view and wrote that the case is one where he said, "By this document I waive the debt to you," so that if there is no valid document there is no waiver. See Tumim. I wrote that the Rambam holds that whenever validity depends upon wording rather than an objective act, such as counting money and similar matters, numerous legal distinctions arise and the officials may have erred, perhaps using wording that was legally ineffective. The laws governing such formulations are very numerous. See there.
And they were delivered, etc. Even if the non Jewish signatories have unmistakably non Jewish names, so that they will not be confused with Jews, the document is nevertheless invalid because it is internally defective. We decree in the case of unmistakable names because of names that are not unmistakable, lest people think the signatories are Jews and come to rely upon them. See Tumim for what I wrote from seif 2, where according to all opinions we do not make such a decree.
Therefore, a shechiv mera who instructed, etc. The words of a shechiv mera are treated as written and delivered, and they are effective even privately without witnesses. Testimony is needed only against false denial, and these officials are presumed not to lie. The Rama writes "therefore," implying that according to the first opinion this is ineffective. This follows his approach, since he added to the first opinion documents of waiver, which he holds are ineffective, and the same would apply to a will. The Mechaber, however, omitted documents of waiver above because he holds that in any case effective privately between the parties, all agree that such officials are believed. Accordingly, even the first opinion agrees that the will of a shechiv mera is effective in their court. Therefore, although the Mechaber rules here without qualification like the first opinion, in siman 356, seif 32, he rules explicitly and without dispute that a will made in such a court is effective. He follows his own position. According to the Rama, however, the matter appears to remain disputed.
And likewise in the reverse case, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that where validity depends upon halachic law, for example if they failed to write that movable property is acquired together with land, or failed to include the standard clause extending the lien to all relevant obligations, their law does not help according to any opinion. This has nothing to do with the law of the kingdom. For that principle applies only where the king commanded that no document be effective except through the official courts. If the king merely allows either a document valid under our law with Jewish witnesses or one made by their officials, the law of the kingdom is irrelevant. Certainly the king did not command Jews not to write the halachic clauses concerning movable property acquired with land or the standard obligations of a document. We therefore follow our own law. Likewise, if the wording of their documents differs from our usual formula, all agree that it can be valid, for this falls under interpreting ordinary contractual language according to common usage. The real dispute concerns defects such as a document that can be forged, or the manner in which a returned document is handled, and similar matters in which their officials cannot readily observe Jewish documentary rules because they are unaccustomed to them, while the king commanded that they prepare such documents. In those cases I concluded that there is a dispute whether the document is valid. See there.
