See Sema, seif katan 5, who writes that for this reason the authority cited there requires Jewish witnesses to testify that the non-Jewish witnesses who signed the document are presumed trustworthy, and likewise regarding the official courts. At first sight this is difficult. If so, even ordinary non-Jewish witnesses should be acceptable whenever Jewish witnesses authenticate their signatures. The official court itself knows nothing of the underlying transaction. It merely authenticates the signatures of private witnesses. If so, authentication by Jews of the private witnesses' signatures should be superior to authentication by the official court. The matter is explained, however, in Tur. An official court would not authenticate the signatures unless it knew the witnesses themselves to be trustworthy. Thus, the primary reliance is upon the official court. The Jewish witnesses testify only that they know of no reason to suspect the officials of taking bribes, but the Jewish witnesses do not necessarily know the character of the non-Jewish witnesses themselves. The officials, by contrast, know the character of their non-Jewish colleagues, and had those witnesses not acquired a reputation for reliability, the officials would not have authenticated their signatures.
From unencumbered property. See Shach, seif katan 3, who questions what the Beit Yosef wrote, that we rule in accordance with the Rambam, meaning that the debtor is not believed to claim that he paid, because the Rashba and the Rosh agree with him. The Shach objects that they are discussing a different matter. Their discussion concerns instruments executed in a non-Jewish court, where the underlying transaction itself was effected before that court, and they hold that such an instrument may be collected even from encumbered property. In that case it is certainly true that the debtor cannot claim that he paid. By contrast, according to the Rambam, who allows collection only from unencumbered property, it is possible that the debtor may nevertheless claim that he paid, in accordance with the reasoning of the Raavad. The Tumim questions the Raavad from seif 2, concerning an instrument executed by ordinary non-Jews and delivered in the presence of Jewish witnesses to delivery, where collection is only from unencumbered property and yet the debtor cannot claim that he paid. This is no difficulty. There, the document has the status of a valid instrument, since it was delivered before witnesses to delivery. It is merely invalidated as internally defective because of a rabbinic decree. It is therefore no worse than an antedated debt instrument, regarding which, according to the Mechaber in siman 43, the debtor cannot claim that he paid. Even according to the view that invalidates it there, that is because this particular instrument contains a defect that could enable improper collection from purchasers. Here, however, the decree is only because of concern for a different instrument, as the Sema writes in seif katan 20. Moreover, since this document bears the legal character of an instrument, the debtor is not believed to claim payment, as the Shach writes in siman 69, seif katan 14. By contrast, an instrument executed in a non-Jewish court has no inherent status of a Jewish legal instrument at all, because non-Jews are not participants in the laws of Jewish instruments, as the Tumim himself writes in seif katan 8. The Sages merely accorded it credibility regarding the underlying transaction, and therefore the debtor may claim that he paid. I will write another resolution below. As for the Tumim's question that, where the witnesses bear names that are not recognizably non-Jewish, the instrument should be considered internally defective because it could be used to collect from encumbered property, this too is no difficulty. It is still no worse than an antedated instrument in siman 43, where there is likewise a concern that encumbered property might be seized improperly, yet collection from unencumbered property remains valid.

And compromises. See Sema, seif katan 13, who writes that compromise is called adjudication and that they are disqualified from adjudicating a legal case. At first glance this is difficult, because if so, all persons who are disqualified from adjudication, such as robbers, should also be disqualified from effecting a compromise. In the Perishah, however, the matter is explained more fully. Specifically with regard to non-Jews, whose adjudication is ineffective even if the litigants accepted them upon themselves, a kinyan likewise does not validate a compromise made through them. This is not true of other persons who are disqualified from serving as judges.
Instruments of waiver. See Sema, seif katan 14, who writes that the case is one where, in this instrument, the creditor writes that he waives the debt. In Shach, seif katan 8 in the gloss, he objects that in such a case even the dissenting opinion agrees that it is valid. It appears that the Sema means a case where the creditor said, “Write and deliver an instrument of waiver.” In that case non-Jews are certainly invalid, because there is no agency for a non-Jew. Even according to the Rif, cited by the Shach in siman 39, seif katan 13, that it is not necessary for the instrument actually to reach the recipient, the Rif explains that this is because the witnesses acquire it on his behalf through their signatures, and non-Jews are not legally capable of acquiring on another's behalf. However, the Sema's further words are difficult. He writes that, according to this, we must say that the case is one where the Jew writes and signs the waiver in a non-Jewish court, and the court authenticates it, even though his signature is not independently recognizable. Certainly, when the signature is that of a Jew, the authentication by the non-Jewish court is merely evidence, and according to all opinions it is believed. Nor did the Perishah explain the case this way. There is also no necessity for the Sema's introductory words, “according to this, we must say,” and his phrase “even though his signature is not recognizable” is likewise difficult, because the words “even though” imply that all the more so it would be worthless when his signature is recognizable. He should instead have said that the case is one where his signature is not recognizable. It therefore appears that there is a textual error in the Sema, and it should read: “According to this, we must say that the Rama in the gloss is discussing a case where the Jew writes,” and so forth. The meaning is that when the waiver itself is effected through the instrument, the Rama's statement cannot be understood in the manner suggested above. The Sema therefore explains that the Rama's gloss concerns a case where the Jew himself writes and signs it. In such a case everyone agrees that it is effective, and the dissenting opinion disputes only instruments of acknowledgment. The Rama's concluding words, “and likewise instruments of waiver,” do not refer back to the dissenting opinion at all. Rather, they refer back to the Rama's earlier statement concerning a gift, where everyone likewise agrees, because there too the document serves only as evidence, as explained.
