Documents Made in Non-Jewish Courts. Two seifim. A document written in any language or script is valid and permits collection from encumbered property if it follows Jewish documentary law, cannot be falsified, added to, or reduced, and its Jewish witnesses can read it. Documents signed by non-Jews are generally invalid, except sale documents and debt documents, provided the money was delivered before them and the document states that a specified person counted out to another a specified purchase price or loan, and provided it was made in their official court. A document made merely at an assembly of their magistrates without authentication by their judge has no effect, even if the court already ruled upon it and placed the buyer in possession. Jewish witnesses must identify the non-Jewish signatories and the judge who authenticated them as persons not known to accept bribes. Such a document permits collection from unencumbered property. If any requirement is missing, it is worthless. {Rama: The court's official register has the status of its document. Some say ordinary courts are presumed not to accept bribes and that documents made before them permit collection even from encumbered property without Jewish delivery witnesses or the judge's authentication. Courts do not compromise their standing by lying about testimony, even if rumored to accept bribes to distort judgments. The document need not state that they saw the money delivered; we presume they did. One or two non-Jewish witnesses suffice when the act occurred before the judge. A document written by an appointed scribe at the judge's direction but outside his presence is ineffective, unless the law of the kingdom validates a royal scribe's document.} Gift documents in which the instrument itself creates the transfer, admission documents, compromises made through their witnesses, and release documents are generally worthless even if all the above requirements are met and delivery occurred before Jewish witnesses. {Rama: Some say admission documents have the same law as loan documents, and release documents made in court are valid. A gift already acquired by formal acquisition or possession is valid when the court document merely records the admission. Therefore, a deathly ill person's will made before a non-Jewish court is effective, as explained below in siman 253, seif 32. Wherever royal law requires all matters to be documented in court, all court documents, even gift documents, are valid under the law of the kingdom; certainly they are valid where custom already recognizes them (Rivash, simanim 493 and 478). See below, siman 369. A document validated through the law of the kingdom must comply with their law; if improperly written under their law, it is invalid even if proper under ours, because we cannot validate it more broadly than they do. Conversely, some validate a document invalid under our law but valid under theirs; others disagree. See also simanim 51, 56, 80, 142, and 205.}
