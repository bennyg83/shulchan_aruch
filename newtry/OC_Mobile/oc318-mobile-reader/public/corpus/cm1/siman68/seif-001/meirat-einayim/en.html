So that it cannot be forged or have words added. In the manner explained by the Tur and Mechaber above at the beginning of siman 42.
It is valid. Meaning, the borrower cannot say, 'I paid,' and the holder may collect from encumbered property. Jewish witnesses generate publicity, and it makes no difference in what language or script they wrote the document so long as the witnesses can read it.
Where the signers are disqualified non-Jews. The general rule is as follows. If the underlying transaction became effective independently of the document, such as a sale completed when the purchaser paid the price, or acquired through a kinyan, chazakah, or money, meaning a perutah used to effect acquisition at the beginning of the sale as explained at the beginning of siman 190, and the document serves merely as evidence that the transaction occurred, then a document prepared in an official non-Jewish court is effective. Even if there are no other witnesses and the seller denies that any kinyan, payment, or chazakah occurred, we say that official non-Jewish courts do not ordinarily damage their own reputation by certifying falsehood, and the event is treated as known just as if it occurred before Jewish judges. But where the underlying transaction takes effect only through the document itself, such as a gift of land effected by writing, 'My field is given to you,' then because the acquisition depends entirely on this instrument prepared through non-Jews, he does not acquire even if it was executed in an official court. Although we trust the officials as to facts, their power is not sufficient to create the Jewish-law acquisition itself through their writing. See below siman 66, seifim 6 and 7.
And promissory notes. The reason is that the borrower's personal obligation arises independently of the document at the moment the money is delivered.
The money before them. Meaning, before the non-Jewish witnesses signed on the document, even if the official judges did not personally see the payment. According to this first view, which is Rambam's, a document produced through official non-Jewish courts means that the substantive transaction occurred before ordinary non-Jewish witnesses, who write and sign it, and they then go to the officials to authenticate their signatures, similar to our document authentication where witnesses write and sign and beit din authenticates. Therefore this view requires Jewish witnesses to testify that the non-Jewish document witnesses have a presumption of honesty and likewise concerning the official judges. Otherwise the document holder may have forged the signatures of trustworthy non-Jews without their knowledge and bribed the official to authenticate them. The second view cited by Rama, the Rosh's view, understands an official-court document differently. The non-Jewish witnesses do not sign until they come with the litigants before the official court, where all details are clarified, and only after everything has been established do the officials instruct the witnesses to sign and authenticate their signatures. Therefore this view does not require testimony that the non-Jewish witnesses are personally honest. Had the facts not been as written, the officials would not have instructed them to sign. Nor is separate testimony needed that the officials themselves are honest, because official judges are ordinarily presumed not to damage their reputations. For the same reason the document need not explicitly say that they saw the buyer or borrower count out the money, because the transaction is presumed properly conducted. This view likewise permits collection from encumbered property, because matters transacted in official courts are publicly known. This is all explicit in the Tur when read carefully, as I explained in Perishah. The Mechaber and Rama abbreviated the presentation.
And the register in which the official court writes. Meaning, their official ledger recording all matters brought before them, as is customary in our time. We likewise say that they do not damage their reputation by making false entries there.
Like their document. See Responsa Rashba, siman 982, concerning an official register stating that Reuven purchased a particular field where the entry had a line drawn through it and Reuven was in possession of the field. Darkei Moshe, seif 3, cites it.
Because official courts ordinarily do not accept bribes, etc. From what I wrote above it follows that this view, the Rosh's, differs from the first view, Rambam's, in four respects. First, no testimony is required that the officials are not known to accept bribes. Second, the document need not state that they saw the money delivered. Third, every substantive matter must be conducted before the officials, which is why no separate testimony about the honesty of the non-Jewish witnesses is needed. Fourth, collection may be made even from encumbered property. Each distinction follows the rationale of the respective opinion. See Perishah.
Rather, anything done in the official court. Meaning, where it is known that all substantive aspects were conducted before them, or the officials write at the end of the document that everything was done in their presence.
But deeds of gift, etc. The same applies to a deed of sale where the property is acquired through the document itself, such as writing, 'My field is sold to you.' The Mechaber mentions gifts rather than sales because land is not ordinarily acquired by a deed alone unless it was sold because of its poor quality, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 191. See Perishah.
That he owes him, see Perishah. In Hagahot Darkei Moshe I explained the reason: according to the Rosh, personal indebtedness through acknowledgment requires the declarant to say, 'You are my witnesses'; according to Rambam it requires a full legal acknowledgment before them, as explained in siman 81. The power of non-Jewish officials is insufficient to create such an obligation through their role alone, even if he says, 'You are my witnesses.' This differs from a loan actually made in their presence, where they merely report what they saw.
He owes him. The wording carefully says, 'he admitted that he owes him.' In that case the personal lien would be created through the non-Jewish officials, which is ineffective. But an admission that a debt was already paid is effective, because the non-Jewish officials are not creating a new obligation but merely evidencing removal of an existing lien. For this reason the Mechaber omits a release document, even though Rambam and Tur mention it. See what I write nearby concerning Rama's gloss.
And compromises, etc. Although a compromise requires a kinyan and the parties made a kinyan to uphold it, the compromise itself was produced through the non-Jewish court. A compromise is regarded as adjudication, and they are disqualified to adjudicate Jewish monetary law. According to this, even without a written document a compromise they impose is invalid. This is also implied by the Tur and Mechaber. See Perishah.
Or documents of release. In Perishah I explained that this does not mean a document merely recording that the creditor orally released the debtor before them. In that case the document serves only as evidence, which we already explained is effective in official courts. Rather, the document itself says, 'I hereby release you,' so the release is created through that writing, analogous to a deed of gift. According to this, the case must involve a Jewish creditor who writes and signs the release document in the non-Jewish court and the officials authenticate it, even though his signature is not otherwise recognizable.
And they were delivered before Jewish witnesses, etc. The reason is a decree lest people come to rely on the non-Jewish signatures in the document alone without Jewish witnesses to delivery.
Therefore, a person on his deathbed who made, etc. The reason is that the words of a person on his deathbed are treated as written and delivered. The document serves only as evidence.
Likewise in the opposite direction, if it is invalid according to our law, etc. The Tur writes that the law of kings validates their documents and trusts their scribes, but does not dictate the methods of acquisition. Even among non-Jews in many places their laws differ, each jurisdiction invalidating another's transactions according to the distinctions of their sages and legal systems.
