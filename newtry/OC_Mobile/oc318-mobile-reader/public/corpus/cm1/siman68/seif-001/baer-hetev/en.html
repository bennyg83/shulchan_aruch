Invalid. The Sema writes that the general rule is as follows. Any matter that was completed and became effective independently of the document, such as a sale that took effect when the buyer paid the purchase price, or through a kinyan, chazakah, or money, as stated at the beginning of siman 190, and the document serves only as evidence, may be established by a document made in a non-Jewish court. Even if there are no other witnesses and the seller denies that the buyer ever acquired it by kinyan or payment, we say that the officials of such a court would not discredit themselves by recording false testimony. But where the transaction becomes effective only through the document itself, such as one who gives a field by a deed stating, "My field is given to you," the recipient does not acquire it even if the deed was made in a non-Jewish court. Although their factual report is believed, their writing has no power to create the underlying Jewish-law acquisition. See siman 66, seifim 6 and 7, and Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 67, as well as the cited responsa.
Debt. The Sema explains that the reason is that the borrower's personal obligation arises immediately upon receipt of the money even without a document. See the cited responsum of Mahari ibn Lev.
And register. This means their official ledger in which all matters brought before them are recorded, as is customary in our times. Here too we say they would not discredit themselves by recording a falsehood. The Sema cites the Rashba concerning an official registry stating that Reuven bought a certain field where part of the writing had been erased and Reuven was in possession of the field. See also Darkei Moshe and the cited responsa.
That it was done. Meaning that it is known that all the acts were performed before them, or the officials wrote at the end of the document that everything was done in their presence. Sema.
Gifts. The same applies to deeds of sale where the acquisition is effected by the deed alone, such as one who sells a field because of its poor quality, as stated in siman 191. See Responsa Rama 109.
Admission. In such a case the underlying obligation would be created through the non-Jewish officials, and therefore it is ineffective. By contrast, an admission that he was already paid merely removes an existing obligation and may be effective. For this reason the Mechaber omitted a deed of waiver. The Sema explains that the Rama's inclusion of it is addressed below.
And compromises. Although a compromise ordinarily requires a kinyan and they made a kinyan to uphold it, a compromise is considered a form of adjudication, and these officials are invalid to adjudicate. Accordingly, even without a document, a compromise made by them is invalid. Sema.
Waivers. The Sema explains that this refers to a document that itself states the waiver, so that the waiver is effected through the document, like a deed of gift. Otherwise the document would merely serve as evidence and would be valid as explained above. The Shach questions this, because the Rama's wording and his citation of an opposing view regarding deeds of waiver do not clearly support that limitation, since even that opposing view would agree that a document which itself creates the act is invalid. The Bach suggests that the written admission may not prove a valid waiver because the party can say he spoke in jest; the Shach finds this strained and leaves the law requiring study. The Taz explains that where Jewish law and the foreign legal system differ concerning the legal substance of the statement, their testimony may not establish that the legal act was effective, since they may have understood an admission or waiver in a manner that has no force under our law. If, however, they record the exact words said by one person to another, they are believed as to those words, and we judge the words under Jewish law as though they had been said before us.
Like earthenware. The reason is a decree lest people come to rely on the signatures of the non-Jewish signatories alone without Jewish witnesses. Sema.
Valid. The reason is that the words of a person on his deathbed are considered as written and delivered, so the document serves only as evidence. Ibid.
Properly. The Tur writes that royal law extends to validating their documents and trusting their scribes, but not to the methods by which acquisitions are created, for even non-Jewish jurisdictions differ from one another in such laws and invalidate one another's procedures according to their respective scholars and legal systems. Sema.
