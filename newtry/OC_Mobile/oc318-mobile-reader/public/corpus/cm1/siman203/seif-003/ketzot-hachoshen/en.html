When he designated the specific money. The Tur explains that this limitation applies specifically where the parties treat the transaction as payment rather than as barter. If the buyer does not designate particular coins, then once he pulls the object he becomes personally obligated for its price. But if he designates specific coins and says, "With these coins," the transaction does not take effect. Since those specific coins remain at his risk only in a limited sense, if they are lost he is not personally liable to replace them. The seller therefore does not rely on the transaction until the coins actually reach him. Just as the seller did not acquire the coins, the buyer did not acquire the object through pulling it. Some later writers understood this to depend on whether the object has a distinguishing mark. That is difficult. The issue with coins is not simply that they lack identifying marks. The correct explanation is that a personal obligation works only where liability attaches to the obligor himself, including responsibility to replace the subject if it is lost. If one merely undertakes to give a particular object without accepting responsibility for it, there is no true personal obligation. It is closer to a kinyan devarim. Maharit states this explicitly. In order for an obligation to attach to the person, he must accept present responsibility so that if the designated property is lost he must pay from elsewhere. If he says only that he will deliver a specific future object and assumes no responsibility, the obligation never attaches to his person. Therefore, when specific coins are designated and there is no responsibility to replace them, no personal monetary obligation arises. Nor can the coins themselves be acquired through barter, because currency is not acquired by barter. Maharit was challenged from the laws of offerings. One who says, "I undertake to bring an olah on condition that I will not be liable for it," appears able to create such a commitment even without continuing responsibility. The answer is that in an ordinary vow, "I undertake an olah," his person and assets become fully obligated until he separates the offering. The condition that he bear no further responsibility applies only after separation. Until he separates it, there is a full personal obligation. This is why the obligation remains real and enforceable. The same applies to one who says he will bring an olah to the house of Onias on condition that he not remain responsible afterward. The central principle therefore stands: an obligation without personal responsibility is not a true personal obligation.
Once the buyer pulls the object, he immediately becomes obligated for its price. Beit Yosef, citing Rashba, discusses one who promised to lend another a maneh and the borrower gave him a debt instrument for that amount, after which the prospective lender retracted. Rashba says he may retract. Although the lender has already acquired a lien on the borrower's property through the instrument, this does not compel him to advance the money. Ramban, however, holds that once the lender acquired the lien on the borrower's property he can no longer retract. The apparent difficulty from Kiddushin can be resolved by distinguishing a loan of specific money. In the Gemara, the lender's lien arises only once the borrower takes possession of the specific coins and becomes liable for unavoidable accident. If those particular coins are then lost before the loan is fully consummated, the lender is not required to supply different coins. Since there is no general responsibility, the lender can still retract, and the borrower does not acquire the money by way of the reciprocal lien. By contrast, where a formal debt instrument has already vested a lien in the lender, the lender immediately becomes obligated in the corresponding value, namely to advance the loan. Because no specific coins were designated, the liability is personal and general. Ramban can therefore say that after the lien has been acquired, the lender can no longer retract.
