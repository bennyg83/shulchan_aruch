(Seif 3, in the gloss: Therefore, one who designates money, etc.) To clarify this law and resolve the difficulty raised by the Sema at the beginning of seif 7, we must first explain the words of the Tur in seif 3. He writes: “How so? If he sold him a cow, etc., although he received the money, the object is not acquired, etc.” He then writes: “Once the purchaser has performed meshichah on the object, he becomes obligated to the seller for the money. This applies, however, only where the money was given as payment and specific coins were not designated. Therefore, once the purchaser performs meshichah, he becomes obligated for the money. But where it was by way of exchange, because he designated the money and said, ‘For these coins,’ he does not acquire. Since he designated them, if they are lost he is not responsible for them. Therefore the seller does not rely upon the transaction until the money reaches his hand. Just as the seller did not acquire the money, so too the purchaser did not acquire the object, although he performed meshichah upon it.” End quote. Certainly, the principal reason that money cannot effect acquisition by exchange is that its stamped form is liable to be invalidated. It is therefore difficult to understand the additional reason based on responsibility. In an exchange involving other movable property as well, the item is the responsibility of the party who has not yet performed meshichah. We should then likewise say that his mind is not settled upon the transaction. Why, then, do we say that once the owner of the cow performs meshichah on the donkey, his cow is acquired by the owner of the donkey? There too he is not responsible for the cow, so why should we not say that the owner of the donkey does not rely upon the transaction? The Sema, seif katan 7, writes that loss is uncommon whereas invalidation of currency is common. This explanation is entirely unconvincing. Moreover, the Tur himself speaks here of money that was merely lost. What difference is there between such a loss and the loss of other movable property? It appears to me that the explanation is as follows. There are two forms of acquisition. The first is by way of sale, where he says in the language of sale, “Sell it to me for this money,” and the purchaser afterward becomes indebted for the money. The second is by way of exchange, this item in exchange for that item. Money cannot under any circumstances acquire an object by exchange, nor can money itself be acquired through exchange, because it is regarded as something lacking enduring substance, since its stamped form is liable to be invalidated, as the Nimukei Yosef writes at the beginning of page 47 in the chapter HaZahav. This differs from other movable property, which is effective even by way of exchange. This also resolves the Sema’s difficulty in seif katan 2, where he asks that even without exchange the movable property is acquired. On the contrary, the Tur teaches a greater novelty: even an exchange, which is ineffective with currency, is effective with movable property. The Tur then explains a sale for money. Once the purchaser performs meshichah on the object, he becomes obligated for the money, since he used the language of sale. Yet even here there is a distinction. If the transaction was by way of payment, meaning that he did not designate particular coins but said, “Sell me your cow for ten gold coins that I will give you,” he becomes indebted to him for ten gold coins. But if it was framed as an exchange, meaning that he designated specific money and said, “Sell it to me for these coins,” then since he used the language of sale and performed meshichah on the object, ordinarily he should acquire it through meshichah and remain obligated for the designated money. The Tur therefore teaches that because we must assign a personal obligation to the purchaser, and he bears no responsibility for those particular coins, the seller does not rely upon that obligation. This differs from an exchange of movable property, where no personal obligation need be imposed upon the purchaser. Immediately, the object in one party’s possession enters the responsibility of the other, since exchange is itself effective with movable property, and the parties rely upon the exchange as placing the property immediately in the other’s domain. With money, however, the principal obligation must necessarily rest upon the person who acquired the object to provide these designated coins, yet in truth no such obligation exists. Since the seller did not acquire the money, the purchaser likewise did not acquire the object, although he performed meshichah. This is unquestionably the Tur’s intent, as it appears to me. The wording of the Rama, however, is not entirely precise. He begins, “One who designates money,” implying that this is the premise upon which the following distinction rests, but then writes, “However, where it is by way of payment,” which sounds as though the transaction is effective even where specific money was designated, provided that it was treated as payment. We have already demonstrated from the Tur’s wording that this is not so. When the Gemara gives the reason concerning an exchange of money, the very designation of the coins is itself what makes it an exchange. The two matters are interdependent. Without doubt, wherever R. Moshe Isserles addresses this point, his intent accords with the explanation I have given as the principal interpretation.
