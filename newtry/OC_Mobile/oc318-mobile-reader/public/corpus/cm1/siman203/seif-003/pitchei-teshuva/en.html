His reference to Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 16, means that Chacham Tzvi describes another method by which ownership of money may be transferred, namely by formal admission, as we find in the case of Isur Giora in Bava Batra 149a. See Shulchan Aruch below, siman 250, seif 3, and siman 255, seif 2, that even a healthy person may transfer ownership through an admission. Tosafos in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim, Bava Batra 44b, write that even though we know with certainty that Isur Giora's admission was factually untrue, the recipient nevertheless acquired. Chacham Tzvi further explains that by this method one can transfer even money located elsewhere and not presently before him. See there. See Shach above, siman 60, seif katan 31, which is seif katan 32 in newer editions. Baal HaTerumot cites authorities who disagree and hold that a formal admission does not transfer ownership for a healthy person, but Shach writes that their interpretation of Isur Giora as applying only to a deathly ill person is incorrect, and all the poskim disagree with them. See the glosses of the Rav, Av Beit Din of Lvov, printed at the end of Responsa Chacham Tzvi, who notes that the same is clear from Responsa Rosh, rule 66, siman 8, cited by Beit Yosef, Even HaEzer, siman 66. Tashbetz, part 1, siman 153, and part 3, siman 325, holds that formal admission works only for a deathly ill person and not for a healthy person, but he did not see the responsum of the Rosh, and Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 194, already rejects Tashbetz's proofs. See also Responsa Radbaz, part 1, siman 325; Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 40, seif katan 1; Netivot HaMishpat there; Meshovev Netivot there; and what I wrote in Pitchei Teshuva there, seif katan 3, in the name of Noda BiYehudah. See also Eretz DeRabbanan, entry 191, in the gloss Afra DeAra, where he cites Responsa Mutzal MeEsh, siman 50. That responsum raises a doubt regarding one who on the first day of Sukkot admitted that a lulav belonged to Reuven. Is it considered Reuven's through this admission even though everyone knows it was not actually his? Perhaps the case differs from Isur Giora because for fulfillment of a mitzvah the truth is revealed before Heaven. He then resolves from Tosafos that the lulav is indeed considered as though it had been given to Reuven. The author then raises a further doubt whether Reuven must afterward make a formal acquisition back to the original owner, or whether it suffices for the original owner to say, "This belongs to so and so," by way of admission, or perhaps, whichever way one looks at it, one of them failed to fulfill the mitzvah retroactively. Afra DeAra writes that in his view it is clear that both fulfill their obligation. Since in monetary law we rule that a mere formal admission is treated as though the property had been given to the other person, even when we know that it was not actually his, that other person acquires through the admission itself. The admission functions as the act of transfer. The same applies to a lulav, where the Torah makes fulfillment depend upon the object being called "his." Once the other person's admission makes it legally his, the same mechanism works in reverse when he later admits that it belongs to the first person.
