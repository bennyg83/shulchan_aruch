(Same Shach) It is explicit that he explains the incident of Rabban Gamliel, etc. Note: It appears to me that the Itur himself agrees that a renter can acquire through the rented courtyard a found object or other ownerless property that enters it, especially if the owner of the courtyard says that he does not wish to acquire that ownerless object and prefers that the renter acquire it. This resembles two partners in one courtyard when one consents that the other alone acquire a found object there. But where the landlord wishes to transfer his own property to the renter, he cannot give it to him through the law of courtyard merely because he rented him the courtyard. Although courtyard acquisition functions as though the item left the transferor's domain and entered the recipient's, here the landlord still owns the body of the courtyard. The item therefore has not fully left his domain. This resembles two partners in a courtyard, where one cannot transfer his movables located there to the other merely through the courtyard. Accordingly, the Itur's ruling that the right of choosing the recipient of tithes is not money can be reconciled with the incident of Rabban Gamliel. There, according to the Itur, the acquisition operated through the courtyard because the tithe was effectively ownerless with respect to Rabban Gamliel's financial ownership, and he consented that Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Akiva acquire it. But the sugya in Kiddushin that treats Rabban Gamliel's act as acquisition through agav must be following the opinion that the right of choosing the recipient is monetary. On that view the tithe remained Rabban Gamliel's property, so the renters could not acquire it by the rented courtyard, because Rabban Gamliel still retained ownership of the courtyard's body. It therefore had to be transferred through agav. The sugya can then properly prove that land sold to one and movables gifted to another may be acquired through agav.
