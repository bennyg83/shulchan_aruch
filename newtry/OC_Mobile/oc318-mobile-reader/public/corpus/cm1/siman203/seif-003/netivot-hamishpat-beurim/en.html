One who designates particular money. Tur explains that the seller's mind does not rely on the transaction because if those designated coins are lost, the buyer bears no personal responsibility. Sema asks that in every barter transaction the party is not personally responsible for accidental loss, and answers that accidental loss is uncommon while disqualification of coin is common, so Tur's word "lost" means that the coin became invalid. I find this very difficult. Why should reliance be greater without designation than with designation? If one simply stipulates payment in money or in the currently circulating coin, and that currency becomes invalid, siman 77 says he pays with the currency that was current at the relevant time. If he stipulated a particular currency, then even if it is later invalidated he may still owe that designated currency. Why then should designation itself undermine reliance? The basic ruling of Tur appears to mean that when the buyer designates particular coins, even if he explicitly says that they are designated as money and not as barter, and even after he draws the movable property, he does not acquire. Proof can be brought from Bava Metzia 46. The Gemara establishes the Mishnah that gold acquires silver in a monetary-payment framework and asks from a baraita saying that the silver is acquired "wherever it is." The Gemara answers that it means "as it is": if one said, "from a new purse," he cannot pay from an old one because the other can say he wants old coins. If designation of money were effective, the Gemara could have answered much more simply that the buyer became obligated to give the exact designated money. It would not need the new-purse distinction. Evidently, where specific money is designated in that manner the acquisition does not work. The reason resembles Taz's principle. In a sale treated as payment, there must be some personal obligation resting upon the buyer. When he says, "Sell to me for these coins," the implication is that the buyer assumes no personal duty at all regarding loss or payment; these particular coins themselves are simply to become the seller's. Even if the parties call them "payment," the substance of the arrangement is barter, not a monetary debt. Coin cannot be acquired through chalipin, and therefore neither side acquires in that form. But if one buys ordinarily and then designates particular money, expressly obligating himself personally to satisfy the price specifically from those coins, or designates other movable property and obligates himself to pay from those particular coins, the personal obligation is valid. A person can obligate himself. The baraita could not be established in that unusual explicit personal-obligation form, because it addresses ordinary sale, not a specially drafted obligation. Likewise, if the buyer says only, "You shall have no recourse except from these coins," the sale can still work. This resembles an explicit apoteiki: there is a personal debt, even though recovery is limited in the first instance to identified property and the debtor can sometimes substitute other money. By contrast, "Sell me for these" means there is no personal obligation at all; the seller is merely to take those coins as his own. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that although a person can obligate himself, if he obligates himself only to deliver an identified object and assumes no accident liability, there is no personal debt and the undertaking is ineffective. In my view this is difficult. According to him, if a buyer designated particular movable property as the sale price, meshichah of the purchased property would not obligate him to give that designated property. The poskim do not imply this. Rashi in Bava Metzia 46 also indicates that such an undertaking can work. The very law of obligations also contradicts Ketzot's formulation. Siman 60 says one can obligate oneself regarding a davar shelo ba la'olam, implying that one can obligate oneself to deliver particular future fruit. Baal HaTerumot derives obligation from lien, and liens can apply to specified property. This is common in practice. MahariT's proof concerning the Torah rule of recognizing the firstborn is also not decisive. He explains that the father cannot simply obligate himself to give the son all future assets because he may need to consume or sell them; he wants only his heirs to give the son a double share of whatever remains at death. The heirs cannot be bound by the father's personal undertaking, and the father never took a present personal obligation to deliver those assets. That is different from a person obligating himself now to deliver a specified object, where the obligation rests on his person. Ketzot cites Beit Yaakov questioning MahariT from the rule, "It is upon me as an olah, on condition that I not be liable for its accidents." This is no difficulty. There remains a real personal duty while the animal exists, namely to bring it to the Temple courtyard, even though he does not bear liability for theft. A person can obligate himself to deliver an identified thing while it remains in existence without assuming accident liability. MahariT rejects only an arrangement like the inheritance case, where the person himself undertakes no present duty to deliver and says only that heirs after death should give it. That is not a personal obligation. The Menachot case concerning one who conditions that he may offer the animal on a private altar is different. If he truly reserves the right to destroy the sacrificial obligation by offering outside, he appears to have removed all personal duty, contradicting his declaration "it is upon me." The Gemara answers that even there the condition does not entirely negate the obligation: unless he in fact offers it outside, he remains obligated to bring it properly, and in other accident settings he can remain liable. The condition merely gives him a specific permitted consequence if he destroys the obligation through that designated act. Thus here, when the buyer says only "Sell me for these" with designated coins, the failure is because there is no personal obligation and the substance is chalipin. With coin, that does not acquire. With ordinary movable property, the same arrangement can acquire as barter. Rashi's language on Bava Metzia 46, which appears to treat some such transaction as payment, can be read where the buyer uses language of personal obligation: "Sell me the meat of this ox, and for the price I will give you this cow," meaning, "I will pay you with this." That creates a personal debt and can work, as explained above.
