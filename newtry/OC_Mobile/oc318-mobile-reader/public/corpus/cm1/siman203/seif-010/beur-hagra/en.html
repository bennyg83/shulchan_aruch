Some say, etc. See Sema in the name of Mordekhai. It requires study why Rama first cites the initial opinion, since we rule in the case of 'you and a donkey' that half is acquired. Below, siman 209, seif 4, he rules without qualification like the latter opinion concerning one who transfers, etc. The latter opinion has strong proof from the Gemara's requirement of acquisition together with land. The first opinion is seemingly rejected because the halakha follows Rav Nachman, and Mordekhai's entire discussion follows Rav Hamnuna. Mordekhai follows Rav Hamnuna despite the rule that the halakha follows Rav Nachman in monetary law because the second chapter of Kiddushin supports Rav Hamnuna, whereas we rule like Rav Nachman and explain that passage as non-normative. Nevertheless, Mordekhai and Rama can be defended according to Halakhot Gedolot and Rambam, who explain in Kiddushin that the passage can accord even with Rav Nachman, since Rav Nachman concedes when everything is included in one statement. That is the case here, and this is why Rama says 'together.' It also answers Rabbi Simcha's question why acquisition together with land was necessary, because there the items were not included in one statement. The second opinion likewise concedes there. Thus, where they are not included in one statement, both opinions agree that it resembles 'you and a donkey.' Rama was therefore correct to cite two opinions here while ruling simply like the latter opinion in siman 209. The reason offered there in the gloss of Rivash is not persuasive. Two, etc.; some say. This follows Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 209, seif 2. Rambam's reason is that an asmachta does not acquire even in a sale, as explained in siman 207. This is why the case involves gambling, consistent with Rambam's view that gambling involves theft. Shulchan Aruch likewise writes below in siman 370 that certain acts were prohibited because of theft. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch therefore rule there that one who gambles with a non-Jew, etc. The objection cited there, that gambling disqualifies only one who has no other occupation, is imprecise, because Rambam agrees, as he writes in the laws of testimony: one who gambles is disqualified only when he has no other occupation, since one who does not engage in productive society is presumed to consume gambling proceeds, which constitute a trace of theft. Rambam nevertheless follows Rami bar Chama that the issue is asmachta, and also Rabbi Yehuda that disqualification applies only where there is no other occupation. The Gemara rules explicitly like Rabbi Yehuda, yet still treats the underlying reason as asmachta. Gambling is called theft in the first chapter of Rosh Hashana and elsewhere. It is only rabbinic theft, and therefore one is disqualified only when supported by it. Gambling with a non-Jew is not theft from a Jew, but is prohibited initially because it removes one from productive life. The dissenters hold that even gambling with a non-Jew disqualifies, because they follow the view that the defect is failure to engage in society, while holding that this form of asmachta is effective. This is the basis of the second opinion here. See cited source 322.
But each one, etc., because the principal, etc. As stated in Gittin 74b, the Sages agree with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that whenever the speaker's purpose is merely to obtain relief, the specified object is not indispensable. Although Abaye objects there at 75a, that is because the person's principal intent concerned the cloak itself. Here it is otherwise. This is the meaning of 'because the principal, etc.,' and accords with Bava Metzia 63a: what difference is there between the produce and its value?
