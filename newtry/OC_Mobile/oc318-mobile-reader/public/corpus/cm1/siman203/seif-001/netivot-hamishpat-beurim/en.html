If the transferor is not particular. See Sema, seif katan 20, who writes that if one says, "This shall be the payment price," even without intending chalipin the acquisition is effective, comparing it to siman 199 where a person holds an uncounted amount of money and says, "Sell to me for this." His words imply that if one buys movable property and gives other movable property as the price without appraisal, then even if he gives it as payment rather than as barter, the transaction acquires. In my view this is very difficult. In Kiddushin 7, Rav Yosef initially says silk requires appraisal, and the Gemara explains that a woman is not expert in valuation. The implication is that men are presumed capable of estimating. Therefore, when two men see the object and agree on it without formal appraisal, we should assume the buyer internally assessed its value and the parties were particular about that value. This is entirely unlike holding an unknown handful of coins and saying, "Sell me for these," where neither party knows the amount in advance. Rashi in Bava Metzia 47, who says an object can similarly be given in the "sell me for these" formulation, speaks of produce sold approximately, where the buyer does not see the precise quantity and cannot truly appraise it. In Bava Metzia 46, regarding "there are prices that function like chalipin," Rashi explains that when the cow was drawn they had stipulated that a donkey would later be given. Ritva asks that this is common and therefore not the exceptional case the Gemara seeks. The other poskim therefore explain that the buyer first became obligated in money for the cow and afterward transferred the ox as payment of that monetary debt. If Sema were correct, the Gemara could simply establish the case where one exchanged cow for ox as payment without being particular about valuation. Evidently even in such a case, if the transaction is formally one of payment, the ordinary rule that money-type consideration does not acquire movable property applies. Nor can one say the concern of "your wheat burned" is absent because if the transfer did not acquire the other side could say the same about what he had already drawn. The Sages did not distinguish. Every transaction formally characterized as payment follows the money-acquisition rule.
And discretionary benefit is not money. See Shach, seif katan 1, who asks from Kiddushin 26, where the Gemara says without qualification that Rabban Gamliel transferred through agav. According to what I wrote above in siman 200, there is no difficulty. The Gemara's proof concerns poor man's tithe that he transferred to R. Akiva while it was still tevel, as Shitah Mekubetzet explains. Poor man's tithe tevel has ordinary monetary value because destructive benefit from it is permitted. It can therefore be transferred through agav. There is another point. Even though we rule that movable property can be acquired agav land, discretionary benefit, which is not money, should not be acquired through chalipin. Coin and a debt instrument can count as monetary value for kiddushin and nevertheless cannot be acquired through chalipin because their bodies are not property in the relevant sense, as Nimukei Yosef explains. All the more so discretionary benefit, which is not classified as property for ordinary purposes, should not be acquired through chalipin. Shitah Mekubetzet expressly states that discretionary benefit is not money for purposes of chalipin. The Gemara in Bava Metzia 10 says that the rejection there is because chalipin is a mode of commerce, but that discussion may be distinguishing even according to the opinion that discretionary benefit is money. According to our ruling that discretionary benefit is not money, it is not acquired through chalipin.
If they appraised them. According to Rabbeinu Tam, the same applies even without formal appraisal, because equal-value barter can acquire even produce. Beit Yosef cites this in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham. There is a difficulty. Mechaber himself appears to follow Rabbeinu Tam, for in seif 3 he rules that bars of gold acquire one another by exchange, while in siman 195, seif 2, he rules that ordinary chalipin must be performed with a utensil and Rama states that other movable property that is not a utensil cannot be used. Necessarily the distinction is Rabbeinu Tam's: equal-value barter does not require the exchanged object to be a utensil. Rama should therefore have noted in seif 3 that some disagree and hold that even equal-value exchange works only with objects valid for ordinary chalipin, such as utensils or work animals that function like utensils. On that view even a disqualified coin can serve only because it can be hung as an ornament for one's daughter. Equal-value barter and kinyan sudar have the same object requirements. According to the view that equal-value barter works with all movable property, acquisition should not require that one side specifically perform meshichah. If one side acquires the other item by any valid mode, the reciprocal item becomes obligated. Thus if owners exchange an ox and cow, and the ox owner transfers the ox to the cow owner through kinyan sudar or agav land, the cow becomes acquired to the ox owner. Kiddushin 26 says, "Once this one acquired, the other became obligated in his exchange," implying any valid acquisition by one side is sufficient. Why then does equal-value barter escape the concern of "your wheat burned in the attic"? Tosafot in Bava Metzia 46 answer that true equal-value barter is uncommon, because ordinary chalipin does not precisely match the sale price. Ramban and Ran, cited by Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Metzia 47, answer that the Sages could not institute that one side's acquisition should fail in chalipin merely because of the fire concern. If they did, the person who already drew one item could turn around and tell the other, "What I drew for you burned in the attic." Both answers are needed. Ramban and Ran alone do not fully answer the opinion that coin can itself become chalipin, where money is exchanged for merchandise. The fire concern applies strongly to the merchandise, while money has different guarding rules and is not ordinarily subject to the same rescue concern. Tosafot's answer is needed there: precise equal-value barter is uncommon and the Sages did not legislate around it. Tosafot's answer alone is also insufficient where ordinary movable property was explicitly appraised against other movable property by experts, making the values genuinely equal. In that case the transaction is not uncommon merely because of valuation. Ramban and Ran's structural reason is therefore also needed. According to Tosafot, since the ordinary form of chalipin is not equal to the full price and exact equality is uncommon, the Sages did not enact a special failure of acquisition even when an occasional transaction happens to be exactly equal. According to Ramban and Ran, even if the exchange involves bars of silver that require ground-storage, or the parties expressly impose accident liability so that the fire concern could theoretically arise, the exchange still acquires because in the usual class of chalipin that concern is absent, and the Sages did not distinguish.
