The reference to Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 16, concerns another method of acquiring money, namely acquisition through an admission, as in the case of Issur the convert in Bava Batra 149 and as codified below in simanim 250 and 255. Such an admission works even for a healthy person. Tosafot in Bava Batra 44b write that even where we know with certainty that Issur's admission was false, the recipient nevertheless acquired, and Chacham Tzvi further explains that in this manner one may transfer money located elsewhere and not presently before him. Shach, siman 60, cites Baal HaTerumot's dissenting view that an admission of this sort works only for a person on his deathbed, but Shach rejects it and writes that all the poskim disagree. The glosses of the Av Beit Din of Lemberg printed after Chacham Tzvi note that the Rosh, responsa 66:8, likewise explicitly recognizes acquisition by admission for a healthy person. Tashbetz disagrees, but apparently did not see the Rosh, and Ketzot HaChoshen has already rejected Tashbetz's proofs. See also Radbaz I, siman 325, Ketzot and Netivot to siman 40, Meshovev Netivot there, and the discussion cited in Pitchei Teshuva. Ar'a DeRabbanan, with Afra DeAra, cites Responsa Mutzal MeEsh, siman 50, which considers whether one who admits on the first day of Sukkot that his lulav belongs to Reuven thereby makes it Reuven's for purposes of the mitzvah even though he knows it was not actually Reuven's. He concludes from Tosafot that the admission is treated as an actual transfer. He then asks whether Reuven must later formally transfer it back, or whether Reuven may simply admit that it belongs to the original owner. The gloss concludes that both can fulfill the mitzvah: since an admission in monetary law is itself regarded as a transfer even when known to be factually false, the same is true of the lulav, whose status depends on legal ownership. Reuven's reciprocal admission can therefore restore ownership just like an actual conveyance. See also Birkei Yosef, siman 40, at length.
