Nimukei Yosef derives from this that if, at the time of sale of movable property, the buyer stipulates that he will give a kor of wheat and then performs meshichah on the merchandise, he must give wheat specifically. His language implies that this is true even if the buyer did not own that kor at the time of the transaction. Meshichah of the merchandise creates the reciprocal obligation, just as Bava Metzia 46 says gold obligates silver, and an obligation can apply even to a davar shelo ba la'olam. I later saw Machaneh Efrayim state this explicitly. Machaneh Efrayim asks from siman 332, where one promised a particular object as a worker's wages but can instead pay its monetary value. He answers that labor is like giving money for the object, and money alone does not acquire movable property. I still find this difficult, because siman 332 implies the employer may pay value even ab initio. If the worker's labor were literally equivalent to paying money toward the object, refusal to deliver the object should at least expose the employer to mi shepara. In my view, if the employer says, "Work for me and this object shall be yours," the labor is indeed consideration for that object and refusal to deliver it should produce mi shepara. Kiddushin recognizes a parallel structure where labor can serve as consideration in a transaction, and Avodah Zarah at the beginning of HaSocher Et HaPoel likewise discusses a non-Jewish woman acquiring a lamb through the reciprocal act. Siman 332, however, concerns the wording, "Work for me and I will give you this object as your wages." The future word "I will give" is not itself a kinyan. It resembles saying, "Lend me money and I will repay you with this object," where the borrower remains permitted to pay its value instead. Even if he says, "You shall have no repayment except from this," or, "I will repay you only from this," the underlying personal monetary debt remains and can be discharged with money in the circumstances explained elsewhere. One might still ask: if a kinyan sudar was made binding the worker so that the worker cannot retract, as discussed in siman 333, why should the employer not become obligated in the specific promised object just as reciprocal obligations arise in a sale of movable property? The answer is that no kinyan takes effect in the worker's body. Shitah Mekubetzet in HaOmanin explains that only an eved Ivri has his body legally acquired; an ordinary worker's body is not acquired. The kinyan that prevents the worker from retracting operates only on the worker's personal undertaking to perform labor. This differs from the rule that gold obligates silver. There, an actual acquisition takes effect in the body of one item, and Torah law imposes the promised reciprocal consideration because that was the basis on which the other person's property was acquired. With a worker, no acquisition took effect in the worker himself; only his service obligation was strengthened. Therefore the promised object does not become the reciprocal acquired consideration in the same way.
