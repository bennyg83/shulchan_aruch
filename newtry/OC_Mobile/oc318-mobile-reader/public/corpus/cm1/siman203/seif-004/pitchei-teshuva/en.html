Sema explains that the principal ruling follows the final view, and therefore in siman 209 Rama cites only that view. The opinion here that nothing is acquired compares the case to "acquire yourself and a donkey," whereas we rule even in that case like Rav Nachman, that half is acquired, as stated below at the end of siman 210. Rama records all three views here only because he copied the wording of Mordechai, but his own ruling follows Rabbeinu Simchah, whose view he places last. See there. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, however, simanim 25 through 28, in his exchange with his son in law, Rabbi Yosef, concerning a shtar chatzi zachar that was not written as an admission of an established debt, indicates that he regards the matter as an unresolved doubt of law, because some poskim rule like Rav Hamnuna that nothing is acquired. Therefore one cannot extract property from the current possessor. See there. The same appears from Noda BiYehudah, second edition, simanim 45 and 47. At the end of siman 29 he offers a sound explanation of Rama's wording, why here, concerning an exchange involving money and movable property, Rama cites three views, while in siman 209, concerning one who transfers something not yet in existence together with something already in existence, he cites only one view, namely that the existing item is acquired and the future item is not. His distinction is based on Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer, siman 41, seif 3, concerning one who betrothed many women, including two sisters. Shulchan Aruch rules that if he said, "All of you are betrothed to me," the unrelated women are betrothed, while some say that even they are not. Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel distinguish this from "acquire yourself and a donkey," where we rule that half is acquired. There the two objects, the person and the donkey, are separate. Here, "all of you" is a single collective expression. In the name of Maggid Mishneh and Ran they further explain that there, although one recipient lacks legal capacity to acquire, the other recipient has capacity. Here there is one purchaser, and a purchaser ordinarily acquires the good and bad together as one transaction; if one part fails, the entire acquisition may fail. According to this, Rama could hold that when one transfers money and movable property together, because the money is physically present, both distinctions are possible. According to the first distinction, the case is one where the money and movable property are all lying before him and he says, "I transfer to you everything lying here," so that it is one collective expression. It is difficult on this explanation to fit Rama's concluding statement, "and the same applies if they made a kinyan," concerning something lacking substance. One would have to understand Rama there not as Sema does, that all the earlier views dispute that case as well, but as referring back to the immediately preceding ruling that the acquisition is effective with respect to the item that is legally transferable and ineffective with respect to the item lacking substance. On that reading all authorities agree, though the explanation is forced. The third rationale also applies here, namely that the recipient may not intend to acquire one without the other. By contrast, where one transfers property not presently in his possession together with property that is, we cannot say that both lie before him, since one is not presently in his possession, and especially in siman 209, where the subject is something not yet in existence at all. It therefore cannot be included physically in one collective act. Nor is the second rationale, that the recipient does not want one without the other, as persuasive there. One item does not yet exist while the other does, and the transfer of the existing item is intended to take effect now. The purchaser knows that the other item does not yet exist and cannot presently benefit him in any way. It is therefore unreasonable to assume that he refuses the present acquisition without the future one. Noda BiYehudah regards this as a sound distinction. On this basis, in his own case, where the gift deed stated "all the objects and movable property," money is certainly included in "objects," for nothing is more desired by a person than money. The case thus resembles an exchange of money and movable property. If at the end of the deed it says, "and we made a kinyan concerning all that is written above," then everything is included in one kinyan, like the phrase "all of you," and both negative rationales apply. If, however, the deed begins, "They made a kinyan from me that I give," and so forth, then each item is a separate matter, and only the concern that there is one recipient remains. But if the deed is phrased as a past admission, "I have already transferred," there is no defect at all. Whatever he admitted is binding like one hundred witnesses, and even the money is acquired, because we assume he already transferred it agav land. From this discussion it appears that Noda BiYehudah treats as clear law that where one transfers property in his possession together with property not in his possession, or existing property together with something not yet in existence, as Rama states in siman 209, he certainly acquires the property already in his possession. The dispute applies only where he transfers money and movable property together. Yet his words in siman 25 indicate that even the former case remains a doubt of law because some poskim rule like Rav Hamnuna. See there. In simanim 26 and 28 he further writes that even if one assumes that when existing property is transferred together with future property nothing is acquired, following Rav Hamnuna, there is still a distinction between future property that is certain to come and future property that may never come. Rashbam explains in Bava Batra 142 that Rav Hamnuna's reason in "acquire yourself and a donkey" is that because one kinyan jointly encompassed both, the giver did not intend to transfer to one without the other. The same logic may apply when transferring present property together with something future but certain to come, such as the fruit of a palm tree. We may estimate that he does not wish to transfer one without the other. But where one transfers all the property he presently owns together with all property he may acquire throughout his life, although he does not acquire the later property, he certainly acquires the present property. There is no basis to say that the giver did not intend one without the other, because it is not certain that he will ever acquire additional property. He therefore surely did not make the present gift dependent on a future event that might never occur. According to all views the property presently in existence is acquired. This also resolves Rama in Even HaEzer, siman 108, seif 3, who rules simply that the recipient takes what the giver owned at the time of writing. Noda BiYehudah brings clear proof from the Tosefta cited by Baal HaTerumot, gate 43, and Beit Yosef, siman 60: one who gives his fellow as a gift what he has acquired and what he will acquire does not transfer the future property. His son in law Rabbi Yosef challenged this distinction, and Noda BiYehudah wrote again defending it. It also emerges there that even in the case of "acquire yourself and a donkey," the rule of Rav Hamnuna applies specifically when the donkey exists. If one says, "Acquire yourself and the donkey if my she donkey gives birth to one this year," even Rav Hamnuna agrees that the human recipient acquires his portion.
