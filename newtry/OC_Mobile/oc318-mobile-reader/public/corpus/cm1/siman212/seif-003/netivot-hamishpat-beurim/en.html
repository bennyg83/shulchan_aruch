On condition that the upper story is mine. Taz asks that this implies the right to extend projections comes only because the language is treated as a reservation. If “on condition” were ordinary conditional language, he suggests it would not help for something intangible. In fact, an ordinary condition can be made even about a davar shelo ba la'olam or an intangible matter. Still, there is a real difficulty. If the phrase means a true condition, namely that the entire sale is void if the upper story does not remain his, then the wording is not superfluous even though the upper story would otherwise belong to him. He may have inserted it precisely to make the whole sale contingent upon retaining that right. Taz also asks that Rambam appears inconsistent. Regarding priestly gifts, Rambam rules that one who sells “on condition that the gifts are mine” may give them to whichever kohen he chooses, treating the phrase as a condition. But regarding “on condition that the first tithe is mine,” Rambam rules that the tithe itself belongs to the seller, treating it as a reservation. Ritva in Kiddushin 23 provides the key. Language is truly conditional when it requires someone to do or refrain from doing an act. But where the clause concerns a state of affairs that is to exist automatically, such as “on condition that your master has no rights in it,” it is not an executory condition but a reservation within the body of the transfer. The transferor withholds a particular right. This explains “on condition that you have no ona'ah claim against me,” or kiddushin “on condition that you have no claim to maintenance or clothing.” These are not naturally read as saying that if the law later gives the other party such a right the transaction should disappear; the intent is to reserve or exclude that legal right from the transaction. The dispute in Chullin concerning “on condition that the gifts are mine” is therefore not whether automatic language is always a condition or always a reservation. Rather, it concerns whether the seller reserved the gifts themselves from the body of the sale, in which case he never sold them and there is no stipulation against Torah law, or sold the whole animal while reserving only the right to decide who receives the gifts, which can conflict with the Torah’s allocation of that right to the buyer. Accordingly, “on condition that the upper story is mine” is an automatic reservation. Since the upper story would have remained his even without the clause, the extra wording must reserve an additional incident of ownership, namely the right to extend projections. There is no need for formal condition rules. Rambam’s rulings can also be reconciled. A stipulation against Torah law is void only where it certainly uproots the Torah arrangement. In the case of priestly gifts, if the animal was sold specifically for slaughter and the seller reserves control over the gifts, the condition certainly contradicts the Torah allocation and is void. But if the arrangement could remain effective without ever triggering the Torah duty, no certain contradiction exists. With first tithe, the purchaser may plant fodder, grow exempt produce, or bring the crop in through a roof or other exempt route. The sale can therefore stand without necessarily uprooting the Torah rule. The reservation of first tithe is consequently effective.
