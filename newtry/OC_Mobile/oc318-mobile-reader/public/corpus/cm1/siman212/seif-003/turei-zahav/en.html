(Seif 3: We say that he reserved it for himself.) This implies that the arrangement is effective only because he reserved a place. If, however, “on condition that” is the language of a condition, it would not be effective, as where one says, “on condition that you give a particular thing,” and that thing has no substance. The Rosh explicitly writes in the chapter HaMokher et HaBayit that the tanna there maintains that “on condition that” is the language of reservation. According to us, however, who rule that it is the language of a condition, as the Beit Yosef writes in siman 209, it should not enable him to project beams into the courtyard. This is difficult for the Tur, who here rules like the opinion that “on condition that” is a reservation, whereas at the end of siman 209 he writes that it is a condition and that one may make a condition concerning something not yet in existence. The Beit Yosef there writes this explicitly. The same difficulty arises from the ruling of the Tur and the Mechaber in Yoreh Deah, siman 61, that one who says, “on condition that the cheeks are mine,” has made a condition. The Rosh’s abridged rulings in the chapter HaZeroa likewise explain that the tanna dealing with priestly gifts disagrees with the tanna of HaMokher et HaBayit regarding the meaning of “on condition that.” The Tur’s rulings therefore appear contradictory. There he rules like the tanna in HaZeroa that it is a condition, whereas here he writes that since the seller said “on condition that,” he reserved a place. The latter law, which cannot be achieved through an ordinary condition, is derived in HaMokher et HaBayit from the baraita concerning a Levite who sold his field and said, “on condition that the first tithe is mine.” Just as there he reserves the place of the tithe, so too here he reserves space for the beams, as the Rosh writes there. Here the Tur rules in accordance with that law concerning the Levite. In Yoreh Deah, siman 61, I likewise raised a contradiction in Rambam’s rulings. See there. The statement here is certainly no difficulty. The Tur did not precisely say that he reserved a place in the courtyard in the technical law of reservation, in order to teach that “on condition that” is a reservation rather than a condition. Rather, he had to explain that although the seller did not mention any reservation in the courtyard at all, and merely sold the house while retaining the upper story, which was already his, we say that he also intended to retain space in the courtyard in which to project beams. Whether “on condition that” is the language of reservation or of condition, it is effective. We interpret the condition that the upper story remain his as referring not merely to the upper story, but also to the projection of beams, for which he retained a right in the courtyard. The Tur is not now explaining the differences between reservation and condition, which he already explained in siman 209. This is simple. I could also have said that the Tur included in his wording the view of Ramban, cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 209, who distinguishes between “on condition that you give,” which is certainly a condition, and “on condition that it is mine,” which is a reservation according to all authorities. This is a sound and acceptable distinction. Ramban writes that Rav Hai Gaon and many other authorities agreed with it. Although the Rosh gives another distinction, one may say that the Tur held that both are legally valid. This would make the Tur’s language especially precise. But there is no need for this, because the matter is simple as I have written. The second difficulty from the Tur’s ruling in Yoreh Deah also falls away. However, the contradiction I raised at the end of Yoreh Deah, siman 61, within Rambam’s rulings is indeed powerful. The Shach’s answer is not correct in my opinion. For if “on condition that” is the language of reservation, what difference is there between land and movables? The correct explanation of Rambam appears to me to be that he adopted a different approach to the Mishnah. He holds that the Mishnah’s statement, “If he said, except for the gifts,” refers to a priest who enters into partnership with an Israelite in the animal itself and uses that language at the time of partnership. Rambam writes similarly in his Commentary to the Mishnah: “If he stipulated at the inception of the partnership that he does not become a partner with them in the gifts, but that they remain in their place.” He likewise writes in his code that the priest stipulated with the other party that he is a partner except for the gifts. The baraita stating that if he says, “on condition that the gifts are mine,” he may give them to any priest he wishes, likewise concerns a stipulation made when the partnership was created. Rambam follows Ramban’s view cited above: since he said, “on condition that it is mine,” rather than, “on condition that you give,” the expression ordinarily indicates a reservation. Here, however, where the priest becomes a partner and says, “on condition that the gifts are mine,” there is independent proof that it is not a reservation, because half the gifts are already his and he did not need to reserve them. But if it is a condition, as is generally the case with “on condition that” in the Talmud, the wording is understandable. He intends to sell the other person all the gifts as part of that person’s share, while stipulating that they nevertheless remain his. This is ineffective because he did not say, “on condition that you give them to me.” This is why Rambam writes that one who says “on condition that” reserved nothing in the substance of the gifts, and because he did not reserve a partnership in them, he did not acquire them. Examine this carefully. After writing this gloss, while I was still occupied with its printing, a work called Peri Chadash was published. I saw that its author also wished to answer the Taz’s difficulty, but in my opinion he did not succeed. He writes: “Although regarding the priestly gifts ‘on condition that’ is not a reservation, that case is different because the gifts already exist and can be acquired, except that the stipulation contradicts a Torah law. Here, however, regarding the tithe, if we call it a condition the transaction is entirely void, because a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence. It must therefore be a reservation of the place of the tithe. The Shach’s words here are incorrect, and Rambam’s position appears primary. The sugya in HaZeroa regarding the gifts treats ‘except’ as a reservation and ‘on condition that’ as not a reservation, whereas several sugyot in HaMokher et HaBayit run in the opposite direction and treat ‘on condition that’ as a reservation. One must therefore explain as I have done.” I copied his words because the work is not commonly available. It appears to me that they have no basis. What difference does it make that the priestly gifts exist and could be acquired if he had transferred them, when in fact his present language is a condition and is therefore ineffective because it contradicts the Torah? He further writes that regarding tithe the transaction is void. If he means the sale itself, that is precisely the difficulty: why should it be void when there too the condition contradicts the Torah, since the seller permitted the buyer to give the tithe to any Levite he wishes? If his wording was imprecise and he meant that the condition is void, the condition regarding priestly gifts is likewise void because it contradicts the Torah. What difference is there whether one or two elements are void? His assumption that Rambam’s reason is necessarily a condition contrary to Torah law also does not appear correct to me. Although the Rosh writes so, Tosafot remained uncertain about it. Ramban’s novellae, citing Ri Migash and Rav Hai Gaon, and agreeing with them, do not imply this explanation. Presumably Rambam follows their approach, and reason also inclines toward it. His statement that Rambam’s words are primary is likewise imprecise, because the Shach also distinguishes between the sugyot; my difficulty was directed at the Shach’s distinction, as stated above.
