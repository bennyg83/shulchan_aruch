And some say that the consecrated interest was not acquired. See Responsa Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 44, concerning the will of a person on his deathbed. The instrument stated that he ordered the will to be written and signed and, for added force, instructed the witnesses to make a formal sudar kinyan from him, without weakening the force of the will, so that both the testamentary declaration and the kinyan would stand together. One clause concerned four synagogue seats. One seat was to belong to a named person for life in return for two gold coins each year. Those two coins, together with the rental income from the other three seats, were to fund an annual yahrzeit lamp, with the remaining money distributed to Torah scholars. At the end of the will there was another strengthening clause stating that everything above had been done in every legally effective and sufficient manner, with the full force of valid Jewish testamentary instruments, gifts, and obligations. The heir sought to invalidate the clause on the ground that the kinyan related to property not yet in existence, relying on the Rama in siman 212:7 regarding a future charitable interest. Noda BiYehuda rejects the claim. Although the kinyan mentioned at the beginning of the will cannot necessarily be read as a kinyan on the substance of the synagogue seats, because it was merely an instruction to the witnesses to perform a kinyan on whatever he was then declaring, the concluding clause is phrased as an acknowledgment that everything had already been done in every effective and sufficient manner. That acknowledgment can be understood as admitting that an effective kinyan had been made on the underlying property itself. There is, however, a distinct issue concerning the seat granted to the named individual for life while its annual rental was designated for charity. The substance of that seat could not simultaneously have been conveyed to charity during the beneficiary's lifetime, since the will gave him the seat itself for that period. Only the rental income was designated to charity, and future rent is something not yet in existence. This resembles the rule in siman 212 concerning one who says that the rent of a house shall go to the poor. Nevertheless, the named beneficiary certainly acquired the seat for his lifetime, and because his acquisition was expressly subject to the condition that the rent be paid to charity, he must fulfill that condition. A condition can obligate a person even where the subject of the condition is not itself capable of present transfer. If, however, the beneficiary refuses to accept the seat entirely, there is no condition binding him; the seat reverts to the heir, and the heir is not obligated to give its rent to charity. The other three seats remain validly devoted to charity. After the beneficiary's death, the fourth seat likewise passes to charity, because the concluding clause that the will was made in every legally effective manner is interpreted as though the substance of the seat was given to the individual for life and, thereafter, to charity.
