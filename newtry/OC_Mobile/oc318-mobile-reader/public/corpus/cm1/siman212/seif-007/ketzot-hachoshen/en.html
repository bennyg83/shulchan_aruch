If a person instructed while a shechiv mera. The authorities challenged this: when he dies, his vow should be nullified. Maggid Mishneh answers that even in a gift of a shechiv mera, although the one who vowed is no longer alive, there is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased just as he would have been obligated to fulfill them had he remained alive. This is the primary explanation. Although we ordinarily do not apply the rule of fulfilling the words of the deceased unless the property was initially placed with a third party for that purpose, here the deceased had himself been obligated in the matter while alive, which is stronger and is no worse than prior placement with a third party. See what I wrote in siman 252, seif katan 2.
And he said, 'Whatever this tree produces shall be for the poor.' Rosh holds that if he says, 'When I acquire it, it shall be hekdesh,' the statement has no effect. Only if he says, 'When I acquire it, I will consecrate it,' must he fulfill his vow and consecrate it. Rambam, however, holds that even concerning something not yet in existence, although the consecration does not presently take effect, he must fulfill his words because of the vow. Therefore, even if he says, 'It shall be hekdesh,' or, 'It shall be for the poor,' he must fulfill his words. See Shach, seif katan 12.
There appears to be proof for Rambam from the Mishnah in Peah: one who collected peah and said, 'This is for such-and-such poor person,' according to Rabbi Eliezer, that poor person acquires it, while the Sages say he must give it to the first poor person he encounters. The first chapter of Bava Metzia explains that the dispute concerns a poor person collecting for another poor person. Rabbi Eliezer holds that through the migo that he could acquire it for himself he may acquire it for his fellow, while the Sages reject that migo. Since the collector himself is poor and, according to the Sages, the named poor person did not acquire, why must he give it to the first poor person he encounters rather than acquire it for himself? According to Rambam the answer is clear. Once he said, 'This is for such-and-such poor person,' he must fulfill his words because of his vow, even though he had not yet acquired the item. Because the obligation is only by reason of the vow, he may satisfy it by giving the peah to another poor person. According to Rabbi Eliezer, by contrast, the named poor person actually acquired it through the migo, and the collector must therefore give it specifically to him.
Responsa Radbaz, siman 134, and Responsa Maharit Tzahalon, siman 264, discuss whether one who vows to a particular poor person may redirect the gift to another poor person. Since Tosafos in the first chapter of Arakhin 6, s.v. 'before it came into the treasurer's hand,' cite Rabbeinu Baruch that one who vowed charity for the poor may use it for another mitzvah, it appears that the same applies to one who vowed to a particular poor person. We do not say that a statement to the poor is equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person. The obligation is only by reason of the vow, from the verse that treats charity as a matter of 'what is expressed by your lips.' It should therefore suffice to give it to another poor person. Hagahot Maimoniyot, however, imply that he may not change the recipient. In chapter 1 of Hilchot Zechiyah they cite a ruling concerning a husband and wife who undertook by kinyan and document to give specified amounts to their relatives, and one spouse died. The survivor could not change the half designated for the deceased spouse's relatives. Since no individuals had been named, the relatives of each side acquired half; a vow arose and it could not be changed. They add that 'a statement to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person,' and say that just as he could not redirect it to wealthy people, he could not redirect it from one poor person to another. Perhaps Hagahot Maimoniyot hold that the rule of statement to Heaven applies also to a statement for the poor and creates an actual acquisition, like a statement to hekdesh. If so, he certainly may not redirect it. According to most authorities, however, a statement to a poor person is not an acquisition and operates only through the law of vows, so he may redirect it to another poor person.
According to Rosh, who holds that where he says, 'It shall be hekdesh,' there is no vow obligation to fulfill the statement, the Mishnah's rule that he must give it to the first poor person can be explained through what Rosh writes in Nedarim 32 in the name of Rabbi Eliezer of Metz. Where a loaf of hefker lies before a person and no one else is close to it, he may consecrate it because it lies within his power to acquire; it is therefore not considered something not yet in existence. Here too, if the collector chooses to acquire the peah himself, his vow saying, 'This is for such-and-such poor person,' would take effect, since acquisition was within his power and the object would not be treated as something not yet in existence. But if he does not intend to acquire it personally and it never comes into his possession, the vow does not take effect at all. Even Rabbi Meir, who permits transfer of something not yet in existence, requires that it ultimately come into the transferor's possession, as Tosafos write in chapter HaSefinah, Bava Batra 79. Therefore he gives it to the first poor person without acquiring it himself, and the vow does not take effect; but if he first acquired it for himself, the vow would take effect retroactively because acquisition had been within his power.
