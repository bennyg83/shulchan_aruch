Something that has no tangible substance cannot be acquired. See Responsa Neot Deshe, siman 113, concerning holders of a meat-tax lease, namely the government levy imposed on kosher meat and poultry at a fixed rate. The holders of the governmental concession would pay two months in advance as security, with the understanding that if they withdrew during the term that sum would be forfeited to the government and they would have no further liability. They in turn would contract with individual residents, each resident renting from them the right for one year to slaughter and consume meat as he wished, and each resident likewise paid two months in advance. The question was whether this rental arrangement is legally binding so that neither party may retract. Neot Deshe writes that although the subject matter is both something not yet in existence, since no one becomes liable for the levy until he actually consumes meat, and also something lacking tangible substance, since what is rented is merely permission to slaughter and eat, nevertheless the arrangement is acquired and neither side may retract. Maharshal, Responsa siman 36, ruled similarly concerning one who rents the exclusive right to sell spirits, holding that a kinyan is effective, as did Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 27. All of Maharshal's reasons apply here as well. In addition, this is established custom and the law of the government, under which a person who rents such a levy, whether for an entire town or for himself individually, acquires the right. The customary forms of acquisition, such as a handshake used by merchants or a written agreement, therefore bind the parties. Merely leaving the two months' advance payment with the concession holders does not free a withdrawing resident unless they explicitly stipulated from the outset that forfeiture of that amount would discharge him. He further discusses persons who had long held rural tavern leases around the city and contracted for a year with the holders of the meat-tax concession. In the middle of the year their tavern leases expired and they wished to move elsewhere. The concession holders demanded payment for the entire year, arguing that even had they remained in town but eaten no meat they would still owe according to the agreement. The residents argued that because they were moving away and would have to pay the levy in their new place, they should be exempt. Neot Deshe analyzes the matter at length and rules that where the residents move during the year, the agreement is canceled prospectively. They must pay in full for the amount of meat they would ordinarily have consumed during the elapsed period even without the special agreement. As to additional meat they consumed because they believed they would not pay further levy, they pay only two thirds of the relevant amount, and the value of the meat itself is included in that calculation. In some cases this may leave no additional levy at all. The concession holders nevertheless have the option to demand the contractual amount for the elapsed period if that produces a larger sum than the two-thirds calculation. Where there is a factual dispute about how much was eaten, the applicable oath rules depend on whether the concession holders claim a definite amount or are themselves uncertain. If they claim with certainty and the residents deny it, the residents must swear. If the residents themselves are uncertain, they must pay, while the concession holders accept the appropriate confirmation of their claim. If the concession holders do not claim with certainty, residents who state a definite amount are subject only to the applicable ban, while residents who are uncertain must accept a ban that they do not know of consumption beyond a stated amount and then pay only for the amount they acknowledge with certainty. These rules apply whether the residents paid two months in advance with an express forfeiture clause, paid it without such a clause, or paid nothing in advance. All of this, however, assumes that both sides either could have anticipated the move or both could not have anticipated it. If the concession holders had no reason to anticipate that the residents would move while the residents themselves should have anticipated it, the residents must pay for the entire year. In that circumstance, if the parties expressly stipulated that the two-month advance would be forfeited upon withdrawal, the residents are discharged by that forfeiture; if the advance was paid without such a stipulation, it does not discharge them and they remain liable for the full year. If the entire year's payment had already been made in advance, the concession holders need not return anything, at least where both sides equally could or could not have anticipated the move, and possibly even where the concession holders could have anticipated it but the residents could not.
