The eating of the fruits of this palm. The wording "he shall eat" can be problematic because it sounds like transferring only an act or benefit rather than a tangible object. The Shulchan Aruch therefore treats such language as something without substance. The Tur, however, appears to distinguish whether the fruits themselves already exist. If the fruits are already present, language referring to eating them can be effective because the subject ultimately consists of tangible fruits. Bnei Yaakov questioned this from Rosh's commentary to Nedarim, where wording such as "I shall not eat" can be treated as a vow concerning an intangible act. But Rosh there actually distinguishes between negative wording, "that I shall not eat," which lacks a tangible object for the vow, and the formulation, "Konam that which I eat," which can attach to the food consumed. In Yoreh Deah, "Konam that which I eat" is a valid vow, while "Konam that which I do" or "that which I speak" is ineffective. Thus eating can have sufficient substance for the law of vows when the formulation attaches to the consumed object. This does not automatically prove that acquisition language, "you shall eat," effects a property transfer. The Shulchan Aruch can still compare it to the deficient promise, "I will give." The Tur's position, however, is not contradicted by the law of vows, because where actual fruits already exist, the eating language can be understood as referring to those tangible fruits.
The distinction is therefore precise. "I shall not eat," phrased as a mere negation of conduct, has no tangible subject. "Konam that which I eat" can attach to the food and is a valid vow. Eating itself can thus be connected to a tangible object. Still, the requirements of acquisition differ from the requirements of a vow. A vow may prohibit one's benefit from an object through language focused on use. A transfer of property ordinarily requires language that conveys the property itself. Therefore the Shulchan Aruch can reject "you shall eat" as defective acquisition language even if it would have significance in the law of vows. The Tur's view that it can work where the fruits already exist is consistent with the understanding that the tangible fruits supply the needed subject.
