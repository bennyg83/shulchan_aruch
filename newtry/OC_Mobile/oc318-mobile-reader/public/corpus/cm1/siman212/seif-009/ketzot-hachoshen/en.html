If the recipient already consumed the funds, there is ordinarily no reimbursement merely because the donor failed to separate the vowed charity in the prescribed form. But if the funds were used to purchase merchandise for him or to discharge his debt, so that he was enriched by the expenditure, there can be liability under the law of direct enrichment. Even this must be distinguished from true priestly gifts. Where the Torah already vested property in the kohen class and a person wrongfully consumes or destroys it, monetary liability can arise for damaging another's property. By contrast, a promise such as, "I will give one tenth of whatever I earn to the poor," does not immediately make a tenth of future earnings the property of the poor under Rosh's view. The promisor is merely obligated by vow to separate and give later. If he failed to separate, he violated his vow, but he did not consume property already owned by the poor. There is therefore no automatic restitution obligation merely because the earmarked fraction was not separated. Even according to Rambam, who gives strong force to declarations for the poor, the words do not necessarily create an ordinary proprietary acquisition in future property. They create a binding religious obligation under the law of charity. Nevertheless, where a person vows, "I will give a tenth of whatever I earn," the better practical understanding is that he did not mean those exact coins. He undertook to give an amount equal to one tenth of the profit. Therefore, even after he spent the original money, the monetary measure of his vow remains outstanding. He must still give the poor an amount corresponding to one tenth of what he earned.
