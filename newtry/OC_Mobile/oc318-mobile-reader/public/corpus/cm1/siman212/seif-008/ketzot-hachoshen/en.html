He purchased land intending to dedicate it to consecrated use. Mordechai discusses one who said, "I give this land so that a synagogue should be built upon it." Even if the community may not change the designated use without the donor's consent, the donor likewise cannot simply retract. Mordechai compares it to one who says, "I will give such and such to a wealthy person," where, if he intended a binding vow, he may not retract. All the more here, since the public includes poor people, the declaration can have the force of a vow. I do not understand the comparison to a promise to give to a wealthy person "if he intended it as a vow." A vow does not ordinarily attach to a bare promise of conduct merely because the speaker inwardly intended it to be a vow. Even where a person expressly uses vow terminology concerning his own future act, the rules depend on recognized language of vows or their derivatives. A simple statement, "I will give," without a valid vow formula is not transformed into a vow merely by unexpressed intent. Charity is different because Scripture gives special force to verbal commitments to charity. But that is not a general law of vows applicable to a promised gift to a wealthy person. Therefore Mordechai's formulation requires study. See also Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah siman 206.
