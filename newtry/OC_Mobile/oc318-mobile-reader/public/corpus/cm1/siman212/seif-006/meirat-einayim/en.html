The renter is not so. Meaning, if the owners customarily leave this land fallow, sowing one year and leaving it unsown the following year so that its strength increases for the third year, and this alternating practice is continuous, the renter may not deviate from the custom. See below in the Tur and Mechaber in simanim 145 and 141. The Tur writes another practical distinction: "A renter may not rent it to another, whereas a purchaser can transfer to another everything he acquired." The Mechaber omitted this because he follows his reasoning in Beit Yosef, where he questions the Tur from Rambam's ruling that one who rents land may sublet it. In the Perishah I wrote that there is a distinction between renting a house and renting a field. With a house, the renter receives it intact and must return it intact, so damage is readily apparent. With a field, he can weaken it by continuous planting or by planting crops that exhaust the soil, and the resulting deterioration may not be readily visible or known. See there.
What the Mechaber writes, "as will be explained in siman 320." See siman 320, seif 4, where the Mechaber writes that whether one is a fixed-rent tenant or a crop-share tenant, if he wishes to deviate from local practice the owner may prevent him. A purchaser of a field for its produce is different. Even if the local practice is to leave it fallow, he may plant it whenever he wishes. As to the phrase "to sow it or plant it," I wrote in the Perishah that this does not necessarily prove he can change from sowing to planting or the reverse. Rather, if the field's normal use is sowing, he may sow it continuously without leaving it fallow. However, this explanation does not fit the word "planting," since leaving fallow does not apply there in the same way. See the Perishah.
