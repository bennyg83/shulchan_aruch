We rule like Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability for dina de-garmi. See Sema, citing Tosafot and the Rosh, for the distinctions between indirect causation of damage, for which one is exempt, and dina de-garmi, for which one is liable. See there carefully. Responsa Masat Binyamin, siman 58, discusses this at length and concludes that according to the Rosh and other great authorities, in common and recurring forms of damage the Sages imposed liability on the damager in every manner. In uncommon cases, however, they distinguished between ordinary indirect causation and garmi. If a person himself performs the damaging act, or the damage follows immediately from his act, it is called garmi and he must pay as though he directly and intentionally caused the damage. If he does not himself perform the damaging act, but the damage comes through his causation, or the damage does not occur immediately, it is merely indirect causation and he is exempt. Masat Binyamin nevertheless concludes that in an uncommon case one should impose liability under dina de-garmi only where both elements are present: the person himself performs the damaging act and the damage occurs immediately. The reason is that we do not know decisively which of these distinctions is the essential one, and it is possible that the Sages themselves did not classify the case as garmi until both were present. The Rosh further writes that liability for garmi requires that the damage be certain and that the person perform an act upon the damaged object itself. Thus inciting a dog is exempt because it is uncertain that the dog will bite, and one who throws a vessel from a roof while cushions are beneath it and then removes the cushions is exempt because he performed no act upon the vessel itself. These are only cases of indirect causation. See the lengthy discussion there and in Shaar Mishpat. See also the end of Shach, seif katan 1, who adduces another proof from the first chapter of HaGozel and asks why Ameimar omitted chametz and mentioned only a document. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, Orach Chaim, siman 20, on that proof. Concerning dina de-garmi, Baer Heitev cites Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 51, that one who merely causes another cause is exempt. See Responsa Noda BiYehudah, siman 37, concerning Shimon who sent Reuven's consignment document, as a result of which the goods were seized at customs, while Reuven thereafter held property of Shimon against his loss. Noda BiYehudah rejects Shimon's claim that he acted inadvertently and did not know Reuven's goods were included. This is no better than one who shows a coin to a money changer, whom the Shach treats as negligent rather than inadvertent because he failed to complete the necessary examination. All the more so here, where Shimon should have considered that Reuven's goods might be included, and there is no greater negligence than writing a letter in Reuven's name and falsifying his signature. The difficulty is that Shimon sent the list together with the consignment document to a merchant in another city, and no damage yet resulted until that merchant showed the list to the customs officer. This might therefore be a cause of a cause, which Baal HaTerumot and Shach exempt. Noda BiYehudah suggests that perhaps it is not treated as a cause of a cause because the merchant acted as Shimon's agent. Although agency does not generally apply to a non-Jew, the Gemara in Bava Metzia 10 concerning agency for a transgression teaches that where an instrument necessarily acts, like a courtyard that acquires involuntarily, the sender is liable. Similarly, perhaps the exclusion of a non-Jew from agency applies only where the agent can choose whether to act, whereas here the merchant necessarily had to show the list to the customs officer and therefore functions like the sender's courtyard. Noda BiYehudah does not rely on that point, however. Since Reuven is in possession, he may say that he follows Ramban, who disagrees with Baal HaTerumot and holds even a cause of a cause liable. He further considers the Sema's distinction at the beginning of siman 386 that garmi requires an act upon the other's property. If Shimon succeeds in having the merchant return the consignment document and then returns it to Reuven, perhaps Shimon never acted upon Reuven's property itself, since he did nothing to the goods. Yet once the damage has already occurred because of the document, Shimon may already have become liable, and the document he later returns is of no legal significance. In any event, since the Sema himself writes that according to Ramban there is no distinction between ordinary causation and garmi and all are liable, Reuven, who is in possession, may say that he follows Ramban. Noda BiYehudah therefore concludes that Reuven is lawfully retaining Shimon's property. See there.
