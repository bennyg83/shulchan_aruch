Dina d'garmi. The Sema discusses its distinctions at length. The Shach writes that he saw the Ramban's extensive treatment of dina d'garmi at the end of his novellae to Bava Batra and began composing a commentary on it. But because the Ramban's foundation is that dina d'garmi is Torah law, he was compelled to distinguish carefully between garmi and ordinary indirect causation so the sources would not contradict one another. In the Shach's view, however, the primary rule is that liability for garmi is a rabbinic penalty. There is therefore no need for a universal conceptual distinction; the Sages penalized the cases they considered common and appropriate, and rabbinic enactments need not be analogized. He therefore abandoned that larger work but records the Ramban's rulings relevant here, first explaining at length why dina d'garmi is rabbinic. See there; see also Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 134; Rashdam, simanim 179, 332, and 400; Maharit, simanim 95 and 98; and Rama, siman 17. Baal HaTerumot writes that a cause of a cause is exempt, as in Shevuot regarding witnesses to a sotah. Thus one who sends a flame in the hand of a deaf-mute, imbecile, or minor (specifically a flame, as stated in siman 418, seif 7) and it burns promissory notes is exempt. The Ramban disagrees and writes that one who causes another's notes to be burned is liable; the Shevuot case concerns the scope of the Torah's oath of testimony, not tort liability. In light of the Shach's conclusion that garmi is only a rabbinic penalty, however, a cause of a cause should be exempt. Shach.

Maharshal, chapter HaGozel Batra, end of siman 47, writes that in all cases of garmi one is exempt for unavoidable compulsion, and this apparently includes accidentally burning another's notes. The Ramban disagrees because he holds garmi is Torah law. According to the view that it is a rabbinic penalty, one is certainly exempt for compulsion, as in siman 385, and likewise for inadvertence.
