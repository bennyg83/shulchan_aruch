We rule like Rabbi Meir. Ramban argues at length that liability for *dina de-garmi*, direct consequential damage, is Torah law. The Shach argues against him and concludes that it is rabbinic. The Shach's first proof is from Bava Kamma concerning one who burns another's debt instrument. After the Gemara discusses Rabbi Shimon's rule that something causing monetary value can count as money, Ameimar says that one who adjudicates according to *dina de-garmi* charges the burner the full value of the document. The Shach asks: if *dina de-garmi* is Torah law, how does Ameimar know that the authority who imposes it would charge for a document? Perhaps he follows Rabbi Shimon, whose Torah rule concerning property that causes monetary value is distinct. This is not a proof. Rabbi Shimon's doctrine and *dina de-garmi* are two different concepts. Rabbi Shimon says that something whose importance is only that it causes money can itself count as money, so direct physical destruction of it is like destroying a purse of coins. *Dina de-garmi* addresses a different problem: the defendant does not directly damage the body of the property, but causes the damage through a sufficiently direct causal act, such as allowing a vineyard partition to remain breached until another's grain becomes prohibited. The grapes are unquestionably money; the issue is the causal form of the damage. Ramban says explicitly that Rabbi Shimon can accept the first principle while rejecting *dina de-garmi*. Rabbi Shimon disputes Rabbi Meir regarding the breached vineyard partition even though he treats property causing monetary value as money. He charges for the sentenced ox or chametz because those items themselves are treated as monetary property, but he can exempt a merely causal act. Ameimar can therefore infer independently that the authority who accepts *dina de-garmi* charges for burning a debt instrument. The Shach next cites Tosafot in Bava Kamma 78. Rabbi Meir imposes fourfold or fivefold payment for a sentenced ox, and Rava explains that Rabbi Meir follows Rabbi Shimon, who treats something causing monetary value as money. Tosafot asks why not simply say Rabbi Meir follows his own rule of *dina de-garmi*. They offer several answers: perhaps *dina de-garmi* applies only where the item is commercially useful to everyone, like a debt instrument that can be sold and collected, whereas the sentenced ox is useful only to this owner; perhaps *dina de-garmi* imposes only principal and not the additional multiple payment; Rivash says *dina de-garmi* is only rabbinic. The Shach argues that the first two answers are difficult, because earlier Rabbah said that burning a debt instrument follows the dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages. If Rabbi Meir's *dina de-garmi* were enough in some cases, why did the Gemara not say the Sages too could impose principal payment for a commercially useful instrument while disagreeing only about sacred property or multiple payments? There is no difficulty. Rabbah himself did not adjudicate *dina de-garmi*. His entire discussion proceeded only through Rabbi Shimon's doctrine that the item itself counts as money. For the Sages, who reject that doctrine, and with *dina de-garmi* not yet being applied, the burner is wholly exempt. Likewise Rav Dimi's initial analysis rested only on Rabbi Shimon's doctrine. Only later does Ameimar introduce the independent authority who adjudicates *dina de-garmi*. Once that doctrine is introduced, Tosafot can distinguish between commercially transferable claims and an ox useful only to its owner, or between principal and multiplied payments. Thus the distinction between principal and multiple payment arises only for one who already accepts *dina de-garmi*. A debt instrument can produce principal liability through causal-damage law even though its body is not itself money for the Torah's special double-payment rules. Rabbi Shimon, by contrast, treats the underlying item itself as money and can therefore generate the stronger statutory consequences. The Shach also argues from the Gemara's question whether the special enactment allowing a victim to swear and collect applies to an informer. He says that if *dina de-garmi* were Torah law, the Gemara should certainly treat an informer like an ordinary tortfeasor. The early authorities already addressed this. The doubt can remain because informing is done by speech alone, and the question is whether the extraordinary evidentiary enactment should be extended to that special kind of damage. As I wrote in siman 388, the doubt can even concern whether an informer should be treated more severely than an ordinary tortfeasor where the informer makes a definite claim. There is therefore no proof that the underlying monetary liability is merely rabbinic. The Shach further questions Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Kamma, who says that even one who does not ordinarily accept *dina de-garmi* can nevertheless charge an informer by special penalty. He cites Ameimar as though one who rejects *dina de-garmi* would exempt the informer too. But Tosafot's proof is from the later passage where Rabbah says that one who voluntarily shows another's property to authorities is liable as though he physically handed it over. The correct textual reading there is "Rabbah," and Rabbah elsewhere does not adjudicate *dina de-garmi*. Thus informer liability can indeed be a separate penalty even under an authority who rejects general *dina de-garmi*. Maadanei Yom Tov likewise preserves the reading Rabbah, not Rava. The Shach emended it to Rava because he assumed Rabbah could not impose such liability, but that emendation erases Tosafot's very proof. There is another difficulty raised from Bava Kamma concerning a pit: why did the Torah need to exclude debt instruments from pit liability if one who causes their loss through *dina de-garmi* is liable by Torah law? Tosafot answers, according to the view that *dina de-garmi* is only rabbinic, that no Torah verse is needed to exclude them. According to Ramban, the answer can follow Tosafot's own distinction that *dina de-garmi* operates where the claim is commercially realizable by the public, such as a debt instrument that can be sold and collected. Rambam holds that sale of debt instruments itself is rabbinic. Before the Sages instituted their sale, a debt instrument was useful only to the creditor himself. On Tosafot's distinction, Torah *dina de-garmi* would then not apply to its destruction at all, so there was no need for a separate pit exclusion on that basis. Once rabbinic sale of documents was instituted, the instrument became commercially realizable to everyone, and Torah-level *dina de-garmi* could apply to the now-existing monetary consequence. I remain troubled, however, by Tosafot's distinction between property useful to everyone and property useful only to the claimant. Bava Kamma proves from the rule that slaughtering an innocuous ox leaves the act effective that one who damages another's lien is exempt. Yet an innocuous ox under Rabbi Yishmael cannot be sold by the injured party before collection, so the lien is useful only to him, much like the sentenced ox. If *dina de-garmi* applies only to claims commercially realizable to everyone, that case should not prove a general exemption for damaging liens at all. This is especially difficult because Tosafot identifies the authority holding that slaughter of the innocuous ox is effective as the Tanna who disputes Rabbi Meir and rejects *dina de-garmi*. But how does that follow? The breached vineyard partition is commercially relevant property, while the lien in the innocuous ox cannot be sold. The case may fall outside Rabbi Meir's *dina de-garmi* even if one accepts that doctrine. At present this remains difficult and requires further study.
One who adjudicates according to the law of direct consequential damage. The Sema develops distinctions between *garmi*, for which one is liable, and *grama*, for which one is exempt. On that basis he understands the Rosh and Tur, who exempt one who pushes or damages an object or throws a vessel from a roof, as treating those cases as *grama*. He argues that Rambam, the Shulchan Aruch, and the other authorities who impose liability in those cases recognize no distinction between *garmi* and *grama*, and that the Talmudic passages exempting *grama* follow the Sages, whereas Rabbi Meir, whose view we follow, imposes liability broadly. This criticism of Ir Shushan is not decisive. Rambam himself clearly exempts many cases of *grama*: one who sends fire through a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor; one who breaches a fence before another's animal; one who bends standing grain toward a fire; one who frightens another; one who places poison before another's animal; and one who incites a dog. Thus Rambam too distinguishes *garmi* from *grama*. His position is simply that pushing, defacing, and throwing fall within the category of actionable *garmi*.
The law of direct consequential damage. Ramban writes that one who merely causes another person to burn his fellow's documents is an indirect cause of an indirect cause and is exempt. He rejects comparison to the law of testimony concerning a sotah, explaining that the Torah oath of testimony applies only where the testimony itself creates or directly causes a monetary obligation; an indirect cause of an indirect cause is not enough. Baal HaTerumot and Maharam of Rothenburg reach the same conclusion and derive from the sotah discussion that an indirect cause of an indirect cause is exempt. They therefore write that one who hands fire to a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, who then burns documents, is exempt. That application requires qualification. Bava Kamma distinguishes between giving such a person an ember, where the giver can be exempt, and giving him a flame, where according to Reish Lakish, whose view the decisors follow, the giver is liable because his act caused the fire. In that case the liability is not merely *dina de-garmi*. It is ordinary liability for fire. If the fire then burns documents, one must ask whether the document exclusion applies to this form of fire damage, rather than simply calling it an indirect cause of an indirect cause. A clearer case for exemption is fire whose force as one's direct act has already ceased, so liability remains only because it is his property. According to Rama, who limits *dina de-garmi* to damage caused by the person himself and not by his animal or property, burning documents in that manner is exempt. The fire is then damage by one's property rather than direct personal *garmi*.
