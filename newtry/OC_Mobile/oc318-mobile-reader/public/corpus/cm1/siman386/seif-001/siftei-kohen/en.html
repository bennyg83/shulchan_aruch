We rule like Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates liability for direct causation, etc. Ramban wrote at great length concerning dina d'garmei at the end of his novellae to Bava Batra. I initially intended to compose an explanation and analysis of his words. However, I saw that his fundamental premise is that dina d'garmei is a Torah law, and because of that premise he was compelled to draw numerous distinctions between what is called dina d'garmei and what is called mere grama so that the various sugyot would not contradict one another. In my view, the primary rule is that dina d'garmei is only a rabbinic penalty. Accordingly, there is no need for such distinctions. Rather, as other poskim write, the Sages imposed a penalty in cases that appeared to them common and recurring, and one cannot infer one rabbinic enactment from another. I therefore withdrew from composing that work, though I will cite Ramban's statements insofar as they concern the laws of this siman. First I will establish that dina d'garmei is only rabbinic. It is true that the Tur and Mechaber above, siman 66, seif 32, rule that where one waives assigned promissory notes and then dies, his heirs must pay, because dina d'garmei creates an ordinary monetary liability rather than a penalty. The Tur states this explicitly, based on Baal HaTerumot, gate 51, part 6, who attributes this view to Raavyah and Raavad. Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe there cite no dissent and therefore rule simply that the heirs pay. Nevertheless, Tosafos at the end of Perek Lo Yachpor, in the name of Ritzba, concludes that dina d'garmei is a penalty, as appears from the Yerushalmi. This is also the view of Mordechai in Perek HaGozel Kamma, Semag, Agudah, Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Rabbi Yakar son of Rabbi Shmuel HaLevi in Responsa Rosh, Shiltei Gibborim in several places in the name of Riaz, Tosafos in Perek HaParah, Perek Merubah, and Perek Eilu Na'arot, Piskei Tosafos, Rabbi Avigdor in Mordechai, Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Kamma, and others. Terumat HaDeshen leaves the matter uncertain. Mahari ibn Lev writes that although Ramban holds dina d'garmei is ordinary law, one who is in possession may say kim li like those who regard it as a penalty. He appears to have thought that those authorities were a minority, but he evidently had not seen all the many poskim I have cited. Their position appears to me primary. It is also the view underlying the alternative opinion cited by the Rama below, siman 388, seif 2, that the heirs are liable only if judgment was entered against the tortfeasor during his lifetime. Maharam, Ritzba, Mahari Weil, Mahari Bruna, and Mahariyu likewise treat liability of a moser under dina d'garmei as a penalty and therefore do not impose it upon his son. This creates a difficulty in the Rama, who below cites that view, while above in siman 66 he rules without qualification like the Tur and Mechaber that the heirs of one who waived the note must pay. The same difficulty applies to Ir Shushan. Ramban, by contrast, holds that dina d'garmei is ordinary legal liability, not a penalty. Therefore, whether one waived a note, burned a note, or caused another loss through dina d'garmei and then died, his son must pay; and Ramban also holds one liable even when the act was inadvertent. He explains that perhaps the Yerushalmi does not adjudicate dina d'garmei. Maharshal follows him. I do not find Ramban's proofs compelling. His proof from a vineyard partition that fell, where the owner did not intend to cause damage, is not decisive. Ramban himself notes the difficulty from one who shows a dinar to an expert; some answer that because he had to learn and nevertheless rendered a ruling, he is negligent and therefore a tortfeasor. The same applies to one who failed to rebuild the vineyard fence. Ramban also discusses the Gemara in Perek HaGozel Batra where Rav Huna bar Chiya asked whether the liability of a moser is law or penalty, and Rav Nachman answered from the Mishnah that if land was taken because of the robber he must provide another field. Those who regard dina d'garmei as ordinary law may understand Rav Nachman to have resolved the question in favor of ordinary liability. Rambam and Maggid Mishneh appear to read the passage that way, and Rav Hai, Ramban, Rashba, and Raavan likewise. But those who regard it as a penalty can explain, as Ramban himself suggests elsewhere, that Rav Nachman merely cited the operative rule and did not resolve whether its basis was law or penalty. Indeed, how could the Mishnah prove the point, when the Mishnah itself may impose a penalty? The Yerushalmi explicitly says concerning that Mishnah that although land is not subject to theft, the robber was penalized. The continuation of the Bavli also implies that the question was not conclusively resolved, for after Rav Huna bar Chiya left, Rav Nachman asked what practical difference there was whether it was law or penalty. Had he already resolved that it was law, the language would have been different. Ramban also argues that since we rule that one does not derive one penalty from another, dina d'garmei must be ordinary law. This too is not compelling. His main proof is that we rule like Shmuel in monetary law and Shmuel holds in Perek HaNizakin that one does not derive one penalty from another. But Rambam appears to rule like Rav there, as Maggid Mishneh writes, and I gave sound reasons for this above, siman 385. Ramban further argues from the discussion in Perek HaNizakin concerning one who performs labor with purification water, where the Gemara asks why he is exempt if invisible damage is considered damage, and he explains that if invisible damage is not damage, even an intentional act may remain exempt because one does not derive one penalty from another. This is not proof. The questioner may merely have chosen one line of questioning, though in truth he may hold that penalties can be analogized. Moreover, Rabbi Elazar is the one who asks there, and perhaps he follows Shmuel, whereas we rule like Rav. The Gemara could also have answered, according to your reasoning, as Maggid Mishneh notes elsewhere. Even according to Raavad, who understands that the question only follows the view that invisible damage is legally damage, the passage does not prove that penalties can never be analogized. Raavad explains that one who acts with purification water is exempt because he intends for his own benefit rather than to damage, and therefore the Sages did not penalize him. If no penalty could ever be analogized, that explanation would be unnecessary. It therefore follows that even Raavad understands the sugya as compatible with the view that penalties may sometimes be compared, except where the actor did not intend to cause damage. Ramban's claim that the Yerushalmi does not accept dina d'garmei is likewise strained, because Reish Lakish himself says in the Yerushalmi, in chapter 7 of Kilayim, that this is Rabbi Meir's penalty, while in the Bavli Reish Lakish says that we rule like Rabbi Meir who adjudicates dina d'garmei. I find an additional proof that dina d'garmei is rabbinic from the end of Perek HaKones. The Gemara asks whether the enactment favoring a robbed person, permitting him to swear and collect, applies to one who informs against another. It says that according to the authority who does not adjudicate dina d'garmei there is no question, because liability for informing is also not adjudicated; the question exists only according to the authority who adjudicates dina d'garmei. If dina d'garmei were Torah law, why should there be greater uncertainty about this enactment than about fire? Tosafos in Perek Lo Yachpor appears to have intended this proof when Ritzba says that dina d'garmei is rabbinic, as proved in Perek HaKones. Tosafos in Perek HaParah states this argument explicitly: if dina d'garmei were Torah law, why would there be any question whether the enactment of the robbed person applies to a moser just as it applies to other tortfeasors? Maharshal attempts to answer that perhaps the enactment was made only where the tortfeasor damaged the property itself, not in dina d'garmei, even if Torah law. Tosafos certainly considered this distinction, but it is not persuasive. If the liability is Torah law, what difference is there whether the damage was to the object directly or through garmei? Ramban and Baal HaTerumot note that the expression used is dina d'garmei, not a penalty of garmei. This terminology proves nothing. On the contrary, the regular formulation, Rabbi Meir adjudicates dina d'garmei, rather than Rabbi Meir holds that one who causes damage is liable, suggests that this is an imposed judicial liability rather than inherent Torah liability. Ramban also argues that if it were a penalty, where one informed and then died or burned a document and then died, the son would not be penalized, just as the son is not penalized for the father's invisible damage. That is indeed correct according to the many poskim cited above. I find another proof from the fact that a moser who acted under compulsion is exempt. If dina d'garmei were Torah law, ordinary Torah liabilities generally apply to compelled and willing acts alike. Ramban senses this and answers that the Sages were lenient concerning mere speech and did not treat compulsion like voluntary action. This is strained. The simpler explanation is that informing, where he only points out the property, is a rabbinic garmei liability, and therefore he is exempt when compelled; but if he actually takes the property and hands it over, he performed a direct act and is liable even under compulsion. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Teshuvot Maimoniyot, and Mordechai say this explicitly: dina d'garmei is rabbinic, and therefore a Jew who was compelled to point out his fellow's property is exempt, while one who physically delivered it is liable. One should not ask why Rav Nachman did not resolve Rav Huna bar Chiya's question from the baraita that one who is compelled by non-Jews to point out his fellow's property is exempt. Perhaps Rav Nachman did not know that baraita, or perhaps it could be explained according to the authority who does not adjudicate dina d'garmei at all, in which case even one who pointed it out voluntarily would be exempt. The baraita mentions compulsion because its latter clause teaches that one who physically took and delivered the property is liable even when compelled. Indeed, Rava comments on that baraita that one who voluntarily points it out is like one who physically delivers it; if the baraita itself already taught that, Rava's statement would be redundant. I find another strong proof from Perek HaGozel Kamma. After the discussion whether burning a promissory note depends upon the dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages concerning an object that causes monetary liability, Ameimar says that one who adjudicates dina d'garmei collects the full value of the note. If dina d'garmei were Torah law, the earlier Gemara had already concluded that even Rabbi Shimon, who treats something causing monetary liability as money under Torah law, agrees that a document whose essence is not money is exempt. From where, then, would Ameimar know that one who adjudicates dina d'garmei imposes liability for a document? It is strained to say that Ameimar rejects the preceding analysis. Moreover, had that been his position, the Gemara should have said, Ameimar says, rather than, Ameimar said. Nor would it have needed to say, one who adjudicates dina d'garmei. It could simply have said that one who treats something causing money as money collects the value of the note. Rather, the earlier discussion concerns Torah liability, while Ameimar introduces a separate rabbinic rule: according to the authority who imposes dina d'garmei as a penalty, one who burns a note must pay its full value, for there is all the more reason to penalize the burning of a note than informing or leaving a vineyard fence unrepaired. Another proof is from Perek Merubah and Perek Eilu Na'arot, where Rava explains Rabbi Meir's liability for fourfold and fivefold payment when one slaughters an ox sentenced to stoning by saying that Rabbi Meir follows Rabbi Shimon, who regards something that causes money as money. Tosafos asks why it is not enough that Rabbi Meir himself adjudicates dina d'garmei. Their first answers draw distinctions, but Ritzba answers that dina d'garmei is only rabbinic. This answer is the clean one. The entire earlier discussion in Perek HaGozel Kamma concerns Torah liability, while afterward Ameimar teaches that according to Rabbi Meir's rabbinic dina d'garmei, the full value of a note is collected rabbinically. This also resolves the many contradictions in the sugyot that Tosafos struggled to reconcile with fine distinctions not stated in the Gemara. According to Ritzba, the Sages penalized only the kinds of common and recurring damage that they saw fit to penalize, and there is no need to force all the cases into one conceptual distinction. Mordechai says that Rabbeinu Yitzchak hesitated about most of the proposed distinctions. Semag likewise says that according to Ritzba all the sugyot are reconciled, and Teshuvot Maimoniyot explains that because dina d'garmei is a penalty, the Sages penalized common forms of damage and thereby all the passages fit. I find yet another proof in the wording of Ameimar. After all of Rabbah's statements in Perek HaGozel Kamma, including his statement concerning chametz after Pesach, Ameimar says only that one who adjudicates dina d'garmei collects the full value of a document. Why did he leave the case of chametz and mention only a document? If the principle were broad Torah liability, he should have said that one also collects the value of the chametz. Rather, he spoke specifically of burning a document because that is common. Robbing chametz and allowing Pesach to pass is uncommon, and moreover the burning actually causes the robber to pay the victim and lose his own benefit, so there is less reason to impose a special penalty. This also better explains the poskim who infer from Ameimar's limitation to a burned document that he did not reject Rabbah concerning throwing a coin into the sea, damaging the form of a coin, or cutting the ear of another's cow. Those cases are all unusual: throwing a coin into the great sea requires clear water and only tossing without lifting; flattening a coin refers to striking it without reducing its metal; and not every animal is destined for the altar. They therefore were not included in the rabbinic penalty. This appears clear to me. It is also possible that Ameimar himself was uncertain whether dina d'garmei is ordinary law or penalty, because Rav Huna bar Chiya's question to Rav Nachman was not conclusively resolved. He therefore states only that according to the authority who treats dina d'garmei as a penalty, the full value of the note is collected, and at the end of Perek HaKones the question is whether, according to that same view, the enactment for a robbed person also applies to a moser.
Who adjudicates dina d'garmei. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 134, at length; Responsa Rashdam, simanim 179, 332, and 460; Responsa Maharit, simanim 95 and 98; and the cited responsa, siman 17, pages 46 and 47.
Dina d'garmei. Baal HaTerumot, gate 51, part 6, writes that an indirect cause of an indirect cause is exempt. Thus, one who sends fire in the hand of a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, meaning where he gives them a flame, and they burn documents, is exempt. Ramban disagrees and holds that one who causes another to burn his fellow's documents is liable. He says there is no comparison to the rule in Shevuot concerning witnesses to a sotah, because the Torah imposed the oath of testimony only for testimony that itself creates monetary liability or directly causes it, while a secondary causal link is merely a denial of information and contains no monetary liability. Ramban sharply rejects the comparison. According to what I concluded above, however, that dina d'garmei is only a rabbinic penalty, a secondary indirect cause should be exempt.
Garmei. See the Sema, who writes at length and criticizes Ir Shushan for distinguishing between garmei, which is liable, and ordinary grama, which is exempt. The Sema's criticism is not justified. Ir Shushan did not attribute his statement specifically to Rambam; he simply held that there is a distinction between dina d'garmei and grama, and that everyone agrees that ordinary grama is exempt. This distinction is explicit in Rashba cited by Beit Yosef, and Ramban also states it in his work on dina d'garmei. The Rif appears to hold likewise, as do Tosafos, the Rosh, the Tur, and other poskim. The Mechaber's own heading to this siman describes it as the distinction between grama in damages and dina d'garmei. Maharshal also writes this. Maharshal nevertheless attempts to say that Rashi too distinguishes between them, and criticizes Ramban for saying that in Rashi's view dina d'garmei and grama are one concept. With respect, Maharshal did not grasp Ramban's point. Ramban never meant that Rashi misunderstood the sugya. Rather, he means that according to Rashi, the two expressions describe one basic category, and Rashi's statements exempting grama were written according to Rabbah, who did not adjudicate dina d'garmei. According to our ruling that dina d'garmei is adjudicated, those cases would also be liable unless another reason exempts them. This is borne out by Rashi's language in Perek HaGozel Kamma, where he explains all of Rabbah's cases as exempt because grama in damages is exempt, without drawing a distinction between burning a document and the other cases. It is also explicit at the end of Perek Lo Yachpor, where Rashi explains the phrase, grama in damages is prohibited, to mean that even according to one who does not adjudicate dina d'garmei and exempts payment, one may not deliberately cause the damage. Likewise at the beginning of Perek HaKones, Rashi says of the four cases exempt from human law that the tanna holds grama in damages is exempt, a formulation that implies a disputed principle. Thus, Ramban correctly inferred that Rashi views garmei and grama as one underlying category. See also my discussion below, siman 418, seif 9, where I show that Rambam and Raavan also appear to treat them as one basic concept.
But with an animal, according to all opinions he is exempt. Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaParah writes this in the name of Raah citing his teacher. This requires study, because Raah's teacher was Ramban, and Ramban himself concludes in his work on dina d'garmei that if one's ox ignited a fire and burned another's documents, or tore or erased them, the owner is liable, because this is the animal's act and differs from damage caused by an odor that comes on its own. Nevertheless, according to what I concluded above, that dina d'garmei is only a rabbinic penalty and that the Sages did not penalize uncommon cases, damage of this kind caused by an animal is exempt. See Shiltei Gibborim in Perek HaParah in the name of Riaz.
Therefore, etc. Maharshal writes that all cases of dina d'garmei are exempt when caused under compulsion, apparently including one who burns documents under compulsion. Ramban, however, distinguishes. He says that informing, waiving a note, and similar cases involve speech alone, and the Sages were lenient and did not equate compulsion with willingness there; but one who burns a document under compulsion is liable because this is an act, not a waiver. According to my conclusion that all dina d'garmei is a rabbinic penalty, however, it is obvious that one is exempt under compulsion, just as one who renders another's property ritually impure, mixes it, or pours libation wine is exempt when acting under compulsion. The same applies to inadvertence. Ramban's liability even for inadvertent garmei follows only from his view that the liability is Torah law.
One who pushes, etc. Rabbeinu Yitzchak, the Rosh, and many other poskim hold that one who pushes a coin into the sea or merely damages the form of a coin is exempt. The Rama cites their view below, seif 3, and Hagahot Ashri and Maharshal rule likewise. This appears primary, as I wrote above. It applies specifically where he did not first lift the coin and merely pushed it into the great sea, specifically where the water is clear and the coin can be seen, and in the case of damaging the coin, specifically where he did not reduce its metal. If he lifted it first, he acquired it as a thief and must perform restitution. Maharshal, citing Tzafnat Paneach, adds that if one struck a silver vessel, gold vessel, or ring and reduced its value, it too is treated like a coin and he is exempt. This is found in Raavan. It is possible, however, to distinguish. With a coin the loss may concern only its form, whereas a vessel was initially a usable vessel and afterward is no longer one. This law is mentioned in the Gemara and poskim only concerning coins, and the matter requires study.
Even though he did not lift it, etc. The Gemara says that had he lifted it, he would have stolen it and would be obligated in restitution. Maharshal writes that even if he lifted it and must make restitution, he can discharge his obligation by paying the cost of a diver and need not personally trouble himself further. He understands Tosafos below, concerning appreciation from dye on wool, to imply this. I do not agree. Once he is obligated in restitution, he does not discharge that obligation until he returns it to the place from which he took it. Tosafos there does not prove otherwise, because there the wool lies before the owner; if the owner insists on actual return, he may insist. This appears correct.
And afterward he waived it, etc. See siman 66 and what I wrote there in seif 32.
