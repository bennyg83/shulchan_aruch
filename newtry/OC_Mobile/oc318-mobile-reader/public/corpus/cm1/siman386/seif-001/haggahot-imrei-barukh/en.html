(Shach, seif katan 13) But if he took it and burned it, he would be liable were it not for the verse. Note: This requires study. If so, Tosafot should have said that if he took it and it was later burned he would be liable, for if the laws of robbery applied to debt documents, the robber would be liable even for unavoidable accidents where the document burned by itself. However, according to Rashi in Sanhedrin 72a, passage beginning "and is he not," who writes that a robber is no worse than a borrower because all the benefit is his and therefore he must pay, the robber's liability for unavoidable accidents stems from the benefit he derives from the stolen object while it is under his control. One can therefore say that this applies only to an object from which the robber can derive use or some other benefit. A debt document written in the creditor's name provides no practical benefit to the robber. Accordingly, even if the laws of robbery applied to documents, the robber would not be liable for unavoidable accidents. If it burned by itself he would be exempt, and the verse excluding documents from robbery would be needed only to exempt even one who takes and burns the document himself. I initially thought more simply that since documents are excluded from the laws of guardians, and even a borrower has no liability for them, there would likewise be no liability for unavoidable accidents even if robbery applied. But this does not appear from Tosafot, Pesachim 29a, passage beginning "by right," who write that one who steals from a gentile becomes liable for responsibility immediately upon the theft. Thus, even though there is no law of guardians toward a gentile, Tosafot took it for granted that theft from a gentile creates liability. We must therefore say that Tosafot do not follow Rashi's explanation in Sanhedrin that the robber's liability resembles that of a borrower.
