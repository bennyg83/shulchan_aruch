We rule like Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates liability under dina de-garmi. Therefore one who pushes another's coin into the sea, etc. The laws of grama and garmi are numerous in the Gemara and among the poskim, while the Mechaber's and Rama's formulations here are brief and difficult to understand. In order to clarify the essence of their dispute, I must set out the principal sources. In Bava Kamma 98 there are four statements in Rabbah's name: one who throws another's coin into the great sea is exempt; one who effaces the form of another's coin is exempt; one who cuts the ear of another's cow is exempt; one who burns another's promissory note is exempt. The Gemara says that the last statement depends upon the dispute whether an item that causes monetary value is itself treated as money. It then cites Amemar: according to one who adjudicates dina de-garmi, one who burns a promissory note pays the full value of the debt; according to one who does not, he pays only the value of the paper. In practice Rafram compelled Rav Ashi, who had burned a promissory note in his youth, to pay its full value. The Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel writes broadly: anyone who causes damage to another's property is liable. He then includes one who removes cushions from beneath a falling vessel, burns a promissory note, sells a promissory note and later forgives it, pushes a coin into the sea, cuts the ear of a cow, or effaces the form of a coin. The Maggid Mishneh observes that the last three cases are stated as exempt in the Gemara according to Rabbah, because Rabbah did not accept dina de-garmi. The commentators, however, rule that according to our halachah all of them are liable, and this is the view of Rabbeinu Chananel, the Rif, and the other poskim. From this it emerges that the Rambam and those authorities treat all these cases as dina de-garmi and impose liability because we rule like Rabbi Meir. They evidently understood Amemar's statement about burning a promissory note as an especially strong example. Even though the note itself was never actual money, Rabbi Meir still imposes liability. All the more so the other causal acts involving actual property. On their approach there is no fundamental distinction between dina de-garmi and ordinary grama in damages. Talmudic passages that exempt grama follow the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir. This is why the Rambam begins, "Anyone who causes damage to another's property is liable," grouping them together. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes that Rabbah's reason in all these cases is one and the same, namely that he did not accept dina de-garmi, while the halachah does not follow him but rather the conclusion demonstrated by Rafram. The Mechaber therefore begins, "We rule like Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates dina de-garmi; therefore one who pushes a coin, etc." He too understands the Rambam as imposing liability broadly because we rule like Rabbi Meir. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who writes according to the Rambam that these cases are garmi but ordinary grama is exempt according to everyone. Rama's "some say" follows Ri, the Rosh, and the Tur, who hold that because we rule both that grama in damages is exempt and that dina de-garmi is liable, the two categories must be distinguished. The Rosh writes that there are three categories when one causes another's monetary loss indirectly. First, an item that causes monetary value, disputed by Rabbi Shimon and the Sages, where we rule like the Sages that it is not treated as money. Second, ordinary grama in damages, from which everyone exempts, as seen in the case of distancing a ladder from a dovecote. Third, dina de-garmi, disputed by Rabbi Meir and the Sages. The Rosh explains that garmi applies where the person himself directly acts upon another's property and the damage is certain. Burning a document or forgiving a debt is direct action causing another's monetary loss. Showing a coin to a money changer and declaring it valid immediately causes the owner to rely upon that statement. Showing another's property to a gentile oppressor is like handing it into a trap and is treated as a direct act. In the law of mixed species, where his produce is intermingled with another's and he fails to fence it after warning, his abandonment is treated like an act upon the other's property. One who renders an erroneous judgment and commands the innocent party to pay, or declares permitted meat forbidden and has it fed to dogs, likewise performs a direct legal act causing loss. By contrast, setting a dog or snake upon someone is uncertain because perhaps it will not bite. Removing cushions from beneath a falling vessel, although the damage is certain, is not an act upon the property itself. Sending a fire in the hand of a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor leaves the direct act to them. Breaking a fence before another's animal merely allows it to leave on its own. Concealing another's grain from the fire does not itself damage the grain. Frightening another causes the victim's own reaction to produce the loss. The Rosh further distinguishes whether the damage occurs immediately at the time of the causal act. Burning a note, forgiving a debt, declaring a coin valid, showing property to an oppressor, failing to fence mixed species once the requisite increase occurs, or rendering a judgment all produce the loss immediately. In the exempt cases the loss occurs only later. The vessel breaks only after it reaches the ground, the dog bites only after being set on the victim, the fire causes damage only after being handed to the incompetent person, and the frightened person is harmed only after his subsequent reaction. Mordechai likewise cites Ri that dina de-garmi is liability where the person himself directly produces the damage to another's property, but then says the distinctions are not fully satisfactory and even Ri had difficulty with many of them. Rivash writes that all these categories are fundamentally similar, but the Sages imposed a penalty in common and recurring cases and not in rare cases, so liability is rabbinic and penal in nature. The Yerushalmi likewise presents liability for tearing another's document as a penalty. Rama's later statement that some say in every case of grama, if the matter is common and recurring, beit din imposes payment as a penalty, refers to this Mordechai approach. This explains why Rama abbreviates and does not list all the Tur's detailed distinctions. According to that view all ordinary grama is exempt unless it is common enough that the Sages imposed a penalty, even if technically it would otherwise be called grama. From this one can also see the difficulty in the Ir Shushan's attempt to explain the terminology itself by Hebrew grammatical intensity, saying that the heavier form "garmi" indicates causation coming more directly from the tortfeasor than the lighter "grama." That does not fit the Mordechai, who holds the categories are legally alike and differ only because common cases were penalized. Perhaps such an explanation could be used specifically according to Ri and the Rosh. In my view one could instead say that exempt cases were called "grama in damages," as though the causation were attributed to the damages arising on their own rather than to the person, while cases imposing liability were called "dina de-garmi," meaning that the causal force is legally attributed to the person who brought it about. This can also fit the Mordechai. Even if the physical categories are similar, where the case is common and the Sages penalized the actor, the causation is legally attached to him. In rare cases it is not. Perhaps this was also the Ir Shushan's underlying intent. With this framework the general words of the Mechaber and Rama become clear, and I will now explain their individual rulings.
