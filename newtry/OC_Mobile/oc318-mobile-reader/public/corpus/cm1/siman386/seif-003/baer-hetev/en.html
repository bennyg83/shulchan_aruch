His. If one threw another's vessel from a roof, the Rambam holds that the thrower and the one who removed the cushion beneath it share the loss, each paying half. Sema.
In this. See Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 189.
Exempt. Hagahot Ashri, Or Zarua, and Piskei Mahari all rule like Rabbah in these cases except burning another's notes, and Maharshal likewise. This appears primary. The exemption applies specifically where he did not lift the object but merely pushed it into deep water, and where the water is clear so it can be seen. Likewise, regarding flattening a coin, only where he did not diminish its substance and did not lift it. Maharshal further cites Sefer Tzafnat Paneach that even striking a decorative silver or gold vessel or a ring and diminishing its value is like flattening a coin and is exempt. This source is Raavan. One can distinguish, however, that a coin without relief remains a coin, whereas a vessel may cease to function as a vessel. This law was therefore stated only regarding a coin. The matter requires study. Shach.
A vessel. Meaning another person's vessel. It seems this refers to a case where he did not lift it, but merely pushed it from the roof to the ground. If he lifted it, he stole it and must make restitution, as stated above. Ibid.
A document. Likewise, one who returns a paid promissory note to a non-Jewish creditor, who then collects from the debtor, is exempt because this is indirect causation. So write the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe in the name of a Rashba responsum. Responsa Maharashakh questions why this differs from burning another's notes. Responsa Rabbi Yosef of the House of Levi resolves the Rashba, as do Responsa Maharam Alshich and Maharam of Lublin, which cite the Rashba as halachah. Responsa Masat Binyamin also resolves the question. Masat Binyamin rules that returning a paid note to the creditor is only indirect causation, but returning an unpaid note to the debtor is garmi and he is liable. This is simple, for it is like burning the note. See Responsa Mabit, part I, simanim 23 and 54. Shach.
Indirect causation. The same applies to setting a dog upon another in siman 395, breaking a fence in siman 396, seif 4, and the cases in siman 418, seif 7, seif 9, and seif 11, and siman 420, seif 28. These are all indirect causation and exempt. The Tur and authorities also state that placing poison before another's animal is exempt under human law because it is only indirect causation. The Rambam likewise writes this at the beginning of chapter 4 of Hilkhot Nizkei Mamon. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted it. Bava Kamma 47b states that even if the animal ate ordinary animal food that happened to be poisonous to it, the person is exempt. It appears the same applies if he mixed poison into food fit for the animal. Ibid.
The damage. Responsa Rashba explains that this obligation applies specifically to damage occurring after the ban is imposed; he need not pay for damage that occurred before the ban. The Raavad and Ran likewise hold this regarding one who sells land to a non-Jew adjoining a Jew's field, where he is placed under ban until he removes the harm, unlike the Rosh who requires payment for all damage. Their words also imply that if he dies before accepting responsibility to remove the harm, he is exempt. Sema.
Common. The Rama's wording does not appear correct. I found no authority saying that every common instance of indirect causation is penalized with payment. Who determines which is common? The Sema himself notes that according to this principle even a third-party depositary returning a note should be penalized. Rather, only cases where the Gemara itself records a penalty are treated as such because the Sages evidently considered them common. Since garmi is a rabbinic penalty, we do not add cases beyond those they enacted. Shach.
