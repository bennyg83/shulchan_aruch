Or a third party who returned a deed. See Rama's gloss above at the end of siman 54 concerning a third party who improperly returned a deed and the matter was not discovered until the entire amount had already been collected from the borrower. There Rama rules, based on Rashba, that the third party is exempt because this is merely grama in damages. According to that, the present conclusion, "nevertheless we place him under ban," must refer to the period before the damage actually occurred or before collection was made from the borrower. But Rama's further statement that in a common and recurring case we impose a penalty and require payment applies even after collection has already occurred and the damage has been done.
Nevertheless, we place him under ban, etc. See Rashba responsum 1052. It is clear there that this applies specifically to damage that occurs after the ban. Damage that already occurred before the ban does not become payable. That is why the ruling first says that all these people are exempt. This accords with the Raavad, cited by the Beit Yosef, and with the Ran concerning one who sells land to a gentile adjoining a Jew's field. There too the offender is placed under ban until he removes the danger, as cited above in siman 175. It does not follow the Rosh, who requires him to pay all the loss. Their words further imply that even if he dies before undertaking to remove the harm, he remains exempt from payment for the prior damage.
And returned Reuven's money. Rabbi Yerucham's wording implies that even if Shimon told the gentile to return Reuven's money, he is still exempt under strict law. An actual incident occurred in which a gentile concluded a commercial agreement with a Jew to buy a quantity of distilled liquor at a stated price, according to the merchants' custom of striking hands to finalize the transaction. The gentile afterward went to another Jew living near the first and sought to make a similar agreement without revealing that he had already dealt with the first Jew, intending that if the second offered a lower price he would buy from him and abandon the first agreement. The first Jew sensed what was happening and sent word to the second, asking him not to sell to the gentile because a deal had already been made. The second Jew ignored the request, completed a sale, and delivered the liquor. As a result the gentile did not purchase from the first Jew. It appears clear that if, in the judges' assessment, there was no deceitful scheme, the second Jew is not punishable for this. The gentile came to him on his own initiative to purchase. Even according to the opinion that it is prohibited to take another person's regular gentile customer, that applies where the gentile regularly does business with the first Jew and the second actively pursues him and induces him to transfer his business. Here the gentile himself approached the second Jew. Although the first Jew asked him not to deal, he has no right to require the second to suffer a loss for his sake. Moreover, even where such conduct was improper, we rule that what the second party acquired is not taken from him. All the more so here, where the full elements of that prohibition are absent. The second Jew also has another defense: Since the gentile already made a deal with you and then also came to me, who can say he would not have bought from both of us in the agreed quantity? Rabbi Yerucham cites an even stronger responsum of Maharam: Reuven lent money to a gentile at interest. Shimon later approached the gentile and offered a lower rate, causing him to repay Reuven. Shimon is not liable to reimburse Reuven, because the loss is not certain. Perhaps Reuven would not have succeeded in lending the money again at the same interest rate. It resembles one who idles another's money. Nevertheless, Maharam says Shimon may be called wicked, even more than a poor person who interferes with another's pending acquisition. That condemnation applies specifically there because Shimon actively pursued the gentile after the gentile had borrowed from Reuven and induced him to take money from Shimon and repay Reuven. In our case the gentile came to the second Jew on his own, and the second did not tell him not to buy from the first. The matter therefore appears obvious. I write this to reject the ruling of one who ordered the second Jew to pay all of the expected profit to the first.
