Or a third party who returned a document, etc. Likewise, if a third party returned a paid promissory note to a non-Jew against a Jew and the non-Jew then collected from the Jew, the third party is exempt, because this is only grama. Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe cite this from Responsa Rashba. Maharshal challenges Rashba by asking why this differs from one who burns another's document. Responsa Rabbi Yosef of the House of Levi answers Rashba, and his explanation is straightforward. Responsa Maharam Alshich and Maharam Lublin also cite Rashba as practical law, and Responsa Masat Binyamin likewise resolves the challenge.
A third party who returned, etc. Responsa Masat Binyamin rules that returning the document to the lender is only grama, but returning an unpaid document to the borrower constitutes garmei and he is liable. He proves this from Responsa Rosh. This is straightforward, because it is equivalent to burning the document. See Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 23, and end of siman 54.
They are all exempt, because this is only grama in damages. The same applies to one who incites a dog against another, one who breaches a fence before another's animal, one who sends fire through a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, one who fans a flame together with the wind, one who bends another's standing grain toward a fire, one who blasts into another's ear, and similar cases. All are grama and exempt from payment. The Tur and poskim also rule that one who places poison before another's animal is exempt from human liability because this is only grama, and Rambam writes likewise at the beginning of chapter 4 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted this law. The Gemara in Perek HaParah teaches that not only where the animal ate literal poison, but even where it ate a plant that is ordinarily animal food but is poisonous to that animal, he is exempt. It appears that the same applies if he mixed poison into food suitable for the animal.
Some say that in every case of grama in damages, if it is common, etc. The Rama's formulation is not precise. I have not found anyone who says that every common form of grama automatically creates liability by penalty. Who could determine on his own which case is sufficiently common? Rather, Ritzba in Tosafos at the end of Perek Lo Yachpor and the other poskim who follow him explain why the Gemara imposes liability in some cases called dina d'garmei and exemption in other cases. Their answer is that dina d'garmei is only a penalty, and in those cases that the Sages of the Gemara regarded as common and recurring, they imposed liability. Thus only cases in which the Gemara itself imposed the penalty are included. The Sema writes that according to the Rama's formulation, even a third party who returned a document should be penalized because such a case is common. I do not agree. Specifically where the Gemara establishes a penalty do we say that the case must have appeared sufficiently common. In a new case, who says that it is common? Since dina d'garmei is only a rabbinic penalty, we do not add to it. We have only the cases the Sages themselves stated.
Therefore, there is one who wrote, etc. This is Maharam of Risenburg, who wrote in his notes about a case where Shimon was present after a sale had been completed and the non-Jew had accepted the merchandise. The Rama in Darchei Moshe questions the ruling, because the damage did not come immediately and the speaker did not himself perform the damage. He suggests that perhaps such conduct was common in Maharam's time and therefore was penalized according to Ritzba. In truth, the case requires study. The Rama also suggests that perhaps it resembles a moser, but this does not appear correct to me, because a moser causes the victim an actual monetary loss, whereas here the non-Jew merely returns the seller's own property. I examined Maharam of Risenburg's note. It describes Shimon owning a hide, Reuven purchasing it from him and bringing it to a nobleman. The nobleman asked Reuven for the final price, and Reuven said no less than one litra of silver. The nobleman accepted it at that price. Levi was present and then said that the hide was poor and overpriced. Reuven claimed that Levi thereby caused him a loss of five dinars. Maharam writes that if witnesses establish the facts, since the nobleman had already accepted the hide, even though he had not expressly said, I take it at that price, the sale had been completed upon his receipt of the hide. It appears that the hide had no fixed known value and could in fact have been worth one litra. Had the sale not already been completed, Levi would not have caused Reuven a loss, because the nobleman had never yet acquired it for one litra. Once the sale had been completed, however, the hide was sold for one litra and Reuven was obligated to pay Shimon that amount, while Levi's words caused the nobleman to reduce the price by five dinars and keep the hide. Thus Levi caused Reuven to lose five dinars from his own pocket, because Reuven remained liable to Shimon for the full litra. The Rama's abbreviated presentation omits the fact that Reuven bought the item from Shimon and brought it to the nobleman. In any event, even according to the Rama, the rule should apply only where the non-Jew keeps the merchandise and, because of Levi's statement, wrongfully withholds part of the agreed price although the merchandise is truly worth the original price. But if the non-Jew returns the seller's property, even if the seller is thereby forced to sell it later for less, Shimon is not liable, because he did not actually take anything from him. This appears correct.
