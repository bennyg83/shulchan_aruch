Likewise, one who pushes. Bava Kamma says that one who throws another's coin into the sea is exempt where he can say, "It lies before you; if you wish, retrieve it." The Gemara distinguishes clear water from muddy water and further distinguishes merely throwing the coin from first taking it into one's hand as a robber. Tosafot and Rosh explain that clear and muddy water are only typical examples. If the coin can be retrieved by a diver, the thrower can be exempt; if retrieval is impossible, he is liable. Nimukei Yosef and Ramban appear to understand differently. In discussing dyes placed on a monkey, they say that according to one who rejects *dina de-garmi* the actor is exempt when the substance is still visible, even though it cannot practically be recovered. This implies that according to Rabbah, visible clear-water placement can remain exempt even when retrieval itself requires unusual means. Thus Tosafot and Rosh make practical recoverability decisive, whereas Baal HaMaor, Ramban, and Nimukei Yosef give greater weight to the object's continued visible existence. The matter requires study.
His fellow's coin. Mordechai cites Rabbi Yakar that even Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates *dina de-garmi*, exempts one who throws another's coin into clear water. He distinguishes bodily injury from property damage. In bodily injury the Torah separately imposed medical expenses and lost labor, even though those are monetary consequences of the bodily harm. For injury to property, however, where the object itself remains intact and the owner must merely spend money to restore it, the additional expenditure can be mere *grama*. Hagahot Mordechai gives the same principle: burning a document, failing to fence a vineyard, or giving bad monetary advice destroys the monetary right itself and is actionable *garmi*. But where the object remains and money need merely be spent to restore its earlier condition, such as retrieving a coin from clear water, restamping a defaced coin, or treating an injured animal, the added expense is *grama*. This raises a difficulty from the rule that the owner of a pit must bring up the carcass. If the carcass is worth one zuz in the pit and four above, the Gemara asks why a verse is needed, since the tortfeasor himself benefits by bringing it up. According to the Mordechai's distinction, without the verse one might have thought the victim bears the retrieval expense, just as the owner bears the cost of retrieving a coin from clear water. The question therefore requires explanation. One could say that once there is some actual physical damage to the property, the tortfeasor must pay the entire cost necessary to present the damaged object for appraisal. Tosafot indeed writes that where there is an actual deficiency, the wrongdoer pays the entire resulting deterioration. Yet the Mordechai's own rule concerning medical treatment of an injured animal suggests that even where physical damage occurred, restoration expenses beyond the direct injury can remain exempt. The better practical distinction may derive from the special Torah duty to bring the damaged remains before beit din. Regarding pit, custodians, and other property damage, once physical damage exists the tortfeasor must bring the broken remains for appraisal. This obligates him to lift the carcass from the pit. The rule of the coin applies where there was no bodily damage at all. Thus if a coin thrown into the sea struck a rock and suffered actual damage, the thrower should also bear the reasonable expense of retrieving it for appraisal.
Or he defaced its form. The Shach writes that if the wrongdoer first lifted the coin, he is liable by ordinary restitution, whether he pushed it away or defaced it. I think that with defacement even prior lifting may not create liability. Rabbah states the qualification about prior lifting only in the case of pushing the coin into the sea. The omission in the other cases suggests that defacing the coin can remain exempt even after lifting. The reason is that mere defacement does not necessarily constitute a legal change that removes the coin from the category of "Here is yours before you." Tosafot links this to a coin invalidated by the government. Rashba, however, says that a coin changed into a bar undergoes a genuine change but the person who merely defaces without taking it can still say, "What have I physically diminished?" The liability is then only consequential. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch rule that a coin invalidated by the government cannot be returned as is, yet they still characterize liability for defacing the form as consequential damage. This fits Rashba: the legal change can prevent ordinary restitution by a robber, while a non-possessory tortfeasor is liable only through the resulting consequential loss. Maharshal also concludes that if one first lifts and then defaces the coin, he is liable because the coin has undergone change. But Tosafot's formulation leaves room to treat the matter as doubtful. Therefore, even after lifting, one should not extract money beyond what the accepted law clearly requires.
