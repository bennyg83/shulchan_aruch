If a person throws his own vessel from a roof while cushions and blankets lie beneath it so that it would not break, and another removes them before the vessel lands, causing it to strike the ground and break, the one who removed them is liable. The same applies in comparable cases. Rama: Some authorities regard this as indirect causation and exempt the one who removed them. According to this view, one who knocks another person's coin into the water or damages its stamped form is also exempt because the harm is indirect. Thus, even if the owner himself threw the vessel while cushions and blankets were below and then removed them, he is exempt: he is not liable for the throwing, since the vessel was not then destined to break, and the removal is only indirect causation. A scribe who mistakenly writes one hundred instead of two hundred in a document, one who causes another a loss through harmful advice, or an escrow holder who returns a document that he had no right to return, are all exempt because the damage is indirect. Nevertheless, the court places such a person under a ban until he removes the harmful condition, and the same applies to any case of indirect damage. Some say that where such indirect damage is common and recurrent, the one who causes it is required to pay as a penalty. Accordingly, some write that if Reuven sold merchandise to a non-Jew and Shimon told the non-Jew that it was worth less, causing Reuven a loss, Shimon must pay; this may even be treated as informing to the authorities, for which liability is imposed. If Reuven lent money to a non-Jew against collateral and Shimon offered the non-Jew a loan at a lower rate, causing the non-Jew to repay Reuven, Shimon is exempt because the damage is indirect, though he is called wicked. See also siman 156, seif 5, and siman 292 concerning one who lifts another person's purse.
