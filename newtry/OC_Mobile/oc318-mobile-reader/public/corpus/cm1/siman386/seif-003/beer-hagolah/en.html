dalet) The language of the Tur, following the Rif, who writes that the halakhah does not follow Rabba's exemption at the end of 26b, because Rabba held that dina d'garmi is not adjudicated. We rule otherwise, following Rafram, who compelled Rav Ashi to pay. The Rambam likewise rules this way in law 8, although he does not state it explicitly because he had a somewhat different textual reading and explanation of Rava's words, as the Maggid Mishneh writes.
heh) The Rosh explains in his rulings in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak that liability applies where he did not perform an act upon the damaged object itself, and writes that this is the reasonable distinction. Tosafot likewise write this in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak at the end of Bava Batra 22b. He further writes another distinction, that dina d'garmi applies where the damage follows immediately from the act.
vav) I searched and did not find that Tosafot or the Rosh explicitly ruled like Rabba to exempt one who pushes another's coin into the sea or defaces it. According to the two distinctions Tosafot and the Rosh give in chapter Lo Yachpor, it appears that they would impose liability in both cases, because the person acts directly upon the damaged object and the loss follows immediately from the act.
zayin) See there at the end of 26b, where Tosafot challenge Rabbeinu Yitzchak's distinctions from the case of one who declared a tamei item tahor and the owner then mixed it with his produce: why is he liable, since he did not damage the other's property directly, and the damage did not occur at the moment of his ruling? Tosafot therefore conclude that dina d'garmi is a penalty imposed for common and frequent forms of damage, as indicated by the Yerushalmi. The Rosh, however, writes that what the owner subsequently does on the basis of his ruling is treated as though the first person performed the act. The Sema ruled regarding a gentile who concluded a commercial agreement with one Jew for a certain amount of distilled liquor at a set price in the customary merchant fashion of striking hands, and then went to another Jew intending to purchase from him if he offered a lower price. When the first Jew realized this, he asked the second not to sell because the deal had already been concluded, but the second nevertheless sold to the gentile, who then bought from him instead of the first. The Sema rules that the second Jew is not penalized, absent deceptive conduct as assessed by the judges, because he did not pursue the customer and the gentile came to him on his own. Even though the first Jew asked him not to transact, the first cannot require the second to incur a loss for his benefit. The second also has the argument that perhaps the gentile would still purchase the agreed amount from the first. The Sema discusses the matter further.
