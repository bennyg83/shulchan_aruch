Therefore. See Baer Heitev in the name of the Shach, who writes that if the person acted inadvertently he is likewise exempt. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 44, concerning Reuven, who owed Shimon one thousand reichsthalers under a handwritten obligation containing a clause of trust. During the war of 1689, when French troops spread through Germany, destroying villages and plundering wine and grain, Reuven loaded his wine onto a ship intending to flee with it to a distant place beyond the enemy's reach. Shimon learned of this and had the authorities detain the ship until Reuven would pay him or provide adequate security. The delay continued until the French troops arrived, burned the city, plundered what they found, and took the ship as well. When Shimon later demanded payment, Reuven argued that Shimon had caused his loss, because but for the detention he would have taken the wine to Holland, sold it there, and paid the thousand reichsthaler debt. Chavot Yair rules that Reuven's claim is ineffective. Shimon cannot be held liable under dina de-garmi, since the Shach at the beginning of siman 387 rules that garmi is a rabbinic penalty and one who acted inadvertently without intent to damage is exempt. Shimon acted only to protect his debt. Moreover, the case involves merely uncertain causation, not certain damage, for which all agree he is exempt. Nor should Shimon be treated as though he directly damaged the wine because this was wartime, analogous to bending another's standing grain toward a fire where an ordinary wind will carry the flames, which is liable. Here the arrival of the troops was not certain and resembles an unusual wind. Although one who bends grain toward a fire is still liable in the heavenly court even where the wind was unusual, that does not permit Reuven to retain Shimon's money. Maharshal, cited by the Shach at the beginning of siman 28, rules that seizure does not help in cases of obligations only in the heavenly court, as does Rivash, siman 392. Furthermore, Shimon is certainly exempt even in the heavenly court, because unlike one who interferes with property to which he has no claim, he acted to protect his own debt and was not required to refrain merely because of an unusual and unforeseeable danger to Reuven's property. See there for further reasons.
