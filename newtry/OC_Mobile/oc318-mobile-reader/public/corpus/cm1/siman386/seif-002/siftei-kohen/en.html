Likewise, one who burns another person’s promissory note, etc. The Baal HaTerumot writes in Shaar 29 that if the holder of the document says that it represented one hundred, while the burner says that it represented only fifty, the burner takes the oath of one who admits part of a claim. If he admits part and says regarding the balance, “I do not know,” he must pay because one who cannot take the required oath must pay. The Beit Yosef cites this in seif 2, and the Darkei Moshe, Bach, and Levush likewise cite it without disagreement. This ruling requires careful study. We rule in siman 66, seif 39, and at the beginning of siman 95 that Torah oaths, whether a custodian’s oath or an oath upon partial admission, are not taken concerning documents. This is explicit in the Mishna in Shevuot 42b. One might have forced an interpretation of the Mishna according to one who does not adjudicate dina de-garmi, or where withholding the document does not itself cause loss and the claim concerns only return of the physical paper. Yet all the authorities state without qualification that no partial-admission oath is taken regarding documents, implying that this is so even where the denial prevents the claimant from collecting the underlying debt. Tosafot at the end of Shevuot 37 expressly indicates this, explaining that the exclusion of documents from an oath is especially meaningful according to Rabbi Meir, who imposes liability upon one who burns them through dina de-garmi. Several possible reconciliations of the Baal HaTerumot’s view occurred to me, but none was satisfactory. The principal explanation is that the Baal HaTerumot identifies dina de-garmi with Rabbi Shimon’s doctrine that an object causing monetary liability is legally like money, and he rules like Rabbi Shimon. We, however, do not rule like Rabbi Shimon, and therefore the Baal HaTerumot’s ruling does not apply. The Baal HaTerumot writes in Shaar 29, part 2, that if an agent admits the essential agency and says, “I was instructed to deliver fifty, but regarding the rest I do not know,” he must pay because he cannot swear. He explains that we rule like Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates dina de-garmi, and that an object causing monetary liability is like money. Just as a burner who admits part of the amount written in a burned document and says he does not know the remainder must pay, so too in the case of the shopkeeper, since an agent is presumed to carry out his agency and the issue causes monetary liability, the claim is treated as one concerning the money itself. He writes similarly in Shaar 51, part 6, that one who burns another person’s documents is liable even though the paper itself is not money, because he caused the loss of money. He derives this from Ameimar’s statement that one who adjudicates dina de-garmi collects the full value represented by the document, and writes that the law follows Rabbi Shimon that an object causing monetary liability is like money, even where its principal substance is not money. Since the person burned or tore the document by his own act, it is dina de-garmi. He further cites a responsum of the Raavad, who states that his practical ruling inclines to fixing the law like Rabbi Shimon and that the doctrine of an object causing monetary liability belongs to dina de-garmi. The dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages appears in the Mishna in Merubah regarding double, fourfold, and fivefold payments; in HaZahav regarding onaah; and in Shevuot HaDayanim regarding oaths. The Sages hold that one does not swear concerning consecrated property. Rabbi Shimon distinguishes between consecrated property for which the person bears responsibility, regarding which he swears, and property for which he bears no responsibility. Rashi explains that property for which he bears responsibility is treated as money because Rabbi Shimon holds an object causing monetary liability is like money. The Gemara in Merubah and HaGozel Kamma likewise states that this is Rabbi Shimon’s reason. The Baal HaTerumot’s own words therefore show that if we rule like the Sages, the burner does not swear. Most authorities rule like the Sages, including the Rambam and Bartenura in their commentaries to the relevant Mishnayot, and the Rif and Rosh in Shevuot HaDayanim, who omit Rabbi Shimon’s opinion. The Rambam and Maggid Mishneh also rule this way at the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchot Geneivah, chapter 13 of Hilchot Mechirah, law 8, the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut, and the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchot Toen. The Maggid Mishneh writes explicitly that even consecrated property for which one bears responsibility is exempt from an oath because the law does not follow Rabbi Shimon. Many other authorities likewise indicate this, and this is the principal law. The Baal HaTerumot’s further assumption that Ameimar adjudicates dina de-garmi because the law follows Rabbi Shimon also does not appear correct. The passage in HaGozel Kamma indicates that Ameimar may agree with the Sages, as I explained above at the end of seif katan 1. I later saw that the Ramban writes explicitly in accordance with my view in his treatise, at the end of page 111b. He states that regarding one who burns documents and similar cases we rule like Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates dina de-garmi, even though we do not rule like Rabbi Shimon concerning consecrated property for which one bears responsibility, fourfold or fivefold payments, or oaths. Although the item causes monetary liability, its substance is not money. If one directly damages it, he pays under dina de-garmi, but if he merely fails in guarding it he is exempt, for dina de-garmi was established only for a damager. Some say that a negligent custodian is considered a damager and must pay. In any event, a Torah oath is never taken except upon an object whose substance is money, not upon something that merely causes monetary liability. Thus no Torah oath is taken concerning documents, and onaah likewise does not apply to them. The Geonim agreed that Rabbi Shimon’s view is not accepted. The Ramban therefore states explicitly that no Torah oath is taken regarding burned documents. His words suggest more broadly that no Torah oath is taken in any case of dina de-garmi. This may be questioned from the responsa of the Rashba and Hagahot Maimoniyot cited below in siman 388, seif 7, seif katan 34, and from the Rambam as I explain there in seif katan 31, all of which imply that a Torah oath may apply to an informer even though his liability is only dina de-garmi. It appears that even authorities who impose a Torah oath in other cases of dina de-garmi agree regarding documents. Scripture specifically excludes consecrated property and documents from oaths. Where Scripture revealed an exclusion, it applies; elsewhere, it does not. Maharshal in HaGozel Kamma, siman 19, also writes simply that if the burner says, “I know there was a debt written in it, but I do not know how much,” he must pay because he cannot take the required oath. He gives no reason or proof, and all the objections above apply. He evidently follows his approach that the Sages who dispute Rabbi Shimon may nevertheless agree with Rabbi Meir regarding a document, because they exempt only leaven or consecrated property useful solely to this person, whereas a document can be sold or collected by anyone. He therefore imposes an oath as well. However, as I wrote above in seif katan 1 and below in seif katan 11, for the view that dina de-garmi is biblical, the principal answer is Tosafot’s second explanation in Merubah and Elu Naarot, that the Sages concede to Rabbi Meir only regarding principal damages, not double payment. It follows that they exempt from onaah and oaths as well. The Ramban’s ruling is therefore primary: no Torah oath is taken concerning a burned document. Scripture excludes documents because their substance is not money, and that exclusion applies even where withholding or destroying the document causes the creditor monetary loss. Above, at the end of siman 95, I concluded like the Raavad that where land has been physically ruined and the excavations cannot be filled, so that the claim is now for money, a Torah oath applies. That case differs. Land is excluded because it is immovable rather than movable. While it remains intact, its status is weaker for purposes of an oath; when it is physically changed and no longer intact, its legal status changes. Documents, by contrast, are excluded because from their inception their principal substance is not money. That characteristic has not changed. All the more so according to what I concluded above in seif katan 1, that dina de-garmi is only a rabbinic penalty, no Torah oath can be imposed here. Maharshal himself writes in HaKones, siman 30, seif 11, that according to the view dina de-garmi is rabbinic, no Torah oath applies at all. The law is therefore unlike the Baal HaTerumot and those who followed him.
One who burns, etc. The Ramban writes in his treatise on dina de-garmi, page 111b, that the same applies where a person stole leaven, Pesach passed over it, and the thief could have returned it to the owner by saying, “Here is your property before you.” If another person then burned it, that person must pay the thief under dina de-garmi, because he caused the thief to lose money from his own pocket. Maharshal, in HaGozel Kamma, siman 19, writes concerning this: “I do not know upon whom he relied to disagree with the passage in HaGozel Kamma, page 98b, which states that one who burns leaven enters the dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages. Since the law follows the Sages, it is explicit that he is exempt,” end quote. This is no difficulty. The Ramban follows the second answer, given in the name of “some explain,” in Tosafot at the end of Merubah, Bava Kamma 71b, and at the end of the first side of Elu Naarot, Ketubot 34, under the words “he holds like Rabbi Shimon.” Tosafot’s first answer is certainly not persuasive, as I wrote above in seif katan 1, for what difference does it make whether the item has value to the entire world or only to this person? In either case he caused that person a loss. I also found that the Ritva in Elu Naarot decided in favor of the second answer and wrote that it is correct. See there. Accordingly, the Ramban rules in accordance with the anonymous Gemara in Merubah and Elu Naarot, rather than Rabbah’s statement that one who burns leaven enters the dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages. Examine the Ramban’s words in his treatise, at the end of page 111b, and you will find exactly this. He cites the passages in Merubah and Elu Naarot and derives from them that the two questions are not dependent upon one another. Maharshal cites only an abbreviated version of the Ramban’s words and appears not to have examined them carefully. Nevertheless, according to what I concluded above in seif katan 1, that dina de-garmi is only a rabbinic penalty, the burner is exempt here because this situation is uncommon, as I wrote there. See there.
A promissory note, etc. The Rambam writes in chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel that one who designates his slave as an apotiki for a debt and then emancipates him must pay the creditor, because he removed the creditor’s lien and caused the loss of his money. The Raavad objects that this follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, while the Sages disagree and the law follows the majority. He distinguishes this case from burning another person’s document, because there the person acts upon the other’s property, whereas here the debtor acts upon his own property. The Maggid Mishneh writes that he does not know whether the Raavad’s reference to “the master” means the Rif, as is usual elsewhere, since this ruling does not appear in some editions of the Rif, though some versions rule like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. If so, the general rule applies that wherever Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel taught in the Mishna, the law follows him. This is a Mishna in HaSholeach. Moreover, the Maggid Mishneh says, it is compelling to rule like him here, because his reason is that one who damages another person’s lien is liable, and since we adjudicate dina de-garmi, this stronger form of damage certainly creates liability, as appears in HaManiach from the discussion of an animal that was slaughtered and whose meat is permitted. These words are not compelling. The Raavad’s intent is that he had before him editions of the Rif that did not contain a ruling like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. The law would then follow the Sages, in accordance with the Rif’s explicit statement earlier in HaSholeach concerning the dispute about a captive slave who was redeemed. The Rif cites a Gaon who rules like the Sages because the law follows the majority. Although the Rashba and Rosh infer from the Rif’s phrase “we saw a Gaon who ruled” that the Rif himself may disagree, the Rashba ultimately concludes that the Rif agrees with the Gaon. The Raavad’s gloss here therefore follows that approach. Furthermore, regarding damage to a lien, unlike the earlier case, the Rif did not use language suggesting disagreement. The Rif also writes in Get Pashut that the general statement attributed to Rabbi Yochanan, that wherever Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel taught in our Mishna the law follows him, is not accepted absent an independent reason. The Rashba in Mi SheAchazo and the Ran there also cite this in the Rif’s name. His reason appears to be that at the end of HaMadir the Amoraim dispute what Rabbi Yochanan held. The Maggid Mishneh’s proof from HaManiach likewise does not compel the Raavad. The Raavad already distinguished burning another person’s document, where the actor performs an act upon another’s property, from freeing his own slave. The Gemara’s challenge there from Rabbah’s rule that one who burns another person’s document is exempt is an a fortiori argument: if he is exempt even when acting upon another’s property, he should certainly be exempt when acting upon his own. The Gemara answers that in the document case one might think he merely burned paper, whereas damage to a lien is more substantial. The conclusion establishes that when a third party damages another’s lien, the same dina de-garmi liability applies as when he burns the other’s document. It does not necessarily establish liability where the debtor himself acts upon his own property. The law may therefore follow the Sages against Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel where the debtor himself caused the loss. This also explains why Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua states without qualification, “This teaches that one who damages another person’s lien is exempt,” and the Gemara challenges from Rabbah rather than saying that the matter depends upon the Tannaitic dispute between the Sages and Rabbi Meir. This implies that even one who adjudicates dina de-garmi may exempt where the person acts upon his own property. Nevertheless, the Rosh in HaManiach and HaSholeach rules like the Rambam, as do the Ramban, Ran, and other authorities in HaSholeach. Maharshal rules similarly in HaManiach, siman 28. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch likewise rule this way above, siman 117, seif 6. Rabbeinu Yerucham also writes so. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite Rabbeinu Yerucham as saying in the name of Maharam that if the property was an apotiki and the creditor forgave the one who damaged it, the landowner may sue him. The quoted wording is confused. Rabbeinu Yerucham himself first writes in Maharam’s name that the landowner may sue the damager, and then in the Rosh’s name that even if the creditor forgave the damager, the landowner may sue him. Meaning, even if the creditor forgave the damager, the owner of the land may still sue the damager. They further cite Rabbeinu Yerucham that if it was an explicit apotiki, the creditor does not collect from the damager just as he does not collect from the debtor’s other assets. This quotation is astonishing. Precisely when it is an explicit apotiki should the creditor collect from the damager. With an ordinary apotiki, the damager may say, “What did I do to you? Collect from the debtor’s other assets.” The comparison to the creditor’s inability to collect from other property proves the reverse: because he cannot collect elsewhere, he should collect from the damager who destroyed his lien. This is the accepted rule that one who damages another’s lien is liable. Tosafot in HaSholeach, the Ran there, the Rambam in chapter 18 of Hilchot Malveh and chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel, the Maggid Mishneh, the Tur in siman 117, and other authorities all explain Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel’s rule of liability for damaging a lien as referring to an explicit apotiki. Although they discuss damage caused by the debtor himself, it is obvious that a third party is likewise liable, and indeed a third party’s act is more clearly damage, as the Raavad himself indicates. The Rashba’s novellae to HaSholeach also state this explicitly. Rabbeinu Yerucham’s own words in Netiv 31, at the end of part 2, show that a damager is liable for destroying an explicit apotiki. He writes that one who damages another’s lien, such as by digging pits in land subject to a creditor, is liable because we adjudicate dina de-garmi. Maharam writes that if a person made his field an explicit apotiki and another dug pits in it, the damager cannot tell the landowner, “You are not my litigant because I became obligated only to the creditor.” The damager is liable to the landowner as well, who may litigate directly with him. The Rosh adds that even if the creditor forgives the damager, the landowner may still sue him. Rabbeinu Yerucham then cites his teacher Rabbi Avraham as saying that if the debtor explicitly said the creditor would have payment only from this property and another person dug pits in “another field,” the creditor does not collect from that damager, just as he does not collect from the debtor’s other assets. The words must be corrected to read “another field.” That is a true and straightforward ruling. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe apparently did not notice the textual error and cited the words as written, which is difficult. Finally, where a third party damages the lien, he is liable not only under the doctrine of damaging a lien, but also under Rabbi Natan’s rule. The Mordechai in HaManiach records that Rabbeinu Meir told his teacher Rabbi Yitzchak of Vienna that the Gemara’s case must involve damage caused by the owners themselves. Otherwise, the third party would be liable even without the special law of damaging a lien. One who digs pits in another person’s land owes the landowner for the loss. Since he owes the landowner, he becomes obligated to the landowner’s creditor under Rabbi Natan’s rule. See below, siman 86.
He must pay, etc. His liability is under dina de-garmi. Rabbah, who exempts him in HaGozel Kamma, Bava Kamma 98, does not adjudicate dina de-garmi. Maharshal writes in HaGozel Kamma, siman 19, that this applies specifically where the person did not take the document into his hand, in which case he is not a robber, or where the document was already in his possession as a deposit. If he took it into his hand and lifted it, however, he becomes a robber according to everyone and is obligated to restore it. He is then liable according to everyone, even Rabbah. The laws of dina de-garmi cannot be less stringent than ordinary grama in damages, regarding which it was concluded above that if one takes the item in hand he is a robber and liable. This is all the more so here. The robbed party therefore swears how much was written in the document, because the Sages enacted the evidentiary ordinance for a victim of robbery. This applies only where the paper of the document is worth a perutah for covering the mouth of a flask, so that he is certainly a robber. If it is not worth that amount, he is not certainly a robber, the laws of robbery may not apply, and the evidentiary ordinance for a robbed party does not apply. These are Maharshal’s words. His position does not appear correct to me. Rabbah states his rule without qualification, implying that even if the person took the document in hand, lifted it, and burned it, he is exempt. Moreover, Rabbah first states regarding one who throws a coin into the Great Sea that the exemption applies only where he rolled or pushed it away, but if he took it in his hand he stole it and must make proper restitution. Immediately afterward Rabbah states without qualification that one who burns another person’s document is exempt because he may say, “I merely burned paper belonging to you.” This implies that regarding a document he is exempt even if he took it in his hand. It also appears that Maharshal overlooked the entire passage at the end of HaGozel Batra, Bava Kamma 117, where land, slaves, and documents are excluded by Scripture from the laws of robbery. Therefore, when land is stolen and a river washes it away or it burns, the robber is exempt. The Gemara states there that even Rabbi Eliezer, who disagrees regarding land, concedes regarding documents. The same is explicit in Shevuot, at the end of page 37. It is clear there that because these items are excluded from robbery, even if a river washes them away or they burn after the theft, the taker is exempt because they remain in the legal domain of their original owner. Although the Gemara uses the expression, “Here is your property before you,” its meaning is that the item remained in the owner’s legal domain. The Rosh in HaGozel Batra writes: “If a river washed it away, he says, ‘Here is your property before you,’ meaning: take it where it is, as though he had never robbed it, because land is not subject to robbery and it was washed away while in the owner’s domain.” The Rambam likewise writes at the beginning of chapter 9 of Hilchot Gezelah: “One who steals land and it is destroyed on its own, such as where a river washes it away or it burns in a fire that descended from heaven, says, ‘Here is your property before you,’ because the land remains presumptively in the domain of its owner and responsibility for its loss is not upon the robber, unlike movable property, as we have explained.” The same applies to documents, since land and documents are excluded by the same verse. Indeed, documents are even more clearly excluded, for Rabbi Eliezer, who interprets the verse through inclusions and exclusions and does not exclude land, nevertheless excludes documents. This is also explicit from the Rambam at the end of chapter 8 of Hilchot Gezelah, where land, slaves, and documents share one law in this respect, as I explain below. The verse must exclude even a case where they burned. If it excluded only a case in which the documents remain intact, no verse would be needed, because even regarding movable property, such as leaven after Pesach, one may say, “Here is your property before you.” Rabbah states this in HaGozel Kamma, page 98b: all agree that regarding items prohibited for benefit one may say, “Here is your property before you,” for otherwise the dispute should have been stated regarding leaven on Pesach. The exclusion of land, slaves, and documents therefore teaches that they are never legally stolen at all. It is as though the taker never acquired them through robbery, and wherever they are, they remain in the original owner’s domain. HaGozel Kamma, at the beginning of page 96, expressly explains that one may say “Here is your property before you” regarding land and slaves because they remain in the owner’s domain. The Gemara challenges Rav’s rule that one who forcibly takes another person’s slave and uses him is exempt: if a slave is like land, why is he exempt? The answer is that the slave remains in the owner’s domain, and the case involves use during a time when the slave would otherwise not have been working, like Rav Huna’s rule that one who lives in another person’s courtyard without permission need not pay where the owner suffered no loss. Even the Raavad, who disputes the Rambam regarding slaves at the beginning of chapter 9 of Hilchot Gezelah, clearly concedes regarding documents. His disagreement concerning slaves is based only upon a verse indicating that slaves may be acquired through despair, “and he recovered the captives,” demonstrating that they are unlike land. Therefore, if the slave dies or burns, the robber pays his value, because the slave died in the robber’s domain. This reasoning does not apply to documents, for there is no verse teaching that they are acquired through despair. They therefore remain like land. In any event, regarding slaves themselves the principal law appears to me that they are like land, as I wrote above in siman 363. This is also explicit from Tosafot at the end of Shevuot 37. Tosafot writes that according to the conclusion that the dispute is not about an oath but about restitution after a change, it is difficult why documents must be excluded, since even if one burns them Rabbah exempts him because he does not adjudicate dina de-garmi. Tosafot answers that the passage may follow Rabbi Meir, who adjudicates dina de-garmi, or that the exemption ordinarily applies where he burns them in their place, but if he takes them and then burns them he would be liable. Tosafot means that absent the verse he would be liable if he took and burned them, and that the verse therefore excludes documents from robbery even in that case. Accordingly, one who does not adjudicate dina de-garmi exempts him even when he took and burned them. The Tosafot rulings there state that one who takes another person’s documents in his hand and burns them is liable according to everyone. It appears that the compiler misunderstood Tosafot’s meaning. The same is proven from the Rambam at the end of chapter 8 of Hilchot Gezelah: “There is no additional fifth payment for slaves, documents, or land, because the verse states, ‘and he denied his fellow concerning a deposit’; everything stated in that passage concerns movable objects whose substance is money. This excludes land, slaves, which are compared to land, and documents, whose substance is not money. Likewise, if these were property stolen from a convert who died without heirs, they are not returned to the kohanim. Similarly, land is never acquired by a robber but remains in the owners’ domain.” One should not infer from his final mention of land alone that documents differ. His word “similarly” refers back to his statement that slaves and documents share the same law as land. This is proven by his immediate statement at the beginning of chapter 9, “unlike movable property, as we have stated.” Even according to the Raavad’s textual version, “unlike slaves,” the Raavad’s own comments show that documents remain like land according to the Rambam. The Maggid Mishneh has already demonstrated that the correct version is “unlike movable property.” What source would there be to distinguish land from documents? The Maggid Mishneh also writes at the end of chapter 8, regarding the Rambam’s statement that land is never acquired by a robber, that this follows the Sages in HaGozel Batra. There the Sages’ exclusion also includes documents according to everyone. Furthermore, otherwise the Rambam’s statement that if these three items were stolen from a convert without heirs they are not given to the kohanim would have no source. The Maggid Mishneh discusses this statement but does not identify an independent source, and the Semag, page 132a, likewise writes that if these three items were stolen from a convert without heirs they are not returned to the kohanim. One cannot say that a separate generalization and specification excludes them from the law of stolen property of a convert. The verse “and he denied his fellow concerning a deposit” is in Vayikra, where the passage contains a generalization, specification, and generalization and excludes them. The law of property stolen from a convert appears in Naso, where no such interpretive structure exists. Rather, it was obvious to the Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, and Semag that because land, slaves, and documents were excluded in Vayikra from the category of robbery altogether, and if destroyed they are treated as destroyed in the original owner’s domain, the law of stolen property of a convert cannot apply. There was never legal robbery, and wherever the items are, they remain in the convert’s domain. This appears entirely clear to me. The resulting law is that land, slaves, and documents are not subject to robbery and are regarded as remaining in their owner’s domain. Therefore, if they burn on their own in the robber’s house after he took them, he is exempt. If the robber himself burns them, his only liability is under dina de-garmi, as a penalty imposed by the Sages.

Provided that the damager admits it, etc. If the damager does not know the amount, Maharshal writes in HaGozel Kamma, siman 19, that the claimant does not swear and collect. Burning another person’s document is weaker than informing, and according to everyone the evidentiary ordinance for a robbed person was not enacted for a burner, since he may say that he merely burned paper. Maharshal appears to derive this from the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef. Although this may appear from the Rosh, in my humble opinion there is no basis to distinguish one case of dina de-garmi from another. All cases of dina de-garmi should follow one law. When the Gemara at the end of HaKones asks whether the evidentiary ordinance for a robbed party was enacted for a victim of informing, the question concerns all cases of dina de-garmi. This follows from the Gemara’s wording: according to the one who does not adjudicate dina de-garmi there is no question, since claims against informers are not adjudicated either; the question arises only according to the one who adjudicates dina de-garmi. The reason HaGozel Kamma asks, “If there are no witnesses, how do we know?” rather than saying that the claimant swears and collects, is that Rabbah certainly did not address an issue that the Gemara itself leaves unresolved. Maharshal also attributes his view to the Ran, meaning the Nimukei Yosef, but nothing conclusive follows from the Nimukei Yosef. He says only that one cannot state simply that the claimant swears and collects. Just as the Gemara leaves the matter unresolved regarding an informer, so too it remains unresolved regarding a burner. The case therefore remains doubtful. The Rosh may likewise be understood as speaking only according to the Gemara’s initial assumption that dina de-garmi is not adjudicated. Even if the Rosh intended what Maharshal says, the practical law may still be as I have written. I found this explicit in Hagahot Maimoniyot responsa to Sefer Nezikin, siman 4, which cites a responsum of Rav Hai Gaon. It clearly treats one who burns another’s document and an informer identically regarding the evidentiary ordinance for a robbed party. Just as the Gemara leaves the matter unresolved regarding an informer, so too with a burner. It is well known that one does not disagree with a Gaon absent a clear proof, especially here, where the plain sense of the Gemara supports the Gaon. I later found in the Mordechai in HaKones: “The question whether the evidentiary ordinance was enacted for a victim of an informer remained unresolved. Rabbeinu Yitzchak rules leniently, while Rav Hai Gaon in Shaar 20 ruled that they divide the amount. In a responsum, however, he ruled that since the questions regarding fire and an informer remained unresolved, the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.” Some editors deleted the word “fire,” and Maharam of Tiktin strained to explain it because regarding ordinary fire the Gemara explicitly says the ordinance was enacted. In my humble opinion, “fire” means burning a document. This is the responsum of Rav Hai cited by Hagahot Maimoniyot. Hagahot Maimoniyot likewise states explicitly that in Shaar 20 the Gaon ruled division, while in his responsum he retracted. The same responsum is therefore also alluded to in the Mordechai.
