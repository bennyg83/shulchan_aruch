Likewise, one who burns. Baal HaTerumot rules that if the owner of a burned document claims it represented one hundred and the burner admits only fifty, the burner takes the Torah oath of partial admission; if he admits fifty and says he does not know about the remainder, he is one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, and therefore pays. The Shach argues that there is no Torah oath because documents are excluded from the Torah oath, citing Tosafot and Ramban. In my view Baal HaTerumot is correct under the view of Raavad. The exclusion of documents applies when the claim itself is for the documents while they exist, because their bodies are not money. Once they have been burned and the present claim is for the monetary loss caused by their destruction, Raavad's principle that we look to the present form of the claim can make this an ordinary monetary claim. This parallels Raavad's view concerning damaged land. When the land itself is still the subject of the claim, the Torah oath is excluded; when the land has been destroyed and the claim is now for money, the present monetary claim can carry the Torah oath. Rambam, who looks to the original character of the property, would exempt even after destruction. The Shach's citation of Ramban is not conclusive, because Ramban's words can be read as discussing documents while they remain intact. Likewise, Tosafot's discussion of the exclusion of documents from theft does not prove the absence of an oath on a later damages claim. Nor does the fact that some authorities regard *dina de-garmi* as rabbinic necessarily eliminate a Torah oath. Once the Sages create an enforceable monetary obligation, the property becomes legally owed under the power of beit din. I discussed this in siman 87. Only according to Maharshal's view that *dina de-garmi* is a penalty from which one is exempt by self-admission would a Torah oath be impossible, because even an admission would not generate payment. According to the Shach, who rejects that feature of penalties in this context, the Torah oath can still apply.
His fellow's documents. Rambam rules that if one made his slave an express apotiki for a debt and then freed the slave, the person who freed him is liable because he directly caused the creditor to lose his lien. Raavad disagrees, holding that where one acts upon his own property he is exempt, following the Sages against Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. In the Shach's conclusion, damaging another person's lien through an act done to one's own property can likewise be exempt. Tosafot appears to support Rambam. In discussing an innocuous ox that was slaughtered, Tosafot treats the case as evidence concerning liability for damaging another's lien even though the act was done to property belonging to the person who acted. This indicates that acting upon one's own property does not automatically remove *dina de-garmi* liability. If a third party digs pits in land designated as an express apotiki, even Raavad should impose liability, because the third party acted upon someone else's land. Mordechai goes further and says the creditor can collect from that third party even without *dina de-garmi*, through Rabbi Natan's rule. I find that difficult where the apotiki was express and the creditor stipulated that he has no collection except from this property. In such a case the debtor owes nothing from his other assets. Rabbi Natan's lien ordinarily requires that the third party owe the debtor and the debtor owe the creditor. If the debtor has no personal liability beyond the designated property, that chain is missing. Ran similarly says Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply where the creditor cannot presently demand payment from the intermediate debtor. This explains Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, who says that with an express apotiki the creditor does not collect from the person who damaged it, just as he does not collect from the debtor's other property. The Shach emends the text because he assumes the creditor should have an even stronger claim when the apotiki is destroyed. But Rabbeinu Yerucham can be speaking not of *dina de-garmi* but of Rabbi Natan's rule. With an ordinary apotiki the debtor remains personally liable and Rabbi Natan can operate. With an express apotiki, the debtor has no liability beyond that land, so Rabbi Natan does not. The practical distinction matters where the damage was accidental or where the wrongdoer died. If liability rests only on the penalty aspect of *dina de-garmi*, some authorities would exempt accident or heirs. If Rabbi Natan's rule applies, the liability is ordinary law. In an express apotiki, however, that alternate route is unavailable.
He must pay the entire debt. Maharshal holds that although one who burns another's debt instrument can be exempt according to the authority who rejects *dina de-garmi*, this is only where he never lifted the document. If he first took it, he became a robber and must perform restitution. The Shach rejects this. The Talmud excludes documents, land, and slaves from the ordinary law of robbery. Just as land swept away by a river does not create ordinary robbery liability, so too a stolen document that is later burned or lost is not converted into an ordinary robbery debt merely because it was first lifted. Raavad's special view concerning slaves relies on a verse indicating that a captive slave can be restored and therefore differs from land. There is no comparable verse making documents subject to robbery. The principal rule is therefore that documents are not acquired through theft and ordinary robbery liability does not arise even if they later cease to exist. I discussed this in siman 371.
He caused monetary loss. I was uncertain about one who burns another person's chametz during the festival in a case where the owner may lawfully retain it until after the festival, such as rabbinic hardened chametz. At present it has no market value, but after the festival it will again have value. Is the burner liable because of that future value? It appears he is exempt. Pesachim discusses one who eats consecrated chametz during the festival and links liability for misuse to the dispute whether something that causes future monetary value is presently treated as money. Rashi explains that although it has no present permitted use, its future value makes it a cause of money according to Rabbi Shimon. We rule like the Sages that such a cause of money is not itself money. One must distinguish this from other applications of Rabbi Shimon. Rabbi Shimon's doctrine primarily creates liability when destruction of the object causes the owner to lose money, such as consecrated property for which he bears responsibility. It does not necessarily make the object fully transferable property while it remains intact. Pesachim itself distinguishes an item that is still present and returnable from one already destroyed. Rashi explains that Rabbi Shimon's rule is strongest once the object is no longer present and the loss has materialized. This is why property for which one bears responsibility can create liability for theft or destruction while still not necessarily being saleable by the person who bears the responsibility. A custodian who accepts full responsibility for another's deposit certainly does not become entitled to sell it. I later found Tzemach Tzedek making this same distinction: Rabbi Shimon's rule treats a cause of money as money primarily after destruction, for purposes of liability, not while the object remains intact for every proprietary purpose. There are passages that appear to treat such property as the owner's money even while it remains intact, including the law of an apostate city and sacrificial property for which the owner bears responsibility. Those sources require further reconciliation. Baal HaMaor also interprets the Pesachim passage differently, as dealing with consecrated chametz for which the person bears responsibility. The question therefore retains some difficulty. But for our practical case, since we follow the Sages who reject the general doctrine that future causative value is present money, the burner of currently valueless chametz is exempt from the future post-festival value.
To cause monetary loss. Nimukei Yosef, citing Raah, limits *dina de-garmi* to damage caused by a person and exempts comparable consequential damage caused by one's animal. Ramban disagrees and writes that if one's ox tears or erases another's documents, the owner can be liable, because this is an act of the animal itself rather than a merely remote occurrence. Rama follows the narrower Raah view. Pnei Yehoshua attempted to use Ramban as though he too exempted consequential damage by an animal, but Ramban before us says the opposite. Even according to Raah, however, if one follows Rabbi Shimon that something causing monetary value is itself money, liability through one's animal plainly applies. Under Rabbi Shimon the document or other interest is treated like an ordinary purse of money, not merely as *garmi*. The Tosefta likewise supports this. The suggestion that the Mishnah should have been established with consecrated property for which the owner bears responsibility under Rabbi Shimon also fails. If so, the phrase "property not subject to misuse" would be unnecessary, because even property subject to misuse could be treated as the owner's money through the responsibility. The Gemara instead establishes it like Rabbi Yosei HaGelili, whose rule that lesser sacred offerings are privately owned depends precisely on their being property not subject to misuse.
