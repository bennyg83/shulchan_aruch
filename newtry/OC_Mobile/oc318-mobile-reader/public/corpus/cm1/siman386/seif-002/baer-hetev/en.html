One who burns. The Shach cites Baal HaTerumot: if the owner of the note claims that it represented one hundred and the burner admits only fifty, the burner must swear as one who admits part; if he admits part and says regarding the remainder, "I do not know," he must pay because one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, Bach, and Levush cite this without objection. The Shach questions the rule, since siman 66, seif 39, and the beginning of siman 95 establish that oaths of bailees and partial admission are not taken regarding documents. The Mishnah might perhaps be limited to a view that rejects dina de-garmi, but the unqualified language of the authorities indicates that no oath of partial admission is taken even where denial prevents collection. Tosafot in Shevuot 37 clearly implies so. The correct explanation, in the Shach's view, is that Baal HaTerumot holds both dina de-garmi and the doctrine that an object causing monetary liability is itself treated as money, following Rabbi Shimon. Since we do not rule like Rabbi Shimon, this ruling of Baal HaTerumot does not apply. The Shach also cites Ramban regarding one who stole chametz and retained it over Pesach, where the robber could otherwise have returned the forbidden chametz in its present state. If another person then burns it, Ramban obligates the burner to reimburse the robber because he caused an out-of-pocket loss. Maharshal objects from Bava Kamma 98b, but Ramban follows the alternative explanation cited by Tosafot in Bava Kamma and Ketubot and rules according to the anonymous sugya rather than Rabbah's statement. Nevertheless, according to the Shach's conclusion that dina de-garmi is only a rabbinic penalty, the burner is exempt here because the case is uncommon.
An unpaid bailee. See the Shach's lengthy explanation of the Rambam's law concerning one who designated his slave as an apotiki and later freed him. He concludes that land, slaves, and documents are not subject to ordinary theft acquisition and are considered to remain in the owner's domain. Therefore, if after being taken they burn accidentally in the taker's house, the taker is exempt. If he himself burns them, liability arises only through dina de-garmi, as a rabbinic penalty.
The debt. Its value is assessed according to what the debt could have been sold for, as explained in siman 66, seif 32. There is no reason to distinguish between one who forgives a debt and one who burns its note. This is explicit in Responsa Rosh, rule 69, siman 11, and also in Responsa Rashdam, siman 32. See further Rashdam siman 408; Maharam Alshich siman 54; Responsa M"A, simanim 52 and 89; and Maharam Sasson, end of siman 140. Mahari ben Lev writes that the burner is not believed to claim that he already paid the damages according to the view that dina de-garmi is Torah law, although he might claim that he relies on the view that it is rabbinic. In my view he is believed according to all opinions. Shach.
That he admit. If the damager does not know the amount, Maharshal rules that the claimant does not swear and collect, because burning a note is less direct than informing, and the enactment for a robbed person was not extended to a burned document, since physically it is only paper. He appears to derive this from the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef. In my view this distinction among cases of dina de-garmi is unsound. All such cases should share one law. When the Gemara at the end of HaKones asks whether the enactment for a robbed person applies to an informer according to the view that dina de-garmi is judged as monetary liability, the question concerns the entire category of dina de-garmi. This is implied by the Gemara's language. I likewise found in the responsa appended to Mishneh Torah, Nezikin, a responsum of Rav Hai Gaon explicitly indicating that for the enactment of a robbed person there is no distinction between burning a note and informing. Since one should not disagree with a Gaon without clear proof, especially where the plain Gemara supports him, his view is primary. Shach.
