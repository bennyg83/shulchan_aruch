On an uncertain claim. The Sema asks from the ruling later in siman 290, seif 6, that when beit din appoints a guardian they make an accounting with him at the outset because on a definite claim they can require him to swear. This implies that certainty is specifically required. The Sema answers that the later passage concerns a claim of less than two silver ma'ot and therefore requires a definite claim. The Taz correctly objects that the ordinary oath under discussion is a Mishnah oath, and even on a definite claim there is no such oath with an amount below two silver ma'ot together with an admission of a perutah. This is especially clear because, as will be explained, the inference that an accounting is made at the beginning comes from a baraita that disputes only whether an accounting must be made at the end, and hesset did not yet exist at the time of that baraita. The Taz therefore explains that the later passage includes a guardian appointed by the father of the orphans. Although the father appointed him, beit din hands the estate to him, and therefore an initial accounting is made. The Derishah likewise writes this in siman 290 in the name of the Ri ibn Lev. That can fit the language of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, but the source is the Baal HaIttur, whose wording creates a sharper difficulty. He cites the dispute between the first Tanna and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel whether the guardian must render an accounting at the end, connects it to the dispute of Abba Shaul and the Sages whether such a guardian swears, rules like Abba Shaul, and concludes that one appointed by the father must account at the end while one appointed by beit din need not. He then adds that this concerns the final accounting only, whereas initially beit din must make an accounting with them and record the movables, land, and everything received, "for on a definite claim we make him swear, as explained below." Yet below the Baal HaIttur expressly rules that a guardian appointed by beit din swears even on an uncertain claim, while one appointed by the father swears only on a definite claim. How then can the phrase about a definite claim explain the initial accounting specifically in the case of the guardian appointed by beit din? It appears that the Baal HaIttur's statement, "for on a definite claim we make him swear," refers back to the entire discussion. One might ask why a guardian appointed by the father must render an accounting at the end if he has no oath obligation on a mere uncertain suspicion. The answer is that he can still be required to swear upon a definite claim. Both the initial and final accounting are therefore necessary because they may later reveal a concrete discrepancy and create a definite denial. From the fact that the Tannaim dispute only the final accounting, rather than whether any accounting is required, the Baal HaIttur also infers that an initial accounting is required according to all, even in a situation in which no oath is imposed merely on suspicion. This resolves his wording. Accordingly, the Tur and Mechaber can likewise be understood as referring to the guardian appointed by the father when they explain the initial accounting by the possibility of a definite claim. There is still a difficulty regarding the guardian appointed by beit din, who need not provide a final accounting and is required to swear even on suspicion. What purpose does the initial accounting serve? It cannot itself establish the truth of the later expenditures, since the guardian does not itemize all that he spent but merely states what remains. Nor is it needed simply to impose the oath, since he is obligated to swear in any event. The language also suggests that recording how much he received can itself lead to an oath, and it is not obvious how. If later he denies having received the recorded amount, he should not be believed against the formal act of beit din. If he admits receipt but says he expended the money properly, the original inventory does not establish the expenditures. We also cannot say that without the record he would have a migo to say he never received the funds, because according to the Mechaber a migo does not ordinarily exempt from an oath. Perhaps the reason is this. A guardian who is subject to an oath may choose not to swear and instead pay the full amount concerning which he is suspected. Beit din must therefore know how much he originally received so that, if he declines the oath, the maximum amount he must restore can be determined. For that purpose the initial inventory is necessary. The same applies to one appointed by the father if a later definite claim triggers an oath and, by gilgul, the broader guardian's oath. If he prefers payment to swearing, the record identifies the amount that can be demanded. This explanation is somewhat strained. Nor can one simply say that knowing the amount received prevents exaggerated claims about expenses, because that consideration would apply regardless of whether any oath obligation exists and would not explain why the Baal HaIttur ties the accounting specifically to the law of oaths.
Until he suspects him, etc. This is the Rambam's language. The Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh infer that the Rambam follows Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, who does not require a claim of two silver ma'ot and a perutah together with an admission of a perutah. It is enough that the suspicion itself amount to two silver ma'ot even if the guardian admits nothing. This creates a difficulty, because later, in siman 290, seif 9, the Mechaber states without qualification the Raavad's view that a guardian appointed by beit din does not swear unless he is suspected of two silver ma'ot and a perutah and admits a perutah. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. The Sema says that the Shulchan Aruch relies here upon what it writes later in siman 290. Even if we tolerate that strained answer, it remains difficult to understand why the Mechaber would set aside the Rif, Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, the Rambam, and the Ramah and rule simply like the Raavad. It therefore seems that the Mishnah's phrase, "these swear on an uncertain claim," teaches that the special enactment concerns the uncertainty. Had there been a definite claim, an oath would have arisen under the ordinary rules. If the guardian denied everything, however, even a definite claim would not generate a Torah oath. The principal element would then be missing from the Mishnah. We must therefore be speaking of a situation that includes a partial admission. Since the Mishnah concerns partners and guardians before their relationship has been fully terminated, property of the partnership or guardianship remains in the defendant's hands. If the claimant suspects him of an additional two silver ma'ot and he denies it, while property worth at least a perutah admittedly remains due, there is effectively a partial admission. It may technically be heilech, but the Sages did not demand perfect identity with Torah law when instituting a rabbinic oath, as the Ran explains regarding Rabbi Yehudah's requirement of a partial admission even where the admitted sum is immediately available. This understanding is explicit in the Raavad later in siman 290. When he requires a partial admission, he writes that this can also be satisfied where the guardianship property is still in the guardian's possession. Thus, if assets of the estate remain with him, he is treated as admitting part of the claim. Although the Raavad holds that a deposit is not necessarily heilech, the admission may have preceded the later suspicion, for the guardian first reports what remains and only afterward is suspected of withholding more. The point is that the Sages required a case analogous to a Torah partial admission, not every technical element of the Torah category. The same is suggested by the French sages cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of this siman, some of whom explain that the oath of partners ceases after division because the Sages instituted only an oath resembling Torah law. Before division there is ordinarily still property of the partnership in the defendant's hands, so a definite claim would resemble partial admission. After division there is no such standing admission, and a merely occasional later admission was not enough to extend the enactment. Accordingly, the Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch here group partners, sharecroppers, guardians, and the others together and speak of cases in which the relationship has not yet been fully divided or terminated. They therefore state simply that the amount denied through the suspicion must be two silver ma'ot. A perutah of admission is normally already present because property of the partnership or guardianship remains in the defendant's hands. This is particularly so according to the Rambam, who also holds that a deposit is not automatically treated as heilech. The Raavad therefore did not object to the Rambam here, because within the Rambam's factual framework even the Raavad's requirement is satisfied. Later, in siman 290, the discussion is devoted specifically to guardians and includes a guardian who may already have transferred or expended everything that was under his control. There the Shulchan Aruch must state explicitly that an admission of at least a perutah is required, because the Sages instituted the uncertain guardian's oath only in a form analogous to Torah law. This also explains why the Shulchan Aruch omits there the Raavad's alternative phrase, "or some of the guardianship property remains in his possession." That situation has already been covered by the present siman and need not be repeated. One might still infer from the Rambam's wording that he requires no admission at all. Even that inference can be softened. The Rambam may be stating the uniform rule concerning the amount of denial, namely that the defendant's denial of the suspicious amount must be at least two silver ma'ot. It is possible that he nevertheless assumes an admitted perutah. For example, even a person otherwise liable only to hesset might admit a perutah but say heilech. That admission may not create a Torah oath, yet it is sufficient for the rabbinic oath of partners because the enactment is modeled only broadly on Torah law. Those who disagree understand the existing partnership property differently. In their view, the property already acknowledged to belong to the claimant is not itself the partial admission relevant to the suspicious claim. After the guardian states what he acknowledges is due, the claimant must suspect him of an additional two silver ma'ot and a perutah, and the guardian must admit a perutah from that additional disputed amount. Only then is there a meaningful indication that his denial is weakened. This is especially plausible according to the Ramah regarding a guardian appointed by the father, where a partial admission can create an oath even though an uncertain claim alone does not. The ordinary guardianship property cannot itself count as the admission, for if it did every guardian would already satisfy the condition. Rashi's wording likewise supports this stricter view: "I suspect that you withheld from mine two silver ma'ot and a perutah, and he admits a perutah." The admitted perutah is described as part of the very amount suspected of having been withheld. Thus the approaches can be reconciled: the Rambam's general formulation deals with an ongoing partnership where an admission is inherent in the relationship, while the later ruling spells out the admission where it may not otherwise exist. This also resolves an apparent contradiction in the Ramah as cited by the Tur. Here the Ramah holds that even a guardian appointed by the father can be made to swear on an uncertain claim if he admits a perutah. Yet in siman 290 the Tur cites the Ramah that such a guardian swears a Torah oath when there is a definite claim and a partial admission. There is no contradiction. For a definite Torah oath, assets of the guardianship that the guardian acknowledges holding may themselves constitute the partial admission when the claimant says, for example, that one hundred is due and the guardian says ninety nine remains while denying one. For the special uncertain rabbinic oath, however, the guardian must admit a perutah specifically from the additional amount of which he is suspected. The two rulings address different kinds of admission. This also explains the Gemara in Ketubot 87 concerning a wife's waiver of the guardian's oath. The Gemara asks how she could have known in advance that she would become a guardian and therefore requested, "Write for me that you will not make me swear." One might have answered that she was already acting as guardian. But the stronger question, under the strict view, would be why she needed a waiver at all. Surely she did not intend to steal a perutah from her husband, and without an admission from the suspected amount no oath would arise. According to what has been explained, the ordinary assets of her husband's estate remaining in her hands can themselves function as the needed admission in the ongoing guardianship setting. She may therefore face the guardian's oath without any assumption that she is actually a thief. The formulation of the sugya is thus compelling evidence for this understanding.
Two silver ma'ot. The Taz holds that for less than this amount a hesset oath is still required. Although the claim is uncertain, where there are grounds for suspicion one can impose hesset even on an uncertain claim. Rabbenu Baruch in the Mordechai derives the rule that one may require hesset regarding an opened or tampered chest from the Mishnah, "these swear on an uncertain claim," because the circumstances give the defendant room to rationalize his conduct. This shows that an uncertain claim can support hesset where there are substantial grounds for suspicion. The Mordechai does indeed imply this. I found an even stronger proof in the Tur later, siman 290, where it cites the Raavad that a guardian requires a denial of two silver ma'ot and an admission of a perutah for the Mishnah oath. If the claim lacks that structure, the Raavad says he is not made to take the Mishnah oath, but he may be made to take hesset and the other matters may be included by gilgul shevuah. The Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit is troubled that the claim there is merely uncertain, and strains to explain why hesset should apply. According to the Taz there is no difficulty, because this is precisely a situation where hesset may be imposed due to grounds for suspicion. Nevertheless, the Mechaber in siman 290, seif 19, states explicitly that when the claim is not definite and the denial is not two silver ma'ot with an admission of a perutah, there is no hesset at all, and the Rama makes no qualification. It is difficult to rule against both of them. Perhaps a tampered chest creates a much stronger objective presumption that someone took the contents, whereas the ordinary guardian's case is merely a suspicion without such an evidentiary basis. Thus, although the Taz's position is persuasive in principle, it is difficult to apply it against the unqualified ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Rama except where the circumstances, in the judgment of the dayan, amount to a genuine evidentiary indication.
