That beit din. Baer Heitev cites the Sema, who explains this according to the Mechaber's supposed view that these oaths imposed on uncertain claims do not require partial admission. Rabbi Akiva Eiger challenges the Sema, because the Mechaber in fact requires partial admission, as the Sema himself writes below and as Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 290. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Choshen Mishpat siman 21, answers that the Sema distinguishes between a guardian and the other categories of uncertain oaths: according to the Mechaber, other uncertain oaths do not require admission, whereas a guardian does. A gloss by the son of Noda BiYehudah proposes instead that there is a scribal error in the Sema and the text should read that these uncertain oaths do require partial admission. On that reading, the Mechaber omitted the case of a guardian appointed by the orphans' father because, since even a court-appointed guardian swears only with partial admission, one appointed by the father is entirely exempt.
Until he has grounds for suspicion. The Taz writes that the claimant must possess some indication giving him reason to suspect the other party. If he admits that he has no basis whatsoever for suspicion, he may not impose the oath, as explained in siman 87. Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 63, adds that even using the threat of an oath merely to pressure the other party into a compromise and recover part of an alleged loss is forbidden where there is no genuine suspicion. The Taz further writes that the requirement of two silver ma'ah here concerns the Mishnaic oath with an object held in hand, but a heset oath should apply even to a claim of one perutah, because heset has no minimum amount. Since this uncertain claim was strong enough in earlier generations to support a formal oath, after the enactment of heset it should be treated like a definite claim for that lesser oath. Shaar HaMishpat disagrees, citing siman 290, where on an uncertain claim of less than two silver ma'ah no heset oath is imposed unless the claim is definite. Tumim also raises this difficulty but does not entirely reject the Taz. He notes support from the Raavad and concludes that the Taz's view has substantial basis, though it is difficult to rule against the plain Shulchan Aruch and the Ramah, absent circumstances that persuade the judge otherwise.

Knesset HaGedolah writes that Rashi's language implies that the person imposing the oath must suspect the partner of withholding at least two silver ma'ah from his own share, even where there are many partners; it is not enough that the aggregate claim of all the partners is two silver ma'ah. Maharit, however, was uncertain only where one partner appears alone and sues on behalf of all. If all the partners are present and together claim two silver ma'ah, Maharit considered it obvious that they may administer the oath even though no single partner's share reaches that amount. His doubt was whether one partner acting for the others is treated as their agent for this purpose, or whether the defendant can answer that perhaps the absent partners do not suspect him and therefore there is no two-silver claim. Birkei Yosef and Shaar HaMishpat discuss challenges from Kiddushin, Bava Metzia, and other passages concerning whether separate claims may combine to the minimum value. It appears, however, that even Maharit speaks only about the special two-silver threshold. He would agree that each partner must have at least one perutah, because less than a perutah is not legally considered money. One can further distinguish: the two-silver requirement in partial admission relates to the amount denied by the defendant, so where the partners jointly confront one defendant it may be measured on the defendant's side. The perutah minimum, by contrast, reflects whether each claimant has a legally cognizable monetary claim, and therefore must exist for each partner. The matter requires further study; see also what I wrote above, siman 6, seif katan 1.

Teshuvot Knesset Yechezkel, siman 90, writes that he long wondered why courts routinely impose the partners' oath without any admission, since a defendant should apparently be able to say that he relies upon Rashi, the Ramah, Rashba, and Tur, who require a partial admission. Rashdam and Maharam Sasson likewise write that where major authorities disagree, one should not compel the oath and at most a general ban should be imposed. He then offers a reconciliation based on Baal HaIttur, who also requires an admission of a perutah but writes that if even one perutah of partnership property remains undivided, that itself is treated as partial admission, even if it is not physically in the defendant's possession. Accordingly, there may be no real disagreement: Rif, Rambam, and Rosh may also require a perutah of admission but omit the requirement because they rely on the later rule that the partners' oath is imposed only while some partnership property remains undivided. Thus the remaining partnership property itself supplies the equivalent of partial admission. Tumim advances a similar explanation. Knesset Yechezkel also challenges the Radbaz, who writes that the practical difference in our time is only whether the oath is taken while holding a sacred object, since heset now exists. Knesset Yechezkel objected that heset ordinarily requires a definite claim. But the Radbaz himself expressly answers that while heset generally requires certainty, partnership and similar oaths are founded on uncertainty, and therefore even a complete denial may generate heset. The objection thus falls away. See also Teshuvot Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 53.
