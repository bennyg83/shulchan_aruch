Seif 10: Or, “I was never your partner,” etc. Note: It appears to me that this applies specifically where he says, “We were never partners.” But if he admits that they were partners, or there are witnesses that they were partners, then according to the authorities who use migo to exempt from an oath, he is nevertheless obligated to swear if his defense is that they had a condition that he was not to deviate from specified instructions and that if he deviated the partnership would be void retroactively. Although such a condition would render the legal result as though no partnership had existed, he must still swear. The reason is that the oath imposed upon partners is itself only a rabbinic enactment prompted by concern that a partner rationalizes taking more than his share. Whether his defense is one formulation or another, that same concern remains, so the enactment should apply. The Sages refrained from imposing the partnership oath only where he says, “We were never partners,” or where he says, “We already divided and settled the partnership.” Otherwise there would be no end to litigation: anyone could seize another person and say, “We were partners,” or, after a partnership had ended long ago, could revive the claim whenever he wished. For those cases the Sages did not institute the special oath. Nor does ordinary Torah oath law fill the gap, because a denial of having been partners is not itself a denial of a present monetary debt; even if he admitted the partnership, the immediate consequence would only be an oath. But where he admits that the partnership existed and merely raises another defense that would cause it to be treated as retroactively void, the concern of “he rationalizes taking” remains and there is no danger of unlimited fabricated partnership claims. The rabbinic oath therefore applies. There is a proof from the Rosh, who requires witnesses that the parties were partners because otherwise the defendant is believed through a migo. The Rosh’s summary makes the same point, and the Ran records opinions requiring proof of the partnership. What is gained by such proof? The defendant could still claim that there was a condition that if he deviated from the agreed terms the partnership would be void, and he could say that the condition occurred. It follows that such a migo does not exempt him, for the reason stated above. One might answer that this is simply an uncommon migo. But that is difficult, since in the law of bailees a migo that an unavoidable accident occurred can be effective even though such accidents are uncommon. One might distinguish because a claim of unavoidable accident is not brazen, yet the Rosh and Ran also write in the law of a hired worker that even a slight migo can restore the ordinary law. Why then should this migo not suffice? It follows as I have written, that even with such a conditional defense he remains subject to the rabbinic partnership oath. I saw that Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the defendant says there was a condition exempting him from an oath, he is exempt. Netivot HaMishpat says that this is incorrect but gives no reason. According to what I have written, the Netivot is correct. The concern that the partner rationalizes taking remains even where he alleges such a condition, and the Sages therefore imposed the oath in that case as well. The Rosh and the other authorities cited above are a proof, for otherwise the defendant could always claim that there was a private condition exempting or undoing the arrangement. Witnesses to the partnership would not help, since witnesses are not attached to a person’s wings and may not have heard a later private condition. It necessarily follows that such a defense does not remove the oath. This appears clear to me.
