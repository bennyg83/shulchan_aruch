He said, “And he said to him that he should do him.” After some of them, Reuven asked him to make a bill for him because he had been separated from each other in the wilderness and called for a column called Rashi:
He is not obligated to make a promissory note for him. The reason is that in the preceding case the debt against Reuven was known, and Reuven had already given the gentile a promissory note obligating himself. Therefore Shimon too became obligated to give Reuven a note for his share. Here, by contrast, Reuven did not give a note obligating himself, the debt against him was not established, and it is very likely that the matter will never arise. See the Perishah.
The witnesses should write Shimon's admission, etc. This is more favorable to Shimon because the witnesses do not write language creating an obligation, but only record his admission. By contrast, if Shimon gives Reuven a promissory note, the document itself contains language of obligation. In the Derishah I wrote that the matter can be explained differently. Reuven's request was that Shimon give him an unconditional document stating that whatever Reuven might later have to pay the gentile, Shimon would reimburse one half. Shimon did not wish to do this, but wanted that if Reuven were eventually required to pay, Reuven would then sue him and the law would be determined according to their claims at that time. The Rosh ruled in favor of Shimon. Nevertheless, the first explanation appears primary as a matter of law. This is clear.
