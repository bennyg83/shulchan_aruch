Migo. The Tur writes that the Rosh disagrees and holds that in such a case the person is exempt from the oath by virtue of a migo. There is no distinction between money and an oath, because an oath can lead to monetary liability if he refuses to swear. Although this still appears to be a migo involving brazenness, and for that reason at the beginning of siman 296 the authorities rule that a custodian must take a Torah oath and is not believed to say, 'It was stolen,' through a migo that he could have denied ever being a custodian, since outright denial would be brazen, a partnership oath is different. That oath is only a rabbinic enactment, so even a weak migo restores the matter to Torah law, under which no oath is required. I explained similarly in the Perishah to siman 89 regarding a worker where there are no witnesses to the hiring. This is not, however, a conclusive proof. See what I wrote there, seif katan 13. Sema. The Shach writes that in his view the proof from there is conclusive. See his lengthy discussion, and see above siman 82 in the laws of migo, paragraph 6; below siman 146, seif 2; Shiltei HaGibborim to the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim; Tosafot to Bava Metzia 4b; Responsa Maharit, siman 112; Rashach, part 2, siman 176; Rabbi Yosef Beit Levi, siman 22; Maharanach, siman 64; and Maharshdam, siman 134. The Taz also agrees with the Sema that a migo involving brazenness is sufficient here because the entire oath is only a rabbinic enactment, and he writes that the Rosh explicitly gives this reason regarding the worker's oath.
