Regarding the lease-tax arrangement known as arenda in these countries, which is administered through witnesses as is well known, it requires study whether the holder of the arenda can impose an oath on individuals suspected of concealing amounts due. There is a royal law permitting oaths concerning what is called the quart levy, but arenda is not included in that law. Under the government's own law the Jewish leaseholder has no independent right to enforce several of its customary practices, some of which even conflict with royal directives. He may perhaps impose an acceptance of a ban regarding whether someone brought liquor from outside or the like, but whether he may impose an oath requires study. This is not analogous to an open account where a special presumption exists, nor can one ordinarily impose an oath from mere doubt. It also differs from the oath of partners, where there is an established possibility of self-justification, the ox eating from its threshing. Still, judging from ordinary human behavior, perhaps there is even stronger room for self-justification here. Although when a Jew leases these rights from the government there is a prohibition against concealing what is lawfully due, especially because the royal quart levy involves substantial sums and formal oaths, the government has also arranged for a tenth measure to be taken at the mill. In that respect the law of the kingdom applies, and once a Jew leases the right, concealment from him can involve theft. Even so, people treat these matters lightly, and perhaps an oath can therefore be imposed. A similar question arises in reverse. Just as select individuals may suspect the holder of the arenda of excess collection, so the arenda holder may be suspected of taking more than the fixed tenth measure based on estimation, because constant exact measurement is difficult. There is an argument not to impose an oath, since the rationalization of "the ox eats from its threshing" does not justify taking more than the fixed amount. On the other hand, because a person does not readily see his own fault, he may estimate too generously in his own favor and take more than is proper. Where the established custom is that a trusted person supervises the measurement, and that supervisor says the holders collect without him, it appears that an oath can be imposed in favor of the individual taxpayers. The oath of partners can also relate to conduct from before the active business dealings began, provided the suspected conduct is connected with the enterprise and the same self-justification applies. The metaphor of the ox eating while threshing is only an illustration; the underlying point is that once the business has been entrusted to him, there is room for rationalization. But before any business property was placed in his hand, where he is suspected only regarding property he held independently and had not yet begun to use for the joint venture, perhaps the oath does not apply. This is especially so if the partners could still have retracted from entrusting him with the enterprise. If a partner purchases merchandise on his own and later sells it to the partnership at a fixed price, his acquisition was undertaken for his own profit and not as partnership business. He therefore should not be subject to the partners' oath regarding that purchase.
