For we do not say migo, etc. The Rosh disagrees and holds that generally a migo can exempt a person from an oath. Only regarding an oath that would require an especially brazen denial is the migo unavailable. With a rabbinic oath too, the Rosh recognizes such a migo, as in the case of an employee. He therefore disagrees with the ruling here, as the Tur records and as the Shach discusses at length. The Sema accordingly asks: since the Rama later in siman 293 cites the Rosh's opinion that a migo can exempt from an oath, why does the Rama state here without qualification that we do not say migo, without mentioning the Rosh's disagreement? The Shach attempts to distinguish between matters involving outright denial and other cases, following his interpretation in the rules of migo of Tosafot in Bava Kamma. I have already demonstrated there that this interpretation is extremely strained and cannot be maintained, so the difficulty returns. The straightforward answer, also implied by the Perishah, is that even in a rabbinic enactment we do not use a migo based upon a more brazen plea to avoid an oath. The employee case is different because the practical consequence is monetary. The employer must pay whether the employee swears or not; the employee's oath is merely a condition of collection. In monetary matters we may accept even a weaker migo based upon a more brazen alternative plea, because without it money would be extracted. Here, however, the entire purpose of the migo is merely to avoid an oath. For that purpose a migo of brazenness is not accepted. This is simple and correct. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Ketubot 87 regarding one who partially impairs her ketubah. We do not say that she has a migo that she could simply have refrained from acknowledging the partial payment, because a person is reluctant to lie where the facts can be checked. Tosafot ask from the employee case, where despite the employer's preoccupation with many workers a comparable migo is recognized. According to what has been explained, there is no difficulty. It is indeed a weaker migo because the alternative involves an outright falsehood, but in the employee case the consequence is payment of money, and for monetary possession even a weaker migo is considered. In the case of an impaired document the consequence is only whether an oath must be taken, and such a weak migo does not remove an oath. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 107 ask why a bailee who claims the deposit was lost through accident should not be believed by a migo that he could have denied ever receiving it. The Maharsha asks that according to the Ri Baal HaTosafot this is a migo of brazenness, while according to Rabbenu Tam the claim of accident must accompany an admission, so a total denial would itself produce a partial admission problem. I discussed other resolutions above in siman 72 and in the rules of migo. Another answer is possible. According to Rabbenu Tam, the Sages themselves imposed an oath even on a bare claim of accident. Tosafot can therefore ask: since the defendant has a migo to deny receipt entirely, did the Sages really institute an oath in a manner that uproots an existing migo? If so, one could infer from there that rabbinic enactments do not recognize a migo to avoid an oath, which is not obvious. Even according to what I concluded above, that Rabbenu Tam agrees a person is less willing to make a denial that the other party knows to be false, in a rabbinic setting Tosafot may still consider such a migo, as the Rosh does. This indicates that Tosafot may follow the Rosh in recognizing even a migo involving a more brazen plea in rabbinic cases. But I have offered other explanations of that Tosafot in the places cited, and the reader may choose among them. The Rama also has support from the Tur's citation of the Raavad later in siman 290, who requires a partial admission for the guardian's oath. Why should the guardian not be treated as one returning a lost object, since on the uncertain claim he could have denied everything? There is no ordinary brazenness problem because the claimant himself is uncertain. According to the Rosh one could answer that the oath was instituted even without a partial admission, as his discussion in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim may imply, for the uncertain oaths were modeled on the oath of bailees, which does not require ordinary partial admission. But the Tur itself rules that a partial admission is required. This suggests either that the Sages fixed the enactment in precisely this form regardless of migo, or, as the Perishah explains, that the reason for the oath is that the partner or guardian rationalizes retaining some property. Once he actually admits the disputed perutah, that rationale exists; if he denied everything, there would be no such indication. Especially since the rationale of self permission is itself somewhat strained, it is better to view the structure as a fixed rabbinic decree. In either event there is no migo to remove the oath. Compare what I wrote above in siman 89 regarding Rabbi Yehudah's requirement of a partial admission and whether a migo of total denial applies there.
