Each may administer an oath to the other. Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 151, writes that the custom is to draw lots to determine who swears first, and not to administer both oaths simultaneously. The same applies generally where two litigants have claims against one another and both become obligated to swear. Even if one owes a rabbinic oath and the other a Torah oath, neither necessarily has priority. Teshuvot Galya Masechta, siman 12, reports a different practice: when reciprocal claims result in each party having the right to impose an oath on the other, he would require the party pressing most urgently for final resolution to swear first, reasoning that the one seeking immediate enforcement is in the position of the party extracting and must first establish his claim. Apparently he did not see the ruling of Shevut Yaakov. I have also heard from some senior judges that where a defendant answers, 'You owe me more than the amount you claim from me,' thereby becoming a claimant for the excess, and the original claimant denies it, they do not impose an oath upon the second defendant concerning that excess but only a general ban. The reason may be that a setoff claim is intrinsically weak and serves to exempt the defendant only because of the migo that he could have claimed payment. As to the excess amount, where that migo no longer exists, the claim should not itself generate an oath, especially where there is no independent monetary basis. The matter may depend on the judge's assessment of the circumstances. See also what I wrote above, siman 87, seif 30, seif katan 33.
