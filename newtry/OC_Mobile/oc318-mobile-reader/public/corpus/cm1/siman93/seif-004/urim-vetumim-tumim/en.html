The principal who sends, etc. It appears that Rambam speaks of an agent instructed to buy for the principal, or to sell the principal's produce or goods, as circumstances permit. In that setting the agent may rationalize retaining something for himself. He may say: even had I bought somewhat more expensively, or sold somewhat more cheaply, the principal would have had to accept the transaction; it was my own effort and skill that obtained this favorable price. He therefore has a motive for self permission and is included in the Mishnah oath. By contrast, if one merely sends money through an agent to deliver to another person, and the agent later says that he returned or delivered it, there is no Mishnah oath according to any view. What self permission exists there? He received money and was expected to hand over the same money. If the principal definitely contradicts him, the agent is subject only to hesset. This removes the Rashba's objection to Rambam from Kiddushin, where the Gemara says that "these are his agents" and indicates that before the institution of hesset they were believed without any oath. Rambam himself rules so in Laws of Agents 1. That case concerns simple delivery, where no rationale of self permission exists, and Rambam agrees that the Mishnah oath was never instituted. This also resolves the difficulty discussed by the Sema, and which I myself found pressing in Urim, concerning the difference between one who tells a shopkeeper, "Give the workers what they are entitled to," and one who incurs expenses on another's behalf. If the workers do not contradict the shopkeeper, the shopkeeper does not take a Mishnah oath, whereas a person who claims reimbursement of authorized expenses must swear. The distinction is now clear. Expenses are not fixed. The spender may rationalize: had I spent another dinar, the owner would have had to bear that cost too; my thrift and effort saved him money, so I may retain something. Such self permission supports the rabbinic oath. But when the shopkeeper merely pays the workers their fixed wages, he has produced no saving for the employer. The workers would not waive even a perutah, and if he did not pay them in full they would return to the employer. There is therefore no comparable self permission. As I wrote above, however, if the instruction was to provide the workers whatever food and drink they needed, the shopkeeper must swear how much he supplied, because the quantity was not fixed and he can rationalize that his restraint saved the employer expense. That case falls within the same principle.
