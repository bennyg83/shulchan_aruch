(Where even the smallest matter remains undivided.) If partners have divided all aspects of their partnership and only one disputed amount remains between them, it appears that an acceptance of a ban need not precede the oath, because the partners' oath still applies to the unresolved item itself. If the opposing party asks that the other accept a ban, that request is itself effectively a demand for the partners' oath, at least in the form of an acceptance of a ban. So long as this particular matter remains undivided, his claim is valid. But if he does not request an acceptance of a ban, it appears unnecessary. Elsewhere I considered whether the partners' oath is imposed without the claimant expressly saying, "Swear regarding all of this." That discussion concerns a situation where the parties have at least come to beit din and one partner accuses the other of wrongdoing. In the present type of case, where only Reuven is claiming money from Shimon, who is in possession, perhaps only Shimon can be compelled to swear. This requires study. Another question concerns the rule that the partners' oath remains available where even a trivial part of the partnership has not been divided. Suppose the party seeking the oath no longer has any possibility of collecting even a perutah from the other, and the only uncertainty in the accounting is that perhaps he himself owes something. Perhaps, from the perspective of his own claim, the partnership should be viewed as already divided. Logically, however, the Sages made no such distinction. They enacted that so long as any matter remains undivided, the partners' oath applies. The reason is not that by dividing the property the partners are presumed to waive the oath to one another. Rather, this is the fixed scope of the rabbinic enactment. This requires study. If there is a doubt whether the partners already settled one specific item, or whether no accounting was ever made concerning it, then consequently there is a doubt whether the partnership should be viewed as fully divided. An oath cannot ordinarily be imposed from a doubt. If the party seeking to impose the oath is himself in possession, or the procedural posture resembles one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, yet the rule of partial admission does not apply because no payment is due before the partners' oath, one might say that the burden of proof lies on the party seeking to extract and perhaps no partners' oath can be imposed. One might also say that once a doubt arose whether the partnership had already been divided, before the claimant later obtained possession, the oath may already have fallen away completely and cannot be revived merely because of his subsequent possession. This requires further study.
