There is an opinion that if he exempted him, etc. The Mechaber states this as though no one disagrees, but the Nimukei Yosef cites Ramban in the name of a Gaon who does disagree. The source of the French sages' proof, cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of this siman, is the Rosh in HaKotev. The sages of Narbonne ask why the Mishnah needs to say that the heirs may make the widow swear for future guardianship but not for the past. Rashi explains that the husband had exempted her from an oath, but that rule was already taught earlier. They therefore explain that the Mishnah is not merely distinguishing two separate periods. Rather, it teaches that even when the heirs have a valid oath claim regarding her later conduct, they cannot use gilgul shevuah to include the earlier period from which she was exempt. Their ruling therefore follows from the redundancy of the Mishnah. This proof appears unavailable according to Rav Hai Gaon and the Rif. They explain the clause concerning the heirs not as a continuation of an express exemption, but even where no exemption was given: if the husband died without making her swear, the heirs cannot impose an oath for the earlier period because his failure is deemed a waiver. On that reading the Mishnah can simply distinguish the past from the future and need not teach a law of gilgul. The Beit Shmuel argues that even according to the Rif, the Mishnah means that if the heirs make her swear for the future they still cannot include the past by gilgul, and that if the rule applies after an implied waiver it certainly applies after an express exemption. This is not compelled. The Rosh makes clear that the sages of Narbonne derive their rule precisely because otherwise the Mishnah would be redundant. According to the Rif there is no redundancy, and the Bach and Chelkat Mechokek correctly observe that his view supplies no proof that an express exemption defeats gilgul. Even under the Gemara's initial assumption that the exemption concerned the guardian's oath, while the heirs retained some power, the later clause may be read according to the Rif as a case without an exemption. The husband failed to make her swear and thereby waived the earlier matter. As explained in the preceding seif katan, a completed waiver is stronger than a prospective exemption from an oath. If the husband merely says, "I will not make you swear," he has not necessarily relinquished the underlying money or extended the exemption to heirs. By contrast, if he could have demanded an oath after the claim arose and deliberately did not, the waiver can extinguish the claim itself. The two situations therefore cannot simply be equated. Perhaps the Rif's position can itself be derived from the structure of the Mishnah. It says that if she went from her husband's grave to her father's house, or did not become guardian afterward, the heirs cannot make her swear. If this referred only to Rashi's case where the husband had expressly exempted her against his heirs as well, the rule would already have been taught. The following clause, that if she returned to her husband's house and acted as guardian the heirs may make her swear for the future but not the past, would likewise add little, because the prior clause already established that the past is protected. Rather, the Mishnah teaches two independent points and speaks in both situations, whether he gave an exemption or not. First, if she did not continue as guardian, the heirs cannot make her swear for the past even absent an exemption, because the husband's failure to make her swear before death is deemed a waiver. Second, if she did continue as guardian, the heirs may swear her regarding the future, but they still may not revive the past by gilgul. Where there was no exemption, that follows all the more from the completed waiver. Where there was an exemption, which by itself is not identical to waiver because he was unable to demand the oath during his life, the Mishnah teaches that gilgul still does not circumvent the exemption. Thus the Mishnah supports both the Rif's doctrine of waiver and the sages of Narbonne's doctrine concerning gilgul. This reading also answers a possible objection from the Gemara at the end of Shevuot, which asks whether gilgul applies to a rabbinic oath. Why not resolve the question from our Mishnah, since the heirs' oath against the widow is rabbinic and yet the Mishnah appears to deny gilgul? Under one initial reading, the clause may refer to a case with no exemption and teach precisely that gilgul does not apply to that rabbinic oath. Alternatively, as the Beit Yosef cites in the name of French sages, when partners divide and the prior oath is suspended until another oath obligation arises, the later inclusion is not ordinary gilgul at all. The original obligation was deferred conditionally until a new oath arose, and when that condition occurs the first oath revives. For the same reason, the Rashba's question regarding whether an uncertain claim can be rolled into another uncertain oath is not decisive here. The law can be understood as revival of the earlier obligation rather than creation of a new gilgul.
