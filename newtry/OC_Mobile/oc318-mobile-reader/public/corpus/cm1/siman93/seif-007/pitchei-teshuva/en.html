If he exempted him from an oath, he cannot impose it through extension. Darkei Moshe cites Nimukei Yosef, who holds that even after one party has exempted the other from the oath, he may still require it by way of gilgul shevuah. Ramban, citing a Gaon, rules similarly. Beit Yosef and the authorities in Even HaEzer cite this opinion. Nevertheless, because neither the Mechaber nor the Ramah mentions it in their rulings, either here or in Even HaEzer, it appears that they did not accept it and instead follow the Rosh and the French rabbis that an exemption from the oath also prevents later extension. Bach likewise rules this way. See Shach, siman 71. Panim Meirot, part II, siman 137, discusses two partners who litigated and were each held liable for the partners' oath, then compromised and mutually waived those oaths. Later one became obligated to the other in an oath concerning different money, and the other sought to attach the original partners' oath through gilgul. Some judges required it, reasoning that the mutual waiver did not expressly waive future gilgul. Panim Meirot rules that they erred. First, even in Nimukei Yosef's case, where credibility had been granted and the oath waived in advance, Shulchan Aruch and Ramah appear to hold that gilgul is no longer available, and the possessor can certainly plead that he relies on that view. Second, Nimukei Yosef spoke only of an advance waiver before any dispute arose. Where the parties already litigated, an oath was adjudicated, and they then knowingly compromised and waived it, their waiver is complete. No authority would revive that first oath by gilgul after a final waiver, for after forgiveness nothing remains.
