If he died, etc. This is explicit in Rambam, Laws of Agents and Partners 9. He writes that his teachers ruled that if a partner died, the heir cannot impose the partners' oath upon his father's partner on an uncertain claim, because the heir does not know that his father suspected the partner of two silver ma'ot. Others ruled that the heir may also impose the oath on an uncertain claim, and Rambam says this is the proper ruling, for heirs may require an oath from a woman who served as guardian during her husband's lifetime. The Kesef Mishneh asks what proof there is from that case, since perhaps the heirs there themselves suspect her of two silver ma'ot. The Lechem Mishneh struggles with this comment. The Maharash already addressed this responsum, and his explanation is correct. The entire dispute necessarily concerns heirs who themselves suspect the former partner of having taken two silver ma'ot from their father. If they do not suspect him at all and regard him as certainly trustworthy, what relevance is their father's former suspicion? The money is now theirs, and their acknowledgment of his trustworthiness is effectively a complete waiver. Thus the dispute is where the heirs themselves have an uncertainty and therefore a suspicion. Rambam's teachers hold that the partner can answer: I accepted a partnership with your father because I trusted that he would not suspect me; your suspicion does not bind me, for you were not my litigants and the partnership with him has ended. Their stated reason that the heirs do not know whether the father suspected him of two silver ma'ot means that they cannot say with certainty, "Our father told us that he suspected you of that amount." If they can say that, even Rambam's teachers would allow an oath, because they then stand in the father's place. Although Rambam rules elsewhere that an heir who says, "My father told me you owe him," does not create the definite claim needed for an ordinary oath, here the oath itself may be imposed on an uncertain claim, so a report from the father is sufficient to establish the necessary suspicion. This is precisely the Kesef Mishneh's question. What proof does Rambam bring from the Mishnah in HaKotev that heirs may make a widow swear? Perhaps that case is one in which they say that their father instructed them that he suspected her. The Gemara's unqualified language can rely on the general rule that the suspicion must concern two silver ma'ot. The Kenesset HaGedolah cites this understanding from a manuscript responsum. The proof can nevertheless be defended. If the heirs' own suspicion were irrelevant because the former partner could say they are not his litigants, then only the father's suspicion could authorize their oath. Consequently, if the father had granted the woman or partner an exemption from his oath, even without expressly extending it to heirs, the heirs would have no oath. Their personal suspicion would have no force, and to the extent they came through their father his waiver would defeat them. Yet the Mishnah says that when a husband writes, "I have no vow or oath against you," he himself cannot make her swear but his heirs can. The Gemara initially explains that this concerns her guardianship during his lifetime. If the heirs could act only through his own suspicion, his waiver should defeat them. The Mishnah therefore demonstrates that their independent suspicion is enough. Although the Gemara later abandons that particular interpretation because of another question, namely how the woman knew in advance that she would become guardian, it did not reject the legal premise about the heirs' power. Rambam's proof is therefore sound. And by this we can also resolve the old difficulty concerning the Rif and those who follow him. They explain the Mishnah's case of a widow who went from her husband's grave to her father's house to mean that the heirs cannot make her swear for the past even if he gave no express exemption, because by dying without having made her swear he is deemed to have waived the oath. From where do they know that the reason is waiver? Perhaps it is simply that an heir's claim cannot create the oath, as Rambam's teachers held. The answer is that if heirs had no independent power to impose the oath, then according to the opinion that the Mishnah's clause "vow and oath" refers to the guardian's oath rather than to an impaired ketubah, the statement "he cannot make her swear but his heirs can" would be impossible. The legal ability of heirs to impose the oath must therefore exist. The later inability to make her swear for the past must come from waiver. Once the Mishnah is interpreted as granting an exemption from the guardian's oath, it is also understandable why the heirs can still swear her where there was an exemption during the husband's life. The rationale of implied waiver from failure to demand an oath during life does not apply, because he could not demand it after expressly exempting her. Where there was no exemption, however, his failure to act before death can be treated as a complete waiver. Such a waiver is stronger than a mere contractual exemption. A contractual exemption phrased only as "I will not make you swear" need not include heirs, but a completed waiver of the underlying monetary claim removes the matter itself, like a gift, and binds heirs as well. The same reasoning applies to the French sages cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of this siman, who say that after the parties divide the partnership the oath cannot later be imposed because division is comparable to waiver. If it is a true waiver, the heirs likewise cannot revive it. This is especially compelling according to Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh, who rule like Rabbi Shimon that a husband cannot impose the guardian's oath upon his wife until she claims her ketubah. We follow Rabbi Shimon, but the first Tanna necessarily recognizes the heirs' independent power to make other guardians and partners swear. Rabbi Shimon's disagreement is limited to the special presumed intention of a husband toward his wife, not to ordinary partners and guardians. This also resolves the Taz's question, cited by the Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 96, why the Mechaber here rules simply that an heir can impose the oath, whereas in Even HaEzer he also cites Rav Hai Gaon and the Rif that where the husband did not make the woman swear in his lifetime he is deemed to have waived the oath. Here the Mechaber's purpose is only to establish the heir's legal capacity to demand the oath, contrary to Rambam's teachers. A practical case can arise where the father granted the partner a personal exemption during his lifetime but not to his heirs. There is then no implied waiver from failure to demand the oath, because the father was unable to demand it, yet the heir still has the independent right to impose it. Where no exemption was granted, whether death itself implies waiver remains the separate dispute discussed in Even HaEzer. Thus there is no contradiction.
