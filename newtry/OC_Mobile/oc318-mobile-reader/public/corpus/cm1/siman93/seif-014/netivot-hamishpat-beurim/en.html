Levi has already been discharged by Shimon's admission. At first glance this is very difficult. Whichever way the case is understood, there appears to be a problem. If the document is in Shimon's possession and he has a migo that he could have destroyed it, then even if the document expressly states that it belongs to the partnership, Shimon should be believed even for his own benefit by virtue of the migo, as in seif 13 where he held funds equal to the debt. This is also evident from siman 108 seif 5. If, however, the document is in Reuven's possession and Shimon has no migo, then again either way there is difficulty. If it is known that the document derives from partnership funds, Shimon is not believed even with respect to Levi, as is evident from siman 71 seif 23. If it is not known to derive from partnership funds, Shimon should be believed even with respect to himself, just as he is believed with respect to Levi by virtue of the migo that he could have claimed that the entire document belonged to him. However, according to the Rambam, who is the author of this opinion, the matter is straightforward. The Mechaber rules in his name in siman 66 seif 11 that one who produces a promissory note and claims that another sold it to him by writing and delivery does not need witnesses regarding his acquisition, but does need witnesses in order to collect from the debtor. If Levi admits that he paid, he must pay Reuven. Accordingly, here, since Reuven says that the document belongs to the partnership and the document is in his possession, he is believed by virtue of a migo that he acquired it, at least with respect to the consequence that if Shimon admits to Levi that the debt was paid, Shimon must reimburse Reuven, just as in siman 66 regarding one who claims to have purchased the note. But vis-a-vis Levi, Reuven is not believed to say that it belongs to the partnership, just as one is not believed against the debtor merely by claiming to have purchased the note. Although it is explained there that if Levi claims there was no sale he is exempt, and likewise if he admits the sale but claims that payment was made beforehand he is believed by a migo, as explained by the Shach there, it appears that if Shimon claims that the debt was paid afterward and that he returned the payment to Reuven, he is not believed. Were that so, why would the note still be in Reuven's possession? Just as a debtor is not believed to say, “I paid but did not take back my note,” because of the argument, “Why is your note still in his hand?”, so too the seller of the note is not believed to say that he later transferred the payment to the buyer while the note remains in the buyer's possession. Accordingly, here Shimon is not believed to say, “I collected and returned the money to the partnership fund,” while the note remains with Reuven. The Rama's failure to comment, however, implies that he agrees with the Mechaber, and the foregoing resolution cannot explain his position, because in siman 66 the Rama concludes that even regarding acquisition, possession of the note does not make the claimant believed. It can also be asked why Shimon should not be believed when claiming later payment by a migo that he could have claimed that the debt had been paid earlier. It therefore appears that the matter depends on the rule in siman 62. Where one of several brothers conducts the household business, documents are made in his name, and he claims that they are his personal property, he bears the burden of proof. If he dies, however, the brothers bear the burden of proof. See the Sema there for the reason. It is known that for a creditor and a purchaser, beit din advances claims just as it does for heirs. Therefore, when Shimon admits that the debt was paid, the creditor is analogous to a purchaser, as the Shach writes in siman 86, seif katan 5 and 7. Levi is therefore discharged because we advance for him the claim that the note belongs to Shimon personally, since the note was made in Shimon's name and it is not established from the document that it belongs to the partnership, just as we would make that claim if Shimon had died. But against Reuven, Shimon himself is not believed merely because the documents are in his name, since he was conducting the partnership's business. See Tumim to siman 62, who concludes that there is no distinction whether only one partner conducts the business or both do. According to the Shach there, who holds that even a migo does not help a partner who conducts the business, Shimon would not be believed even if the note were physically in his possession. However, the Rama in Even HaEzer siman 88 concludes that where he has a migo he is believed. Therefore, our case must involve a note in Reuven's possession, so Shimon has no migo and must pay. This is unlike the case below in siman 108 seif 8, where he does not have to pay, because there he is believed by virtue of a migo and is therefore exempt even when there are witnesses to the transaction. Here that is not so. The case concerns partners in all their business dealings. It is possible that even where they are partners only in a specific enterprise, if it is known that this debt arose from that enterprise and from the jointly owned shop, it is comparable to a partnership in all business dealings, and Shimon is not believed to claim that he carried out personal business of his own through the common shop.
