For if he exempted him from an oath, he cannot impose it through gilgul. Darkei Moshe writes concerning this: "However, Nimukei Yosef in the chapter HaKotev, folio 514, writes that even though he exempted him from the oath, he can still impose it through gilgul, and the Ramban likewise writes so in the name of a Gaon." Beit Yosef, at the beginning of siman 71 above, also mentions these words of Nimukei Yosef concerning a clause of trustworthiness. See there. Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 58, seif 4, likewise cite the view of Nimukei Yosef. Nevertheless, the view of the Mechaber and Rama, who do not mention this opinion at all in the Shulchan Aruch, neither here nor in Even HaEzer there, demonstrates that they gave this view no weight and follow the Rosh in the chapter HaKotev and the French rabbis cited by Beit Yosef at the end of this siman. This is also the view of the Bach in this seif katan. See the Shach above, siman 71, seif katan 14, concerning this. See Responsa Panim Meirot of my grandfather, part 2, siman 137, concerning two partners who sued one another before beit din. Beit din ruled that each must take the partners' oath according to law. They subsequently compromised and mutually waived the oath. Several days later one partner became obligated to the other in a different monetary oath, and the latter sought to impose upon him, through gilgul, the original partners' oath. Some judges ruled that he was obligated to swear through gilgul, because although they had exempted and mutually waived the original oath, they had not exempted one another from gilgul, following the above view of the Ramban and Nimukei Yosef. My grandfather answered that they erred. First, even in the case of Nimukei Yosef, where he initially granted trustworthiness and exempted the other party, the Shulchan Aruch and Rama hold that he cannot make him swear even through gilgul, and the Shach in siman 71 leaves the matter requiring study. Certainly the party in possession can say, "I rely on the authorities who exempt me." Moreover, Nimukei Yosef speaks only where he initially wrote a clause of trustworthiness and exempted him before any claims and counterclaims arose between them. But after claims and counterclaims had already arisen, they came to judgment, an oath was imposed, and afterward they mutually waived it, they certainly accepted the waiver completely. Even if he had acted improperly in the partnership business and the other party waived it, no authority ever suggested that the original oath could then be imposed through gilgul. Once there is waiver, nothing remains. See there at length.
