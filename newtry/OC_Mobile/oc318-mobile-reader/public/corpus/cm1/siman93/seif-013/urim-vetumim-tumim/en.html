For a partner is not among those who swear and collect. Above, siman 91, we rule that whenever one person was authorized to incur expenses and one party knows the amount while the other does not, the one who knows swears and collects. The Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat responsum 7, asks why the same is not true here. A partner likewise transacted and paid with authorization. Rambam himself writes in Laws of Robbery 10 that a partner is like one who descends into property with permission and that his expenses are reimbursed on the favorable basis, with an oath. The Shev Yaakov answers that when two partners each placed a fixed sum into a common purse for business, each authorized the other only with respect to the money placed into the partnership. A partner never intended that the other could later swear and extract additional money from his private property. If the loss exceeded the partnership capital, the claimant must prove it under the ordinary rule that the burden of proof rests on one who seeks to extract. He adds that where one partner contributed nothing but they agreed to share equally in profits and losses, the intention is clearer that, if a loss is established, each must bear his portion, and an oath may suffice. His approach is taken from the Radbaz, who similarly writes that the partners were joined only in the six hundred gold coins placed in the venture and that Reuven never intended to authorize Shimon to swear and collect from assets in Reuven's house. With respect, this distinction has no firm basis. Why assume that one partner contemplated losses up to the precise amount deposited but not a greater loss? If he authorized the other with respect to the invested amount, why should even the last fifty gold coins of that amount be collectible merely on the partner's oath? The Radbaz's language that he did not intend to let the other swear and take from his house equally leaves the question why any portion should be different. In truth, people do not enter a partnership with the intention that a loss will certainly occur, whether large or small. Their entire purpose is profit. They agree only that if an actual loss occurs they will share it. This differs fundamentally from one who authorizes another to incur expenses. In the expense case, the owner knowingly places the other person in a situation where expenditure is necessary; the uncertainty is only how much will be spent. Since the person entered with authority to spend, the Sages allowed him to swear and recover. In a partnership, no one enters with the expectation of loss. Loss is merely a contingency, and the partner was not authorized in advance to create a personal claim by his own oath. Of course, if the loss is established by witnesses, or the other partner knows it himself, he must bear his agreed share. We are not dealing with people who deny an actual known loss. But to allow one partner to swear and take whatever sum he asserts from the other's private assets would have no limit; he could claim enormous losses. There is no source or sound rationale for such a power. Even if the competing arguments were balanced, money could not be extracted from the party in possession without a decisive proof from Gemara and poskim. All the more so here, where the rule allowing reimbursement of authorized expenses is itself a rabbinic enactment. In any doubt concerning the scope of such an enactment, even a modest factual distinction returns the law to the Torah baseline, under which the claimant cannot collect without proof. The Radbaz himself offered his point only as an additional consideration among several reasons. It should not be used independently to extract money, and one who does so distorts the law.
And they collect. This implies that if the other partner admits that the partnership capital was diminished, he must pay his share from his private property. The Bach and Mishneh LaMelech ask from the Tur and Mechaber later, siman 176, seif 6, who rule that a partner need not pay a partnership loss from his private assets but merely loses what he placed into the partnership. The Bach attempts to distinguish between a loss caused through the business transactions and one arising otherwise, but this is strained. A close reading of Rambam resolves the issue. Rambam says that "five hundred gold coins were diminished from the principal." The word "principal" is deliberate. The case is one in which the partnership had already earned a definite profit, the partners divided and each took his share of that profit, and only afterward the remaining principal suffered a loss of five hundred. If the partner admits the loss, then according to all authorities, including the Ramah, the earlier profits must first be restored to cover the diminution of principal, because profit remains subject to the restoration of capital. It would make no sense for one partner to retain distributed profit while the other alone loses his principal. Rambam's point is only that the partner who alleges the loss is not believed merely by swearing to take from the other. A partner is not among those who swear and collect. The apparent contradiction therefore disappears.
