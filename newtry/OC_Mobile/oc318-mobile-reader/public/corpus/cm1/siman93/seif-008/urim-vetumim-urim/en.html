Where they did not make an accounting. The same applies if they borrowed from someone, placed the money into the partnership fund, lost the entire amount, and never made a final accounting. This is not considered a completed division, and each partner may still require the other to swear. Sema. Beit Yosef likewise cites the sages of France at the end of the siman that if the entire partnership was lost, or if one partner divided the assets without the other's participation, they may still require oaths from one another. The Mishnah says "they divided," meaning with the knowledge and participation of both.
One another. If the wife of one partner handled the partnership business, she too must swear. Maharshal writes this in the eighth chapter of Bava Kamma, siman 28, in the name of many authorities, and Baal HaIttur likewise writes so regarding a partner. He there appears to hold, according to Rabbeinu Tam and his supporters, that a husband cannot make his wife swear merely because she served as his guardian unless she later claims her ketubah; if so, perhaps his partner likewise cannot compel her to swear. Yet the unqualified language of the authorities cited by Maharshal does not imply that limitation, and this is more correct. A husband may waive his own right to an oath, but what effect does that have on his partner, and what connection does the partner have to the wife's ketubah from her husband? It is even possible that if the partner granted the other partner exemption from oaths, he may still require the wife to swear unless she was expressly included, because appointing her is like authorization to appoint another guardian, and the principal's exemption does not automatically extend to a second guardian. Rosh states this in the chapter HaKotev, and see Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer 98. It is strained to distinguish a wife from any other appointee, because the Mishnah speaks generally of "those who come through her authority," which includes even her sons.
