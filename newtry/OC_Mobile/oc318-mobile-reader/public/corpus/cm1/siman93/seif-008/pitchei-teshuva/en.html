And the debt that remained owing to them from others is a known matter. See Responsa Zikhron Yosef, siman 4. The rabbi who posed the question explained that this means where they know with certainty the amount of the debt and how much will reach each partner, such as where the debt is very secure or there is collateral for it. He therefore wished to say that in his case, where they had to litigate with the debtor and at present do not know how much he will ultimately be obligated to pay them, this is not called a known matter and is not considered as though they have divided. The author there extensively refutes this and concludes that it is clear and true, without any doubt, that the Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch refer even to debts owed by others whose total amount is not presently known. It is nevertheless considered a division because both partners know of the debt and know what proportion each will receive from every unit collected. The authorities did not write 'known debts owed by others,' but simply 'debts owed by others,' implying whether the amount is known or not, such as an unquantified business venture or brokerage, an oral loan whose amount is not known, or a written loan on which partial payment was made and the amount paid is unknown. Likewise, they later wrote that 'the debt is a known matter,' not that 'the amount of the debt is known.' This plainly indicates that even where its present amount is unknown, it suffices that the partners know that this debt remains jointly owned and know how much each will take from whatever is collected. It is therefore properly deemed divided. Proof follows from the authorities' distinction between debts and an item whose weight is unknown. Had uncertainty in the amount of the debt prevented division, they could have made that distinction within debts themselves. The reason is that debts cannot presently be divided in another manner, and the partners have therefore made every division presently possible. Since neither demanded the partners' oath from the other, each has waived it. By contrast, if an unquantified item remains and they could divide it if they wished, each may say that he has not yet waived the partners' oath because he intentionally left that item undivided in order to retain that claim. But debts, although unquantified, cannot presently be divided differently; since they know what share each will receive from every amount collected, they are treated as quantified and divided. He concludes that this is clear and true in halakhah and practice; see there.
And they administer an oath to one another. See Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 8, siman 28, that if the wife of a partner also conducts business in the partnership, she too must swear. His words are cited by Urim VeTummim and Ketzot HaChoshen. Urim VeTummim further writes that even if one partner granted the other an exemption from the oath, he may nevertheless administer an oath to the other's wife if she was not expressly included in the exemption; see there.
