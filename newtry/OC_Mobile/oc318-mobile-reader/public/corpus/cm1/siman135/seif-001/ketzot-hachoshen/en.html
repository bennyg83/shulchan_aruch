Sema writes that even Raavad, who generally does not require a severe oath when one holds collateral through migo, agrees where the possessor claims that the animal damaged him. Sema explains that the migo is one of audacity: the owner does not know exactly how much damage his animal caused, so the possessor can speak without directly contradicting him. By contrast, when one says, "You owe me money," the owner makes a certain denial, and both the present plea and the alternative plea require equal audacity. Tumim objects from Baal HaTerumot, who cites Raavad's criticism of the Geonim. Raavad says the case of goats eating grain certainly requires an oath because the goat owner cannot know how much damage occurred, and every matter impossible for him to know is collected only with an oath. Tumim concludes that the reason is simply uncertainty of amount, not an audacious migo.
I do not regard Tumim's objection as valid. Sema's explanation is exactly the reasoning of Nimukei Yosef in the case of goats that ate barley. Nimukei Yosef writes that even though the claimant has a migo that he could have said the goats were purchased, it is not a complete migo. His present statement that the goats caused damage does not directly challenge the owner, who does not know what happened, whereas saying "I purchased them" would directly challenge him. We do not use a migo from an audacious plea to a nonaudacious plea merely to exempt from an oath. The leading decisors therefore require an oath. This is precisely the approach of the authorities who disagree with Rambam concerning ordinary collateral, including Raavad. Baal HaTerumot's statement that the goat owner does not know the amount is another way of describing why the present plea lacks audacity. It is not a separate unrelated reason. If the owner did know the damage amount and denied it with certainty, then the present plea would itself be audacious and the migo would be from one audacious plea to another, just like ordinary collateral. Under Raavad no severe oath would then be required. It makes no sense to say that ignorance creates greater credibility for the owner than certain denial. Tumim's distinction therefore cannot stand.
