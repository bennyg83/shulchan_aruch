He possessed it for three years. This means by consuming its produce. A protest made outside his presence is also effective, because once we see that the owner was particular, his protest has force. Conversely, if he did not protest, even though the chazakah was established outside his presence, it is effective. Although the Gemara explains the rule by saying that news travels from person to person and therefore the owner should protest so that the possessor will preserve his deed, while movable property is not normally documented by deed, nevertheless the Gemara compares freely roaming livestock to slaves, and slaves have the full law of land in this respect. The same therefore applies to freely roaming livestock. See my novellae to the sugya. If, regarding such livestock, the claimant says, "You stole it," or "You robbed it from my house," he is not believed through a migo that he could have said, "You seized it from the marketplace when it wandered there," because we do not presume people to be thieves even through a migo. Tosafot in Shevuot 46, who write that with freely roaming livestock the claimant is believed even to say that it was stolen, mean specifically that he says, "It was stolen," not, "You stole it." The same is their intent regarding objects ordinarily lent out. Otherwise they would contradict what they write immediately afterward, that according to all opinions one is not believed when he says, "You stole it." Likewise, in Bava Batra 128, concerning the claim "You stole my slave, if you wish swear and take," Rashbam explains the case as after three years, implying that within three years the original owner is legally believed even when he claims theft. That too can be understood where he says, "You seized it from the marketplace." But if he says, "It was stolen or robbed from my house," he is not believed.
