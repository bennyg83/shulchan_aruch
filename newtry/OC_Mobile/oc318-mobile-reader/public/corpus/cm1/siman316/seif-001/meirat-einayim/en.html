And the renter wishes to rent it onward, etc., he may do so. Rambam derives this from the law of a ship stated in the Gemara and recorded by the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 311. If one rented a ship to travel with his merchandise to a particular place and sold the merchandise halfway there, he may put another person in his place from that point until the contracted destination. Although the Gemara says that the shipowner may have a grievance against him, that is because the shipowner lives together with the renter on the ship and can say, "I was more comfortable with you than with this person." Here, by contrast, the owner of the house does not live together with the renter. The principle "do not withhold good from its owner" therefore applies. The Sages said that a renter may not sublet only with movable property, lest the second bailee abscond with or lose it, and with a field, lest the second renter weaken the land, as stated in siman 212 and above in siman 315. With a house, the owner can show the subtenant all the details of the property and stipulate that it be returned intact. All this was explained in the Perishah from Rambam and Maggid Mishneh. See there.
If the owner says to him, "Why should you trouble yourself, etc." Rambam also writes this, and gives the reason of "do not withhold good from its owner." See the Perishah.
The owner may rent it to others, etc. Mordechai explains that if the owner already received the rent, he need not return the money to the renter even if the renter says, "If so, leave the house empty." The owner need not heed him. But if the owner has not yet received the rent and then rents the house to someone else, he deducts from the first renter's payment whatever he receives from the new renter, even if the first renter says, "I will pay the full amount, provided that no one else lives there." Although the owner need not heed that demand, one can say the opposite: the renter thereby revealed that he was willing to pay the full rent only if no one else lived there. If others do live there, how can we extract the full amount from him? This is straightforward.
He may sell his right to another, etc. This requires study in light of what the Tur and Mechaber write immediately below, seif 2, that where two people jointly rent a house, one cannot place another person in his place. The same should apply here. Although the law concerning a ship is explicit in the Gemara, that a renter may put another in his place and the shipowner has only a grievance, a ship is different because it is normal for one traveler to leave and another to enter when merchandise is sold during the voyage. At the outset the shipowner accepts that this may occur, though he still has a grievance because his companions have changed. One must distinguish that whenever the owner expressly reserves a right for himself in the property, the renter accepts from the outset that another person may enter in the owner's place if the owner wishes, because a person has dominion over his own property and the owner specifically retained a right to live there. The same therefore applies to another who comes in his place. Examine this carefully, and see Darkei Moshe.
And the recipient need not, etc. The reason given is that the recipient's right is no worse than the right of one who reserved it for himself. Darkei Moshe likewise writes that regarding the principle that a giver gives generously, the recipient's power can be stronger than the giver's. For example, if one sells his house and retains a pit or cistern, he does not retain a right of access, whereas if he sells or gives away the pit or cistern while keeping the house, he must grant the recipient access. See below. So it appears also from Responsa Rashba, siman 1013. I say, however, that this proof is not compelling. We find the opposite as well: one who reserves a right for himself may have stronger rights than one who sells or gives the corresponding thing to another. If one sells two trees within his field, the purchaser acquires no right in the soil and, when the trees dry up, has no right to plant replacements. But if the owner sells the field and retains the trees, all agree that he retains sufficient soil to plant replacements; they disagree only regarding sowing between them. This appears in Bava Batra 37 and 71 and is recorded by the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 216. Likewise, one who sells a palm tree but retains its future fruit is deemed to have retained a sufficient interest in the tree to acquire those fruits. And one who sells his house while retaining a right to live there is deemed to have retained an interest in the house sufficient for his dwelling. But if he separately transfers to another the future fruit of his palm tree or the right to live in his house, the Gemara, Tur, and Mechaber above in simanim 209 and 212 say that the recipient acquires neither the dwelling, because it lacks independent substance, nor the fruit, because it has not yet come into existence. Rama's proof from selling a house while retaining a pit or cistern, compared with selling the pit or cistern while retaining the house, does not establish the point. Tosafos in Bava Batra 71b distinguish the cases. Their general principle is that where the seller or giver did not expressly reserve something for himself, we say that he sold or gave generously. Thus, when he sells the house and the pit and cistern are not included in the sale, they remain his automatically. Since he did not expressly reserve anything, he must acquire for himself a right of access. Conversely, when he sells the pit and cistern and the house remains his automatically, he need not grant the buyer a path. In both directions the rationale is that because he did not expressly descend to a reservation, what he did sell or give was transferred generously. This applies only to something necessary to make the transfer meaningful, such as access to the pit or cistern. By contrast, one who buys two trees in another's field has no right to replant after they dry, because that is not included in the original sale. Where the seller does expressly reserve something that would otherwise have been included in the sale, such as future fruit on a tree or dwelling rights in a house, we say that he reserved generously for himself. Similarly, when he sells the field, the trees would ordinarily be included, and because he expressly reserves them we say that he reserved broadly. I explained all this in the Perishah to simanim 214, 215, and 216. Therefore no proof can be brought from the fact that one who retains a dwelling right may rent it to another, because there the owner expressly reserved that right, and one who expressly reserves is deemed to reserve broadly. If, however, one gives another a right to live in a house as an independent gift, the recipient certainly may rent his acquired right onward; proof appears from Responsa Rashba, siman 1013, cited in Darkei Moshe. Rama also discussed this at length in a responsum cited by Maharshal, responsum 17. From Rama's responsum it appears that even a father who gave his daughter and son-in-law the right to live together with him in one heated room may allow them to transfer that right of residence to another when they do not need it. He ruled there that the daughter need not contribute anything for firewood to heat the room in which they all live together, showing that the case involved joint residence. Rama's proof was from one who reserves a right to live with a renter and may rent that reserved right to another, together with the principle that the recipient's power is no worse than the giver's, as he writes here. But I say that this proof is not decisive for the reason just explained from Tosafos. Only with a right expressly reserved for oneself do we say that the reservation was broad enough to permit living together with the renter. Where a dwelling right was given to another, the heir may say, "I am comfortable living with you but not with a different person," as the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber write immediately below in seif 2. Maharshal likewise disagrees with Rama in responsum 17, though he does so based on the estimated intent of the donor rather than by rejecting Rama's proof. Rama's words here, that the recipient is no worse than the one who reserved the right, taken together with his earlier ruling that one who reserved a right to live together with the renter may rent that right to another, imply that he likewise holds that one given a dwelling right may transfer it to another. I have explained why, in my opinion, the cases should be distinguished. This applies not only when they would live in one room, but even in one house where each has a separate doorway from the public domain to his room and back, because there can still be a legitimate objection to living with a different person. The Ir Shushan copied Rama's ruling; I have written what appears correct to me.
