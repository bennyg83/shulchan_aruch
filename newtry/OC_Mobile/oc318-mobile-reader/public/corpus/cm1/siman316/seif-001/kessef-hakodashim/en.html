One household larger than another. The rule in siman 316 that a tenant may sublet only to a household no larger than his own may have been particularly appropriate in earlier times, when all structural repairs fell upon the landlord and not upon the tenant. Since land is excluded from the ordinary laws of custodians, and according to some even negligence creates no custodial liability, a larger household could cause greater deterioration of the structure that the landlord would have to repair. Today, however, in places where established custom places upon the tenant the obligation to return the building at the end of the lease in the condition in which he received it, and this is so fixed that it need not be written, the landlord may have no legitimate objection to a somewhat larger household where a separate room is rented and the landlord has no use of it or of the courtyard. This can resemble 'one benefits and the other suffers no loss,' such that objection may be a trait of Sodom. One must nevertheless examine whether additional residents create weakness or deterioration in parts of the building for which custom does not impose repair on the tenant, such as ordinary wall darkening caused by residence. Similarly, if tenant and landlord agreed that the tenant would return the house before the original term and the discussion expressly assumed that the landlord would live there, absent an express condition the landlord may still be able to rent it to another, because the tenant suffers no loss and may not use his surrender merely to create a benefit for himself. Regarding the Sema's statement there that once the first tenant has paid, rent later collected from a substitute tenant does not belong to him, where the landlord merely seized payment from the first tenant based on a separate debt, it appears that so long as the year's rent has not truly been paid the first tenant may retain an interest in later rental income. In another case, one rented from his partner the right to install a still in a jointly owned distillery. Under the partnership agreement the lessor could install two stills for himself but could not rent out the right to a third still without his partner's consent. Later the lessor wished to install the third still himself, and his partner did not object, but the tenant claimed that he had rented on the assumption there would be no third still. Although he had seen the partnership agreement and knew the third-still possibility, he assumed the other partner would not agree. At the time of the lease the lessor also expected to live far away throughout the term, but that arrangement later ended and he remained local. There was some basis to argue that the tenant's lease should be adjusted, yet when the lessor stayed local the tenant did not immediately object, which indicated that the lease had initially been absolute. Because the law of implied conditions in such circumstances is difficult, we arranged a compromise. In another matter, a son had a written document establishing ownership of one room in his father's house. For years he did not live there and his father rented it to others, without claiming to have purchased it from the son. A father also does not readily acquire chazakah in a son's property merely through years of use. A prospective tenant later asked the son whether he needed the room, and the son replied that he would live elsewhere. After the tenant rented it from the father, the son retracted and said he had never authorized the rental, but merely meant that he did not presently intend to quarrel with his father over use of the room. Since his statement did not expressly appoint his father as agent to rent it, the lease may be void. This requires further study.
