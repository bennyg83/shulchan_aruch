To rent it to others. See Baer Heitev in the name of Sema, who writes from Mordechai that if the landlord already received the rent, he need not return it to the first tenant even if he later rents the premises to another. Ketzot HaChoshen strongly questions this. How may the landlord profit from property whose possessory use for the rental term belongs to the tenant? Even if Mordechai holds that where rent was prepaid the tenant cannot sublet, the landlord's option may be only to leave the premises vacant or allow someone to live there without charge. If the landlord rents it for money, why should he retain the second rent? It should belong to the first tenant. Ketzot further argues that Maharam of Rothenburg appears to dispute Mordechai's premise that prepayment bars subletting, and Beit Yosef to siman 312 rejects most of that Mordechai passage except the point that the tenant cannot insist that the house remain vacant, because a house is preserved through occupancy. Darchei Moshe likewise omits the prepayment distinction. Ketzot therefore disagrees with Sema on two points: first, practical law does not accept that prepayment alone prevents the tenant from subletting; second, even if it did, where the landlord actually rents the premises to another, the second rent should belong to the first tenant. Netivot HaMishpat also questions Sema, but differently. He says Sema should first have stated the rule that after the landlord receives prepaid rent the tenant cannot sublet, and only then could one understand why rent collected from a replacement occupant belongs to the landlord. Since Sema omitted that first rule, he apparently did not accept it. Netivot explains that Mordechai's prohibition against subletting concerned a case where the landlord himself lived together with the tenant. There, because the landlord has a legitimate personal objection to a substitute occupant, the tenant cannot sublet whether or not he prepaid. Where the landlord does not live there and the tenant may sublet, any rent obtained from a substitute belongs to the first tenant even if he prepaid. Conversely, where the tenant may not sublet, such as where he lives together with the landlord, any rent the landlord later obtains belongs to the landlord, though the first tenant is entitled to an idle use deduction analogous to an idle worker. Responsa Brit Avraham, Choshen Mishpat, siman 34, discusses one who rented half a shop while both parties occupied it. The tenant paid quarterly, then left during a quarter. The landlord seized merchandise as security for that quarter and, to avoid leaving the shop empty, placed his daughter there with merchandise in the vacated half. The tenant demanded return of his collateral, arguing that another occupant had replaced him. The questioner thought that if the landlord received rent from his daughter the first tenant might still owe full rent under Sema, and that holding collateral is equivalent to having received payment. Brit Avraham rejects this. He argues at length that whether replacement rent belongs to the original tenant does not necessarily depend on whether the original tenant himself had the right to sublet. Even according to Raavad and those who allow the landlord to object to a substitute tenant, once the original tenant has left and the landlord himself chooses to rent the premises to another without objecting to that person, the replacement rent belongs to the first tenant, because the landlord has demonstrated that he has no personal objection. Rama in siman 363, seif 10, supports this: where the tenant had authority to sublet, all replacement rent belongs to him; even where he lacked authority, only the excess above his own rent belongs to the owner, implying that the amount corresponding to his original rent remains his. Brit Avraham then analyzes Mordechai's prepayment distinction and notes that Mishneh LaMelech used it to reconcile apparently conflicting rulings of Rambam and Tur, but Darchei Moshe, Sema, Taz, and Bach offer other reconciliations, showing that they do not accept prepayment as the controlling distinction. Even if prepayment sometimes bars subletting, it is still not clear that replacement rent collected by the landlord belongs to him rather than to the first tenant. Brit Avraham ultimately explains that if prepayment has legal significance, it is not because the landlord's objection prevails merely through possession of the money, but because prepayment indicates the tenant accepted that the rent should become the landlord's absolutely even if the tenant later left through some circumstance. This resembles the rule that when rent was prepaid and the tenant dies during the term, the landlord may retain it because the tenant accepted the risk. Accordingly, only voluntary prepayment creates such an inference. Mere seizure of collateral by the landlord does not. In the shop case, therefore, if the landlord actually received rent from his daughter, that amount must be deducted from the first tenant's obligation, and possession of the seized collateral does not alter the rule. The tenant may even impose a general communal ban upon the landlord if he denies receiving rent from the daughter, because the landlord may be withholding replacement rent. Brit Avraham elsewhere states explicitly that even where the tenant could not personally sublet, if the landlord in fact rented to another and did not object to that occupant, the replacement rent generally belongs to the first tenant unless the first tenant had prepaid under circumstances showing acceptance that the rent would remain the landlord's. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that Brit Avraham's proof from Rama in siman 363 can be distinguished. There the first tenant had some residual right to sublet to a suitable person with no larger household, and only violated that right by renting to an unsuitable person or larger household. Rama may therefore give the owner only the excess rent. Where the landlord lives with the tenant and the tenant has no right to sublet to anyone at all, Netivot HaMishpat may still be correct that if the landlord later rents to someone else all replacement rent belongs to the landlord, subject to an idle use deduction. The matter requires study.
