The recipient need not. See Sema, seif katan 5. Without his words, Rama's ruling appears primary. There is a basic difference between a renter and one who owns the relevant use-right. Where two people are full partners, each can sell or transfer his own share even if doing so causes some inconvenience to the other, so long as he does not inflict direct actionable damage. A person may generally do as he wishes with his own property. A renter is different. Rental does not transfer full ownership. A tenant may sublet only where the substitution causes no legally cognizable harm to the owner. Therefore a tenant cannot sublet where the landlord lives with him, because the change of co-occupant itself is harmful. The same principle can limit subletting of movable property or land where the new use increases wear or inconvenience. Likewise, when two people are merely co-tenants, each stands toward the other with the owner's protective rights and may not introduce another occupant in a way that harms the co-tenant. But if a person retained for himself a field for its fruits or a house for dwelling in the proprietary form described in siman 212, that retained right is his own property. He may transfer or rent it even if the transfer creates some incidental inconvenience to another, because he is acting within his own proprietary right. The same applies where one gives another "a house for dwelling." To be legally effective at all, such a gift must transfer a proprietary body-interest sufficient to support the dwelling-right. The recipient therefore has a stronger right than a tenant and can transfer it. Sema himself writes in siman 212 that even where a tenant could not sublet, one who purchased a field for its fruits may sell or rent his right. Accordingly, Rama's distinction is sound: a proprietary fruit or dwelling right can be transferable even where an ordinary rental right would not be.
