For the doubt arose in his domain. Suppose goods were stolen or lost after the parties completed their commercial acquisition through the customary handshake, but it is unknown whether the theft occurred before or after the kinyan. It would seem that the purchaser must pay because the party in whose domain the doubt arose bears the burden of proof. Yet perhaps that rule applies only where there is a bodily presumption, as in the cases of a possible tereifah or physical defect. There we say that a body retains its prior status and do not transfer a change from one domain to another. Therefore, if the defect is found in the purchaser's domain, we assume the body changed there unless he proves otherwise. Theft and loss are different. There is indeed a general presumption that property remains where it was, but that is weaker than a bodily presumption. Where the purchaser holds the purchase money, such a weaker presumption should not be enough to extract money from him. There is proof from Rambam in the law of a three party transfer. If Reuven owed Shimon a maneh, Shimon transferred the debt to Levi, and Reuven later proved insolvent, Levi claims that Reuven was already poor and Shimon deceived him, while Shimon says he had been solvent. Rambam places the burden on Shimon to prove solvency. If the general rule were that whoever's domain contained the later discovered uncertainty bears the burden, Levi should have to prove prior poverty. The fact that the loan was documented does not alter the conceptual point, for the bodily presumption rule can compel a purchaser to pay even where money is being extracted. Mordechai concerning a coat that later became cheaper makes the same distinction. He rejects analogy to a slaughtered animal because there the seller is supported by a bodily presumption that the animal was not a tereifah. A market price, however, is naturally subject to change and has no comparable bodily presumption. Even if theft and loss involve some status quo presumption, the bodily presumption is stronger and uniquely capable of supporting extraction. The Tosefta states expressly that if a cow was sold and stolen and each party says it was stolen in the other's domain, they divide; if the thief is later found and the issue is who receives double payment, they divide that too. The Tosefta follows Sumchus, who divides doubtful money. According to the Sages, the one seeking to extract bears the burden, and if the purchaser has not yet paid he need not pay. This proves that the special rule of "the doubt arose in his domain" does not govern ordinary theft or loss.
