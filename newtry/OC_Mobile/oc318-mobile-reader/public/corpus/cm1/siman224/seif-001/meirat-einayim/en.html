The view here is that since the doubt arose, meaning the death became known, only after the meshichah, at a time when the object was under the presumption of the owner of the cow, the burden of proof rests upon him. If he cannot prove it, we maintain the animal's prior presumption of life. In the Perishah I explained why we do not say the same at the beginning of the preceding siman, where one exchanges a cow for a donkey, the owner of the cow pulls the donkey, the cow gives birth, and it is unknown whether the birth occurred before or after the meshichah. One might argue there too that at the time the donkey was pulled the cow belonged to the donkey owner and was presumed pregnant, so when the doubt later arose we should maintain the prior presumption that it remained pregnant until after the transaction. I distinguished the cases. Here the donkey was exchanged for a specific known cow, and when the cow was pulled the other party definitively acquired the donkey. The donkey is therefore considered to have stood in his ownership. In the prior case, however, the dispute concerns the offspring itself, and it was never established that the offspring was included in the transaction. The cow might not have produced a viable offspring, or might not have been pregnant at all, while the transaction could still have taken effect without the offspring. See the Perishah for another distinction.
