Seif 1. And some say, etc. Note: If one buys an article from another and a stain or similar defect is later found, but it is unknown whether it arose while the article was with the first owner or the second, determining who bears the cleaning expense can be learned from Niddah 58 and Tosafot there, s.v. "and regarding the law we learned." Tosafot ask why we do not apply the rule that whenever a doubt arises while the property is in someone's domain, that person bears the burden of proof. They answer first that because the garment is merely borrowed and is not actually hers, it is not considered fully within her domain. They also answer that the rule applies only where someone seeks to extract money, whereas in that case the later borrower is merely being asked to wash the garment. Their second answer is very difficult. Throughout the sugya of defects and the case of one who exchanges a cow, the rule that a doubt arising in a person's domain places the burden upon him applies even where he seeks only to retain money rather than extract it, and this is the practical ruling here. The principal answer is therefore Tosafot's first answer. It follows that where the article was actually purchased, the later purchaser must pay for cleaning even if he has not yet paid the purchase price or happens to possess other money belonging to the seller. Because the doubt arose in his domain, the burden rests upon him. But if the article was only borrowed, the cited Tosafot proves that the rule does not apply, and the borrower is exempt because of the doubt. Thus if one borrows an article or animal and a defect is later found, with no knowledge of when it arose, the borrower is exempt. Tosafot's second answer requires study. Years later I obtained the work Sidrei Taharah on Niddah and found that he too noticed this difficulty. See my work Mei Niddah, where I explained Tosafot properly and derived the practical rule relevant here.
