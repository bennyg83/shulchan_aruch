Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 87, discusses Reuven's sale to Shimon of one hundred kor of grain at one dinar per kor. Shimon paid twenty dinars, was to pay the balance within eight days, and could take delivery within fourteen days. He later paid further sums but retained an amount against the possibility that the full hundred kor would not be present, and Reuven agreed. Reuven then told him to remove the grain because the owner of the loft would no longer store it. Shimon told him to send whomever he wished to move it to another loft. Reuven sent an agent, and when Shimon later came to take the grain twenty kor were missing. Reuven claimed that any loss occurred after delivery to Shimon through Shimon's agent and demanded the balance; Shimon denied this. At first glance, if Shimon admitted Reuven's account, the rule that the party in whose domain the doubt arose bears the burden of proof would place the burden on Shimon, since the doubt arose after the agent moved the grain. Yet the responsum concludes otherwise. We do not know that one hundred kor were present when the agent took possession, because the grain had never been measured. The seller's assertion cannot itself establish a prior presumption. This differs from an animal whose prior sound condition is known and where the rule "here it was found, here it arose" can operate. Accordingly, the purchaser need not pay the amount he retained. If, however, the seller holds money exceeding the price of the eighty kor unquestionably delivered, the purchaser cannot extract it merely on a doubtful claim while the seller asserts with certainty that the full amount was present; the seller need not swear to the quantity corresponding to money already in his possession, though a general ban may be imposed. Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 6, challenges Avodat HaGershuni in light of the Shach in siman 91. Where an agent was appointed to make delivery, there is a presumption that the agent performed his mission. Even if the agent denies finding the full quantity while the seller states certainly that it was there, the seller should at least collect with an oath, like a shopkeeper who says he paid workers at the employer's instruction. Shav Yaakov therefore finds the comparison to a doubt arising in one party's domain difficult unless the seller himself was uncertain whether the full hundred kor remained at the moment the agent received it. Shav Yaakov further discusses a related case involving sacks of grain delivered by ship. The buyer's representative accepted the proper number of sacks without weighing them, and a non-Jew later asserted that their total weight was deficient by the equivalent of seventy sacks. The seller stated that he had weighed them properly before handing them to the boatmen. The court ruled for the seller, because the buyer admitted receiving the correct number and had only a doubtful claim about the weight. The seller was required to affirm before beit din that he delivered the proper weight to the boatmen, while the buyer was to bear the reasonable costs of pursuing the boatmen in civil court. The responsum adds a second ground: even if the deficiency arose while the goods were with the carriers, the buyer or his agent was negligent in accepting the sacks without weighing them when scales had been provided. He thereby caused his own loss. Ultimately, because the buyer's claim was doubtful and the seller asserted definite payment through the goods delivered, the seller was technically exempt even from a hesset oath; nevertheless, due to the large amount involved, the court required a solemn affirmation to satisfy the buyer.
