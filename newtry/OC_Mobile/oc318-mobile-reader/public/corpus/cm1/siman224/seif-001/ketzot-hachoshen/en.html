The seller shall swear that he did not know of the defects. Mordechai says the purchaser cannot be believed through a migo that, had he wished, he could have said, "I have not paid," and then assert definitively that the defect arose in the seller's domain. If the purchaser truly knew that the defect existed before the sale, then he must also have known of it at the time of purchase and is treated as having accepted it. His claim cannot ordinarily be a definite one. Shiltei Gibborim objects that perhaps the purchaser only now learned through witnesses that the defect had already existed before the sale. He personally did not know it at purchase. If so, why should he not make a definite claim now and rely on the migo of nonpayment? This is not a difficulty. Even if he heard the fact from one hundred witnesses, so long as their testimony has not been formally received in beit din before the opposing party, their statements do not become his own definite factual knowledge for litigation. They can suffice as a basis for requiring an oath, even if the information came from a relative, but not to create a definite plea in his own mouth. If he says, however, that the matter was already litigated and witnesses testified in beit din, either before the seller or in one of the recognized situations where testimony can be received in his absence, then he can indeed make the definite claim and can use the migo. Mordechai was not addressing such a case. A difficulty remains from Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch in siman 126, where one who says that the debtor was already poor and the other deceived him can, with a written loan, swear that fact and collect. According to Mordechai's reasoning, how can such a definite plea arise? Perhaps that case more readily allows later definite knowledge, such as learning conclusively that the ship, merchandise, or debts had already been lost or ruined before the transfer.
