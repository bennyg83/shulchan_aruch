Separate property. See the Shach, who writes that this applies even where the husband makes a definite claim. It appears that even if the husband seized the property from her, or borrowed from her, and even if the money had been hidden away, notwithstanding that siman 86, seif 2, states that in such a case he need not return it, nevertheless, once she establishes that she had separate property of her own, her status is entirely like that of any other person.
Movable property. See Shach, seif katan 5, who wonders why the Tur cites this ruling in the name of the Ramah, since it is stated explicitly in Tosafot and the Rosh. This may be resolved as follows. Tosafot and the Rosh, and likewise Even HaEzer siman 86, discuss only a case in which the wife claims ownership of movable property that she deposited with a custodian. Since the custodian could also have returned the property to the husband, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 86, the custodian is himself regarded to some extent as holding the property on the husband's behalf. Thus, where the husband borrowed from her or seized property from her, even in the presence of witnesses, he is exempt from returning it. Property held by the custodian is therefore somewhat comparable to property already seized by the husband. Here, by contrast, the property is wholly in the wife's own possession, and that case is not expressly discussed by Tosafot and the Rosh. The Tur therefore cites the Ramah for it.
And even a widow. See Shach, seif katan 6, who cites the Rambam regarding a wife who does not conduct her husband's business affairs and in whose possession movable property is found. The Rambam distinguishes three cases. First, if she claims that the property is nichsei melug, so that the principal belongs to her, while the husband claims that it came from property belonging to him and therefore belongs entirely to him, she is believed and the property is treated as nichsei melug. Second, if she claims that another person gave it to her as a gift on condition that her husband have no rights in it, thereby seeking to remove it even from his presumptive right to the produce, she is not believed. Third, if she claims that her husband himself gave it to her as a gift, she is believed, even though that claim removes the property from his presumptive right to its produce. Regarding the first category, where the husband claims that the property came from his own funds, it appears that even if she ordinarily manages his business affairs she is believed when the husband concedes that the property is not itself his money and did not derive from the produce of his property. For example, he knows precisely what merchandise he entrusted to her and how much she sold it for, but merely claims that perhaps this property was acquired from his funds. In such a case her law is the same as that of a wife who does not manage his affairs. This resolves the difficulty of Maharlana"ch cited by the Shach from chapter 9 of Hilchot Nachalot. Regarding the second category, where she claims that the gift was given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it, the Shach explains that she is not believed because ordinarily one who gives a gift gives it without such a condition and does not remove it from the husband's rights. According to the Shach, she is not believed even if she has a migo, because this would be a migo against a presumption. By contrast, when she claims that the husband himself gave it to her as a gift, since even an ordinary gift from him does not leave him rights to its produce, she is believed, but only where she has a migo, meaning where the husband did not previously see the property. His words are very difficult, as Tumim already observed. There are additional difficulties. First, what source did the Rambam have for this distinction in the Gemara? It is well known that the Rambam does not ordinarily state a novel rule based solely upon his own reasoning where it has no basis in the Gemara. Second, according to the Shach's reason, if she claims to remove the property from the husband's presumptive right to the produce by some means other than a gift from another person, such as by claiming that she sold her ketubah for its contingent value or that the funds represent the doubled payment received for stolen property, the Shach's reasoning would not apply, and she should therefore be believed. Even more difficult is the Shach's statement that even a widow who claims that property had been given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it is not believed, and that the produce belongs to her husband's heirs. Granted, the case must concern produce that arose during the husband's lifetime, because produce accruing after his death belongs to her, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 95, seif 4. Nevertheless, even if we presume that the gift was originally given to her without any special condition, she should still be able to claim that her husband never knew of it. If he did not know of the property, he never acquired rights to its produce, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 90, seif 12. There is even support for her claim that he did not know of it. Had he known of the property and of its produce, he would have taken the produce from her, since she did not manage his affairs. What, then, are those proceeds doing in her possession? This itself supports the conclusion that they were hers. The Shach's further statement that, according to the Rambam, even where the husband had not seen the property and she therefore has a migo she is nevertheless not believed, is also difficult. Certainly, if the husband is not presently demanding the property and did not know of it at all, but she herself volunteers the information and says that it was given to her on such a condition, even the Shach must agree that she is believed. She could simply have concealed the matter from him. Had she done so, even a gift given to her without any special condition would not have become nichsei melug, as explained in siman 90, seif 12. Whenever a matter is actually within a person's power to bring about as he claims, that is stronger than a migo, which merely means that he could have lied. Rashba writes in Kiddushin, chapter HaOmer, that a claim supported by actual control can overcome even the presumption that a woman is married, and Kiddushin 63 states that such control is equivalent to witnesses. In addition, here she has the principle that the mouth which prohibited is the mouth which permits, which operates even against a presumption. See Tosafot to Bava Kamma 72, s.v. Ein Lecha Bo Ela. Even if the husband previously saw the property and now demands it, but there are no witnesses concerning it, the Shach's position still cannot stand, for she should plainly be believed by virtue of her migo. Rashba states this explicitly in a responsum cited by Beit Yosef, Tur Even HaEzer siman 85: if she admits that she has money in her possession but claims that it was given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it except for what she consumes, she is believed as to whatever she says concerning how the money was given to her, even without an oath, by virtue of the migo that she could have said, "I have nothing in my possession." Thus, although Rashba follows the Rambam that without a migo she is not believed when she says another person gave it to her on such a condition, he clearly holds that with a migo she is believed. One cannot say that the migo there was needed merely to exempt her from an oath. If the husband makes only an uncertain claim, she is in any event exempt from an oath, as Rashba writes there; and if he makes a definite claim, even one who has a migo must swear. It is therefore clear that the migo is required for the substantive credibility of her claim, and with a migo she is indeed believed. Moreover, since the Rambam states that she is not believed when she claims that another person gave it to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it in a case where she has no migo, his subsequent statement that she is believed when she claims, "You, my husband, gave it to me," must likewise apply even where she has no migo, as the Maggid Mishneh and Baal HaTerumot understand, contrary to the Shach. Beit Shmuel and Tumim also agree that the Rambam is speaking of a case in which she has no migo. The Shach's further assertion that according to Tosafot, if she says, "This belongs to so-and-so," she is believed only where she has a migo, whereas if she says, "It is mine," she is believed even without a migo, is extremely difficult. Even HaEzer siman 86 expressly states that with non-hidden money she is believed to say either, "It is mine," or, "It belongs to so-and-so," even without a migo, whereas with hidden money she is not believed to say either that it is hers or that it belongs to another. Tosafot too clearly show that the essential distinction is whether the money was hidden. See Tosafot there. The reasons offered by the later authorities to explain the Rambam are, however, very strained. In my opinion, a reason and source can be given for all of the Rambam's rulings. Consider first the Rambam's first two categories, that she is believed to say, "It was given to me as a gift," but is not believed to say, "It was given to me on condition that my husband have no rights in it." At first sight this is difficult. Since the husband is not believed to assert that the principal itself belongs to him when she is in possession, why is she not believed, while making a definite claim and possessing the property, to remove it from his presumptive rights to the produce? The explanation is as follows. If these assets were known to have been hers at the time of the marriage, and she claimed that her husband later waived his right to their produce by executing a declaration relinquishing claims or the like, she certainly would not be believed, even according to Tosafot. Such a claim is comparable to one who claims, "You waived my ketubah," because it asserts an event that supposedly occurred after an established legal status already took effect. This is elementary. Tosafot believe her only when she claims that another person gave the property to her after the marriage, for she then claims that the husband's legal right never attached to these assets at all. Now, regarding assets that she held at the time of the marriage, there is no basis to suppose that they were exempt from the husband's right to produce. The only possible basis for exemption is that they came into her possession after the marriage. This resembles exactly the rule later in siman 250 regarding one who gives his property effective immediately and continuing until a later time, and the recipient then claims that certain property was acquired only afterward. He is not believed because of the principle that property is presumed to remain in the status in which it was previously held, and on the strength of that presumption the property is removed from the heirs. See there. So too here, marriage is tantamount to the wife granting to her husband the produce rights in all property then in her possession. When she later claims that these particular assets only came into her possession afterward, we apply the same presumption and say that they were already in her possession at the time of marriage and therefore became subject to his rights to the produce. Even if the husband did not know of them at that time, as soon as they become known to him they take the status of nichsei melug, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 90. One cannot suggest that perhaps another person gave her the property before marriage on condition that she not bring it into her husband's control. With regard to property she possessed before marriage, there is a presumption that whatever is in her possession belongs to her outright and is available for her ordinary disposition. Against that presumption, a migo is ineffective, as explained in siman 99. Furthermore, the presumption that property remains in its prior status means that it is treated as having belonged to her and her ancestors from before the marriage. Only when she claims that the property came to her from another person after the marriage do we say, by virtue of that continuity presumption, that at the time of marriage the property was already subject to the husband's rights, and therefore it became obligated to him for its produce. This is different when she claims that the husband himself gave it to her as a gift, and the husband claims that it is his and that he never gave it to her. Since she does not manage his affairs, from where did this property come to her? The presumption is that it is hers. She is therefore believed even without a migo, just as an unrelated person would be. Accordingly, in the case of a widow who claims that her husband never knew of these assets and therefore they were never subject to his produce rights, she is believed even where she has no migo. It appears that even the Shach should agree in that case, for his own rationale does not apply. Granted, there may be a presumption that a gift was given to her without a special condition, but it nevertheless does not become nichsei melug if the husband never knew of it. There is also proof for the Rambam's ruling that the husband is not believed to assert that such property came from his own money and belongs entirely to him. Bava Batra 51 states that when a husband sells a field to his wife, she acquires it, and we do not say that his purpose was merely to cause her to reveal money that she had hidden from him. The Gemara asks: remove the money from consideration, and let her acquire the field by the deed. If the husband were believed to claim that the money in her possession belonged entirely to him, what is the force of this question? The Gemara could have answered, as it does in many places, that the latter clause teaches precisely that we do not say that his purpose was to expose her concealed funds. Had we said that this was his purpose, then even though she acquired the field by deed she would still have to pay him other money, because he could claim that the money he received was his own. If she had no other funds, he could recover the field in payment of that amount. By contrast, once we say that the purpose was not merely to expose her funds, then because he received the money as payment for the sale in the presence of witnesses, that receipt is treated as an admission that the money was hers, subject only to his rights to its produce. The field that he sold her would likewise remain subject to his right to its produce. There is no difficulty from Tosafot, who rule that if the husband actually seizes the money he is believed. One might think that, since seizure creates his possession, if the theory of exposing hidden funds applied she would have to pay other money. This is not so. Once she acquired the field by deed and the husband then seeks to recover the money from her, that is not considered a prior seizure. According to the Gemara's eventual answer that she did not acquire merely by the deed, however, it indeed becomes a case of seizure, because there is then doubt whether she acquired the land at all and the field remains with its original owner. There is proof as well for the Rambam's second ruling, that she is not believed to remove the property from the husband's presumptive right to produce. Tosafot ask in Bava Batra there why the Gemara distinguishes between concealed and unconcealed money. Even with unconcealed money, the husband should be able to say that he intended only to take the money out of her possession, since he had no other way to obtain it. According to the Rambam the answer is clear, because as to the produce he can in any event take it from her possession. With respect to the principal itself, even if he seizes it he is not believed according to the Rambam, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 85. The Rambam's distinction between a seizure by way of sale and a seizure arising from a loan from her is not the subject here, and with Hashem's help I will explain it in my novellae to Even HaEzer. The Rambam's third ruling requires no separate proof. For the reason explained above, a wife who does not manage her husband's affairs is no worse than any unrelated person, and she is believed when she says that her husband gave the property to her. Even a widow is believed, as the Shach himself writes. The Shach's statement that according to Tosafot she is believed to say, "It is mine," even where she has no migo, is in fact correct. It cannot be explained on the basis of a migo that the husband gave it to her, because the Gemara's case concerns property in the hands of a custodian. According to Tosafot, where the husband gives property to his wife she cannot freely sell or give it away, and it belongs to him with respect to the produce of its produce, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 85. The custodian would therefore have had to notify the husband because of those interests. She accordingly has no such migo. It follows that according to Tosafot she is believed to say, "It is mine," without a migo. Finally, when the Shach writes, in the name of Maharlana"ch, that she is not believed concerning items known to the husband, his intention is to items that a husband ordinarily gives to a wife who does not manage his affairs, such as clothing, jewelry, and other personal effects. As Maharlana"ch explains, these resemble articles ordinarily lent out. Likewise, when the Shach writes that an unrelated possessor is believed regarding things not known to the owner only because of a migo, his intention there too is to articles ordinarily lent or rented out. Otherwise, even concerning known property an unrelated possessor can be believed without a migo, as explained in siman 133. Money and movable property that a husband does not ordinarily place in the hands of a wife who does not conduct his business are not in the category of articles normally lent out. With respect to them, she is believed like any unrelated possessor. Maharlana"ch, as cited by the Shach, writes similarly that the wife exercises dominion over such property.
She did not conduct his business affairs. See Shach, seif katan 7. If the husband himself caused the promissory notes to be written in her name, according to the Rosh they belong to her, whereas according to Rashba they belong to him, because he may say that he did so only in order to cause her to reveal money that she had concealed. In my opinion there is no dispute. The Rosh speaks where the notes are in the wife's possession. In that situation the reasoning that he merely wished to expose her hidden funds does not apply. Bava Batra 51, after stating that when a husband sells a field to his wife she acquires it and we do not say that he merely intended to expose her funds, asks: remove the money from consideration and let her acquire through the deed. This shows that with respect to the deed itself the rationale of exposing hidden funds does not apply. See Rashbam and Ran there. Rashba and the Rosh compare a husband who causes notes to be written in his wife's name to the case of one who causes a deed to be written in the name of the Exilarch, discussed above in simanim 60 and 69. There the Tur writes that if Shimon protests and says, "Although the money was not mine, I acquired it because you made the transaction in my name and instructed the witnesses to write the deed in my name," his argument is of no substance, because the principal instructed the witnesses to deliver the deed to him and not to Shimon. The Tur's words imply that if he had also instructed that the deed be delivered to Shimon, Shimon would have acquired it. Here, since the notes are in the wife's possession, it follows that he instructed that they be delivered to her, for the reason that I will explain below. Once he instructed delivery of the deeds to her, the theory that he merely wished to expose her funds no longer applies. As the Gemara says, remove the money from consideration, and she acquires through the deed itself. Rashba, by contrast, must be discussing a case in which the notes are not in the wife's possession, for he compares it to a deed written in the Exilarch's name where the Exilarch did not acquire. There too the case is one in which there was no instruction to deliver the deed to the Exilarch, as the Tur states. If the note is in the wife's possession and the husband claims that at the time it was written he instructed that it be delivered to him, and only afterward gave it to her merely for safekeeping, then, since the note was written in her name, it appears that he is not believed. Yevamot 116 states that a person ordinarily does not deposit an item with another where their names are identical in a way that could create confusion. The same reasoning applies here. Since the note was written in the wife's name, we do not suspect that the husband deposited such a note with her. This distinction is needed specifically for a wife who conducts his business affairs, for in that case it is possible that she herself caused notes to be written in her own name without his knowledge. But we do not ordinarily suspect that the husband knowingly deposited with her a note written in her name. The Shach's statement that she is believed even when the notes remain in the husband's possession does not appear correct to me. Since the husband is believed to claim that the money itself was his and that he caused the note to be written in her name merely to expose her hidden funds, as explained in Even HaEzer simanim 85 and 86, the only basis for her acquisition would be the Gemara's reasoning, "remove the money from consideration and let her acquire by the deed." That reasoning requires that the deeds actually be in the wife's possession, as explained above.
A woman who wrote to others. See Shach, seif katan 9, who explains that this refers to a wife who conducts her husband's business affairs, because otherwise she would be believed even if the source of the property were unknown. This follows the Shach's own position in seif katan 10, where he understands the case as one in which she has a migo. According to the Sema, seif katan 7, however, the case concerns a woman who died and therefore has no migo. On that approach, even a wife who did not conduct her husband's business affairs is not believed unless it is known that she had separate property of her own.
If it is known. See Shach, seif katan 10, who answers the Sema's difficulty from siman 56 by distinguishing between the cases. There, he says, the husband gave the wife authority over all of his property, so that she has the status of a third party entrusted to carry out an arrangement. Here the case is merely that of an ordinary wife who conducts her husband's business affairs. Tumim correctly objects that there is no clear boundary for such a distinction, since the very definition of a wife who conducts his business is that she has control over his property. Moreover, the doctrine of relying upon one whom the parties trusted can apply even to an ordinary deposit. Tumim therefore concludes, contrary to the Shach, that where she has a migo she is believed, like any wife who conducts her husband's business affairs. There is a further difficulty. The law of an entrusted third party does not apply here to a wife who conducts her husband's business even in the form described by the Taz and Tumim, namely, where she says that the husband gave her the property to deliver to this other person. According to the Shach's own explanation in siman 56, when the owner denies the alleged entrustment and claims, "You were merely my custodian," the custodian is not believed. Here too the husband denies that he appointed her as an entrusted third party and says that the property was placed in her hands only for purposes of conducting his business. This is precisely the claim, "You were my custodian." All the more so if she concedes that she is merely holding the property for her husband but says that she knows that it belongs to another person. In that case she certainly is not believed, even with a migo, according to the Rosh in siman 108, as I demonstrated in siman 56, seif 6. See there. Still more difficult is the Shach's statement in siman 56 that even if she has already delivered the property to beit din and now has no migo, she remains believed. I already wrote in siman 56 that where a possessor would not be believed to say, "I purchased it," as in the case of articles ordinarily lent or rented out where there are witnesses who saw the property in his possession, he likewise is not believed to say, "I am holding it as an entrusted third party." Tosafot in Gittin 65 write this explicitly. A wife who conducts her husband's business likewise is not believed, without a migo, to say that she purchased property or received it as a gift from her husband, just as a possessor of articles ordinarily lent out is not believed. Accordingly, she should likewise not be believed to say that she holds it as an entrusted third party. Why should that claim be stronger than a claim of purchase? In my opinion the difficulty can be resolved simply. There is another question on the end of siman 56, where a wife who conducts business is believed by virtue of a migo to say that property belongs to others. Yet in siman 99, seif 1, the Mechaber and Rama rule without qualification that a person is not believed, even with a migo, to say that property in his possession belongs to others, because there is a presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him, and a migo does not prevail against such a presumption. The alternative view is cited there only as "some disagree," indicating, as is well known, that the primary ruling follows the first view. The Shach there also rules that the first view is primary and writes that all authorities agree to it. Why, then, is this wife believed here by virtue of a migo to say that the property belongs to others? Is that not a migo against the presumption that property in one's possession belongs to him? Upon examination there is no difficulty. In siman 56 the husband has died. Even if the assets are nichsei melug, after his death they belong to the wife, because his heirs have neither produce rights nor inheritance rights in them. Thus, when she says that they belong to another, she is not asserting a claim against the husband's heirs in order to remove property from a presumption that it is hers. From the heirs' perspective there is no practical difference whether the property belongs to her or to someone else. Rather, she is only seeking to overcome the special presumption applicable to a wife who conducts her husband's business, that everything in her possession came from her husband. Against that presumption a migo is effective, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 85. By contrast, in siman 62 the husband is alive. If the property is hers, the husband has rights to its produce and to inherit it. When she says that it belongs to others, she seeks to remove it from the presumption that it is her own property, and against the strong presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to that person, a migo is ineffective, as explained in siman 99. This would resolve the Sema's difficulty. That resolution, however, works only according to the Shach's statement in seif katan 6 that according to Tosafot a woman is believed to say that property belongs to another only where she has a migo, because the presumption is that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. According to what I wrote above, that Tosafot hold that regarding unconcealed money a wife who does not conduct her husband's business is believed to say that it belongs to another even without a migo, the answer does not suffice. For a wife who does conduct his business, her lack of credibility then rests only upon the presumption that the property came from her husband, and with respect to that presumption there should be no distinction whether the husband is alive or dead. A second resolution therefore appears. The view cited in siman 99 is that of the Maggid Mishneh in the first chapter of Hilchot Malveh. His language is that a person is not believed to say that property in his possession belongs to others, because the presumption is that whatever he possesses is his, and a migo does not prevail against a presumption, since a person may enter into collusion in order to remove his fellow's money. The Maggid Mishneh's final words, that a person may engage in collusion, appear at first to be superfluous, since he had already supplied the sufficient reason that a migo does not prevail against a presumption. It seems that his intent is to answer the rule in the chapter Zeh Borer, codified below in siman 255, concerning one who says, "I saw your father conceal money and say that it belongs to so-and-so." If the witness himself could take the money, he is believed. The rule is stated without qualification, even where the money lies in the heirs' premises. The Rosh likewise states this explicitly in responsa, klal 68. Thus, such a person is believed by virtue of his ability to take the money even against the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to that person. How can the one who actually concealed the money be disbelieved if he himself says it belongs to another, while a person who merely heard him say so is believed? The distinction is straightforward. Where we suspect a person of lying for his own benefit, but argue that had he wished to deceive he could have chosen a different lie, that is the ordinary concept of migo. It may be that in his own calculation the present lie serves him better than the alternative. Such a migo is subject to the ordinary rules governing migo, and there are many situations in which it is not accepted, including where it conflicts with a presumption, as set forth in the Shach's rules of migo in siman 82. This is exactly the case in siman 99, where a debtor says that property belongs to others. We suspect that he says so to prevent his creditor from collecting, and that he is colluding with the alleged owner, as the Maggid Mishneh concludes. One cannot answer that if he wished to deceive he could simply conceal the property in his house, for perhaps he prefers this device so that he can continue to display or use the property. Although such a consideration does not ordinarily destroy a migo, it does justify concern for collusion. He still has the technical migo that he could have given the property to the other person, but perhaps he calculated that it was better to make this assertion before beit din so that, once accepted by beit din, the property would remain with him indefinitely. He need not even identify the supposed owner, as Tosafot in Bava Batra 51, s.v. Kibel, write. Or he may have some other reason for preferring this particular false claim. That is the ordinary situation of migo, and such a migo does not prevail against a presumption. By contrast, in the case of siman 255, where one says, "I saw your father conceal money and say that it belonged to so-and-so," there is no independent basis to suspect him of collusion or lying for his own benefit. If he wished to steal the money for himself, why would he collude with another? Since he could himself take it and say, "It is mine," his statement that it belongs to another is testimony rather than a self-serving claim. Where a matter is within a person's actual power to effect, even a single witness is believed. Indeed, a single witness is believed even to overcome the presumption that a woman is married where the matter is within his power, as shown by the rule that a husband who says, "I divorced my wife," is believed on that basis. Since matters of forbidden relationships are derived by verbal analogy from monetary law, if one witness is believed in a matter of forbidden relationships when the matter is within his power, the same is certainly true in monetary law. By contrast, one who lies for his own benefit is not called a witness but a litigant, and even though he has a migo he is not believed against a presumption. This explains the conclusion of the Maggid Mishneh's language. In any event, his words expressly show that two elements are joined: a migo against a presumption, and the concern that a person may collude for his own benefit. Accordingly, the Sema's question from siman 56 is answered. There the husband had died, and the movable property had not previously been seen in the wife's possession. Had she shown it to beit din and said, "It is mine," she would have been believed by virtue of a migo, as I demonstrated above in seif katan 21 and as Beit Shmuel rules in Even HaEzer siman 85. Therefore, when she instead says, "It belongs to another," there is no concern for collusion. Why would she collude with another when she could say, "It is mine," take everything for herself, and, once believed in beit din, remain believed thereafter just as in any case supported by a migo? She is therefore functioning as a witness, and because the matter is in her power she is believed. The case closely resembles siman 255, where one saw the deceased conceal money, and all the more so here, where the property is literally in her hand and she can give it to the person she identifies. Indeed, the responsum of the Rosh that is the source of the rule in siman 56 expressly adduces the case in siman 255 as proof. Here, however, where a wife wrote property over to others while the husband is alive, matters differ. If she had said, "It is mine," she might be believed through a migo that she could have denied the matter entirely and claimed that the property had been given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it. Once accepted by beit din she might remain believed. Nevertheless, such a gift is still subject to the husband's inheritance right, as the Shach writes in seif katan 6. A person may therefore collude with someone else in order to defeat the husband's eventual inheritance. Moreover, even where she has a migo, if the husband seizes the property or had borrowed it from her he may prevail, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 85. She therefore has a motive to collude and claim that it belongs to others. The case is thus exactly like siman 99, where one is not believed by virtue of a migo because the migo conflicts with a presumption and a person may collude, as the Maggid Mishneh writes. It follows also that the Shach's rule in siman 56, that even a retrospective migo is sufficient after she has already handed the property over to beit din, is not correct. The primary ruling is as Tumim writes: she must make the statement that it belongs to so-and-so at the time she produces the movable property, while she still has the migo. That is the correct rule. According to this explanation, there is also a qualification to the rule in siman 99. The debtor himself is not believed to say, "This belongs to another." But where the debtor has fled and another person comes and says, "I know that the fugitive hid money in such-and-such a place, and I know that it belongs to so-and-so," then even though the money lies in the fugitive's premises, if this witness has the power to take it he is believed, just as in siman 255. The Maggid Mishneh's reason, that a person may collude for his own benefit, does not apply to him. This is clear.
If she died, we advance the claim on behalf of her heirs. See Shach, seif katan 11, who writes that if she died during her husband's lifetime, we do not advance on behalf of her heirs the claim that the property had been given to her as a gift on condition that her husband have no rights in it even with respect to inheritance. Tumim questions this according to Rashba and Tosafot, who hold that when the wife herself makes such a claim she is believed. Why should we not make the same claim on behalf of the heirs? There is no difficulty. It is uncommon for a donor to give a gift in this particular form, and we do not advance an uncommon claim on behalf of orphans.
He must bring proof. See Shach, seif katan 15, who writes that if one brother claims that the other brothers gave the property to him as a gift, he is believed where he has a migo that he could have denied the matter entirely or made an equivalent claim. At first sight his comment appears to apply even to promissory notes, since the Mechaber is discussing notes and the Shach places his comment there. This cannot be correct. Certainly, if he claims that the brothers gave him the note itself while the money underlying the note belonged to the partnership when the note was written, they could not transfer the note to him without both a written assignment and delivery, as explained in siman 66, seif 5, and Shach there, seif katan 18. But even if he claims that they gave him the money itself as a gift and that he then lent his own money and had the note written in his name, he is not believed merely because he has a migo. Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 44, part 16, states explicitly that although regarding movable property, where there are no witnesses, one who possesses it may be believed by a migo that he could have denied the matter or claimed that he returned it, the rule concerning brothers is different with respect to deeds. The Gemara in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim teaches that where deeds and documents are found in the name of one of the brothers, the law follows Rav that he must bring proof. Baal HaTerumot explains that this applies specifically to documents because possession of the document does not clearly establish possession of the money represented by it. By contrast, if movable property is physically in his hand and he says, "It is mine because they gave it to me," he is believed where there are no witnesses. Thus, he states expressly that with notes he is not believed even if he has a migo. There is also proof from Shach, siman 64, seif katan 4, that the ordinary doctrine of migo does not apply to promissory notes, and all of the reasons he gives there apply here as well. Even according to the Sema there, who accepts a migo concerning notes, that is where the possessor says, "I took it as collateral." Where he claims complete ownership, he is not believed through a migo, as I will explain there. Certainly, where the borrowers named in the notes are known, he has no migo at all. If he were to say, "There was never such a transaction," or, "I returned the notes," the borrowers could come to beit din and have a receipt recorded. Even where the borrowers are not presently known, his supposed migo is deficient. He fears that the borrowers themselves may eventually disclose the matter if they know that the money belonged to the partnership and learn that he denied the transaction. Thus, the present claim of gift is safer for him than denial. He also cannot effectively sell the notes, for the reason stated by the Shach in siman 64, seif katan 4. Since they are partners, the note is legally treated as though it were written in all of their names. So long as he has not established a purchase, a buyer from him would be unable to collect on it just as he himself could not. A claim that the brothers gave it to him is therefore materially stronger than a claim of complete denial, and there is no migo. This is clear.
And he left orphans. See Shach, seif katan 15, who writes that the same applies if he gave the property away during his lifetime and then died. From the Shach's addition "and then died" it appears that while the original owner remains alive we do not advance on behalf of the recipient the claim that perhaps the owner has proof but chooses not to produce it. His silence cannot be used to harm others. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that because the subject is a promissory note, the owner could waive the debt and thereby destroy its value. He is therefore believed to act to its detriment, just as in siman 66, seif 15, where a seller who refuses to swear may thereby cause the purchaser to lose collection. According to that explanation, with a deed conveying land, where there is no analogous migo that the original owner could waive the debtor's obligation, even if the original owner is alive we should advance the relevant claim on behalf of the purchaser or gift recipient. His failure to bring proof should be no worse than an express admission that the property was not his, and such an admission cannot prejudice the purchaser. These words cannot be accepted. In siman 66, where the seller refuses to swear that the debt was not paid, his refusal is tantamount to an admission to the debtor that the debt was paid. Such an admission either itself operates as a waiver, or the seller is believed because he has the migo that he could waive the debt. Neither rationale applies here. Failure to bring proof is not an admission to the borrower, but at most an admission to the other brothers that the note belongs to them. Such an admission is not a waiver of the borrower's debt. Nor is there a valid migo from one person to another. As Tumim repeatedly writes in the name of the authorities, we do not say a migo from one person's possible act to support another person's position. Perhaps the owner is unwilling to waive the debt and enrich the borrower, even though he is willing that the brothers benefit. Moreover, if he actually waived the debt he might become liable to reimburse the purchaser, whereas if he simply returns the property to the brothers he may owe only the money he actually received. A distinction as fundamental as this, between a promissory note and a deed conveying land, would also have been stated by the authorities. Since they make no distinction, it follows that the rule applies even to a deed of conveyance. Rather, it appears that so long as the original owner stands before us and fails to establish his right, the document itself is impaired. Taz states an analogous principle in siman 133, seif 4. When the relevant person is absent, we may say that perhaps he would have produced proof; but when the seller is before us and fails to do so, property may be removed from the present possessor because the circumstances themselves indicate the contrary. We do not say that the seller's inability to establish his title cannot prejudice the buyer, because once the circumstances make the buyer's title doubtful there is no serious concern that the seller is colluding. The same applies here. This is also evident from the Mordechai cited by the Shach and Taz in the discussion beginning "the brothers must." That case concerns movable property, as the Taz explains. The reason given is expressly that Leah had died. This implies that had she remained alive, even though her husband was the purchaser, we would not advance the claim for him. See also Even HaEzer siman 96.
Concerning a known matter. According to the explanations of the Sema, seif katan 8, and the Shach, seif katan 17, this cannot mean movable property that is itself known to have belonged to the brothers. Rather, it means that it is known that the note arose from a particular item or fund belonging to the brothers.
For perhaps he saved it from his allowance. See what I wrote immediately above, that even with respect to movable property we say that perhaps he accumulated it gradually from his allowance, even where the amount he could ordinarily have saved does not equal the value of all the movable property now in his possession, contrary to Tumim. This is also evident from the responsum of the Geonim cited by the Shach at the end of the siman.
But a member of the household. See Shach, seif katan 21, who explains that with respect to a household member we always say that perhaps he found lost property or received a gift, because a person may conceal his true wealth so that others will not think him affluent. According to the Shach, only regarding a wife and brothers who labored with the common property do we suspect that they rationalized taking it for themselves. A paid household member, by contrast, receives wages and is not suspected. Alternatively, brothers have an existing proprietary connection to the common estate and are therefore more readily suspected, whereas a household member has no such connection. His words are very difficult. If the sole reason is that we do not suspect the household member of dishonesty, why, when he dies without saying anything, do we presume that property found in his possession was his more readily than we would in the case of a wife or brothers? It is strained to say that had it not been his he would have instructed someone concerning it before death because we do not suspect him. What if he died suddenly, or was killed in a collapse? There was no opportunity for such an instruction. There is a further difficulty from a responsum of Rashba cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 86 concerning one who gave property to his daughter or sister. The case there involved a woman who conducted the business affairs of her father or brother and then died. Such a woman is exactly comparable to a sharecropper or household member, since she had no proprietary interest in the assets and there is no reason to suspect her merely because she handled the property of another. Whether she conducts the affairs of a stranger or of her brothers should make no difference. We should say that perhaps she found lost property or was given a gift, in which case her husband would inherit it. Why, then, did Rashba not leave the property with the husband as her heir? There is also a difficulty from siman 112, regarding one who gives his property effective immediately but with possession or benefit deferred until his death. Some authorities hold there that later property is placed in the presumptive ownership of the gift recipient. Why should the donor not be treated like a person who received another's property merely to manage it? If he dies, according to the Shach we would then presume property found in his possession belongs to his heirs because perhaps he found it or received it as a gift. The rule at the end of siman 250 concerning movable property given effective immediately with enjoyment after death does not present the same difficulty, because there the reason is the rule that property is presumed to have been found where it is now located, a presumption applicable specifically to movable property and not to all assets, as I wrote above in siman 60. But the rule in siman 112 presents a genuine difficulty. Furthermore, the Yerushalmi, cited by the Rif in the chapter HaKones and by Nimukei Yosef there, and quoted by the Shach in seif katan 22 concerning the sharecropper of Bar Ziza, gives as its reason the verse and principle that there is one who appears poor yet possesses great wealth. This shows that the essential issue is not suspicion of theft. Rather, the primary explanation is as follows. In the case of a wife, at the time of marriage she certainly brought all property then belonging to her into the marital framework and had nothing independently outside it. When she subsequently begins to conduct her husband's business, all assets have already acquired a presumptive status as the husband's. The same is true of brothers who become partners in all that they own. All property is initially established as partnership property. Their only possible claim is that afterward they found lost property or received a gift. One who asserts that a new event occurred bears the burden of proof, as Rashba writes in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 86. Maharit likewise states explicitly in responsa, part 1, siman 39, that once all property has at one time been established as belonging to a particular person, we continue to presume it remains so. A sharecropper or household member is different. Even at the moment he became a sharecropper or household member, it may be that he already possessed property of his own. We can never say that all assets were once established as belonging to the employer. There is room from the outset to say that the sharecropper or household member also had property, even if ordinary social estimation was that he owned nothing. Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Chiya therefore disagree whether we follow such ordinary estimation. Rivash, siman 50, cited by Tumim, gives substantially this reason for distinguishing between a wife and a sharecropper. Once the dispute is understood as whether to follow human estimation, the Yerushalmi appropriately compares the question whether an injured party may claim property that he was not considered wealthy enough to own to the dispute of Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Chiya concerning the sharecropper of Bar Ziza. This also explains Rashba's case cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 86. A woman who conducts the business of another person is nevertheless a woman who was previously established as owning nothing independently in the relevant circumstances, and therefore the property is presumed to belong to the person whose business she conducted. The rule in siman 112 is likewise explained. The authorities dispute there a question closely related to the dispute discussed by Tumim, seif katan 12, whether with respect to movable property we also say that perhaps the possessor accumulated the property gradually from his allowance. Many authorities hold that this reasoning applies specifically to notes, where the fact that the documents were written in his name supplies some evidence in his favor, whereas with movable property he must show independent funds sufficient to account for the property he possesses. In siman 112, where a person gave all his property to another, his situation is certainly comparable to that of a wife or brothers. All assets were established as belonging to the recipient, and the donor retained only what he needed for his own support, as Maharit writes expressly in part 1, siman 39. However, because a gift effective immediately but continuing until death leaves the produce with the donor, as explained in Bava Batra, chapter Yesh Nochalin, and below in siman 257, one may argue that he accumulated later property from that produce. This is exactly analogous to the claim that one brother saved money from his allowance. The dispute in siman 112 therefore turns upon whether that reasoning applies to movable property. The Rama there rules that the recipient must bring proof. It follows that the primary halachah is that even regarding movable property we say that perhaps the possessor saved the property gradually from his allowance, contrary to Tumim, who leaves the question unresolved. The case in siman 112 closely parallels a wife and brothers, and Maharit, siman 39, states that it is even stronger. According to this explanation, the authorities are not disputing at all the principle that a person may conceal his true wealth so as not to appear affluent. Their dispute is whether ordinary estimation of a person's means has evidentiary force. Nimukei Yosef in the chapter HaKones proves this by citing the case in which it was known that Chasa was not a person of means. The Shach further writes that where the possessor has seized property he may say, "I rely upon Rabbi Yochanan son of Berokah," and Mahar"a Sasson writes similarly. Mahar"a Sasson adds that this is why Rabbi Chiya did not recover from the adult orphans what they had already received, since they could assert that they relied upon Rabbi Yochanan son of Berokah. Tumim objects: if one partner seizes partnership property and says, "I rely upon a particular opinion," is he thereby exempt from returning his partner's share? In my opinion there is no difficulty. The case was one in which Rabbi Yochanan son of Berokah, acting through beit din, had already divided the property between the adult and minor heirs. He gave the adults their shares and left the minors' shares with another custodian. Had the minors' portion then been lost through unavoidable accident, the adults certainly would not have had to return what they had received, since the division had already been effected by beit din. Once the adults can say that they rely upon Rabbi Yochanan son of Berokah, Rabbi Chiya's later refusal to order payment to them is no different from an external accident occurring after division. In such circumstances the law indeed appears simple. The Shach also cites Beit Yosef in the name of a responsum of the Geonim concerning a son who conducted his father's business affairs, that the son must bring proof that he had separate property of his own. The Shach gives a strained explanation of the source for this rule. According to what I have written the rule is simple. The case is one in which the son began conducting his father's affairs while still supported at his father's table, when it was known that he had no property of his own. This is exactly like brothers who begin conducting business with the undivided estate and are therefore treated as having had nothing independently at the outset. This analysis also provides a practical arrangement for a fugitive debtor who wishes to have an unrelated person conduct business with him but fears that creditors will later say that everything found in that person's hands actually belongs to the debtor and seek to collect it. Before the arrangement begins, the parties can go to beit din, and the debtor can acknowledge there that all existing property belongs to the person who will conduct the business, while the debtor owns only what is used for his immediate maintenance. Once all assets have thereby been established in the business manager's presumptive ownership and the debtor retains only his allowance, no one can later be believed to say that the assets are actually the debtor's, as Maharit writes in part 1, siman 39. The dispute in siman 112 applies only to a gift effective immediately with benefit deferred until death, where the donor retains the produce and may therefore have accumulated property from it. In this arrangement even Maharit would agree. For greater security, the debtor may also bind himself as a hired worker and obligate himself through an effective acquisition that even anything he later finds or receives as a gift shall belong to the person whose property he manages. Then everything later found in his possession will presumptively belong to that person.
