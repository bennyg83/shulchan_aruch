These. Even if the amount proven by the witnesses does not equal the value of the documents, we can say she accumulated the rest from household economies. She must bring proof specifically that she had separate money after the marriage. Proof that she had substantial funds before the marriage does not help, because we assume she brought everything into the marriage as tzon barzel property. Even land and movables known to have been hers before marriage are treated as having been transferred to the husband as tzon barzel, and we do not preserve her original ownership presumption. If she proves that she had separate money after marriage, she is believed even if the husband claims certainty to the contrary. Even if the husband seized it from her or even borrowed it from her, and even if the money had been hidden, he must return it.
Even a widow. We do not say that because beit din honored her by appointing her as administrator, it is obvious that she personally owns the property. Even so, she must bring proof. The Shach explains that with a wife who does not conduct the household business, if money or movables are found in her hand, according to the Rambam she is believed when she claims they are melog property and the husband says they came from her earnings and therefore belong to him. But if she claims they were given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in the produce or inheritance, she is not believed even with a migo. Likewise, a widow is not believed to make such a claim even with a migo because the property is presumptively subject to the husband's rights. If she says, "My husband gave it to me as a gift," she is believed through a migo. Tosafot are more lenient and hold that a wife who does not conduct the household business is believed even without a migo to say it was given to her on condition that her husband have no rights. If she says the property belongs to others, everyone agrees she is believed if she could have handed it to them, unless the movables were known to the husband. Beit Shmuel rules that even according to the Rambam she is believed through a migo to say it was given on condition that her husband have no rights, and the dispute between Rambam and Tosafot is only where she has no migo. If she says her husband gave it to her, she is believed even without a migo. This is primary.
And she gives. If the husband himself wrote the documents in her name, the Sema holds they belong to her even if she conducts the household business, and if he wrote them in both names they belong to both. The Shach disagrees because the husband can say he wrote them in her name only to induce her to reveal money she was hiding. The primary distinction is this: if the documents are in the wife's possession, the Sema is correct, because the claim that he wanted her to reveal money does not fit. If they are in the husband's possession, the Shach is correct.
And ordinarily. Even if the husband merely entrusts money and movables to her, she is considered one who conducts the household business. This is primary, unlike the Shach who requires that she conduct the business independently. Urim VeTumim.
Known. This refers to a wife who conducts the household business. A wife who does not do so is always believed to say the property belongs to another. In siman 56 she is said to be believed through a migo, implying even when the property was not previously known. Here the case is where she died and no longer has a migo. The Shach writes that even with a migo she is not believed unless the property was known, and explains siman 56 as a wife whose husband gave her control over all his assets, making her credible as a shalish. The Beurim rejects this because every wife conducting the household business has control over her husband's property. The primary rule is that the reason she is not believed through a migo against the presumption that whatever is in a person's hand belongs to that person applies specifically where there is concern of collusion with outsiders. Thus, while the husband is alive and she could conspire with others by claiming the property belongs to them in order to remove it from his estate, a migo does not help and the property must be known. After the husband dies, that collusion concern is absent because she could simply say the property is melog property, and a migo can help.
In her name. Even if the husband's heir claims certainty, that does not help. If the wife died during the husband's lifetime and her heirs claim the property had been given to her on condition that the husband not inherit it, the husband is believed, because beit din does not advance such an unusual claim for heirs. Shach.
A found object. This proves that if one partner finds ownerless property it belongs to him personally. This is primary. The Shach notes an apparent difficulty from siman 176, where a partner who steals or robs must share the gain, while the Yerushalmi compares the two cases. Concerning brothers, the rule here applies when only one brother conducts the household business. If all of them do so and one has documents written in his own name, the Shach says they belong to him alone. Tumim disagrees and holds that the same rule applies even if all conduct the business. This is primary.
Proof. This applies even if the brothers assert certainty and even if the documents are physically in their possession. The reason is that since the document is written in his name, the natural assumption is that the money was his. While he was alive we could say that if it truly was his, he should have proved through witnesses that he possessed separate funds. After his death, however, his son is not expected to know his father's private financial affairs. With movables, even after death we do not shift the burden of proof to the brothers. Rama's phrase, "but with something known," means that it is known that the document arose from property known to belong to the brothers. The Taz concludes that even with movables, the possessor can say that he relies on the authorities supporting him. The Shach writes that the same rule applies where the brother gave property away during his lifetime and then died. This is specifically after his death; while alive, beit din does not advance claims for the recipient. With movables, if he says the brothers gave them to him as a gift, he is believed where he has a migo. With documents a migo ordinarily does not help, except according to the broader principle explained above, where a migo can make him believed even to say they were given to him as a gift by others.
