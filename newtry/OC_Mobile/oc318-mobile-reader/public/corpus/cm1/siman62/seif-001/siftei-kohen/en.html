They are mine, having fallen to me from my father's estate, etc. This applies even if the documents are in her possession. The implication is that even where both the documents and the movable property are in her possession, and she has a migo that she could have denied ever receiving them or could have claimed that she returned them, she is nevertheless not believed. Maharam Alshich, responsum 83, writes that she is not believed because the assets remained in the possession of the husband's heirs and she seeks to extract them, but that if they were in her possession she would be believed, just as the Rosh ruled concerning a widow who says, "My husband gave me one hundred as a gift before his death," that if she possesses the money she is believed through a migo. He adds that this is certainly so according to the Mordechai in the name of Maharam, in the responsum beginning with Reuven who married Leah and had a son Chanoch, that anything which is neither land nor a known object belonging to her husband is deemed in her possession and she may claim it came from elsewhere. Mahari ibn Sasson likewise writes this at the end of responsum 34. Their words are astonishing, for the Gemara and the authorities imply that the rule applies even when the documents and movable property are in her possession and she has a migo. This is also evident from Tosafos, the Rosh, and the other authorities cited below in seif katan 6. The responsum of the Rosh is different because she claimed that her husband himself gave her the property as a gift, as explained below in seif katan 5. Maharam Alshich also did not read the Mordechai precisely. The Mordechai there, in the name of Maharam, expressly speaks regarding orphans or those claiming through the woman. Indeed, earlier there the Mordechai writes in the name of Maharam that if, before marriage, she deposited money, she is believed to say that it belongs to another person through a migo that she could take it and give it to him. But if she deposited it after marriage, since she manages the household property, Rabbeinu Yitzchak explains that she is not believed to say that it is hers or another person's, as stated in Perek Chezkas HaBatim concerning a woman who manages the household. Thus, even where she has a migo she is not believed. Maharam Alshich's understanding of Rivash, siman 169, is likewise incorrect. See there, and see below seif katan 11.
She must bring proof. If she does not bring proof, she must return even the physical documents themselves. So writes Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 1, in the name of Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir, and so appears from all the authorities. See what I write below, siman 66, seif 11, seif katan 38.
That she had separate property. In that case, even if the husband is alive and claims with certainty that the assets were produced from his money, he must bring proof. So writes the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 86 in the name of a responsum of the Rashba, beginning with the question of one who gave to his daughter or sister. See there.
Separate property. This implies even if that separate property did not equal the amount represented by these documents. So writes the Beit Yosef in the name of Hagahos Ashri, citing Maharam Kohen, Raavan, and Rivan, because in a case of doubt we attribute it in a manner that leaves the property in their possession, as we say below, perhaps he saved it from his own allowance.
The same applies if movable property was found in her possession, etc. This is the Tur in the name of Ramah, and Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef. Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 49, part 16, writes that possession is effective with movable property, and the Beit Yosef cites him below in siman 179. He writes similarly in Shaar 51, end of part 1, in the name of Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir. However, Tosafos in Perek Chezkas HaBatim, end of 51a, passage beginning "one who accepts from a woman," and the Rosh there clearly support the Tur's ruling in the name of Ramah. The Mordechai there says the same. I therefore wonder why the Tur attributes this specifically to Ramah, since the rule is explicit in the Rosh and Tosafos, that even money or movable property follows this law. It is also evident there that even though she has a migo that she never received it or that she returned it, she is not believed, because ordinarily everything in her possession is presumed to belong to her husband. See also above, siman 56, beginning of seif katan 32. The Tur and Mechaber likewise rule in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 86. See also below seif katan 14, and the responsum of Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer, siman 85. This is the primary ruling.
And even a widow, etc. The Rambam writes: Likewise, a widow who managed and conducted transactions with the property of the orphans, etc. Maharlbach writes at the end of responsum 38 that although the Rambam already stated the law concerning a married woman, he repeated it concerning a widow because of the later distinctions, such as where they had separate accounts or where she died. I do not think so. The Rambam first states the law of a married woman found in the Tosefta, Perek 9 of Bava Basra, as Baal HaTerumos writes at the end of Shaar 6, and afterward states the law of a widow in accordance with the Gemara, 52b. One might have thought that since it is to her credit that people say she labored on behalf of the orphans, she would not steal from them. Therefore the law teaches otherwise. This means even a widow who managed the household after her husband's death, with documents made in her name after his death. We do not say that because they trusted her with the orphans' affairs, and because she gains standing from serving them, she would not misappropriate from them. The Rambam writes in chapter 22 of Hilchos Ishus: If money or movable property is found in the woman's possession, and she says, "They were given to me as a gift," while he says, "They are proceeds of your handiwork and are therefore mine," she is believed. He may impose a cherem upon whoever makes a false claim. Land is purchased with the funds and he enjoys the produce. If she says, "They were given to me on condition that my husband have no authority over them and that I may do with them whatever I wish," she must bring proof, because whatever is found in a woman's possession is presumed to belong to the husband for purposes of his right to the produce until she proves otherwise. If she says, "You gave them to me as a gift," she takes a heset oath that her husband gave them to her, and he does not enjoy their produce. The Tur and Mechaber copy this in Even HaEzer, siman 85, seif 12. Maharlbach asks from the Rambam's first ruling, where she is believed, against what he writes in chapter 9 of Hilchos Nachalos. He resolves that in Hilchos Ishus the Rambam speaks of a woman who does not manage the household property. I indeed found that Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir gives this answer in Baal HaTerumos at the end of Shaar 6, and the Kesef Mishneh cites it briefly. Mahari ibn Sasson also writes so according to the Rambam. Yet this proof alone is not decisive, because one could instead say that the Rambam follows the view cited by Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 8, in the name of that same Rabbi Nasan, that Rav required proof specifically for deeds and documents, whereas if they hold movable property, or debts were collected into movable property, they are believed to say that it belongs to them. Nevertheless, the Rambam's wording in Hilchos Ishus itself supports Maharlbach's distinction. The Rambam specifies that the husband says, "They are from your handiwork and are mine." Why add this? He could have said simply, "They are mine." Rather, if she were a woman who manages the household and he claimed that the money came from his own property, she would not be believed. The case therefore concerns one who does not manage the household, so he cannot say merely that the assets are his, but only that perhaps they are proceeds of her handiwork. Regarding the Rambam's second ruling, that she must bring proof when she claims the gift was conditioned to exclude her husband's rights, the Drishah in Even HaEzer, siman 85, thought that all authorities agree and wrote that Maharshal's words are not clear. This is astonishing. Maharshal's position is explicit in Tosafos in Perek Chezkas HaBatim concerning accepting deposits from women, at the end of 51a, and in the Rosh there, who disagree. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes that Rabbeinu Yonah holds that a woman is believed to say that the property was given to her as a complete gift on condition that her husband have no rights in it, even to its produce, and that this is also the view of Rashba and Ritva. The Maggid Mishneh likewise cites Rashba and other authorities who disagree with Rambam. The Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer, siman 85, cites Rashba as inclined to the view of Rabbeinu Yonah, received from his teacher Rabbeinu Yonah, that she is believed if she does not manage the household property. The Bach also challenges the Drishah from the wording "mine" used by Tosafos and the Rosh, which implies entirely hers, although he could have challenged him even more strongly from their other proofs and from Nimukei Yosef, Rashba, and Ritva. Still, the Drishah's challenge to the Rama's gloss there is correct, as I write below in siman 127, and the Bach's objection to him on that point is unwarranted. It appears that when she claims, "Others gave it to me," the discussion concerns movable property or money not known to the husband. If the property was known to the husband, she is not believed according to any view, even regarding ownership of the movable property itself. This also appears from Maharlbach cited below. Furthermore, in this law there is no distinction between whether the property was seen presently in her possession, so that she has no migo, or was not seen presently, so that she could say she never received it or returned it. According to Rambam and those who follow him, she is not believed because the husband has an established right to enjoy the produce, and this remains so even if the property was not seen in her hand. According to Tosafos and Rashba, even if it was seen in her hand, if it was not known to the husband she may say it came from others and was never his, like the ordinary rules of claims below in siman 133, and she is believed to say it was given to her absolutely on condition that he have no rights in it. When Tosafos and the Rosh write that if a woman says to the custodian, "Give it to so and so because it belongs to him," she is believed through a migo that she could take it and give it to him, implying that without a migo she is not believed, that applies specifically where she says the property belongs to another. Since it is under her control, she is not ordinarily believed to say it belongs to someone else, for otherwise why is it under her control? The migo that she could give it to him makes it as though it were already in the other's hands. But if she says, "It is mine, and others gave it to me," then since it is in her possession she is believed to say that it came to her from others as a complete gift. This appears clear to me. Baal HaTerumos, end of Shaar 6, cites Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir, who asks on Rambam: Why is she not believed through a migo that she could say, "You gave it to me"? He answers that by claiming a gift from others on condition that the husband have no rights, she would remove the assets even from his inheritance, whereas if she claimed that the husband gave them to her, he would inherit them. We do not say a migo where the alternative claim weakens the claimant's position. Gidulei Terumah asks that this would in any event be a migo involving brazenness, but that is not a difficulty, since such a migo is accepted in this context, as shown by Tosafos, Mordechai, and the authorities cited below. According to that answer, if the husband died and she claimed that movable property had been given to her during his lifetime on condition that he have no rights in it, Rambam would believe her through a migo that she could say her husband himself gave it to her, because the alternative claim would no longer weaken her position. But their entire explanation is difficult. Her credibility to say, "You gave it to me," surely applies where there are no witnesses who presently saw the property in her possession, because then she has a migo that she never received it or that she returned it. Otherwise she is not believed to say the husband gave it to her, as I explain below. If so, why should she not be believed that others gave it to her on condition, through that same migo? Moreover, Rambam's statement that she is not believed to say others gave it to her on condition is written generally, without distinguishing whether the property was seen in her hand or not. If it was not presently seen, she should be believed through a migo that she never received it or returned it. The statement that her first claim would remove the property even from the husband's inheritance is also not compelling. Even according to Shmuel in the last chapter of Nedarim, who says the husband does not acquire the gift for produce or ordinary ownership, it is possible that he still inherits her. It is no better than where the husband writes that he has no claim to her property and its produce forever, where he nevertheless inherits her, as stated at the beginning of Perek HaKosev and in Even HaEzer, siman 92. The giver may still give the daughter such a gift because she receives it from the giver, not from the husband, and she is free to spend it all. If she dies leaving it, the husband inherits from her, not from her father. This is also implied by the Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and Rashbam in Perek Chezkas HaBatim. Rather, there was no difficulty initially. Since all property found in a woman's possession is presumed to be subject to the husband's right to its produce, a migo cannot remove it from that established presumption, because we do not say migo against a chazakah. Rambam therefore carefully writes that everything found in a woman's possession is presumed to be the husband's for purposes of produce. Ordinarily, one who gives a gift to a woman gives it without special conditions and does not remove it from the husband's rights. But when the husband himself gives a gift to his wife, he transfers it from his own domain to hers, so it belongs to her completely and he has no right even to the produce. Accordingly, a widow who says that others gave her movable property during her husband's lifetime on condition excluding his rights is not believed according to Rambam, and the produce belongs to the husband's heirs from the time the gift came into her possession. The Prishah in Even HaEzer, siman 85, asks how Rambam can believe her when she says, "You gave it to me," if the husband is considered in possession. He cites the Maggid Mishneh's explanation that although she is not believed to say another gave it to her on condition, she is believed to say the husband himself gave it, because a woman does not brazenly lie to her husband about a matter whose truth he knows as well as she does. In my humble opinion, both the objection and this reading are incorrect. Rambam's intent is as I have explained. She is believed to say, "You gave it to me," even when the husband denies it with certainty, and once believed, the property is entirely hers. If she says another person gave it to her and the husband knows with certainty that it was an ordinary gift, for example because he was present at the transfer, she is not believed, since the gift came from another. The distinction between a certain claim and an uncertain claim concerns only heset or cherem. Rambam speaks of the husband claiming that it is proceeds of her handiwork, which ordinarily is only an uncertain claim, and therefore he says that he may impose a cherem. Even if one reads the Maggid Mishneh literally, the principal intent of Rambam remains that she is believed to say that the property was given to her, but where another gave an ordinary gift, the wife's hand is like the husband's hand. By contrast, when the husband himself gives her the gift, it is fully hers. The Gemara at the end of Nedarim, 88b, says that Rabbi Meir concedes where she acquires directly from her husband's hand. The Ran explains that even though Rabbi Meir says that ordinarily a woman cannot acquire independently of her husband, he agrees that when the husband gives a gift to his wife she acquires it and he does not enjoy its produce. Regarding Rambam's third ruling, that if she says "You gave it to me" she is believed, it appears to me that she is believed even if she manages the household. Although ordinary property in the hands of one who manages the household is presumed to belong to her husband, this is no stronger than a case where witnesses saw that the property came to her from her husband, where she could later say that he gave it to her through a migo that she returned it. Maharam states this explicitly, as cited by Darkei Moshe in Even HaEzer, siman 85, and I cite it below in siman 127. Maharam himself holds, as the Mordechai records, that even with movable property a woman who manages the household is not believed merely to say that it is hers or belongs to another. The distinction is therefore that when she says the property came from elsewhere, the household presumption contradicts her claim, but when she says her husband gave it to her, her management of the household does not rule out a genuine gift from him. This is also shown by the responsa of Rashba and Rosh and by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer, siman 96, seif 4, regarding a widow who seized movable property and says her husband gave it to her. She is believed where she has a migo that she never received it or returned it. Those same authorities hold that if she merely says it came to her from elsewhere she is not believed, even regarding movable property. Nor can one say those cases concern a woman who did not manage the household, because the rulings are stated without qualification. In truth, there is no reason to distinguish in a claim of "he gave it to me." There is also no difficulty that she should then be believed to say it came from elsewhere through a migo that she could say her husband gave it to her. That would be a migo against the chazakah that, since she managed the household, what is in her possession ordinarily came from her husband and not from elsewhere. Rambam's wording "You gave it to me" does not exclude a widow. It teaches that even while the husband is alive and denies her claim with certainty, she may be believed when she has the appropriate migo. The Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam believes her because a woman does not brazenly lie to her husband, comparing this to a woman who says, "You divorced me." At first glance that would imply a distinction between a certain and uncertain denial, and would mean a widow could not use this special credibility. But that cannot be correct. More fundamentally, why require the rule that a woman does not act brazenly? A person who says to another, "You gave me these movable items," is ordinarily believed when he has a migo that he never received them or returned them. Perhaps the Maggid Mishneh means only a case where she has no migo, such as where the items were presently seen in her hand. Yet even that is difficult, because the special credibility of a woman who says she was divorced is limited to freeing herself from the marriage. In monetary claims we do not find that she is believed more readily than her husband. Indeed, most authorities hold that even in a divorce claim, if she simultaneously demands her kesubah there is concern that desire for money prompted the claim. Therefore I conclude that Rambam did not create any special monetary credibility based on lack of brazenness. He means that where the usual migo exists, she is believed even against her husband's certain denial. Maharlbach's responsum and the Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer support this reading. Maharlbach further writes that whenever Rambam says a woman is believed concerning movable property in her possession, he means property not previously known to belong to the husband. If it was known to belong to him and merely placed under her control for use or adornment, she is not believed, like objects commonly lent or rented. Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir in Baal HaTerumos similarly says that the Rambam's case concerns movable property like money, not known property of the husband. Nevertheless, these words are difficult to me. In Rambam's view, if the items were not presently seen in her hand, then even known items can be claimed as a gift through a migo that she never received them or returned them. If they were presently seen in her hand, then even items not previously known to the husband do not create a migo for her, since whatever a woman acquires ordinarily falls under the husband's rights, even if another gave it. Thus, according to Rambam the decisive distinction is not whether the items were previously known, but whether she has a valid migo. According to Tosafos and Rashba, however, who hold that she may say another gave them to her even regarding produce, there is a distinction when she says, "You gave them to me" and the items are presently seen in her hand. If they were not known to the husband, she has a migo that others gave them to her. If they were known to him, she does not. This appears clear.
She was not, etc., through "they belong to both of them." The Sema writes that the Tur in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 86 says that even if she manages the household, because the husband himself wrote the document in her name, whereas the Rama here speaks where the woman arranged the writing herself, and therefore requires that she not manage the household. In my humble opinion this is not the Rama's intent. If so, the Rama would have omitted the Tur's principal ruling and inserted an unnecessary new ruling not mentioned there. Rather, the Rama follows what he wrote in Darkei Moshe, citing Rashba, that the ruling applies only where she is known to possess nichsei melog. If she is not so established, she must bring proof, because everything in an ordinary wife's possession is presumed to belong to her husband, since an ordinary wife manages the household. I found the Tur's source in a responsum of the Rosh, which states that where land purchased by the husband is recorded in both spouses' names, one half belongs to the wife, because people do not ordinarily place their wife's name on a purchase deed unless part of the purchase money is hers. If the deed were entirely in her name, all the more so. This language sounds applicable even if she manages the household. Nevertheless, I consider Rashba's view primary, because the fact that the husband wrote the document in her name may merely have been intended to induce her to reveal hidden funds, as explained in Perek Chezkas HaBatim and Even HaEzer. The wife's assertion that the funds were not hidden is not accepted. The Prishah says that if the husband merely wished not to appear wealthy he would not have delivered the documents into her hand. I disagree. According to the Tur, the same rule applies even if the documents remain with the husband, because once he initially caused the deed to be written in her name before witnesses, the usual claim of avoiding an appearance of wealth does not apply. See below.
An ordinary wife manages the household. The Rama writes this in Darkei Moshe based on Mordechai and a responsum of Rashba. His formulation requires study. Mordechai says merely that an ordinary wife exercises control over her husband's assets and that the husband trusts her with them. Rashba likewise seems to mean only that during the husband's lifetime she ordinarily conducts transactions with his property, so that a deed he caused to be written in her name may have been intended to reveal hidden money. He does not necessarily say that documents which she herself caused to be issued solely in her name are presumed his. As I explain below, documents solely in her name are stronger than movable property. Certainly where both spouses equally transact in the household, a document solely in her name should be hers. Nor is it obvious that an ordinary woman manages the household alone. Maharlbach expressly writes that an ordinary woman does not. This distinction also matters regarding whether the husband must pay debts she incurred with others, as discussed at the end of siman 96. With a widow, likewise, we do not presume that she manages the household unless this is known.
A woman who wrote to others, etc. This concerns a woman who manages the household, or one whom her husband fully trusts with his property. The Rama relies on what he wrote immediately before, that an ordinary woman manages the household. Rashba's responsum also concerns such a case. Otherwise, even if it is not known that she had separate property, she is believed to say that the property belongs to another, as the Tur and Rama write at the beginning of Even HaEzer, siman 86, based on Tosafos and the Rosh, because she has a migo that she could take it and give it to him.
If it is known, etc. Above, at the end of siman 56, the Rama accepted the Mechaber's ruling without qualification, implying that she is believed even without proof of separate property. The Sema resolves that here she has died and therefore lacks a migo. The Bach objects and says that there the husband died and the heirs cannot claim with certainty. I challenged both explanations above at length. Here, even if she has a migo that she could give the property away, and even if the husband has died, she is not believed where she managed his assets, because all property in her possession is presumed to be his until it is known that she had separate funds. The earlier case in siman 56 concerns the special law of a third party holder, where the husband explicitly trusted her with his assets. Once he invested her with that role, she has the law of a shalish and is believed. An ordinary wife who merely manages the household without such express trust follows the rule stated here.
There is one who says, etc. The Rama cites this as an individual view in accordance with his Darkei Moshe, to exclude a responsum of Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef. That responsum concerns a man whose wife died childless, leaving debt instruments issued in her name. Her relatives claimed they had been given to her on condition that her husband have no rights, while the husband claimed they were produced from his assets. Rashba ruled that everything a wife possesses remains under the husband's presumption until she proves otherwise. He rejects the analogy to brothers and orphans, where after death the other brothers must bring proof, because there the opposing parties do not have a certain claim. Here the husband claims certainty, and in addition she died while still married, when all she possessed was presumptively subject to his rights. The Rama notes another responsum of Rashba and Rivash that appear to say we plead for her heirs even where she managed the household. Gidulei Terumah leaves the authorities in contradiction. In my opinion there is no contradiction. Rashba himself compares the wife to brothers. His point is that the case before him differed because the husband had a certain claim and because she died while still married. The other responsa concern a widow. If the husband died first, the assets leave his immediate presumption. Even if the documents were issued in her name during his lifetime, after his death we may plead for her or her heirs that perhaps she received a gift or inheritance. But if she died during the marriage, even if property had been given to her ordinarily or she inherited it, the husband is her heir. Thus the assets remain within his presumptive right unless she proves that the gift was made on a special condition excluding him. This also explains why the fact that the documents are physically in the husband's hands is not decisive. Since they are written in her name, he can enforce them only through her title. Accordingly, in the case where she died after her husband, her son would prevail even if the documents were not physically in his hands, provided the husband's heirs have no certain counterclaim. Indeed, as explained below, even a certain claim by the husband's heirs does not necessarily overcome documents solely in her name. This is the principal ruling.
Or I found a lost object, etc. This implies that if one of several brothers or partners finds a lost object, it belongs to him alone. Hagahos Ashri cites this in the name of Rivam, contrary to Rabbi Baruch of Regensburg, who ruled in practice that they divide it. Baal HaItur also rules that it belongs to the finder and cites proof from the Yerushalmi, as does a responsum of Ramban cited by the Beit Yosef below in siman 176. This is the primary ruling. It creates a difficulty with the Rama's ruling below in siman 176, seif 12, that where a partner steals or robs, he must divide the proceeds with his partner, since the Yerushalmi in Bava Basra compares the cases. See there.
Or a gift was given to me. Meaning, it was expressly given to him on condition that his brothers have no rights in it. An ordinary gift to one of the partners is shared, as implied by the Yerushalmi cited below.
He must bring proof. All the more so if movable property is found in his possession. It is not presumed his and he must bring proof where the brothers have no separate accounts at all. This differs from siman 179, where one is believed concerning property not known to belong to the partnership, because there the parties are separated in other matters. Accordingly, with movable property the same law applies even if the brother is not the head of the household and does not manage alone, but is equal to the others, or all manage together. The requirement that he be the one who manages the household applies specifically to documents issued in his name. If he were not the principal managing brother, the fact that the documents were written specifically in his name would itself indicate that they belong to him. Rashbam expressly explains that the rule concerns the eldest brother who alone manages the family assets. Maharam Alshich similarly writes that only where one brother alone manages do we require the additional consideration of separate accounts. If both manage equally, a document in one brother's name belongs to him alone even if they do not keep separate accounts. The same distinction applies to a wife. With movable property, even if she does not manage the household, if the husband entrusts his assets to her she is not believed to say they are hers or another's. With documents solely in her name, however, she must actually be one who manages the household for them to be presumed his. If she does not manage, the documents are hers, for there is no ordinary reason for her to transact with outsiders in her husband's property and take documents in her own name.
To bring proof. This refers to a claim that the property fell to him from his maternal grandfather or another outside source. If he says the other brothers gave it to him as a gift, he is believed where he has a migo that he never received it or returned it. There is no reason to distinguish in that respect between whether they have separate accounts. He is not believed to say it came from elsewhere through a migo, because that would be a migo against the household presumption. One might ask why, regarding movable property, we do not plead for his heirs that the other brothers gave it to him, since he himself could have made that claim. The answer is that a gift from brothers while they all live from one common estate and have no separate dealings is highly unusual, and we do not plead an entirely unusual claim for heirs. Even authorities who sometimes plead unusual claims for heirs agree where the event is exceedingly uncommon.
And he left orphans. The same applies if he gave the property away during his lifetime and then died. Likewise concerning a woman, Maharlbach and Maharam in Mordechai state that we advance claims for those deriving title through her just as for heirs. This follows the general rule that we plead for an heir, purchaser, or gift recipient. See below, siman 146, seif 10.
Then the brothers must bring proof, etc. Since the documents were written in his name, this indicates that they are his. During his lifetime we say that if they truly belonged to him he should have been able to establish the matter. After his death, however, his heirs may rely on the fact that the documents are in his name. Tosafos, the Rosh, Mordechai, and Rivash explain the rule in this manner. If he left movable property, however, his heirs must bring proof, because movable property is not comparable to documents issued in his name. Rabbeinu Yerucham says this explicitly, and the Sema agrees. The Rama's wording, which speaks specifically of documents when discussing claims for heirs, also implies this. Tosafos and the other authorities distinguish on this basis the case in which Rava removed a garment from orphans and did not plead for them.
The brothers must bring proof. Even if the documents are physically in their possession and they assert with certainty that they came from their father's estate, their certain claim is not enough. The reason Rashba ruled otherwise in the case cited earlier is connected to a woman who died while still married, where the husband inherits her. That consideration does not apply among brothers. Since the documents are written in the deceased brother's name, a certain claim by the others does not overcome them, especially because with documents there is no ordinary migo based merely on present possession. Accordingly, if a widow died and her husband's heirs hold documents issued in her name, their certain claim likewise does not prevail, whether the documents were issued during the husband's life or afterward.
For perhaps he saved it from his own allowance. This applies even if the amount represented by the documents is far greater than anything that could naturally have been saved. The documents must, however, be dated after the brothers separated their accounts. So write the Tur, Baal HaTerumos, and later authorities. See Responsa Maharshal, siman 336. The Mechaber writes below in siman 176 that if one of partnered brothers holds money and claims it belongs to others, he must bring proof because they do not have separate accounts, but if he dies the others must bring proof. Some editions mistakenly read "the brothers must bring proof." The correct reading is "the others must bring proof." Even so, this latter rule cannot be extended to ordinary money in the same way as documents. As explained above, only documents written in his name carry this special presumption for his heirs. We do not plead for heirs concerning ordinary money a claim their father himself could not have sustained.
The orphans must bring proof through witnesses. They must prove that he transferred the document to them through writing and delivery, as explained below in siman 66, seif 11. Even the authority who disagrees there agrees in the present context of brothers, who may improperly take from one another. Nor is proof merely that he had some separate money enough here according to any view.
But a household employee, etc. The Bach challenges the Rama and says that Rabbeinu Yerucham refers only to someone known to possess some property of his own, so perhaps he saved from it, and that the Yerushalmi case of Bar Ziza's sharecropper similarly involved one who ate from his own provisions. He therefore sees no distinction between brothers, partners, and a household employee. This is incorrect. Rabbeinu Yerucham plainly distinguishes the cases. Otherwise, why cite proof from the Yerushalmi concerning Bar Ziza when the Bavli in Perek Chezkas HaBatim already states regarding brothers that perhaps he saved from his own allowance? Nor does the Yerushalmi say the sharecropper had separate provisions. Rabbeinu Yerucham's language, that even if he was not a person of means and it was known he had nothing except what came from the householder, likewise indicates that he had no separate estate. Rather, a sharecropper or paid household worker may always be said to have found something, received a gift, or the like, because a person may conceal his wealth. Brothers and a wife, who work without pay and have a direct connection to the common property, are more readily suspected of treating it as their own. A paid employee has no such justification and is not suspected in the same way. The Yerushalmi dispute between Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Chiya can then be understood as whether a person continues to conceal his wealth even after death, paralleling their dispute in Sanhedrin 29b. Rivash likewise writes that a wife is not comparable to the sharecropper of Bar Ziza, who is believed for the reason stated in the Yerushalmi. This is the primary ruling.
It is presumed to belong to him, etc. The Sema derives from the Yerushalmi at the end of Perek HaKones that the presumption not only prevents removal from the household employee or custodian, but can even allow him to recover from the householder or the householder's heirs, provided they do not make a certain claim that it is theirs, or where it is known that the property came to them from the employee and they were seen holding it in circumstances where they have no migo, or where the employee has a document against the householder. Thus, if the employee holds a mamram note signed by the householder for a specified sum, the householder cannot say that the note belonged to someone else to whom he owed money and that he merely sent the employee to redeem it. The employee may collect the amount from the householder or his heirs. It appears that the Sema says this only while the employee is alive and asserts with certainty that the property is his. Even if he was not known to have such wealth, a person may avoid publicizing his prosperity. If, however, he deposited the property with another person and then died without saying anything, we do not remove it from the householder or his heirs once it came into their hands. Rabbi Yishmael disputes Rabbi Chiya specifically after death, because silence before death may indicate that the property was not his, whereas during life even Rabbi Yishmael agrees that a person may conceal his wealth. This is explicit in Sanhedrin. It also explains the Yerushalmi's question whether one may assert claims involving property beyond what the employee was known to possess. During life, according to all views, he may do so, because a person may avoid publicizing his wealth. Maharshal writes that the original award to the elder sons stood only because Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose, the leading authority of the generation, ruled according to his own considered judgment, which is comparable to an error in discretionary judgment and is not reversed. Today, however, since the law has been decided according to Rabbi Chiya, a contrary judgment would be reversed. In my humble opinion, this is not compelled. Although Rabbi Chiya disagrees, it was not conclusively decided that his view prevails in this precise case. Where the money remains deposited with a third party, we initially give it to the sharecropper's sons, since the sharecropper deposited it and they therefore possess the stronger presumption. But if it has already come into the hands of the householder or his heirs, perhaps they may say, "I follow Rabbi Yishmael." The Yerushalmi, Rif, Hagahos Ashri, Baal HaItur, and Hagahos Maimoniyos imply that the earlier judgment can stand. The Rosh's ruling that a statement made by a person before death can be disregarded as an attempt not to make his sons appear wealthy does not prove otherwise. There the property was unquestionably his and remained within his own domain, so the Bavli in Perek Get Pashut supports Rabbi Chiya. Here, by contrast, he was a sharecropper and the property was deposited with another. Had it truly been his, one might expect him to have said so, since ordinarily property connected with the sharecropper's work belongs to the householder. This precise point may remain subject to Rabbi Yishmael's view. Even where there is a legal doubt and neither party is ordinarily in possession, I have ruled elsewhere that they divide, but here the property came from the sharecropper into the custodian's hands, so his sons are treated as possessing it for purposes of the initial ruling. Moreover, Rabbi Yishmael may disagree only because the sharecropper deposited the gold with another person without saying at the time that it was his, and then died without instruction. If the gold itself had remained in the sharecropper's possession, even Rabbi Yishmael would agree that the sons could say he expected them to find his property and therefore did not wish to publicize his wealth during life. The same is even more true with documents issued in his name. Such documents are stronger than movable property, as explained above, and his reliance on the document's being in his own name explains why he gave no instruction before death. Even if he deposited such documents with others, they remain his and may be recovered from the householder or his heirs. The Beit Yosef, at the end of siman 276, cites a responsum of the Geonim concerning Reuven, known as wealthy, who had an older and younger son. The older son managed the father's money. After the father's death, the older son claimed that almost all the money was actually his, because his grandfather had given him five dinars and he had generated all the wealth from them. The Geonim ruled that since it was known that his grandfather gave him five dinars, he is believed with an oath, like the rule of one brother who manages the household where documents are issued in his name and, if they have separate accounts, we say perhaps he saved from his own share. The Beit Yosef asks why knowledge of the five dinars is required. Even if it were not known, why should he not be believed with an oath that he possessed separate money and generated the wealth from it? It appears that the Geonim considered a son during his father's lifetime like a wife during her husband's lifetime, rather than like an unrelated paid sharecropper. A wife and son ordinarily serve without salary and have a direct connection to the common property, so they are subject to greater suspicion, as explained above. Rules derived from this siman. 1. A married woman or widow who alone manages the household, where deeds of purchase of land or servants, or debt instruments, are written in her name, is not believed to say, "They are mine and came to me from elsewhere," or, "They belong to another," even if the documents or movable property are in her possession and were not previously seen there. She must produce witnesses that she had at least some separate property, even if its amount was less than the value now claimed. Once she proves some separate property, even a living husband who asserts with certainty that the assets came from his money is not believed. Without such proof she must return even the physical paper of the documents. With money or movable property, even if she does not formally manage the household but her husband entrusts all his property to her, she is not believed, even after his death and even with a migo, because there is a chazakah that what such a woman holds came from her husband, and we do not say migo against chazakah. If she claims that the husband himself gave her the money or documents as a gift, she may be believed as explained in paragraph 4. 2. If she died as a widow, her heirs or those claiming through her prevail regarding documents issued in her name, even if the documents are in her husband's heirs' hands and they assert with certainty that they arose from their father's estate, whether the documents were made during the husband's life or later. We plead that, had she lived, she could have established that she received a gift or inheritance, and the fact that the documents are in her name supports her heirs. We do not advance the same claim concerning ordinary money or movable property merely because it was found in her possession. If she died during her husband's lifetime, even if she was an ordinary wife, the property remains within the husband's presumption, and we do not plead that it was given on a condition excluding his right of inheritance. Some say that if it is known that the husband himself caused documents to be issued solely in her name, they belong to her even if she managed the household, and if issued in both names they belong to both. Others disagree and require proof of separate money even for an ordinary wife, because the husband may have used her name merely to induce disclosure of hidden funds. This latter view is primary. 3. If she did not manage the household, even if the husband generally trusted her with his assets, she is believed regarding documents issued solely in her name that they came to her from elsewhere. The same is true where both spouses conduct business equally. If the husband does not entrust his property to her, then according to Tosafos and Rashba she is believed even regarding money or movable property to say it was given to her on condition that she alone control it and that her husband have no rights even to the produce, even if the property is presently seen in her hand. She may also say it belongs to others if it is not presently seen and she could have given it to them. According to Rambam, however, she is not believed to exclude the husband's right to produce merely through a migo. She is believed only that it was an ordinary gift, in which case land is purchased and he enjoys the produce. An ordinary gift from others during the husband's life remains subject to his right to produce. If the movable property was known to the husband, she cannot say it belongs to others or was given by others even as to ownership of the principal according to all views. 4. If she claims against her husband, "You gave me these movable items as a gift," and the items were not presently seen in her hand, she is believed through a migo that she never received them or returned them. If they were presently seen in her hand, she is not believed according to Rambam's approach, whether or not they were previously known to belong to him. According to Tosafos and Rashba, if she is a woman whom the husband does not ordinarily entrust with his property and the items were not known as his, she may be believed through a migo that others gave them to her. In all these laws, whether the husband denies with certainty affects only whether a heset oath or cherem applies. Therefore a widow holding money or movable property who says her husband gave it to her may likewise be believed when she has the migo. For this particular claim there is no difference whether she managed the household, since she says the husband himself gave it to her. 5. The same principles apply to one of several brothers who is head of the household and alone conducts transactions. If deeds or debt instruments are issued in his name, or movable property is in his possession, and he says it came to him from elsewhere or belongs to another, he must bring witnesses that he had at least some separate property. If he says the brothers gave it to him, he is believed where he has a migo that he never received it or returned it. If he dies leaving heirs or other successors, the other brothers must bring proof regarding documents issued in his name, even if they physically hold the documents and assert with certainty that they came from the common estate. We plead for his heirs that they came to him from elsewhere unless it is known that the property was originally the brothers'. If he was not the head of the household, but all brothers conducted business equally, he is believed regarding documents issued in his own name, though not regarding ordinary movable property. Even where he was head of the household, we do not plead for his heirs concerning ordinary movable property. If the brothers maintained separate accounts even in some small matter, he is believed to say the property came from elsewhere, provided the documents are dated after the separation. 6. These presumptions apply specifically to a wife or brothers. A paid household employee or sharecropper who manages the householder's property is treated differently. Even if he has no separate account, whatever he deposited or is found holding is presumed his unless it is specifically known to belong to the householder, even if he was not known to have independent means. If he dies, it is given to his heirs. It may even be recovered from the householder or his heirs where they do not assert a certain claim, or where it is known to have come from the employee and they lack a migo, or where the employee has a document against the householder and asserts it is his. If, however, the employee deposited ordinary movable property with a third party without explanation and died, and the householder or heirs already obtained it, it is not necessarily removed from them. Documents issued in the employee's name are stronger and may be recovered. A son who manages his father's property during the father's life is treated like a wife during marriage, not like an unrelated paid employee. He cannot establish that property in his possession is his unless it is known that he had some separate property.
