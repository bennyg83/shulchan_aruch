A woman who conducts the household's financial affairs, etc. This is the Tur's wording. It applies whether she makes the claim against her husband during his lifetime or against his heirs after his death. Rambam writes the same in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nachalot in two separate clauses: first, 'a woman who conducts the affairs of the household,' etc., and afterward, 'likewise a widow who conducted the affairs,' etc. This is also Rama's intent in writing, 'even a widow,' etc. He says 'even' because the Gemara might have led one to think that a widow need not bring proof, since it is to her credit that she was trusted by the beit din to serve as administrator for the orphans and surely she would not steal from them. The ruling teaches that even so she must bring proof.
She must bring proof through witnesses. This excludes the view that authentication of the document alone suffices, on the ground that once authenticated it is treated as though its testimony had been investigated in beit din. The ruling teaches otherwise. See Perishah.
The same applies if movable property is found in her possession, etc. One might have thought that proof is required only for promissory notes, whose physical paper is not itself the money and which still require collection, unlike movable property. The ruling teaches that no such distinction exists. Once this is established, we also say nearby that movable property has an even stronger aspect: if the woman or one of the brothers dies, the heir may need to bring proof, as explained below in seif katan 8.
The correct reading is: 'even a widow, and even if the documents were under her control.' Otherwise Rama would repeat himself, since he already wrote, 'even if the documents were under her control.'
She did not conduct, etc. At the end of Even HaEzer, siman 86, it is written that even if she did conduct the financial affairs of the home, if it is known that the husband himself wrote the document in her name, it belongs to her. If he wrote it in both their names, it belongs to both. Rama here speaks of a case where she herself had the document written in her name alone or jointly in both names. In that case, to rely on its wording she must specifically not have been the one conducting the household finances.
An ordinary wife is presumed to conduct, etc. The reason is that 'conducting the household affairs' does not mean only active trading. Whenever the husband trusts her and places what he owns in the house under her control, she has that status. The Tur says this explicitly at the end of Even HaEzer, siman 86.
If it is known that she had independent money, etc. See the end of siman 56, where it was not stated in this way, but rather that she is believed through a migo. Such a migo could apply even without prior proof that she owned independent money. We must therefore say that the case here differs because she transferred the property to others and then died, so she no longer has a migo.
Then the brothers must, etc. The reason is that when the document is written in a brother's name there is a presumption that it belongs to him. During his lifetime, however, we say that if it were truly his he surely could bring proof that he had separate property. After his death, a son does not necessarily know all of his father's affairs. For this reason Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, writes that if a woman dies leaving movable property, we do not say that the brothers must prove it belonged to them, because there is no document or other factor assisting the heir's possession. Darkei Moshe, siman 133, similarly cites Rashba, siman 961.
If they were separate in their food, etc. Meaning, each ate separately. The Mechaber adopts the wording of the Gemara and Rambam.
Because perhaps he saved it from his food. The Tur writes that even if the total amount of the documents is so large that it cannot reasonably have been saved merely from food or other expenses in which they were separate, we may say that perhaps he invested the small amount he had saved until it grew greatly. The Tur limits this to where the loan dates in the documents came after they became separate in their food or other affairs. If the loan date preceded that separation, we do not say that someone may have lent him money in advance knowing he would later repay after separating from his brothers. See Perishah.
Or in their father's name, etc. This applies even according to the view that a person holding a document is believed to say that the seller wrote him a transfer of 'acquire it together with all its liens,' without needing proof of that transfer. With brothers, however, everyone agrees he is not believed, because brothers are prone to rationalize in their own favor and appropriate from one another. See Perishah.
But a household manager, etc. In Meisharim it appears that this refers to movable property deposited by the household manager, and proof is brought from the Yerushalmi at the end of chapter HaKones. It is evident there not only that property is not removed from the household manager or custodian, but that he can even recover it from the homeowner or the homeowner's heirs by virtue of the presumption that whatever had been found in his possession belonged to him. Accordingly, if a mamram promissory note signed by the homeowner is in his possession, the homeowner cannot say, 'This debt instrument belonged to another person whom I owed money and I sent you merely to redeem it.' Rather, the household manager can collect the amount from the homeowner or his heirs. It follows that Rama's phrase 'but a household manager' does not refer only to documents written in his name, but to all the rules above in this siman. The reason appears to be that only with a wife or brothers, who naturally exercise control over the property, did Chazal suspect that they might rationalize claiming the whole as their own. A household manager has no connection to the property except through the homeowner's trust. Once the homeowner trusted him, that trust supports his possession.
