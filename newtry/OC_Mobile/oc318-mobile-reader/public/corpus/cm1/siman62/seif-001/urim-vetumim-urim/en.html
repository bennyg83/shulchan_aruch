A woman, etc. This applies both during her husband's lifetime and after his death, where she conducts business with the property of the orphans. Since this was not stated explicitly in the Mechaber's wording, Rama repeats, "Likewise a widow, etc." One might otherwise have thought that a widow is different, because it is praiseworthy for her to exert herself on behalf of the orphans and she would therefore not lie. Rama teaches that nevertheless she has no special credibility, Sema.
Even if the documents are in her possession. See Tumim, where I established that if she has a migo to say, "There never was such a transaction," or, "I returned them," then even a woman who manages the household is believed, as Rama writes in Even HaEzer siman 85. Everything here concerns a case where she lacks a migo. This is why the Mechaber speaks of documents in her possession rather than movable property, because with documents she has no migo regarding the underlying debt itself. This is unlike Shach, who holds that even with a migo she is not believed. See Tumim, where I established that his view is not the law.
She must bring proof. If she says that her husband gave the property to her as a gift, Shach, based on his understanding of Maggid Mishneh, and also Beis Shmuel, hold that she is believed. See Tumim, where I concluded that since she manages the household, the property remains presumptively her husband's and according to everyone she is not believed without proof.
To bring proof. If she fails to bring proof, she must return even the documents themselves to her husband, Baal HaTerumos and Shach.
That she had, etc. Even if the amount of money she proves she had does not equal the face value of the documents, Shach. See what I wrote above. This applies even if the husband makes a definite claim that the money represented in the documents belonged to him.
If movable property is found in her possession. One might have thought that only documents are affected, since their bodies are not money. The rule therefore teaches that even movable property is included. Even if she conducts business only in one type of merchandise and the movable property found with her is of another type, we say that perhaps she exchanged the merchandise with which she was dealing, and everything remains under her husband's ownership. Certainly if cash is found in her possession it is presumed to belong to her husband. See Tumim. All this is where she lacks a migo. If she has a migo, she is believed in every case, as stated above.
They are hers. She is believed to say that they were given to her as a gift, whether by her husband himself or by another person on condition that her husband have no rights in them regarding use of the fruits. This does not exclude his right of inheritance. But if she says they were given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in them at all, she is not believed. This applies during her husband's lifetime. A widow who makes such a claim after his death is believed even in that case. See Tumim at length.
Belong to both of them. This concerns a woman who had the documents written in both names. If the husband himself caused the documents to be written in both their names, Rosh and Rashba disagree. According to Rosh, since the husband wrote them in both names, half belongs to the wife even though she manages the household. According to Rashba, the property remains the husband's until it is clearly established that she possessed separate funds, where she was managing the household. See Tumim, where I concluded that this is a doubtful question of law.
An ordinary wife. Even if the husband merely entrusts his money and movable property to her, she is included in the category of one who manages the household. See Tumim.
If it is known, etc. This refers to a woman who manages the household. If she does not manage it, she is always believed to say that the property belongs to another person, as is clear from the Gemara and the poskim.
She is believed. If ownership by the other person is not known, she is not believed. This is where she has no migo, or where the husband is in possession, so that perhaps a migo cannot help because it would be a migo used to extract property.
And the husband, etc. Above at the end of siman 56 we say that she is believed to say that property belongs to others. That case is where she has a migo, Sema, and this is correct. As I explained in Tumim at length, the case there is that when she delivered her husband's estate to beit din she stated that a certain amount belonged to a particular person. At that moment she had a migo that she could have refrained from delivering that amount to beit din. This is not a retroactive migo. Shach sought to explain her credibility there through the law of a third party and to distinguish between a woman who controls all her husband's affairs and one who merely conducts business. His words do not appear correct. See Tumim.
This likewise appears to me. If she died during her husband's lifetime, and he claims that documents written in her name were funded by his money, while her heirs say perhaps someone gave her money as a gift on condition that her husband have no rights in it, then even though such a condition would not necessarily prevent the husband from inheriting her and the property might be characterized as merely expected, it nevertheless appears that the husband is believed. He makes a definite claim, they make only an uncertain one, and he is her Torah heir. A doubt does not remove property from one with a definite right, Shach. See Tumim.
One of the brothers, etc. Shach holds that this applies specifically where one brother manages the joint estate and the others do not. If all the brothers manage the estate equally and one has documents written solely in his own name, Shach says the other brothers must bring proof. See Tumim, where I proved that this is incorrect. There is no distinction. Whether one or many brothers manage the joint property, everything remains presumptively partnership property, and the brother claiming separate ownership must bring proof.
Or, "I found it." See Tumim. According to the view that a partner's found property belongs to all the partners, this must mean that at the time he lifted it he said, "I acquire this for myself and not for the partnership." Everyone agrees that in such a case it is his alone. Likewise, the claim, "It was given to me as a gift," must mean that it was expressly given on condition that the other brothers or partners have no share in it. Otherwise a gift too would be acquired for the partnership. This is obvious.
He must bring proof. This applies where he lacks a migo. If he has a migo, he is always believed and need not bring proof, just as I wrote above regarding a woman who manages the household and as below in siman 179 regarding a partner. Accordingly, there is no need for Shach's separate ruling that if he claims the brothers themselves gave it to him as a gift he is believed where he has a migo. With a migo he is believed in every case, and without one he is not believed even in that case, as Shach himself writes. Shach follows his earlier view that even a migo does not help a woman who says, "I found it," but that is not the practical law.
And he left orphans, etc. The same applies to other heirs, representatives, or a widow. They all have the same law, Shach.
Then the brothers must, etc. Even where the disputed property is movable, the orphans still must bring proof according to this ruling. The reasoning that had the deceased brother still been alive he could have brought proof, and that his failure to do so weakened the documents, is compelling only with documents written in his name, not with movable property. See Tumim, where I brought sources showing that the matter is a doubtful question among the poskim. I also established that if the deceased brother had said the property belonged to another person, then after his death the brothers need not bring proof, because that other person is still alive and must establish his own right just as he would have had to do while the brother was alive. There is no relevant difference between the third party and the deceased brother. See there.
To bring proof. If the documents are actually in the heirs' possession and they make a definite claim, the matter requires study. Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of Even HaEzer siman 86 implies that even there the orphans must bring proof, Shach. See what I wrote above in seif katan 13.
If they were separate in their dough, etc. Meaning that each brother eats separately at his own expense and they do not eat from the common estate. In that case one may say that although the small savings from food do not initially equal the value of the document, perhaps he gradually accumulated them until he had enough to produce the amount represented by the document. If the document was created before they separated their food expenses, later separation does not help, and the brother must bring proof, Sema. See Tumim, where I wrote that this applies to documents, but regarding movable property the matter requires study. I also wrote that if he claims the property belongs to another person and his migo is that he saved it from his own food allowance, then he must plausibly have saved an amount equal to the document or movable property. Otherwise there is no genuine migo. This requires study.
But a member of the household, etc. See Tumim, where I cited the dispute between Sema and Shach regarding a householder who seized property. Sema holds that we remove it from him. If there is a document payable to any bearer, the household member may collect from the householder, and the householder cannot say, "I sent you to pay someone I owed, and you took the document into your own hands and are now suing me with it." Shach writes that if the householder is already in possession, or happens to seize the item, he may say that he follows the opinions favorable to him. This applies specifically where the property is not in the active possession of the household member who manages the affairs, but was simply deposited with a third person without the household member stating to that custodian that it belonged to him. If the property is found in the household member's own possession and the householder then seizes it, Shach initially indicates that it should be returned. See Tumim, where I ruled like Shach but concluded that even if the property had been in the managing household member's possession, once the householder seized it we do not remove it from him. See there at length. This applies to movable property. But if a document is written in the household member's name, then because the document itself identifies him, everyone agrees that if the householder seizes it we remove it from him, as Shach concludes. Bach writes that all this concerns a household member who is not supported at the householder's table. Shach disagrees and holds that even if he is supported there, property in the hands of the managing household member is presumptively his. See Tumim, where I left this requiring study, because Bach's position appears strongly supported. See there.
