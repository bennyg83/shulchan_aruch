Even if the documents are in her possession, etc. The Shach understands the same rule to apply to movable property in her possession, even where she has a migo such as a claim of complete denial. Nevertheless, she is not believed, because where a wife conducts the household's business, everything is presumed to belong to the husband, and this is therefore a migo against a chazakah. He challenges the Sema, Maharam Alshich, and Mahara Sasson, who hold that where she has a migo she is believed. They cite Responsa Rosh, which rules that a widow who conducted the household business is believed to say that her husband gave the property to her as a gift if she has a migo. The Shach rejects the proof, arguing that this is not a migo against the chazakah, because it remains true that everything in her possession is presumed to have belonged to her husband, except that the husband later gave it to her as a gift. He strongly maintains this distinction several times in this siman. His words are not clear. What difference does it make if she says that her husband gave it to her? Ultimately, we are removing the object from the husband's presumed ownership. The present question is whose object it is, the husband's or the wife's. If we say it belongs to the wife, we have removed it from the husband's chazakah, for whatever is in her possession is presumed to be the husband's because she conducts the household business. It makes no difference that it once belonged to him. Consider a person who says regarding property in his possession that it belongs to another. If he is not believed because whatever is in his possession is presumed to be his, could he become believed by saying, "It truly was mine, but I gave or sold it to the other person," on the theory that this does not contradict the chazakah? Certainly not. This is also compelled by Baal HaTerumot, end of gate 6, in the name of Rabbi Moshe ben Natan. He asks against Rambam, who holds that a wife is believed to say, "You gave it to me as a gift," that she should then also be believed to say, "It belongs to another person who gave it to me on condition that you have no rights in it," by a migo that she could have said her husband gave it to her. According to the Shach, there would be no question, because saying that the husband gave it to her would not oppose the chazakah, while saying that another gave it to her would. I am therefore surprised that the Shach interpreted the Rama as saying she is not believed even with a migo. The Rama writes explicitly in Even HaEzer, siman 85, seif 12, that where she conducts the household business and there are no witnesses who saw the property in her hands, so that she has a migo that she does not have it or that she returned it, she is believed in every respect. As Darchei Moshe explains, the Rama derives this from Responsa Maharam, who rules that a wife conducting the household business is believed to say that her husband gave it to her if she has a migo. Thus the Rama does not distinguish between property of another and property given by the husband, contrary to the Shach. This is the correct view, because there is no sound basis for such a distinction.
However. The Shach's principal proof is from Tosafos and the Rosh in Perek Chezkas Habatim 51b. They write that where a wife does not conduct the household business and her deposit is in another person's possession, so that the teaching says, "If he received it from the wife, he returns it to the wife," then if she says it belongs to a particular person, it is given to that person. She is believed by a migo that she could take it from the depositary and give it to him. Even if the husband says it is his, he is not believed, for the teaching says to return it to the wife and does not require first notifying the husband. Tosafos then writes that if she conducts the household business, she is not believed to say that it is hers or belongs to another, as is stated later: a wife who conducts the household business and says, "It is mine," must bring proof. She is not believed through a migo that she could have said it belongs to others and is deposited with her. The Shach learns from this that migo never helps a wife conducting the household business, and Beit Shmuel in siman 85 also regards this as a clear proof. I do not understand their proof. The case there must involve witnesses who saw the property. Otherwise, why did Tosafos need the specific migo that she could say it belongs to others and was deposited with her? She could simply claim complete denial or that she returned it. Rather, the property is deposited with another person and therefore is seen. When she does not conduct the household business, she has the migo that she could take it back from the depositary, who has no right to withhold it and need not notify the husband. Tosafos therefore emphasizes that the depositary cannot withhold it and need not inform the husband, so she could take it and give it to the other person. But when she conducts the household business and deposited the item with another, there is no migo of complete denial, since it is visibly deposited with that third party. Nor does she necessarily have a migo that she could take it from him. According to Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, who disagree with Rivash in Even HaEzer, siman 86, if a wife who conducts the household business deposited property with another, the depositary should not return it to her because it is presumed to belong to the husband. Even according to Rivash, who requires the depositary to return it to her, he agrees that the husband must immediately be informed, because he may then prevent her through beit din from giving it to the alleged third party. Beit Shmuel in siman 86, seif katan 3, says explicitly that because the husband is immediately notified she has no migo that she could have given it to another. Thus there is no migo in the Tosafos case at all. The point of Tosafos is different. One might have thought that a wife who conducts the household business, because the husband has entrusted his property to her and her hand is like his, should be believed even without a migo to say, "These objects belong to another." This resembles the credibility of a third party who was entrusted with an object. The Shach himself at the end of siman 56 accepts something close to this, although he strains to distinguish between a woman who controls all of her husband's property and one who merely conducts the ordinary household business. Tosafos proves that no such inherent credibility exists. Otherwise, why would we say that a wife conducting the household business is not believed to say, "This is mine"? She would have a migo that she could say it belongs to another, if that latter claim were inherently believed. Necessarily, she is not believed to say it belongs to another without a migo. Tosafos therefore actually implies the opposite of the Shach: if she did possess a genuine migo to say it belongs to another, she could also be believed to say it is hers despite the household chazakah. See what I wrote below in siman 99, seif 1, where I brought proof from Tosafos and many authorities that a migo is said even against the ordinary chazakah that property in a person's possession belongs to him. Even those who disagree there concerning a borrower who says that property in his own possession belongs to others can agree here. In that ordinary case the chazakah that whatever a person possesses is his is very strong. Here, however, the property is physically in the woman's possession, and she says it is truly hers. The only opposing chazakah is the secondary rule that property in the possession of a wife conducting the household business is presumed to belong to her husband. That chazakah is weaker. Therefore everyone can agree that a genuine migo overcomes it, as the Rama, Sema, Maharam Alshich, Mahara Sasson, and other authorities hold. There is also proof from the Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 97, who cites Rabbeinu Tam concerning a man who appointed his wife over his affairs. She said that a large amount of money in her possession belonged to others, and the husband wanted to impose an oath upon her. Rabbeinu Tam exempted her from the oath, and the Rosh agreed. If a wife conducting the household business were never believed to say that the property belongs to others, what would be the point of exempting her from an oath? Necessarily, the case was one where she had a migo and therefore was believed, although the husband still wished at least to impose an oath upon her. Rabbeinu Tam exempted her. Thus it is compelled that with a migo she is believed even though she conducts the household business. See below, siman 97.
She must bring proof. Rambam writes in chapter 22 of Hilchot Ishut that if money is in a wife's possession and she says it was given to her as a gift, she is believed, but land should be purchased with it and the husband enjoys the produce. If she says, "It was given to me on condition that my husband have no rights in it," she must bring proof, because whatever is in a wife's possession is presumed to belong to the husband for purposes of his right to consume the produce. If she says, "You gave it to me as a gift," she takes a heset oath and the husband does not consume the produce. The Maggid Mishneh explains the last ruling, where she says her husband gave it to her, on the ground that a woman does not brazenly lie to her husband's face in this manner. The Shach, seif katan 5, assumes that Rambam believes her because she has a migo that she could deny having the property or say she returned it. He therefore struggles with both the Maggid Mishneh, asking why this special reason is needed if there is already a migo, and Rabbi Moshe ben Natan in Baal HaTerumot, gate 6. Rabbi Moshe ben Natan asks that she should be believed to say another person gave it to her on condition that the husband have no rights in it, through a migo that she could say her husband gave it to her. He answers that this is not a migo because the latter claim is inferior for her: if the husband gave it to her, he will inherit it, whereas if another gave it on the stated condition, she seeks to remove even his inheritance right. The Shach asks why this special migo is needed when she already has a migo of complete denial or return. He similarly struggles with Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's statement that if the movable property is known to the husband she is not believed to say it was given as a gift, since she would still appear to have a migo of denial. In truth, these sources are not speaking where she has any migo. Where there is a real migo, everyone agrees that she is believed even if she conducts the household business. The Maggid Mishneh therefore needed his reason, and Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's analysis is entirely appropriate. His approach also appears to differ from the Maggid Mishneh's. If her credibility rested on the principle that she does not brazenly lie to her husband, how could Rabbi Moshe ben Natan ask that she should be believed through a migo that she could say her husband gave it to her? The whole reason we believe that claim would be precisely because she does not wish to be brazen, so it would be illogical to use it as a migo for a different claim. Gidulei Terumah already sensed this point. The Shach responds that in monetary law we use even a migo involving brazenness, but that misses the point. According to the Maggid Mishneh, the entire credibility of the alternative claim itself is based on the fact that she will not be brazen. It therefore appears that Rabbi Moshe ben Natan does not accept the Maggid Mishneh's explanation. Rather, when money is in her possession and she says it was given to her as a gift, she is believed even without a migo because possession itself supports the claim that it was given to her. The remaining uncertainty is only who gave it. If she says her husband gave it, we assume another person did not give her a gift, which is uncommon, and the Yerushalmi says that a gift becomes known. If there were publicity that another gave it, she could not plausibly say her husband did so. If there is no such publicity, the natural inference supports a gift from the husband. But if she says another person gave it to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it, her possession may support the fact that another gave her a gift, but it does not independently prove the special condition excluding the husband. She therefore is not believed concerning that condition. Rabbi Moshe ben Natan asks why she should not nevertheless be believed through the migo that she could have said her husband gave it to her. He answers that the proposed alternative is inferior because in that version she does not eliminate the husband's inheritance rights. According to this, if she says, "Another person gave it to me on condition that my husband have no right to the produce, but I do not claim to exclude him from inheritance," she would be believed through the migo that she could have said the husband gave it to her, because the reason that the alternative claim is inferior no longer applies. This also resolves the Nimukei Yosef, Rashbam, and Rosh, who write that if a depositary received property from a woman and she then dies, he returns it to the husband. Although perhaps it had been given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it, the husband still inherits her. Why do they not fear that it was given on condition that the husband not inherit? Because she herself would not have been believed in such a claim without the special migo, and after death we do not make that unsupported claim for her. But a condition excluding only the husband's right to produce is a claim she could have made with the migo, so that possibility is stronger. There is therefore a practical distinction between the Maggid Mishneh's reason and Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's. If a wife conducting the household business says, "My husband gave it to me as a gift," is she believed? According to Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's approach, no. Her ordinary credibility depends upon her possession supporting her ownership, and a wife conducting the household business lacks that chazakah. Likewise, with movable property known to belong to the husband, comparable to objects normally lent or rented, there is no ownership inference from her possession. According to the Maggid Mishneh, however, one might think she remains believed because she does not brazenly lie to her husband's face. This is the Shach's and Beit Shmuel's approach. It nevertheless appears that even the Maggid Mishneh relied upon a combination of factors. He did not mean that lack of brazenness by itself can remove money from the husband's possession. Rather, because the property is in her possession and that possession lends some support, the additional consideration that she will not brazenly lie to her husband makes the claim credible. Beit Shmuel in siman 85, seif katan 30, writes similarly. Where she conducts the household business and has no independent possession at all, or where the specific movable property is known to belong to her husband and is comparable to items usually lent or rented, even the Maggid Mishneh would agree that one cannot rely on the lack of brazenness to remove property from the husband's chazakah. Most authorities in any event appear to follow Ramban's explanation, which Baal HaTerumot also accepts. See what I wrote above concerning whether a widow is believed.
The same applies if movable property is found in her possession, etc. The Shach, seif katan 6, writes that Baal HaTerumot, gate 49, part 16, disagrees and limits the rule to documents rather than movable property. Baal HaTerumot there discusses a partner who has movable property in his possession and says it does not belong to the partnership. If he has a migo of complete denial or a similar claim, he is believed. He writes that this is not contradicted by the case of a bundle or documents issued in the name of a son who conducted the household business, because a bundle and documents are different: possession of them is not independently clear ownership. According to the Shach there would be a contradiction in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. Here the Tur rules, allegedly against Baal HaTerumot, that even regarding movable property the woman is not believed. Below, siman 179, however, he rules that where a partner has a migo he is believed. Baal HaTerumot explicitly treats the partner and the family manager as governed by the same principle. The answer is that the Shach follows his own position that the rule here applies even where the wife has a migo, and on that understanding Baal HaTerumot indeed disagrees. But according to what I have explained, where she has a migo everyone agrees she is believed. Documents usually do not allow such a migo because their existence and wording are visible. Thus, when the Mechaber cites Ramah that the same rule applies to movable property, he means only where she lacks a migo. If she has a migo regarding the movable property, she is indeed believed. The same is true of a partner: with a migo he is believed, without a migo he is not. The two laws are identical. There is therefore no contradiction between the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, and no dispute between Ramah and Baal HaTerumot. This also provides a clear proof for what I wrote at the beginning against the Shach. The Shach asks why this rule is attributed to Ramah, since Tosafos and the Rosh explicitly write that a wife conducting the household business is not believed regarding property claimed as her own. This requires study. Perhaps the case is that she conducts business in a particular type of merchandise, while other movable property in her possession is of a different kind. Ramah may hold that even such other movable property lacks a chazakah in her favor, because perhaps she exchanged one type for another. Tosafos does not clearly establish that additional point.
She did not conduct the household business, etc. Rambam, according to what I explained above, holds that she is not believed to say, "Another person gave it to me on condition that my husband have no rights in it." The Maggid Mishneh writes that Rashba disagrees, as the Beit Yosef cites him in Even HaEzer, siman 85. The Shach, seif katan 5, and the Bach in Kuntrus Acharon to Even HaEzer, siman 85, say that many authorities disagree with Rambam. I have not found so many. Tosafos merely says that she is believed to say, "It is mine," which may mean it was given to her as an ordinary gift or by her husband. Nimukei Yosef cites Ri, Rashba, and Ritva that she is believed to say that it was given to her as a gift and that the husband has no right to the produce. I wrote above that the Rosh also appears to support this, because he says that if she dies the property returns to the husband even though it might have been given to her on condition that the husband have no rights in it. This indicates that she would be believed to assert such a condition regarding produce, while not regarding inheritance. According to Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's explanation, however, even Rambam can agree that she is believed to say that another gave it to her on condition excluding the husband from produce but not from inheritance. In that case she has a migo that her husband himself gave it to her, and the alternative claim is not worse concerning inheritance. There may therefore be no disagreement. The Maggid Mishneh creates the dispute only because he does not accept Rabbi Moshe ben Natan's understanding and instead grounds her credibility in lack of brazenness. This also resolves Ramban's question: if she dies, why not give the property to the person she instructed, perhaps it was given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it? The answer is that everyone agrees she is not believed to exclude the husband from inheritance, because she has no migo for that. Therefore, after death we do not make that claim on her behalf. The Rama in Even HaEzer writes in Rashba's name that there is a distinction between hidden money and nonhidden money, but later authorities already note that such a distinction is not found clearly in Rashba. Perhaps the Rama introduced it to resolve Ramban's question. According to what I wrote, no such distinction is necessary. The Tur's rulings in Even HaEzer, siman 85, following Rambam, and in siman 86, that she is believed to say the property is hers, are also reconciled: she is not believed only when she seeks to eliminate the husband's inheritance rights, not merely his right to produce. This appears correct and avoids the strained explanations of the Perishah and Bach. As a matter of law, once the husband has died and a widow who conducted the household business says that her husband gave it to her as a gift, or that another gave it to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it, she is believed where she has the required migo. As the Shach also notes, the concern of worsening her position through inheritance no longer applies after the husband's death.
They are jointly owned by both of them. I already wrote in Urim concerning the Shach's statement that the Rosh and Rashba disagree where a husband caused property to be recorded in his wife's name. According to the Rosh, since it was written in her name, it belongs to her. According to Rashba, because she conducts the household business, it remains the husband's property. In my humble opinion, Rashba's responsum, siman 957, shows that even if the husband placed the property in the wife's name, it resembles the rule in Perek HaGozel where one places property in the name of the Exilarch and the Exilarch does not thereby acquire it. Thus the wife does not acquire it merely because her name appears on it. According to this, the matter depends upon the dispute I cited above regarding an Exilarch who claims that because the document was written in his name he did acquire it. Some authorities uphold that claim. If so, here too, although the wife supplied none of the purchase money, she may nevertheless acquire because the husband deliberately placed the property in her name. I demonstrated above from the Rosh's responsum that in his view the Exilarch does acquire in such a case, and therefore the Rosh likewise holds that the wife acquires. Baal HaTerumot takes the opposing view. This law therefore requires study because it depends upon that earlier dispute. In a case of doubt, who can remove property from the wife? This applies where the property exceeds the amount of her ketubah. Up to the amount of the ketubah, however, the heirs are regarded as holding the property and the wife has no superior claim. See what I wrote above in the laws of seizure.
An ordinary wife conducts the household business. The Shach, seif katan 8, questions this and writes that an ordinary wife may control her husband's property but does not necessarily conduct the household business in the technical sense. I do not understand the basis for his doubt. Tosafos and the Rosh in Bava Batra write that even if she does not actively conduct her husband's business, but only has ready access to property of the same kind as her husband's, there is already doubt whether she should be treated as one conducting household business. What Tosafos leaves uncertain, the Rosh resolves explicitly: even if her husband's money is merely commonly in her possession, she is called one who conducts the household business. The Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 86, writes likewise. Therefore, once the Shach himself agrees that an ordinary wife controls her husband's property, meaning his money is ordinarily accessible to her, it follows that she is treated as conducting the household business. This is also the ordinary practice. The Shach later, seif katan 14, seeks to distinguish between movable property found in her possession, for which mere trust by the husband is enough to require her to bring proof, and documents written in her name, for which full household business activity is allegedly required. I do not understand the source of this distinction. If her ordinary control is included in the Talmudic category of one who conducts household business, then the rule applies to documents as well. If it is not included, where is the source to extend it to movable property? Tosafos and the Rosh derive the movable property rule from the very law of documents written in the name of a wife conducting household business. There is therefore no basis to distinguish.
If it is known, etc. The Sema asks that above, at the end of siman 56, it appears she is believed even when the matter is not known. He answers that above she has a migo, whereas here she has no migo because the case concerns one where she died. He writes correctly. Although the Responsa Rashba from which this law derives does not expressly say she died, it plainly concerns a case without a migo. In addition, there the husband was in possession of the movable property, so even if she had a migo it might not help, because it would be a migo used to extract from the possessor. The Shach follows his own view that a wife conducting the household business is not believed even with a migo, and therefore he struggled greatly with the ruling at the end of siman 56. He also raises the objection of the later authorities that in Responsa Rosh, the source of the law there, she seems to have delivered the objects to beit din without saying anything and only afterward claimed that they belonged to another. That would appear to be a retroactive migo. The Shach therefore follows Maharit, who explains that the Rosh believed her as a third party. Since all of her husband's property was entrusted to her, the husband had given her credibility. The fact that she no longer had a migo at the later stage would therefore not matter, because she had already been made credible from the outset. To avoid the result that every wife conducting household business should have the status of a third party, the Shach is forced to distinguish between a woman who controls all of her husband's affairs and one who merely conducts ordinary household business. This is the essence of his lengthy discussion at the end of siman 56. But after all of that, the distinction is unclear. What is the meaningful difference between "controlling everything" and "conducting the household business"? The latter itself means that she handles her husband's affairs, receives and pays, and manages transactions. If property is entrusted to her, she has also been trusted. This boundary is new and has no clear source among the authorities. At first I thought Maharit's approach could still be defended in a narrower way. A wife might have the credibility of a third party only when she says that the husband himself delivered the object to her for the benefit of another person. That would be a true third party relationship, like where the husband says, "I gave it to you as a deposit for someone else." She says that the husband placed this particular object in her hands in order that it belong to the other person. The Rosh's responsum could perhaps be read that way. But where she says that an object belonging to another came into her hands directly as a deposit and never came from the husband at all, how can the husband's general trust in her create third party credibility over an object that, according to her own account, never passed from him to her? Third party credibility is created by entrusting the very object now in dispute, not by general trust in unrelated matters. The Taz offers a distinction close to this, though not in these terms. Even this does not satisfy me after examination. Responsa Rosh, rule 68, does not say that the objects came into her hands from the husband in the capacity of a third party. Moreover, the Tosafos and Rosh question in Perek Chezkas remains. Why is a wife conducting household business not believed to say, "It is mine," through a migo that she could say, "It belongs to another"? If she can always say that the husband gave it to her to give to another and thereby be believed as a third party, the migo would remain. Also, the Rosh's responsum begins and ends by saying that because, after her husband's death, the widow had the power to give the property to whomever she wished, she is believed. This implies that if beit din or guardians had immediately taken control of the estate at the husband's death, she would not have been believed. But if her credibility rested upon third party status conferred by her husband during his lifetime, then even without a present migo she should remain believed. Further, why does the Rosh prove his ruling from the case of one who saw his father conceal property in a field? There the credibility is based upon the migo that he could have given the property away, not upon third party status. If the widow's credibility rested on third party status, the proper source would have been the law where one party says an object was entrusted and another says it was given as a loan or similar third party cases. There is an even more basic problem. If her credibility is based upon being a third party, once she has delivered the property to beit din without saying anything, the object is no longer in her hands. A third party who no longer possesses the entrusted object ordinarily loses the special credibility attaching to possession. Maharit and the Shach strain to say that handing it to beit din without qualification is not regarded as the entrusted object leaving her possession. I do not know a source for this. On the contrary, once the object is no longer in the third party's possession, that special credibility ends. I explained above in siman 56 that when a third party delivers the object to one of the litigants and then testifies in that person's favor, there can be circumstances in which he remains believed, but delivery to beit din is certainly a transfer out of his possession. The correct reading of Responsa Rosh is therefore the straightforward one. When the widow brought the estate to beit din, she immediately stated that particular items belonged to others. At that moment she could still have given those items to whomever she wished. Her statement is therefore believed through an ordinary present migo, not a retroactive migo. This is what the Rosh means when he writes that if they did not return the property and instead came to beit din, they are believed through a migo that, had they wished, they could have returned it. Clearly, he means that when they came before beit din they immediately made the statement while the practical power to deliver the property was still theirs. This is why the Rosh brings the case from Perek Zeh Borer where a person saw property hidden in a field and immediately told the heirs that the property belonged to a particular person. His credibility there rests upon the migo that he could have given it to that person. If he waited and said nothing until later, he would not be believed. The two cases are parallel. The Rosh concludes likewise: if the money left the widow's possession and came into the hands of beit din or a guardian, beit din should act in accordance with whatever the widow says through the migo that after her husband's death she could have returned each person's property. "Whatever the widow says" means what she says at the time she delivers it to beit din, not an unlimited later power to diminish the orphans' estate. Otherwise there would be no end to the matter. At the time of delivery, however, the migo is complete, because she could simply have handed the item to the person she names rather than to beit din. This is exactly analogous to the buried property case. Because I saw important later authorities uncertain about this, I have explained it at length, but the matter is straightforward. Nor is there a question why a wife conducting the household business is not believed as a third party even when she presently lacks a migo, since her husband initially trusted her. Her hand in the household business is treated like the husband's hand, and everything is presumed to belong to him. Special third party credibility therefore does not arise. This parallels the Rosh's statement regarding property hidden in a field that everything is presumed to belong to the heirs. See what I wrote above, siman 56, that according to Tosafos, where an object is one ordinarily lent or rented and the possessor claims it was lent to him, the usual third party rule does not apply even though the owner did in fact trust him, because ordinary human conduct leaves the item within the original owner's chazakah. All the more so with a wife conducting the household business, where the normal order is that all property is presumed to be her husband's. See below, siman 96, concerning her possible migo of giving a promissory note to others and thereby obligating the orphans from their father's estate.
And so it appears to me. The Shach writes that there is no real dissent here. Since we rule regarding brothers that they must bring proof, the same should apply to a widow. Rashba's responsum, he says, concerns a woman who died during her husband's lifetime. We do not make for her heirs the claim that perhaps the property was given to her on condition that the husband not inherit it. At first sight this is insufficient according to Rashba as understood by the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef, who hold that a wife can claim, "It was given to me as a gift on condition that my husband have no rights in it." Why then should we not make that claim for her heirs? According to what I explained above, Rashba can agree that she cannot make such a claim to eliminate inheritance, but only to eliminate the husband's right to produce. We therefore do not make a claim for the heirs that would uproot the husband's inheritance while he is both heir and possessor. Even this is not entirely sufficient. If we merely claim that the property was given on condition that the husband have no present rights in it, then the husband did not acquire the sums represented by the documents during her lifetime, and they may be regarded as merely expected property with respect to him, as stated in Even HaEzer, siman 90, seif 1, in the gloss, in Rashba's name. It therefore appears that the principal reason is that the husband makes a definite claim while the heirs have only an uncertain claim. In such a situation, especially where the husband is in possession of the assets, a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one.
And similarly one of the brothers, etc. The Shach holds that this applies specifically where only one brother conducted the household business. But if all of the brothers conduct it, then whatever document is written in one brother's name belongs to him, and the other brothers must bring proof. He writes that Maharam Alshich also ruled this way. Although this distinction is stated by great authorities, I do not find it correct as practical law. We have not heard or found any early authority who distinguishes in this manner. Why should the fact that another brother also participates in the business cause the document written in this brother's name to be presumed his? In joint commercial dealings it is common to have a document written in the name of one partner in order to avoid the disorder of a jointly managed pot. Why then should he be believed to say it belongs only to him? There is proof from Baal HaTerumot, gate 49, part 16. He writes that a partner who seizes an item and says it is his is believed in certain circumstances, and he says not to challenge this from the case of a son who conducted the household business. But in a partnership both partners are involved in the business, and nevertheless Baal HaTerumot treats the cases as comparable. Necessarily, there is no such distinction. The Tur and Mechaber below, siman 179, rule that a partner is believed only where he has a migo. Without a migo he is not believed, and they do not distinguish whether both partners participate in the business or only one. The Rama likewise writes that an ordinary wife conducts the household business. Does that mean she alone conducts all business while her husband's hands are tied and he does nothing? Such a situation is found perhaps in one woman among a thousand. How could the Rama formulate the rule for ordinary wives if the husband's participation would automatically mean that every document written in the wife's name belongs to her? The Talmud gives the same rule for a wife and for one brother among brothers. It is therefore clear that this distinction has no foundation in practical halachah and is a novel idea not found among the earlier authorities.
Or I found a lost object. The Shach asks from below, siman 176, where the Rama rules that if a partner steals or robs, the gain belongs to the partnership, and the same principle would seem to apply to a found object. Yet here it appears that a found object belongs to the individual. There is no difficulty. Below concerns an ordinary case. We presume that he acquired it with the partnership in mind, and one who lifts a found object on behalf of another can acquire it for that other person. But if, at the moment he lifts it, he says explicitly, "I am acquiring this for myself and not for anyone else," it is plainly his, because he did not intend to acquire on behalf of his partner. The case here can be understood in this manner: "I found a lost object and intended to acquire it for myself alone."
The brothers must bring proof. The Sema, citing Rabbeinu Yerucham, writes that with movable property even the orphans must bring proof. With documents, the fact that the document is written in the brother's name supplies some evidence. Movable property contains no comparable proof, so even after his death it remains presumed to belong to the brothers. The Shach agrees and says this appears from Tosafos. It appears, however, that Maharam in Mordechai disagrees. At the beginning of Perek Chezkas he discusses a wife conducting the household business with land and movable property that are presumed to belong to the husband. He writes that although we rule that the brothers must bring proof, that applies to property not known to belong to the brothers. But where the property is known to be theirs, they need not bring proof. This implies that where it is not known to belong to the brothers, even with movable property the burden is upon the brothers. He further writes there concerning a woman who brought books and movable property into her second marriage, then died, after which her son from the first husband contested the property because she had never sworn concerning her ketubah. Maharam says that although the husband had conducted the household business and ordinarily would have to bring proof, once he died the rule is that the brothers must bring proof. This plainly demonstrates that even with movable property the heirs need not bring proof. I later found that the Taz also cites Maharam in Mordechai as disagreeing with Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the Shach cites Maharshadam as likewise holding that even regarding movable property the burden is upon the brothers. This also appears to be the Ir Shushan's view below, siman 176, seif 46, where in the case of a deceased partner who had money belonging to others in his possession, the text as corrected by Ir Shushan places the burden upon the brothers. That fits the view that there is no distinction between movable property and documents. Even according to the alternative reading there, which places the burden upon the outside claimant, there is no proof for Rabbeinu Yerucham. One can distinguish. When the deceased claimed the property was his own, if he were alive he might have been expected to clarify his source of ownership, and failure to do so could weaken his claim. After his death, however, how could his orphans, who do not know their father's affairs, be expected to produce such proof? Therefore the brothers must prove their claim. But if the deceased said, "This belongs to a particular living person," then just as the deceased could have produced proof during his lifetime that the item came from that person, so too the living alleged owner can produce proof that he entrusted the item to the deceased. Indeed, the giver is more likely than the recipient to preserve proof for future need. If that living person produces no proof, his claim is weakened even more than the deceased's would have been. Many authorities in any event reject the distinction between life and death. Thus, where the alleged giver is still alive, everyone can agree that he must prove the transfer just as though the recipient were alive.
For perhaps he saved it from his maintenance, and even if the documents total more, the matter remains in his possession. Many authorities disagree with this reasoning. Baal HaTerumot, gate 51, cites authorities who require that the amount saved be sufficient to account for the documents. Rashba cites this in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel, whose words are regarded as received tradition. Raavad in Temim De'im was uncertain about it, and Hagahot HaRosh cites Rabbeinu Baruch that even where an inheritance fell to him, the inheritance must equal the value of the documents. All the more so would that be necessary where he merely saved from his maintenance. It is true that the Tur rules like the authorities who do not require the savings to equal the full value. But the Tur and Baal HaTerumot say this only regarding documents, where the claimant already has stronger support because the documents are written in his name. With movable property, by contrast, they are presumed to belong to the brothers, as I wrote above. Even after death, according to that view, the orphans must bring proof. Why should a very small inference, that he saved a little from his maintenance, remove a large amount of movable property from the brothers' chazakah? This is why neither the Tur nor Baal HaTerumot gives any indication that a tiny saving is enough for large amounts of movable property. There is no reason to expand the dispute unnecessarily. Since many authorities require in all cases that the savings equal the value of the documents, it is quite possible that regarding movable property everyone agrees. At minimum, even if we extend the lenient rule to movable property, it should apply only where he himself claims, "I saved this from my maintenance." But if he makes no such claim and instead says, "It belongs to another," and we want to believe him through a migo that he could have said he saved it, that migo should work only where the amount he could plausibly have saved actually equals the value of the documents and movable property. If the known saving is only small and we would have to imagine that perhaps he accumulated it little by little until it became a large sum, then although such a direct claim might suffice, it is too unusual to serve as a migo for another claim. We do not use an inferior and uncommon claim as a migo. This resolves what the Beit Yosef cites below, siman 176, from Responsa Rashba, that brothers and partners are not believed to say that property in their possession belongs to someone else. Bedek HaBayit objects that if they have separate maintenance, they should be believed, and the Shulchan Aruch rules that way there. According to what I have written, there is no contradiction. Ordinarily, the amount saved does not equal the large property claimed. If it did, the ruling would be obvious and there would be little novelty. Rashba can therefore hold that the migo is not used where it rests upon the unusual possibility of gradually saving far beyond the known amount. This requires study.
But a member of the household, etc. Because there is a dispute among the later authorities concerning this law, I will clarify it somewhat at length. The source is the Yerushalmi cited by the Rif and Rosh in Perek HaKones, in connection with the robbed person's special oath. The Yerushalmi asks whether the robbed person may claim items beyond what he is reasonably estimated to own, and answers from the following incident. A sharecropper of Bar Zuzai deposited one litra of gold with another person. Both the sharecropper and Bar Zuzai died. The depositor came before Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose, who said: "Who does not know that whatever Bar Zuzai's sharecropper owned belonged to Bar Zuzai?" He therefore gave it to Bar Zuzai's sons. Bar Zuzai had both adult and minor sons, and the adults took half. In the meantime Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose died. The case then came before Rabbi Chiya. He said: "From this fact you cannot prove anything. A person sometimes possesses wealth without publicizing it." He therefore awarded the money to the sharecropper's sons. When told that half had already been given to Bar Zuzai's adult sons, he said: "What you gave pursuant to a ruling of beit din remains given." The minor sons then said to the adults: "Divide with us what you received." The adults could answer: "We found a lost object." From this incident Rabbeinu Yerucham and the Rama derive that property found with a household member can remain presumed to belong to that household member or his heirs, following Rabbi Chiya. Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, Perek HaKones, writes that only in their time, when Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose was the leading sage and ruled according to his judgment, was the first ruling considered an error in discretionary judgment that could stand. Nowadays, after the law has already been determined in accordance with Rabbi Chiya, such a ruling would be reversed and the property removed. The Shach disagrees. He asks what source there is for saying this. Even if the initial ruling should ordinarily be like Rabbi Chiya, if the homeowner already seized the property, perhaps he can say, "I rely on Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose." His proof is that all the authorities copy the continuation that the minor sons could demand half from the adult sons, and the adult sons could answer that what they received was like a found object. If nowadays the judgment were simply reversed, why preserve that discussion at all? The property could be removed even from the adult sons. One would have to force the discussion into the general laws of a judge who erred in discretionary judgment. The Shach is correct against Maharshal to this extent: the first ruling is not an error regarding an explicit Mishnah. Nevertheless, his further conclusion that a possessor can simply say, "I rely on Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose," does not follow merely because the prior judgment stands as an error in discretionary judgment. We do not generally find that a litigant may plead that the halachah follows a view rejected by decisive discretionary judgment. If a judge who rules against settled discretionary judgment must sometimes pay from his own pocket, how can the litigant possess greater strength than the judge and retain the property by saying he follows the rejected view? Baal HaTerumot, as cited by the Shach in Tekfo Kohen and as I discussed in the laws of seizure, explains that a litigant can sometimes say "kim li" because a person may enforce his own rights and a judge who rules like that authority has a valid judgment. But if a judge who followed that opinion would be considered to have erred in discretionary judgment and would have to pay, then the litigant should not be stronger than the judge. Likewise, the Gemara's category of a judge who mistakenly acquits the liable party would be impossible if every issue of discretionary judgment automatically allowed the possessor to say "kim li." The very fact that the Gemara recognizes such an error shows that a possessor cannot invoke any rejected discretionary position. On the Shach's premise, the Sema would therefore be correct that even if the homeowner seized the property it should be removed. Nevertheless, there is room for the Shach's practical ruling for a different reason. The Rif at the beginning of Perek Dinei Mamonot explains the dispute in Perek Zeh Borer between Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Chiya, where Rabbi Chiya says, "An elder has already ruled." The Rif writes that this was not a true error in discretionary judgment, because if it were, the judgment would have been reversed since the judge had not personally transferred the property and would therefore have been exempt from payment. Rather, they disagreed in reasoning alone, and for that reason the first ruling was not reversed. The Rosh explains similarly, and I discussed this above in siman 25. The same must therefore be true in the Yerushalmi incident. It does not appear that Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose personally transferred the property. If his ruling had been a true error in discretionary judgment, it should have been reversed. Necessarily, their dispute was one of legal reasoning rather than a settled discretionary rule, and Rabbi Chiya lacked authority to overturn the earlier judgment. Once that is so, although Rabbi Chiya regarded his own reasoning as clear, who says it is strong enough to remove property from a present possessor when Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose supports the opposite view? The issue may remain one in which possession matters. This understanding is also prompted by the Rosh. The Rosh rules concerning the robbed person's special oath that the claimant cannot claim property beyond what he is reasonably estimated to own. He says this does not contradict the incident of Bar Zuzai's sharecropper, where the deposit remained with the sharecropper's side under Rabbi Chiya, because there the principle that "a person may impoverish himself outwardly while possessing wealth privately" was used only to maintain the deposit in the possession of the party who held it, not to extract property from another possessor. Hagahot HaRosh, Maadanei Melech, and other authorities ask that the Yerushalmi itself introduced the Bar Zuzai incident precisely as evidence for the question whether a robbed claimant may claim items beyond his apparent means. The Yerushalmi therefore seems to connect the cases directly. See below, siman 90, where the Shach struggles to explain this, and the strained nature of the usual solutions is evident. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah writes that we should make every reasonable effort to avoid interpreting the Yerushalmi as contradicting the Bavli. It therefore appears that the Yerushalmi did not intend to decide definitively like Rabbi Chiya. The Bar Zuzai incident presents an unresolved dispute between Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Chiya. Although an initial ruling may be rendered like Rabbi Chiya, if the opposing side has possession we do not remove it, because it can rely upon Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose. The Talmud does not state a final ruling between them. Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose was a major sage whom even Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi treated with honor. If a possessor may rely upon him, then certainly a robber against whom a robbed person invokes the special oath may say that the law follows his position and that the claimant cannot recover items beyond his apparent means. We then do not apply Rabbi Chiya's principle that a person may secretly possess wealth in order to extract unusual property. Thus the Yerushalmi properly derives the limitation on the robbed person's claim from this incident, and the Yerushalmi, Bavli, and Rosh all agree in practical law. The Shach's ultimate rule is therefore correct, although for this different reason. I later found Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 113, which likewise writes that a possessor may say "kim li" like Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose. He goes too far, however, in saying that this was why Rabbi Chiya did not remove from Bar Zuzai's adult orphans the half they had already received, because they could say "kim li" like the earlier ruling. That cannot be correct. If the adults had a legal right to retain the half on the ground that the law was like Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose, why would they not be required to divide it with the minor sons, who shared the same legal right? A possessor cannot say "kim li" in order to avoid giving a coowner his proportionate share in jointly owned property. That is impossible. Rather, Rabbi Chiya himself regarded the law as clear in favor of the sharecropper's heirs, but he did not overturn the earlier ruling because the matter depended upon legal reasoning and the first judgment had already taken effect. Therefore, what the adult sons received was treated for this purpose like a found object, and they were not required to share it with the minors. This is clear. Still, regarding the core practical issue, Mahara Sasson agrees that a possessor may rely upon Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose and cannot be dispossessed. The Shach seeks to limit this to a case where the sharecropper deposited the money with another person. Because he deposited it without saying that it was his, one might infer it belonged to the owner for whom he worked. But if it was found in the sharecropper's own house, the Shach says Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose would agree that it belongs to the sharecropper. His words are remote from logic. What difference should there be whether the property is in his own hand or another's? Why should the depositary know that it belongs to the sharecropper's employer? He will naturally return it to the depositor or his heirs. If anything, the fact that the sharecropper deposited it elsewhere without explaining that it belonged to his employer is evidence in his own favor. If it remained in his own possession, he always had time to deliver it to the true owner later. There is also a weakness in saying that the employer died without leaving instructions concerning property that supposedly had been deposited through his sharecropper. More importantly, according to the explanation above, the Yerushalmi's use of this incident for the robbed person's claim depends on the possibility of relying upon Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose even when the property is presently in the servant's possession. Under the Shach's distinction, Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose would concede when the property remains with the servant, and the incident would have nothing to do with a robbed person claiming an unusually large amount. In the robbery case there is no special defect of having deposited the item without declaring ownership. The entire comparison would collapse. It is therefore clear that the Shach's distinction is not correct and that the cases are genuinely comparable. The Bach disagrees with the Rama and understands both the Yerushalmi incident and Rabbeinu Yerucham's law as concerning a sharecropper who had separate maintenance and did not eat at Bar Zuzai's table. But where the servant had no separate maintenance, like ordinary household servants, whatever is in his possession is presumed to belong to the householder. The Shach objects that if the case concerns one with separate maintenance, why did Rabbeinu Yerucham need the Yerushalmi at all? The Bavli itself states that where brothers have separate maintenance, the brothers must prove that property belongs to the partnership, because perhaps the managing brother saved from his maintenance. Moreover, what would Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose's reasoning be? Saving from one's own maintenance is an accepted principle in the Bavli and should not be disputed. The Shach therefore understands the Yerushalmi as dealing with one who did not have separate maintenance. Although regarding brothers and a widow conducting household business we place the burden upon them, the case of a household member is different. He then labors to explain why. I ask the reverse. If the Yerushalmi concerns a household member without separate maintenance, while everyone agrees that one with separate maintenance can own what he saved, how can the Yerushalmi use the Bar Zuzai incident to illuminate a robbed person's claim to property beyond his apparent means? The robbed person has his own separate household. No one disputes such a person's ability to own property. That situation is not comparable to a sharecropper eating from his employer's table. The Bar Zuzai case would not be relevant to the question at all. Likewise, the Rosh asks how one can rule that a robbed person cannot claim property beyond his apparent means in light of Rabbi Chiya's ruling in the Bar Zuzai case. Why did the Rosh not simply ask from the Bavli's accepted rule that a brother with separate maintenance may have saved from it? Why did he need the Yerushalmi at all? The answer follows what I wrote above. When we say that saving a small amount from maintenance can account for a large amount, that special leniency is strongest with documents written in the person's own name. With movable property, according to Rabbeinu Chananel and the authorities who follow him, the amount saved must be sufficient to account for the amount claimed. The Yerushalmi's question about a robbed person concerns property so far beyond his apparent means that ordinary savings could not reasonably have accumulated to that amount. The Bavli's rule about saving from one's maintenance therefore does not resolve it. The Bar Zuzai sharecropper similarly could not reasonably have accumulated a whole litra of gold from his known wages or savings. Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose therefore presumed it belonged to Bar Zuzai, while Rabbi Chiya said that a person may possess hidden wealth and not publicize it. We follow Rabbi Chiya for retaining property, but the possessor may rely on Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose to resist extraction. The cases are thus parallel, and the Rosh's use of the Yerushalmi is exact. Why, then, in the case of brothers who conduct household affairs do we require the amount of savings to equal the movable property? Rivash, siman 50, suggests a useful distinction. With brothers or a widow it was known initially that they personally had nothing. The only new possibility is that afterward they found something, inherited, or saved. Without a plausible source, we retain their original chazakah that they had no independent property. But with the Bar Zuzai sharecropper or the robbed claimant, was it ever established that they were truly poor? Perhaps they possessed hidden wealth from beginning to end. The fact that they were publicly regarded as poor is not conclusive, because a person may deliberately avoid publicizing his wealth. Therefore one does not remove money from their possession. This is what Rivash means when he distinguishes the sharecropper from the wife by saying that a person may fail to publicize himself. According to the authorities who do not require movable property savings to equal the amount, one can explain the distinction from the opposite direction. A managing brother or wife, while engaged in business and holding documents and movable property, was not necessarily publicly regarded as destitute. They were buying and selling, receiving and paying, and outsiders could not know whether the wealth belonged to them personally or to the family enterprise. The only adverse chazakah is the known fact that before they began this business they had no private property. Once they show even a modest independent source, such as savings from maintenance, that original chazakah is weakened, because one can say they accumulated wealth gradually. At present they may even be publicly regarded as wealthy. By contrast, in the Bar Zuzai case and in the case of the robbed claimant, they were publicly regarded throughout as poor people who lacked resources. The sharecropper lived by hired labor and was known as a person without means. That continuing chazakah is stronger. Rabbi Yishmael son of Rabbi Yose therefore relied upon it, while Rabbi Chiya held that even here one may have hidden wealth and avoid publicity. This also explains why the Yerushalmi compares the cases. According to this understanding, even if the sharecropper did not have separate maintenance, there may still be room to say that he initially possessed some private funds or secret wealth. The incident can therefore apply in either arrangement. This appears to me the proper conclusion, though the matter requires study.
