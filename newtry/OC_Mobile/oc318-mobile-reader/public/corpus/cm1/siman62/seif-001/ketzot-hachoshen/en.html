The documents are under her control. Even if she has a migo, she is not believed. Shach.
She must bring proof. If the woman waived the debt to the borrower, it appears that the borrower acquires the benefit of the waiver. If she died, we would advance for her heirs the claim that the documents were hers. We do not advance that claim for her personally because, if they were truly hers, she could bring proof. Now that she waived the debt to the borrower, we advance for the borrower the claim that the documents were hers, even while she remains alive. See what I wrote in seif katan 4.
I found it as a lost object. The Shach proves from here that when a partner finds an ownerless object, the found object belongs to him alone. He therefore questions Rama in siman 176, who rules that where a partner steals, robs, or finds a lost object, it belongs to the partnership. Tumim answers that there Rama speaks where nothing was specified, so we assume the partner acquired it on behalf of the partnership, and one who lifts a found object for his fellow acquires it for his fellow. But if at the moment he lifts it he explicitly says, 'I acquire this for myself and not for my partner,' Tumim regards it as obvious that it belongs to him alone because he never intended to acquire it for the other. The present case, where she says, 'I found a lost object,' would mean that she intended at the time to acquire it solely for herself. What Tumim regards as obvious is very doubtful to me. According to Raavad and Rashba, partners can transfer to one another rights in property not yet in existence. They explain that this resembles saying, 'Your hands are consecrated for what they produce.' Because their bodies already exist, they can transfer their labor-power to one another, like slaves. Beit Yosef cites this in siman 176. If ordinary partnership intent is that all gains, whether found, stolen, or robbed, go to the partnership, the partners effectively transfer their hands and persons to one another for all work they perform. One partner can no longer say, 'I acquired this find for myself,' because his hand is already committed to the partnership equally. The view that even stolen or robbed property goes to the partnership is that of Hagahot Maimoniyot, and that authority holds that even without a separate kinyan the partners cannot retract from the partnership as to future profits. All the more so they cannot later retract by saying, 'I found and acquired this for myself, not for my fellow.' Perhaps Rama means to distinguish a found object: in an ordinary case, where there was no explicit retraction, it goes to the partnership, but if he explicitly withdrew from that aspect before acquiring it, perhaps not. Still, the principal inference from our case, as the Shach writes, is that a found object does not automatically belong to the partnership.
And she left orphans. The Shach writes that the same applies if she gave the property as a gift during her lifetime and then died, and so too in the corresponding case of a woman. He cites Maharl ibn Chaviv, responsum 58, and Maharam in the Mordechai, chapter Chazakat HaBatim, in the responsum beginning, 'Reuven married Leah and had from her Chanoch,' that those who derive their rights from her receive the same claims beit din would advance for an heir. This is straightforward, because throughout the Torah we advance claims on behalf of an heir, purchaser, or gift recipient. I do not understand why the Shach writes that she gave the gift and then died. Even if the transferor remains alive, we ordinarily advance claims for the purchaser, and even if the seller now supports the opposing claimant's version we disregard him. A person cannot make admissions that harm another's acquired rights, only his own. Here, because she herself is not believed merely because she fails to bring proof, once she transferred the right to another we should say on the recipient's behalf that she could have had proof, and her failure to produce it should not harm another. Perhaps the Shach added the condition that she died because the rule of advancing claims for heirs applies here specifically to documents but not to ordinary movable property, as he writes immediately afterward. A seller of a promissory note can later waive the debt. Thus if she gives the note to another while alive, we do not advance claims for the recipient, because the personal debt obligation remains with the transferor and she retains power to waive it. She can therefore impair the recipient's right, as explained in Shulchan Aruch siman 66, seif 15, where a seller of a promissory note who refuses to swear causes the purchaser to lose because the seller could have waived the debt. So too here, while she retains power to waive the note she can impair the gift recipient, and her failure to bring proof is equivalent to admitting that the note is not hers. According to this, if the documents were deeds of real-estate acquisition written in her name or similar rights that cannot be waived through the special law of promissory notes, then even while she remains alive we would advance claims for the purchaser or gift recipient. Her failure to bring proof is no worse than an express admission by the seller that the property was not hers, which cannot prejudice the recipient's already acquired right.
