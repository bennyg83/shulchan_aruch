On Erev Shabbos. Even if the litigants consent, there remains a prohibition upon the judges, because they must deliberate carefully in judgment and on Erev Shabbos and Erev Yom Tov they are preoccupied. The prohibition concerns adjudicating the case itself. It is permitted for the judges to receive the parties' claims and issue the ruling after Shabbos or Yom Tov. Even for this, however, the litigants cannot be compelled to appear. Alternatively, even where the judges themselves are not preoccupied, such as where others attend to their preparations, the litigants need not come. Thus the Sema.
We do not penalize. Because he may excuse himself by saying that due to the burdens of preparing for Shabbos or Yom Tov he forgot that he had been summoned. Sema.
A prohibition. Meaning matters such as gittin and chalitzah, regarding which one may not be lenient.
In Nisan. In Sivan, however, court dates are set, because it contains only two days of Yom Tov. Sema.
In the city. If he is in the city, the court adjudicates him. Likewise, if both litigants voluntarily come from one city to another, the court may adjudicate them throughout the month, except on Erev Yom Tov. Sema.
To complain. See Orach Chayyim at the end of siman 545, where the Mechaber rules without qualification that one may lodge a complaint concerning debts during Chol HaMoed, and Rama adds nothing. One must say that there the case is one in which a complaint would also be permitted during Nisan, such as where the opposing party is a resident of the city or where beit din observes evasion or deceit. Thus the Sema. His statement concerning a party who is a resident of the city requires study, since Rama's wording implies that if he is a resident of the city it is plainly forbidden to complain, as Rama writes "even against villagers," etc. Examine this carefully.
At night. The current custom is to be lenient and begin adjudication at night, because when a person comes to court after being summoned it is regarded as though he accepted the judges. Responsa Rama writes that if the parties consent, they may be judged at night. At first glance one might offer another reason: we rule that a person blind in one eye may serve as a judge because he can see, while one blind in both eyes is disqualified because for him even daytime is like night. Conversely, where lamps are lit at night and the judges can see and recognize people, perhaps they may begin judgment. The Sema found something analogous in an ancient collection, stating that a kinyan sudar is not made at night except by lamplight. He concludes that this may be said as a matter of halacha, but not as practical authority to compel a person to litigate at night even when lamps are lit, since he found no such distinction in any posek. The Shach responds that the proof from kinyan sudar actually disproves the suggestion, because the Gemara in Bava Batra explicitly says that a kinyan sudar may be made at night while judgment may not be conducted at night. Responsa Maharam Brisk likewise rules so.
If they transgressed. The Sema writes that although at the end of siman 28, regarding receiving testimony, the Mechaber rules that if testimony was received at night one may not adjudicate on its basis, there is a distinction. Receiving testimony is not itself the final judgment or a completed judicial act, and therefore it may not be used initially. Here, however, the judgment has already been completed. The Shach writes in the name of the Ramban, Ran, Rosh, and other poskim that a judgment rendered at night is invalid, and he concludes that this is the primary ruling.
