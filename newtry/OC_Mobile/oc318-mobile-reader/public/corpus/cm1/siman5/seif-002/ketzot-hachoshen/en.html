They ask that one cannot convene beit din on Friday, so how may she marry then, and answer that the husband may at least arrange and present his claims even on a day when formal adjudication is not held. Tumim asks from Nisan and Tishrei, when litigants are not summoned. This is no difficulty, because in one's own city summonses are issued even in Nisan and Tishrei. The enactment concerning a virgin's marriage necessarily assumes a city that has a beit din. In a city without beit din, the concern that the husband's anger may cool always exists because reaching the court may require several days of travel. Since the enactment concerns a city with beit din, summonses may indeed be issued in Nisan and Tishrei. Therefore Rosh and Ran ask only from Friday, when even in one's own city there is no summons.
Some say that if they transgressed and judged. In siman 28 the Shulchan Aruch rules that if testimony was received at night, judgment may not be rendered on the basis of that testimony. Rama cites no dissent there, implying agreement. The Sema distinguishes the cases. The Rosh in Perek Mitzvat Chalitzah writes that if chalitzah was performed at night it is valid according to one view and invalid according to another, depending on whether chalitzah is treated as the opening of judgment or the conclusion of judgment. From there it appears that if the beginning of a case was conducted at night, the act is invalid. The same follows from the case of three people who entered to visit a sick person at night: they may write testimony but may not sit as judges based on what they saw. The Rosh rejects a reading of the Jerusalem Talmud that would validate an entire night judgment after the fact. It appears, however, that the underlying law can still be explained. The prohibition at night applies to the beginning of judgment, namely the deliberative phase, while the final ruling may be issued at night. If a court improperly conducted the entire case at night, the opening deliberation was indeed improper, but the final ruling itself was delivered at a time when a final ruling is valid. The judgment therefore survives after the fact, much as a court's conclusion is not void merely because the judges failed to deliberate adequately beforehand. This explains the Jerusalem Talmud. It first sought to derive validity after the fact from the verse, "they shall judge the people at all times." It then recasts the verse as teaching that a case begun by day may be completed at night. From that very rule one can infer that a case entirely conducted at night is valid after the fact, because the final pronouncement itself occurred at a valid time for final judgment. By contrast, receiving testimony at night is itself part of the beginning of judgment, and that act is invalid even after the fact. The same is true of chalitzah if it is treated as the beginning of judgment. Thus Rama's ruling that a completed night judgment is valid after the fact is entirely consistent with the rule that testimony received at night is invalid.
