We do not adjudicate at night. See Sema, who says that if the parties accepted the judges it is as though they accepted nighttime adjudication. It appears that acceptance of two judges at night without a kinyan is insufficient to make an admission before them equivalent to an admission before beit din or to validate reception of testimony. For matters that specifically require beit din, nighttime is invalid even after the fact, as I explain below. There are thus two disadvantages, and mere acceptance without kinyan does not cure them, as explained in siman 22.
And some say that if they transgressed and judged at night, etc. Sema asks from siman 28, where reception of testimony at night is invalid. Rambam explains the prohibition of nighttime adjudication through the comparison of disputes to leprous lesions. Kesef Mishneh asks that Sanhedrin says the Mishnah is not according to Rabbi Meir, who makes that comparison. The matter can be resolved by distinguishing proceedings that require formal beit din from ordinary judicial decision. The verse concerning daytime inheritance teaches that acts requiring the status of beit din, such as receiving testimony, cannot be performed at night. Ordinary legal adjudication, however, resembles other halachic rulings and might otherwise be valid at night even by one judge. The comparison to leprous lesions therefore teaches that even such adjudication should initially be by day. According to Rabbi Meir, who requires three for every adjudication, the daytime verse itself already reaches the whole judicial act, and his comparison must extend further, including final judgment. This also resolves the sugya in Yevamot concerning chalitzah. On this basis Sema's question disappears. Receiving testimony, which specifically requires beit din, is invalid after the fact at night because Scripture expressly denies that judicial status at night. Ordinary adjudication, whose nighttime restriction is learned through comparison, may have been restricted only initially and can remain valid after the fact. Ketzot HaChoshen says that because final judgment is valid at night, a nighttime proceeding can be sustained even though the initial stage was invalid, comparing it to judges who issue a ruling without deliberation. This cannot be correct. If there was no beit din status at the initial stage, the claims and admissions were not judicially fixed, and the final judgment cannot simply cure that defect.
