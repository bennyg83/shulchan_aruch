We do not adjudicate on Friday or on the eve of a festival, etc. This prohibition concerns completing a judgment. The litigants' claims may be heard and the ruling issued after Shabbat or the festival. Ran and Sema. Sema adds that the litigants cannot ordinarily be compelled to appear merely to present their claims. See Tumim, where I wrote that even on Shabbat the claims can sometimes be arranged, and that where the matter arose only that day and delay creates a real risk of loss, beit din can compel appearance and receive the claims. In such an urgent case even on Shabbat the claims may be organized, though not ordinarily. Sema explains that courts do not adjudicate on Friday or the eve of a festival because preparation for Shabbat or the festival may cause the judges to hurry and err. The Sages said, "Be deliberate in judgment." Therefore even willing litigants do not automatically remove the concern. If they expressly accepted the judge even if he errs, however, the procedural concern is different because they accepted that risk. Sema says similarly regarding judgment at night. If the case began on Thursday and the court then slept on the matter before final judgment, it appears they may finish on Friday. The concern of insufficient deliberation is already removed by the overnight delay. Otherwise capital cases could never begin on Thursday when overnight deliberation is required before a guilty verdict, for final judgment would then fall on Friday. If this concern does not prevent capital adjudication after such deliberation, it should not prevent monetary adjudication either.
He is not fined. Because preparation for Shabbat may have caused him genuinely to forget the summons. His failure to appear is therefore not automatically treated as defiance.
But in a matter of prohibition there is no room for leniency. For example, arranging a get or chalitzah on Friday should be avoided because the pressure of Shabbat preparations may cause haste and an error in one of the detailed requirements.
One does not set a court date in Nisan, etc. Rambam explains this because of the festivals. Shavuot is only one day, so no comparable month long enactment was made. Knesset HaGedolah and Gidulei Terumah question whether one may set a date during Nisan or Tishrei for after the festival. It appears that the entire month is included because the festival's pressures and aftermath still occupy people. Knesset HaGedolah writes that a groom receives three weeks: seven days before the wedding, the seven days of celebration, and seven days afterward. Shevut Yaakov says that even the seven celebration days themselves do not exempt him from a summons. I cannot accept this. During all seven days one does not inspect a groom's tzaraat, and when new guests arrive the seven blessings are recited. How could one compel him to litigation during that period? One cannot prove otherwise from the High Priest, whose entire year is compared to a festival and yet the Mishnah says he judges and is judged. A High Priest has no comparable preoccupation, and the entire reason here is distraction. Furthermore, if his ceremonial status prevented summons he could never be judged. A groom's condition is temporary, so beit din can simply wait until the seven celebration days pass. After those days, however, there is no basis for an additional general exemption. Before the wedding, three days appear appropriate because the Sages required a groom to labor over wedding preparations for three days. Those days resemble the eve of a festival and no summons should issue then.
To one who is not in the city. If he is in the city, he may be summoned. Likewise, if the litigants willingly travel from one city to another to appear before judges, the judges may hear them, except on Friday or the eve of a festival, when the judges themselves should not adjudicate for the reason stated above. Sema.
And some say one should not make a public complaint in the synagogue, etc. The Mechaber in Orach Chaim 545 permits making a public complaint concerning debts during Chol HaMoed. Sema answers that there the debtor is a local resident, or there is deception, where such action is permitted at any time. Another distinction is preferable. Here the complaint would require summoning the other party to litigation, and that is not done on these days. In Orach Chaim the creditor merely seeks public pressure upon a debtor to pay an established debt. Such collection can proceed during the festival, for the Gemara concludes that monetary cases can be handled during Chol HaMoed and a person who refuses to comply can be excommunicated. The Yerushalmi source likewise speaks of collecting debts. The two rulings therefore fit together.
They do not adjudicate, etc. Sema derives the daytime requirement from the verses "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" and "they shall judge the people at all times," one for beginning a case and one for final judgment. Rambam derives it from the comparison of disputes to tzaraat. I discussed the Talmudic basis in Tumim. If the litigants consent to judgment at night, the court may adjudicate, since their acceptance is no worse than accepting a relative or other disqualified judge. Maharam. Sema writes that this is the basis for widespread leniency in judging at night: when people come after being summoned, it is treated as acceptance. He also suggests that candlelight makes the setting like daytime. Shach rejects that second suggestion. See Tumim, where I wrote that it has no firm basis, although I found limited support for it.
If they transgressed and adjudicated at night, etc. This applies where the case was actually decided and the judgment completed. If testimony was merely received at night, no one may adjudicate on the basis of that testimony, because receiving testimony is like the beginning of a case. This is why the Rama below in siman 28:24 does not qualify the Mechaber's rule about receiving testimony. Sema. It appears that if a court already adjudicated and completed a judgment on the basis of testimony improperly received at night, the judgment may still stand according to this opinion. See Tumim for my explanation of that view.
