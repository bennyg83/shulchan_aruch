We do not adjudicate on Friday, etc. There is also a prohibition upon the judges themselves to adjudicate, even if the litigants agree to appear, because judges must deliberate patiently and on Friday or the eve of a festival they are preoccupied. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who says the reason is only that ordinary people are busy and should not be summoned. Rama writes that in a matter of prohibition one should not be lenient lest the judges err because of their preoccupation. This shows that the concern is the judges' distraction, not merely compelling the litigants. In monetary law there is greater room for leniency because beit din has the power of hefker beit din hefker. Meaning: even if their preoccupation causes them to impose money improperly, the court's enactment effectively renders the property ownerless. This was accepted because of communal needs, such as preventing interruption of teachers. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 227, writes that one should be stringent regarding chalitzah because it is a matter of prohibition, and all the more so regarding giving a get on Friday because a get requires even greater precision. Specifically adjudication is forbidden. The judges may hear the parties' claims and issue the ruling after Shabbat or Yom Tov. Ran writes this at the beginning of Ketubot. The court cannot, however, compel a litigant to appear for that purpose on the eve of Shabbat. One who is not in the city, etc. The wording is precise: a person outside the city is not obligated to travel from one city to another for judgment during this period. If he is already in the city, however, he may be adjudicated. The Tur writes this in the name of the Ramah. It follows that if both litigants voluntarily travel from one city to another and wish to litigate, the court may adjudicate throughout the month except on the eve of Yom Tov, because on the other days there is no prohibition from the judges' side. Rama's gloss that some say one should not lodge a public complaint in the synagogue during Nisan should be compared with Orach Chaim 545, where the Mechaber rules simply that one may lodge such a complaint during Chol HaMoed for debts, also from Mordechai, and Rama does not comment. One must say that there the case is one where such a complaint is otherwise permitted during Nisan, for example where the litigant is local or the court sees evasion or deceit. The Orach Chaim ruling teaches only that the festival itself is not an additional obstacle.
We do not adjudicate at night at the beginning of a case, etc. This is derived in Sanhedrin 34a. Rava contrasts the verse, "They shall judge the people at all times," with "on the day that he causes his sons to inherit," which refers to monetary adjudication. The Gemara resolves: daytime is required for the beginning of the case, while night is valid for the conclusion. Rashi explains that the essential act of judgment is the conclusion. Tosafot hold the essential act is the beginning, but agree that "at all times" refers to the conclusion. See the Perishah for their precise dispute. Responsa Rama, siman 28, writes that if both parties accept adjudication at night, it is permitted, no worse than accepting a relative or otherwise disqualified judge. Rama rules similarly below concerning reception of testimony at night, based on Rashba. This is why the current practice is lenient to begin cases at night. Once a litigant comes when summoned, it is as though he accepted the court. One might have suggested another reason from the rule below in siman 7 that a judge blind in one eye may adjudicate even the beginning of a case because he can see, whereas one blind in both eyes is disqualified because even daytime is like night to him. Perhaps the reverse would follow: when lamps are lit at night and the judge can see and recognize people, the court may begin the case. I found an old collection stating that a sudar acquisition should not be made at night except by lamplight or moonlight so the witnesses clearly identify the parties. Similarly, the saying that one who has a meal should eat by day is explained by the principle that a blind person is not satisfied, yet if lamps are lit at night there is no concern. Nevertheless, this is only a halachic possibility and not a basis to compel a person to litigate at night, because I found no distinction of this kind in the poskim.
