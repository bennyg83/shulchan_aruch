One does not adjudicate on Erev Shabbat or Erev Yom Tov. Sema, citing Ran, writes that this prohibition concerns issuing judgment, but that beit din may hear the litigants' claims on Erev Shabbat and issue the ruling after Shabbat. I do not know why Sema cited this in Ran's name, because Tosafot, Rosh, and Tur in Even HaEzer siman 64 say the same. They explain the contemporary custom of marrying on Friday by noting that we no longer have fixed courts. A person who has a dispute gathers three people and arranges his claims before them, and this can even be done on Shabbat. If so, certainly the same is true on Erev Shabbat. Indeed, Tur there writes that a woman may marry on any day, whereas here he rules that one does not judge on Erev Shabbat. Necessarily the explanation just given resolves the apparent contradiction. Shiltei Gibborim in Zeh Borer raises a strong objection. One cannot hear the claim of one litigant without the other, because Scripture says, "Hear between your brothers." If both parties must appear, there is concern that the judges may write down the claims. The answer is that Sema's assumption regarding the concern for writing is not exact. We are not concerned that they will write the claims. The primary concern is that they may write the final judgment. Rashi in Beitzah says this explicitly. Tosafot write that even in capital cases there is concern for writing. That presents no difficulty, because in capital cases the court must record the words of those who acquit and those who convict, as the Mishnah states. There a genuine writing concern exists. In civil cases no such record is required, since before final judgment a judge can still move from acquittal to liability. Merely hearing claims therefore does not create the same concern. Shiltei Gibborim nevertheless questions whether litigants themselves may present monetary arguments on Shabbat because of "refraining from your affairs." Even if judging can be considered a mitzvah act for the judges, the litigants are discussing ordinary monetary affairs. According to his approach, the Gemara's statement that judging is a mitzvah can refer to a case where the claims were already presented before Shabbat. The judges then deal with a matter of mitzvah. But for the parties themselves to advance monetary claims on Shabbat may remain prohibited. It appears that if the dispute existed already on the previous day, ordinary presentation of claims should indeed be prohibited. But if the matter arose only on Shabbat and immediate presentation is necessary, because delay may enable a party to formulate false claims or manipulate the facts, it can be permitted. Chazal permitted several comparable acts on Shabbat where immediate need exists. That is why, after a wedding night on Friday night, the husband may present a claim that the bride was not a virgin on Shabbat. The claim could not have been raised earlier because intercourse had not yet occurred. If he waits, his concern may cool and she may learn to offer a false explanation, such as claiming an accidental injury. Therefore the claims may be arranged before beit din on Shabbat. Sema further writes that although claims may be heard on Erev Shabbat, the opposing party cannot be compelled to appear, because the Gemara says summonses are not issued on Erev Shabbat or Erev Yom Tov. This is difficult. Ran's original question would then remain: how could the enactment require marriage on Thursday if the husband would be unable to bring his complaint to beit din the following day? He cannot arrange his claims without the wife's presence because "hear between your brothers" requires both sides, and why should she voluntarily appear? She may prefer delay in the hope of calming him, which was precisely the concern behind the original marriage enactment. It therefore appears that where the cause for litigation arose only on Erev Shabbat and immediate hearing is necessary, beit din may even summon and compel the other party to appear. The same proof comes from the rule that summonses are ordinarily not issued during Nisan and Tishrei. If that prohibition were absolute, women should not marry during those months, because a claim arising from the wedding night could not be brought promptly. Since no such restriction exists, matters of immediate necessity that could not have been litigated earlier are exceptions. Accordingly, where a dispute newly arises on Erev Shabbat or Erev Yom Tov and there is genuine need to hear the claims immediately, beit din may summon the parties and hear them, while postponing the actual judgment until the proper time.
