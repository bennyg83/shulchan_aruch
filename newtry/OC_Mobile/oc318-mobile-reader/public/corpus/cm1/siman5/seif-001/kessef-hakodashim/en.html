Seif 3. The time when the judges sit. The Zohar in Parashat Mishpatim, 122a, states regarding monetary adjudication that one should judge only before eating and drinking. Plainly this does not mean that a judge may not eat at all earlier that day. Rav Nachman said that if he had not eaten ox meat his mind was not sufficiently settled, even while he was adjudicating monetary law, and he constantly served as a practical judge. Nor can it mean that all judges and members of the Sanhedrin, or Moshe Rabbeinu when the people stood before him from morning to evening, sat fasting. One could suggest that the Zohar refers only to a fixed meal, like the rule against eating before burial, but Rav Nachman's conduct indicates that even a fixed meal before beginning judgment is permitted. The early authorities and all generations of judges did not refrain from adjudicating after a meal. Rather, the Zohar's stringency concerns eating after the judges have begun examining a case and before they complete the judgment, where the matter can be concluded before the normal meal time. During that interval they should likewise avoid intoxicating drink even in a quantity below the measure of intoxication, and should avoid casual eating where it is unnecessary. True judgment requires removing love of gain, appetite, and worldly inclinations. It is therefore fitting during active deliberation to restrain even ordinary appetites that are not necessary. In this sense the Zohar says that eating can generate falsehood, meaning through aroused appetite. It also appears that this concern applies principally to a case dependent upon judicial discretion and deep analysis, or to an emergency ruling made to protect communal order, such as extraordinary fines or assessments based upon circumstances. These matters require extensive deliberation and fit the verse, "Render judgment in the morning," and the command to investigate well. In our generations, by contrast, batei din rarely decide cases on extraordinary assessment, since siman 15 reserves such authority to a preeminent beit din of the generation. Most ordinary questions of judicial discretion can be clarified by accepted practice or relatively straightforward reasoning, and truly difficult questions are postponed while the judges consult greater scholars or another beit din. This too removes much of the concern, and the established practice of Jewish judges does not contradict the Zohar. There is also room to be lenient where there is no coercive judgment at all and both parties voluntarily ask for guidance. Since even ordinary rabbinic doubts are ruled leniently, all the more a matter that is only a pious practice admits reasonable leniencies. If the judge initially did not realize coercive authority would be needed, and only later discovered that immediate enforcement was necessary because the parties were travelers or the matter was urgent, he need not refuse to rule. The Zohar speaks of the ideal conduct ab initio when it is practical.
