Some say that if they transgressed, etc. This is Rashbam's view, derived from the Yerushalmi in Sanhedrin. The Yerushalmi says: judgments are by day. From where? From "they shall judge the people at all times." It then discusses whether this verse can refer to something already done and concludes that if they erred and judged at night, their judgment stands. Tosafot in the chapter Mitzvat Chalitzah explain that the Yerushalmi's text should be reversed. First it says that if they mistakenly judged at night the judgment stands, and associates this with "they shall judge at all times." It then asks rhetorically: can Scripture be speaking only after the fact, about people who violated the law and judged improperly? The Yerushalmi therefore withdraws that reading and establishes the verse instead for final judgment, which is valid at night, exactly as in our Bavli. According to Tosafot, Rashbam's rule has no basis. There is a second reason to reject it. Even if one maintained that the Yerushalmi held a nighttime judgment valid after the fact, our Bavli assigns "they shall judge at all times" to final judgment. The Bavli's interpretation is controlling. A third objection comes from Yevamot concerning chalitzah. R. Elazar holds chalitzah at night invalid because he compares chalitzah to commencement of judgment. According to Rashbam, if ordinary commencement at night is valid after the fact, why should nighttime chalitzah be invalid? I believe Rashbam can nevertheless be defended. In Parashat Yitro the phrase "they shall judge the people at all times" appears twice. The first occurs in Yitro's advice concerning appointment of judges. The second occurs when Torah describes Moshe's implementation: "and they judged the people at all times." The second is naturally written in the past tense, because Torah is reporting what they did. But the first, describing what the judges are to do, should grammatically have said "and they shall judge" in a future form. Its use of a past-form expression is unusual. The Yerushalmi can therefore derive from the first occurrence that even if, after the fact, they judged at any time, including nighttime commencement, the judgment stands. The second occurrence is then available to teach that final judgment is valid at night from the outset. There is no redundancy: one verse governs after-the-fact commencement, the other ordinary final judgment. The question from chalitzah also disappears. Chalitzah is compared to commencement of judgment, and Torah requires its procedure to be performed by day. Even if civil judgment begun at night is effective after the fact, chalitzah is governed by the rule that every prescribed element of its act can be indispensable. Thus nighttime can invalidate chalitzah absolutely even though a mistakenly begun civil judgment may stand. There is also some evidence for Rashbam from Sanhedrin 87b. Rava searches for a civil-law dispute between a rebellious elder and the Great Beit Din that can ultimately produce a consequence involving karet. He cites the dispute between R. Elazar and Shmuel, which is an Amoraic dispute. Why did he not use the simpler disagreement between R. Meir and the Sages concerning completion of judgment at night? Suppose the judges issue final judgment at night and transfer money to one litigant, who then uses it for kiddushin. According to R. Meir the money would be improperly taken, while according to the Sages it would be valid property, potentially creating a karet consequence. The omission suggests that even according to R. Meir, a final judgment rendered at night may stand after the fact. If so, by analogy the Sages may likewise agree that commencement at night, though prohibited initially, is valid after the fact. This proof is not decisive, because Rava there is trying specifically to fit the baraita according to R. Meir. Still, it gives some support to Rashbam's position.
