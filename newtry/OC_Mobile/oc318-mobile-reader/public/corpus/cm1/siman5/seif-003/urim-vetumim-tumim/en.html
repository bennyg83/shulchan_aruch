One does not begin adjudication at night. Sema, seif katan 4, explains that this is learned from the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" together with "and they shall judge the people at all times": one verse teaches that commencement of judgment must be by day, while the other allows completion of judgment at night. This indeed follows the Gemara in Sanhedrin 34b. Rambam, however, in Hilchot Sanhedrin 3:3, writes that one does not begin a case at night because, by received tradition, negaim are compared to judgments: "By their mouth shall every dispute and every nega be decided." Just as negaim are examined by day, so too disputes begin by day. Kesef Mishneh asks that this hekesh is associated with R. Meir, and according to R. Meir even final judgment is invalid at night. We rule like the Sages, who derive the distinction instead from "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" and "they shall judge the people at all times." Kesef Mishneh answers that the Gemara itself asks what R. Meir does with "on the day he causes his sons to inherit," and explains that it teaches the law of three people who enter to visit a sick person: if three are present, they may either write as witnesses or constitute themselves as a beit din; if only two are present, they may write testimony but may not constitute a beit din. Since we rule in accordance with this law, the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is needed for that purpose. Consequently the daytime requirement for commencement of judgment must come from the hekesh. "They shall judge the people at all times" then teaches that final judgment may be rendered even at night. This correctly identifies Rambam's intent, but a difficulty remains. Rava himself juxtaposes the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" with "they shall judge the people at all times," assigning one to commencement and the other to final judgment. Why does he not explain the law according to Rambam, through the hekesh plus "they shall judge"? Moreover, why does the Gemara declare the Mishnah, which permits completion of civil judgment at night, to be unlike R. Meir? Perhaps even R. Meir uses the hekesh only for commencement of judgment, because "they shall judge the people at all times" permits final judgment at night. The same issue appears from the law of a blind judge. Complete blindness disqualifies, but a judge blind in one eye can be valid. Although one-eyed blindness would disqualify from examining negaim, the verse "they shall judge" broadens civil judgment. Rif and Rosh indicate this, and Rambam must likewise rely on that verse when he permits a one-eyed judge even at the commencement of judgment. Hagahot Maimoniyot and Lechem Mishneh also explain that a judge blind in one eye is included in the same broadening that permits final judgment at night. If so, R. Meir too might be understood as applying the hekesh only to commencement, while "they shall judge" broadens final judgment. One could perhaps say that this would leave the Mishnah in Niddah, "whoever is fit to judge is fit to testify," with difficulty, because its exclusion of a one-eyed blind person could no longer be assigned neatly to R. Meir or the Sages. But that is not the Gemara's stated reason for rejecting R. Meir, so the problem remains. There is a further question. If Rambam accepts the hekesh, we should say, as R. Meir does, that just as relatives are disqualified in disputes, so relatives should be disqualified in examination of negaim. Yet Rambam in Hilchot Tumat Tzaraat rules that a person may examine all negaim except his own, which implies relatives are valid. It therefore appears that Rambam follows his own ruling in Hilchot Sanhedrin 11:7 that in civil cases a father and son can be counted as two for purposes of discussion. Raavad objects that they are relatives. Kesef Mishneh explains that Rambam disqualifies relatives only at the moment of final judgment, when the court says, "So-and-so is liable and so-and-so is exempt." Before that point they may participate in deliberation. Although Kesef Mishneh connects this to Rambam's view that one judge is d'oraisa sufficient, that cannot be the entire reason. Rambam had a textual version of the baraita explicitly saying that in civil cases a father and son are counted as two. Rava, who according to that reading holds d'oraisa three are required, would otherwise remain difficult. We must therefore say that the distinction between discussion and final judgment stands independently. Accordingly, when R. Meir uses the hekesh from disputes to negaim to disqualify relatives, he must be applying the hekesh to final judgment. At the commencement and discussion stage, relatives can participate even in ordinary disputes. Therefore if the hekesh related only to commencement, it could not prove relatives are invalid for negaim. This explains why the Gemara says the Mishnah permitting final judgment at night is unlike R. Meir. R. Meir necessarily extends the hekesh to final judgment in order to derive disqualification of relatives in negaim. Rambam, however, who concludes that the hekesh governs only commencement, accordingly permits relatives in examination of negaim, because even in civil disputes relatives can participate at the commencement stage. This also answers a possible objection against Rambam from the Gemara's question: why does R. Meir need the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit"? Perhaps R. Meir too holds that the hekesh concerns only commencement and therefore needs the verse for final judgment. The Gemara's challenge can be restated. If R. Meir's hekesh applied only partially, then even after we know from "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" that final judgment requires day, perhaps the hekesh should still be limited to commencement. Then relatives would remain valid in negaim, because the comparison from negaim back to disputes would concern only the commencement stage. From where would R. Meir derive their disqualification? The Gemara therefore answers that "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is needed for the rule of three who enter to visit the sick person. The verse thus addresses testimony and commencement of judicial proceedings. Once that verse occupies the commencement category, the hekesh must extend to final judgment, thereby disqualifying night and relatives there. The Gemara is coherent.
However, this still does not fully answer the first question: why does Rava derive the distinction from "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" and "they shall judge at all times" rather than from the hekesh plus "they shall judge," as Rambam does? If we follow Rashba and the implication noted above in siman 3, that Rava disagrees with R. Acha and holds that d'oraisa one judge is not valid, there is an elegant answer. One must first ask why R. Meir understands the hekesh as applying to final judgment, forcing him to interpret "they shall judge at all times" narrowly, for example as allowing judgment on a cloudy day, rather than using it in its simple sense to permit final judgment at night. Rambam's reading shows that the Sages can accept the hekesh while limiting it to commencement. What is the root of the dispute? Tosafot in Bava Batra ask why, according to R. Meir, the verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is needed if the nighttime disqualification already comes from the hekesh. Under Rambam's approach there is no difficulty. Either verse by itself could have been read as applying only to commencement. Once both exist, one governs commencement and the other final judgment, so that all must be by day. Ramban, however, explains that "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" comes to teach that a witness cannot become a judge. By day the witnesses to a will can themselves distribute the inheritance as judges, because direct seeing is no less than hearing testimony. At night they cannot, because a witness cannot become a judge. Ramban writes that the general rule that a witness cannot become a judge is derived from this verse. Rashbam and Tosafot, by contrast, derive that principle from "the two men who have the dispute shall stand before" the judges. Since the verse speaks of witnesses standing before judges, the witnesses themselves cannot be the judges. If so, however, why is "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" needed to teach that commencement must be by day? The law that a witness cannot become a judge already implies that there must be some time when direct observation cannot be converted into judicial action. If judgment were valid equally by day and night, there would be no such situation. One could answer that the case is one where only two people saw the event. Even if judgment were generally possible at night, those two cannot adjudicate because three judges are required. Since they must testify before a third judge, they become witnesses and cannot themselves sit in judgment. Thus the principle that a witness cannot become a judge can be derived even if nighttime judgment is generally valid. But that works only if d'oraisa three judges are required. If d'oraisa even one judge is valid, two observers should certainly be able to adjudicate directly, and the question returns: why can a witness not become a judge? In that system, to preserve the distinction one needs a time, namely nighttime, in which seeing cannot itself become judgment. R. Meir says, "If disputes require three, perhaps negaim too should require three; therefore Scripture says, 'to one of his sons.'" This shows that he holds d'oraisa disputes require three and rejects R. Acha. According to him, the verse concerning the inheritance day can indeed teach the ordinary daytime requirement, because the separate rule that a witness cannot become a judge remains available from the other verse. The Sages, according to Rambam, follow R. Acha that d'oraisa one judge can suffice. They therefore cannot derive the rule that a witness cannot become a judge from "the two men shall stand" in the same way. Instead, following Ramban, they use "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" for that rule. The hekesh then supplies the daytime requirement for commencement, while "they shall judge at all times" permits final judgment at night. This is Rambam's exact arrangement.
Accordingly, Rava's formulation also becomes understandable if he disagrees with R. Acha and holds d'oraisa three are required. For him, the rule that a witness cannot become a judge can be learned from "the two men shall stand," as with R. Meir. Therefore "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" remains available for the daytime requirement itself. Rava thus understands the Sages as rejecting the hekesh entirely. That is the real dispute between R. Meir and the Sages according to Rava. R. Meir derives from the hekesh; the Sages do not. Hence Rava juxtaposes the two verses "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" and "they shall judge at all times" without mentioning the hekesh. We, however, do not rule like Rava on this point. Rambam follows the accepted law that d'oraisa one judge is valid. Therefore the hekesh remains necessary, as Lechem Mishneh notes that Sifrei records it without dispute. There is an even shorter way to state the matter. Ramban's explanation of "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is that by day the witnesses to a will can themselves become judges, while at night they cannot. This sounds odd unless one assumes that however many witnesses there are, they are numerically sufficient to constitute a court. Since testimony never has fewer than two witnesses, this makes excellent sense according to the view that even one judge is d'oraisa sufficient. But according to R. Meir, who holds three judges are d'oraisa required, the verse cannot simply mean that every pair of will-witnesses can adjudicate by day. Two witnesses would still be insufficient. Therefore R. Meir cannot accept Ramban's interpretation as the primary function of the verse. He uses it instead for the direct daytime requirement, leaving the hekesh to disqualify final judgment at night. The Sages, according to Rambam, accept that one judge is d'oraisa sufficient. Two witnesses are certainly enough numerically to form a court. Therefore Ramban's explanation of "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" works perfectly: by day they can adjudicate, while at night they cannot because a witness cannot become a judge. The daytime requirement for commencement then comes from the hekesh. Final judgment remains valid at night from "they shall judge at all times."
However, if, as I wrote above in siman 3, even Rava accepts R. Acha that one judge is d'oraisa valid in civil cases, the preceding resolution of Rava cannot stand, even though the explanation of R. Meir remains sound. Another approach can be built from Rashbam in Bava Batra. Rashbam connects the rule that a witness cannot become a judge to the law that if one member of a witnessing group is a relative or otherwise disqualified, the entire testimony can fail depending on whether they intended to testify. According to Rashbam, if a person intended to be a witness, he acquires witness-status and cannot later serve as judge even if he merely saw the matter by day. Tosafot and other authorities reject that connection. They hold that even one who intended to testify can sometimes judge if he personally saw the event by day, because direct seeing is no less than hearing testimony. Still, one could have made the opposite error. In the law of a relative or disqualified observer, where the person did not intend to testify, he does not invalidate the other witnesses even if he later appears in beit din. This suggests that without original intent to testify he may not receive the full halachic status of a witness. One might therefore have thought that the rule "a witness cannot become a judge" applies only to one who originally intended to testify, whereas one who merely happened to see the event and had no intent to act as witness could still serve as judge. The verse "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" disproves this. It teaches that at night even those who merely entered to visit, with no intention to testify, cannot turn what they saw into judicial action. Thus nighttime itself prevents them from serving as judges. This explains why the Gemara specifies that the three entered "to visit" rather than simply saying they entered. The detail teaches that even without intent to testify, nighttime seeing cannot be transformed into judgment. This distinction has force according to Rebbi, who differentiates in the law of a disqualified witness between one who intended to testify and one who merely observed. R. Yosi, by contrast, does not make that distinction. In his view an observing disqualified witness can invalidate regardless of intent. Therefore there is no room to think that lack of intent removes witness-status for our issue. Ravina, the later authority, in Sanhedrin 39b explains R. Meir according to R. Yosi and the Sages according to Rebbi. If so, R. Meir cannot say that "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" is needed to teach that even a non-intending observer cannot become a judge, because according to R. Yosi there is no distinction between intending and not intending. The verse is therefore left available for the daytime requirement, and the hekesh extends to final judgment. We, however, rule like Rebbi. There is a genuine basis for confusion between intent and non-intent. The inheritance verse is therefore needed to teach that even one who did not intend to testify cannot judge based on what he saw at night. The verse is not redundant, and the hekesh can be limited to commencement. Rava can likewise be understood. In that passage he explains the dispute of R. Meir and the Sages through concern for rumor rather than through the R. Yosi versus Rebbi issue. If R. Meir and the Sages did not differ regarding intent, and R. Meir still required the hekesh for final judgment, Rava could conclude that the Sages reject the hekesh entirely. We, however, follow Ravina's later explanation and therefore can retain Rambam's reading. This also assists with the difficulty raised against Rambam from Yevamot 104 concerning whether chalitzah performed at night is valid. The Gemara suggests that the dispute may depend on whether we compare disputes to negaim, and then says no: everyone agrees the comparison does not extend to final judgment; the dispute is whether chalitzah resembles commencement or conclusion of judgment. At first glance, Rambam is difficult, because he himself uses the hekesh for commencement but not final judgment. Yet Rashba already questions the Gemara from another direction: why not say that R. Elazar follows R. Meir and holds the hekesh extends even to final judgment? The answer is that the Gemara's point is not that no hekesh exists at all. Rather, it asks whether the hekesh is complete, extending even to final judgment. If R. Elazar held a complete hekesh, then final judgment in ordinary civil cases would also have to be by day. Since the dispute is presented specifically in chalitzah, that cannot be the intended basis. The Gemara therefore says that everyone agrees the hekesh does not extend to final judgment, and they dispute only whether chalitzah has the character of commencement or conclusion. This is fully consistent with Rambam, who applies the hekesh only to commencement. There is an additional proof supporting Rambam from the very structure of that sugya. According to Rashi, Ramban, and Shulchan Aruch below, siman 7, if witnesses were formally designated to testify, they may not later serve as judges even when they observed by day. Rambam appears not to accept that; he disqualifies a witness from judging only after actual testimony in beit din. One might have thought that "on the day he causes his sons to inherit" comes to teach precisely Rambam's rule: even if they were designated witnesses through participation in a kinyan, by day they can still constitute a court. Tosafot in Bava Batra 113b explain that the verse's case must involve a kinyan or the opinion of R. Yochanan ben Beroka that a person can distribute inheritance among those fit to inherit him. If it involves a kinyan, the very people who received the kinyan are necessarily the witnesses who later adjudicate. They were designated witnesses in the strongest way. The verse saying that by day they can adjudicate would then directly reject Rashi's view. The difficulty is that perhaps the verse follows R. Yochanan ben Beroka and no kinyan is required. The Gemara in Bava Batra and Gittin 14b concerning one who distributes property by oral instruction indicates that R. Elazar does not follow R. Yochanan ben Beroka. Tosafot there conclude that the correct reading is R. Elazar rather than R. Eliezer. Accordingly, when Yevamot discusses R. Elazar's view concerning chalitzah at night, the inheritance verse cannot be explained through R. Yochanan ben Beroka. It necessarily involves a kinyan, and therefore witnesses were formally designated. The verse can then teach that formal designation does not bar them from judging by day, contrary to Rashi. This is another reason the verse need not be used to disqualify final judgment at night under Rambam's system.
From all this it appears that once we derive the invalidity of commencing judgment at night from the hekesh between disputes and negaim, illumination by lamps should not make nighttime valid for commencement. Negaim themselves cannot be examined at night merely because artificial light is present. Sema permits commencement at night by candlelight, but Shach, seif katan 4, correctly objects. I have found no substantial basis for Sema's leniency except a passage cited by Beit Yosef in the laws of Megillah from an unnamed source: since a Purim meal eaten at night does not fulfill the mitzvah because Scripture says "days of feasting and joy," perhaps lighting lamps at night makes it sufficiently day-like to permit the meal. But even that suggestion has no firm root in the Gemara. Accordingly, artificial illumination does not transform nighttime into daytime for commencement of judgment.
