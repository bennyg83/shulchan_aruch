He stole a lean animal and fattened it, etc. The case is where the thief fattened it through his own expenditures, as Rama explains. The thief can say, "I did the fattening, and you would take the added flesh?" If the animal was fat and became lean, by contrast, even if it died on its own, the victim can say, "What difference is there whether it died entirely or half died?" All robbers pay according to the value at the time of the robbery.
It is as though it increased in market value. We already explained that if the property increased in value on its own and then died naturally, he pays according to its original lower value. If he slaughtered it with his own hands, he pays according to its higher value at slaughter. The same applies to fattening that occurred by itself. This explains the Mechaber's concise words, which address the principal but not double payment or the fourfold and fivefold penalties. The Ir Shushan also discusses double payment and fourfold or fivefold payment, but in my opinion he adopted an independent approach contrary to the Gemara in chapter Merubah, the Rosh there, and the Tur here. He writes that from the verse "if the theft is found alive in his hand, he shall pay double," the Sages derive that the principal is restored according to the item's value when stolen. Thus, if an animal or utensil was worth four at the time of theft but only two when judgment was entered, and the thief slaughtered or sold the animal or broke the utensil, he pays principal of four, while double payment and fourfold or fivefold payment are calculated according to the value at judgment. He further says that if it was worth two at theft and four at judgment, and he slaughtered, sold, broke, or destroyed it with his own hands, the principal remains two while the penalties follow the value at judgment; and if the animal died or the utensil was lost on its own, all payments follow the value at theft. These words are highly problematic. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 65a says in Rav's name: the principal is according to the value at the time of theft, while double payment and the fourfold or fivefold payment are according to the relevant later time. The Gemara establishes Rav's statement where the item was initially worth four zuz and later one. If the reverse occurred, Rava already taught that one who robbed a barrel of wine worth one zuz and later, when it was worth four, broke or drank it, pays four; if it broke by itself, he pays one. Rav's case too concerns loss occurring on its own. The Rosh explains that the Torah's phrase "the theft" and "alive" teaches specifically that the principal is restored as at the time of theft. Double payment and the fourfold or fivefold payment are not included in that limitation. There is also a logical reason: liability for the principal arises at the time of theft, whereas liability for double payment does not arise until judgment, because admission would exempt him from the penalty. Therefore double payment follows the value at judgment. But fourfold and fivefold payment is valued at the time of slaughter or sale, because that is when the liability is created. If the animal increased in value after slaughter but before judgment, he does not pay according to the later value, just as even the principal, if it increased and was then accidentally destroyed, follows the original value because the thief did no later act. Likewise if it decreased, fourfold and fivefold payment follows the value at slaughter or sale. The Rosh's language is cited by the Beit Yosef, and the Tur states the same explicitly. There are therefore two contradictions to the Ir Shushan. First, he treats double payment and fourfold and fivefold payment alike, all according to the value at judgment, based on "whom the judges condemn." But the Rosh and Tur explicitly distinguish: double payment follows judgment, while fourfold and fivefold payment follows slaughter or sale. Second, he says that if the item was worth two at theft and later four, even if the thief slaughtered it or broke it by hand he pays only the original principal of two. He further writes in seif 4 that if an animal was stolen and later became lean or fat on its own, its law is like ordinary appreciation or depreciation and the principal follows the value at theft. This would mean that even if it fattened on its own, analogous to price appreciation, and the thief then slaughtered or sold it, the principal remains the earlier lower value. That is contrary to Rava's statement and all the authorities, who say that where the thief actively destroys the item after it appreciates he pays the higher value at the time he removed it from the world. The Ir Shushan himself writes in siman 360, seif 10, concerning robbery that in such a case he pays four. The laws of theft and robbery are equal in these matters, as the Gemara itself proves from Rava's robbery law to Rav's theft law. The Ir Shushan's own words also contradict themselves. He first says that where the stolen item was worth four at theft and two at slaughter, the thief pays principal of four so that a sinner not profit, implying that without that reason one would follow the lower value at the time of destruction. He then says that where it was worth two at theft and four at slaughter, he nevertheless pays principal of only two, not the value at destruction, even though the same principle about preventing profit would point in the opposite direction. Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchot Geneivah, writes that if it was worth two at theft and four at judgment, and the thief slaughtered or broke it, he pays double or fourfold and fivefold according to the value at judgment. One cannot infer from Rambam's failure to mention the principal that he holds the principal follows the theft value. Rava's explicit rule proves otherwise. Nor can one say that Rambam means the item remained worth only two at slaughter and increased to four only before judgment, so that principal follows the value at destruction while penalties follow judgment. That would conflict with the Rosh and Tur regarding fourfold and fivefold payment, which follow slaughter or sale, and it would be strange for Rambam to omit Rava's explicit case and instead introduce an unmentioned distinction without explanation. The correct understanding is either that Rambam omitted the principal because its law is obvious from the Gemara, or that his text should include the principal as well. The Maggid Mishneh indeed comments that in such a case principal and double payment are equal, as is evident from the Gemara and stated in the halachot. This explicitly warns against inferring a distinction from Rambam's omission. The Ir Shushan also concludes that if the animal died or the utensil was lost by itself, all of principal, double payment, and fourfold or fivefold payment follow the theft value because the item is no longer extant. This is imprecise in several respects. If it died or was lost on its own, there is no liability for fourfold or fivefold payment at all, because that penalty arises only through slaughter or sale. Nor do Rambam or the Tur state such a law. His reason that the item is not extant is likewise difficult, because the verse "whom the judges condemn," from which he derives valuation of double payment at judgment, applies even when the stolen item is no longer extant and the victim sues for its value.
