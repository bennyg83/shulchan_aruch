(Supplement) A thief, etc. Rashi and Tosafot hold that the broken object itself is not appraised against the thief. Rashbam and Ramah distinguish between payment in money and payment with the broken object, and Ramah permits appraisal only where the thief lacks money. This follows his broader view that damages are paid from the best available property only when cash is unavailable. We do not rule accordingly. (End of supplement.)
(Supplement) And he broke it, etc., or it broke, etc. In damages the broken article is appraised whether the tortfeasor broke it directly or it broke by itself. Here the Gemara's contrast with damages shows that even spontaneous breakage is included. See 387. (End of supplement.)
(Supplement) Or its value. Tosafot and Rosh explain that there is no difference between returning the object itself and paying its value. See Bava Kamma 94a. (End of supplement.)
Specifically, etc. Nimukei Yosef cites Ramah that the thief may tender the broken object only where he lacks money. Tosafot in Bava Metzia, however, allows appraisal even when he has money, while Tosafot in Bava Kamma requires either complete objects or money. Thus there are three views. The principal ruling follows Tosafot and Rosh that cash is not always required, unlike Ramah. See simanim 364 and 419.
And if they wished, etc. Maggid Mishneh objects that the thief should acquire the article through its change, for Bava Kamma 94a expressly gives that reason. Sema's answer is unsatisfactory: once the thief acquires it, the owner cannot compel him to return it. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes that appraisal is denied because the thief acquired the broken article through change. Perhaps Rambam learned from the case of depreciation where the article remains fit for its original use. See simanim 101 and 419.
