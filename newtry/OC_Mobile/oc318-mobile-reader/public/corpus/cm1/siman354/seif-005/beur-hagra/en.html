(Likkut) A thief, etc. This follows Rashi and Tosafos there. Rashbam in Bava Metzia disagrees and holds that the broken article is appraised, except that depreciation is not appraised, because the teaching "the owners shall deal with the carcass" applies to depreciation. The Gemara asks, "Even," etc., and answers that it refers to depreciation. Ramah likewise holds this way, as Nimukei Yosef writes in his name in Bava Kamma there. Nimukei Yosef cites the explanation of Rashi and the Rosh and then writes that Ramah holds that this applies specifically when the thief or robber has money. If he has no money, and the robber possesses the broken utensil, he may give it to the owner, as the verse says "he shall return," etc., for all twenty-four primary categories of damages pay from the best property. The only distinction is regarding depreciation; see there. Ramah limits this to where he lacks money because he follows his own view that the same principle applies even to damages, as stated there concerning the derivation from "he shall return." This is what the Rama writes in the gloss, "specifically," etc. We do not rule this way; see what I wrote there. End.
(Likkut) A thief, etc. Rashi and Tosafot hold that the broken object itself is not appraised against the thief. Rashbam and Ramah distinguish between payment in money and payment with the broken object, and Ramah permits appraisal only where the thief lacks money. This follows his broader view that damages are paid from the best available property only when cash is unavailable. We do not rule accordingly. (End of supplement.)
(Likkut) And he broke it, etc., or it broke, etc. In damages the broken article is appraised whether the tortfeasor broke it directly or it broke by itself. Here the Gemara's contrast with damages shows that even spontaneous breakage is included. See 387. (End of supplement.)
(Likkut) Or its value. Tosafot and Rosh explain that there is no difference between returning the object itself and paying its value. See Bava Kamma 94a. (End of supplement.)
Specifically, etc. Nimukei Yosef cites Ramah that the thief may tender the broken object only where he lacks money. Tosafot in Bava Metzia, however, allows appraisal even when he has money, while Tosafot in Bava Kamma requires either complete objects or money. Thus there are three views. The principal ruling follows Tosafot and Rosh that cash is not always required, unlike Ramah. See simanim 364 and 419.
And if they wished, etc. Maggid Mishneh objects that the thief should acquire the article through its change, for Bava Kamma 94a expressly gives that reason. Sema's answer is unsatisfactory: once the thief acquires it, the owner cannot compel him to return it. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes that appraisal is denied because the thief acquired the broken article through change. Perhaps Rambam learned from the case of depreciation where the article remains fit for its original use. See simanim 101 and 419.
(Likkut) And if they wished, etc. See Be'er HaGolah, who cites the Maggid Mishneh's objection. It is certainly difficult. Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Kamma explicitly writes that the reason one does not appraise for a thief or robber is that through the change he acquires the broken object against the will of the victim, unlike in damages. End.
(Likkut) And if they wished, etc. This is difficult. It appears that Rambam learned it from the statement on daf 97a, "if he wishes, its depreciation," etc. The Rashba there, however, answers that that case is different because the object is still fit for its original function. End.
