He pays the principal according to the value at the time of the theft. The Mechaber carefully says "the principal," excluding double payment and fourfold or fivefold payment. He does not explain how those penalties are valued, nor distinguish between appreciation or depreciation occurring between slaughter and judgment, because he is not dealing with penalty law. The Tur does explain the matter because seizure can still create practical consequences. The reason for the first rule is that all robbers pay according to the value at the time of the robbery. Scripture says that if the stolen object is found alive in his hand, he pays double, and the Sages derive that the principal is restored as the object was when stolen. Therefore, if the object was worth four at the time of theft and later fell to two, the principal remains four whether he broke it, slaughtered it, or it died on its own. If it was originally worth two and later rose to four, however, there is a distinction. If he slaughtered or sold the animal, or broke the utensil, while it was worth four, he pays a principal of four, because that later act was a new destruction of the owner's property. Had he not destroyed it then, he could have returned the actual object. But where it declined in value before he destroyed it, we do not reduce the principal to the lower value, for otherwise the sinner would profit from his wrongdoing: if the animal had died by itself he would have owed the original four, yet by slaughtering it himself he would owe only two. When the Gemara speaks of the value at the time of judgment, that is not literally because the principal depends on judgment. It refers to a case where the price remained the same from the time of slaughter or breakage until judgment, and the Gemara uses the customary expression. See the Derishah for why judgment is mentioned at all.
And at the time judgment was rendered. This block continues the preceding analysis. The Mechaber discusses only principal, not the statutory penalties. The principal is generally fixed either by the value at the original theft or, where the thief later destroys the object with his own hands after it appreciated, by the value at the time of that later destruction. The phrase "at the time judgment was rendered" in the Gemara is therefore not meant literally for the principal. It is a shorthand for a case where the market value at judgment remained the same as it had been when the thief slaughtered, sold, broke, or otherwise removed the object from the world. Double payment, however, is assessed according to the value at judgment, while fourfold or fivefold payment is assessed according to the value at slaughter or sale. The Tur discusses these distinctions because there can be a practical consequence if the claimant seizes the penalty. The Mechaber omits them because he does not ordinarily adjudicate penalties.
