He pays according to the value at the time of the theft. The reason is the enactment encouraging wrongdoers to repent, as the Rambam writes. The Tur cites the Rambam that if one stole a pregnant cow and it gave birth in his possession, he pays the value of a cow about to give birth, while the cow and calf remain with the thief. This is not because the thief acquired the cow itself through change, as some explain, for the Rambam later rules that if he stole an empty cow and it became pregnant, the cow returns to its owner, showing that pregnancy is not a change that transfers ownership. Rather, in the first case the thief must restore what he stole, namely a pregnant cow. The cow now before him is empty, while the calf, having separated, belongs to the thief. He must therefore pay as one who stole a pregnant cow. He cannot force the owner to take the empty cow at an appraisal and receive the difference in money, just as seif 5 rules that one cannot return the oxen and merely supplement their loss. The stolen object must be restored in the condition in which it was stolen, not after deterioration with a cash adjustment. If the owner chooses to accept the cow and the difference, he may do so. Any increase after the theft, whether wool or offspring, belongs to the robber. In the reverse case, where an empty cow became pregnant, once the calf is born the robber acquires the calf and returns the cow alone. The owner cannot compel him to surrender the calf for cash. If the owner demands return while the fetus is still inside the cow, however, even though the robber has acquired the increase through a genuine change, the owner may pay for the increase and take the cow together with the fetus. The Sema writes that once the cow gives birth, the thief retains the cow incidentally to the calf. This is untenable, and there is evidently a scribal error in his words.
