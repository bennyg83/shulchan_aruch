He stole or robbed. The Gemara discusses whether property mistakenly or unlawfully held by another person is considered in the owner's legal domain for consecration and related rights. Tosafot RiD suggests that if property entered another person's hands by mistake rather than theft, and that person would obey beit din once informed, it may remain in the owner's legal domain like a deposit. In my view ordinary willingness to obey beit din is not enough. The case of poor people who mistakenly take three stalks as though they were leket proves this. They are not thieves and would certainly obey beit din, yet until the matter is adjudicated the property is treated as outside the owner's legal control. The defining feature of a true deposit is that the depositor can demand it at any time and the custodian has no adverse claim. If the possessor believes he has a legal right, or the owner needs proof and litigation to recover it, the object is not presently in the owner's domain for consecration or transfer. This is also shown by one who buys stolen property before despair. Even if the purchaser is unaware that the object is stolen and would obey beit din once the truth is proven, a later thief of that object does not pay double to the original owner. The reason is that the property was not in the original owner's present legal control. Therefore mistaken possession is still "not in his domain" whenever the owner cannot presently recover it without proving his case and invoking beit din. A deposit is different because it is unconditionally returnable on demand.
Nor may the owners do so. Tosafot asks why the original owner cannot redeem another person's fourth-year produce for that person's benefit, since one can acquire a benefit for another without his knowledge. Rabbeinu Meir answers that in the case of the pious owners, the gatherers themselves have no present ability to redeem the produce because it is not theirs before acquisition. Since the gatherers cannot themselves perform the act, another person cannot perform it merely as their agent. Even according to the opinion that despair later gives the gatherers ownership, the timing creates a further obstacle. The pious owners placed the redemption money before the produce was gathered and stipulated that the redemption would take effect after gathering. At the earlier time the gatherers did not yet own the produce, so they could not redeem it themselves. Their future acquisition is not something already in existence for them. The original owners likewise cannot rely on their own ownership after gathering if the gatherers have by then acquired through despair. After acquisition, the produce is no longer the original owners' property. Therefore the arrangement must be understood as operating before complete despair, under the special legal framework of the Mishnah. One cannot solve it simply by saying that the owners act for the gatherers as a benefit, because the gatherers themselves lacked the present legal power over the future-acquired produce at the moment the redemption was arranged. This also answers the approach that would treat the act as ordinary agency. Agency cannot grant an agent a greater present power than the principal himself possesses.
