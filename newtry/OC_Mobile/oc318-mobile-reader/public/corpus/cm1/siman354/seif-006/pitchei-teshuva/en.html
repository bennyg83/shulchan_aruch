Stolen or robbed. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Tosafot Rid in Kiddushin, who addresses the Gemara's rule that if one mistakenly used maaser sheni money to buy a non-kosher animal and the seller is absent, the purchaser should eat other produce corresponding to the money. Tosafot Rid asks how the maaser money can be redeemed when it is not in the purchaser's possession. He answers that because the seller did not receive the money as a robber but only by mistake, and had he known the transaction must be reversed he would have returned it and obeyed the law, the money is like a deposit held by another person. In his reading, "the seller fled" need not mean literal flight but merely that the seller is not presently before us. Tosafot elsewhere do not give this answer, and Ketzot therefore argues that even where the holder does not know of the defect and would obey beit din, the item is still legally not in the owner's possession. He adduces proofs and concludes that only a deposit against which the custodian has no claim and which stands ready to be returned whenever demanded is deemed in the owner's possession. Whenever the holder has a claim and does not return it whenever the owner demands, it is legally outside the owner's possession and the owner cannot consecrate or transfer it. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 169, discusses a stolen object sold by the thief to an innocent buyer. While it was with the buyer, the original owner consecrated it to charity, and it was later returned to him. The questioner wished to apply Tosafot Rid: since the buyer would never have bought it had he known it was stolen, his possession arose by mistake rather than robbery, and the object should be considered in the owner's possession for consecration. Chatam Sofer defends Tosafot Rid against Ketzot's proof from Tosafot, explaining that Tosafot may simply have found it strained to interpret "fled" as merely absent. Nevertheless, even Tosafot Rid's rule is limited. In the maaser sheni case the seller, if present, would prefer to undo the mistaken sale, return the purchaser's money, and receive other valid payment for the animal. Thus the maaser money is effectively ready to return. This differs from the cases Ketzot cites. Chatam Sofer therefore concludes that Tosafot Rid is correct in its own context, but in the case of an innocent purchaser from a thief the original owner cannot consecrate the object while it remains in the purchaser's possession.
