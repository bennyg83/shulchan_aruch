For example, a ewe that was pregnant, etc. I do not understand what distinction this makes, for in either case it ultimately gave birth. The Raavad merely suggested that one could have explained payment for offspring and fleece even where the animal stood ready to give birth, although he later withdrew this in light of the Rambam's own ruling in the laws of robbery. The Rambam there writes that even if the animal became pregnant or laden while in the robber's possession, so long as the owners did not despair and the stolen object itself did not change, it remains the owners' property. The Mechaber rules likewise in siman 362. It therefore appears that also in the laws of theft the Rambam means even where it became pregnant or laden in the thief's possession, as the plain wording and the Tur indicate. The Maggid Mishneh and Mechaber require study.

Some say that even an improvement arising by itself, etc. The Rama's wording is imprecise. The Maggid Mishneh and those who agree with him do not dispute the Rambam regarding every automatic improvement. Their dispute is whether birth and shearing constitute a legal change. The Rambam holds that they do not, and therefore despair is also required. They hold that they do constitute change. Certainly, an ordinary automatic increase that leaves the object unchanged belongs to the owner before despair, for there is no act by which the thief could acquire it. The Rama must mean that even an automatic improvement such as birth or shearing is considered a change and belongs to the thief even before despair. Thus Ir Shushan correctly explains that a pregnant ewe that gives birth or a laden sheep that is shorn undergoes a substantial physical change. The Raavad and Rosh dispute the Rambam only on this classification. We have not found that they dispute his broader rule that automatic improvement without change is acquired only after despair. Indeed, the Raavad's glosses indicate agreement. The Bach and Sema's broader understanding is therefore incorrect.
And all the more so if he stole a ewe and it became pregnant, etc. The Sema objects that the Rama says 'all the more so,' while the Tur says 'not only that.' He did not read the Tur carefully. The Tur too treats pregnancy in the thief's possession as the stronger case. His phrase 'not only that' introduces the further novelty that even if the owner demanded the animal before it gave birth, the thief still acquires the offspring. The logical hierarchy remains exactly as the Rama states.
