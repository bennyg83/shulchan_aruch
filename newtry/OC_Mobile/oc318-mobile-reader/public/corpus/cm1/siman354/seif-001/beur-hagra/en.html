If it was, etc. The Rambam's approach is that birth and shearing are not themselves an acquisition by change, but fall under the law of improvement enacted for penitents. He understands that the stolen object itself always returns, while payment for an automatic improvement belongs to the robber only after the owner's despair, and an improvement produced by expenditure is treated differently. He was compelled to distinguish automatic improvement after despair from the sugya in Bava Metzia 14b, which indicates that the robbed owner receives the improvement. Rashi and Tosafos there gave other explanations, but the Rif explains that case as automatic improvement and distinguishes it from Rava's rule in Bava Kamma 95a regarding one who robbed, improved, and sold. The distinction is that land is not subject to despair. This also answers the Maggid Mishneh's objection from the Bava Kamma passage, because that passage concerns improvement through expenditure. The same resolves the case of unauthorized use of a deposit, where the depositor is unaware and there is certainly no despair. The Rosh and Tosafos disagree with the Rambam and distinguish movable property from land. The Gemara's discussion of the reason for the rule likewise fits the Rambam: according to Rabbi Yehudah, since the stolen object itself returns and the robber nevertheless receives the improvement, the basis is not acquisition through change but the enactment concerning improvement. Thus the Rambam has three categories: where the object undergoes a true change, even before despair it belongs to the robber and he pays its value at the time of the robbery; where it merely appreciates in price, even after despair the gain belongs to the robbed owner; where the substance improves, the object itself returns to the owner, but improvement caused by expenditure belongs to the robber, while automatic improvement belongs to the robber only after despair. Shearing and offspring are included in improvement under this approach.
(Collected note) If it was, etc. The Rambam holds that birth and shearing are not a change according to Rabbi Yehudah, for the phrase “the stolen object returns as it is” indicates that the object itself returns. It cannot be because of a penalty, since Rabbi Yehudah holds that the robber receives the improvement. Rather, it is the enactment for penitents, and in automatic improvement it applies only after despair. Improvement through expenditure is different, as in Rava's rule concerning one who robbed and improved the object. Rabbenu Tam, by contrast, holds that the robber acquires through change and pays only the original value. The Rosh explains that although immediately before the change the object itself would have been returned, once the change occurs he pays only its value at the time of the robbery, as in every acquisition by change.

Rabbenu Yerucham writes that a robber acquires through a reversible change of form on a rabbinic level, even though one who takes a silver ingot and makes it into a coin does not acquire, because that is an inferior change. Some say, etc. They hold that there is no distinction between before despair and after despair. Certainly they also disagree concerning shearing and offspring, which they regard as an acquisition through change, as Rashi, Tosafos, and the Rosh explain. The Bach therefore correctly writes, unlike the Shach, that there are authorities who dispute the distinction concerning improvement before despair; the Rosh in Bava Metzia explicitly needs a separate distinction concerning land, where despair does not apply.

(Collected note) Some say. The Rama's language is highly obscure because it is taken from the Tur. Tosafos hold that where an animal was pregnant when robbed and later gave birth, this is an irreversible change and according to all opinions the robber acquires both body and improvement, paying only the value at the time of the robbery. If it became pregnant in his possession, whether or not it later gave birth, the change is reversible, but Tosafos hold that a reversible change acquires rabbinically. According to Ri this is because of the enactment for penitents, while according to Rabbenu Tam the change itself is sufficient. Rav Ashi does not dispute Abaye's rule; he merely explains the Mishnah as involving a Torah-level change. The Rif, Rambam, and Rosh, however, rule that a reversible change does not acquire even rabbinically, though the robber still receives the improvement. The Rosh cites the Tosafos analysis in explaining the sugya but does not adopt every element of it as final law. His ruling, and the Tur's, is that shearing and birth can constitute a complete, irreversible change, whereas a merely reversible change leaves the body with the robbed owner while the robber receives the improvement. Thus the apparent contradiction among the Tur's rulings is resolved: the offspring and wool themselves belong to the robber when the relevant irreversible change occurred, but in a merely reversible change the original body returns to its owner.
