Seif 6: He gave money to his agent. The Gemara relates that a woman gave money to a man to buy land for her, and he bought it without a warranty. Rav Nachman told him, “I sent you to improve matters, not to impair them. Buy it from the seller without warranty and then sell it to her with your own warranty.” The Rosh infers from the need for a second purchase that the agent had informed the seller he was buying for the woman. Had he bought without disclosure, he would have acquired for himself and no second purchase would be needed. Since he disclosed the agency, the woman was the purchaser. Because ordinary land is bought with warranty, his express purchase without warranty was a deliberate deviation, the agency was void, and the original sale to the woman did not take effect. The agent therefore had to buy anew. He may instead return her money. Only if neither he nor the seller can return it must he accept personal warranty. The principal need not prove that she appointed him without authorizing such a deviation. The presumption that an agent may occasionally err applies to ordinary mistakes, because otherwise no one would serve as agent. Here, however, everyone knows that she sent him to buy with warranty, since a person does not cast away money for nothing. The agent deliberately added the term “without warranty,” whereas a silent sale would carry warranty. An agent is not presumed authorized to add harmful terms intentionally. Although the Sema requires the principal to prove this as well, the Rosh's view appears as stated. According to the Raavad, one may understand the Ran's view that the agent must return the woman's money because she sent him to improve matters. If she wants the land, she may require the agent to assume warranty, but she cannot be compelled to accept that solution, because she agreed only to the seller's warranty. The Ran discusses a purchase made without disclosure, while the Rosh reaches the same practical conclusion from general reasoning in such a case. I therefore do not understand why the Beit Yosef treats them as disputing. When the Rosh says that the agent may return her money, but if it is not in his possession, he means that it is also not in the seller's possession. Otherwise the agent could compel the seller to return it. The Raavad asks why Rav Nachman required the agent to repurchase rather than simply voiding the sale, since an agent who deviates even slightly can be told, “I sent you to improve matters.” The Ran answers in his name that the principal chose not to rescind, and the choice belonged to her. The Ramban distinguishes that Rav Nachman's case involved no disclosure to the seller, so the sale remained valid. On that view, Rav Nachman's instruction to “buy it without warranty” means: retain and confirm your original purchase, not make an entirely new acquisition. Thus, although the Rosh explains the narrative differently, his practical rule agrees with the Rambam: disclosure that the purchase is for the woman determines the matter, while a silent purchase remains effective for the agent.
