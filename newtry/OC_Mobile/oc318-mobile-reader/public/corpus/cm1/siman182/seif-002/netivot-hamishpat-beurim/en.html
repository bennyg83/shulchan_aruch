Although where an agent was appointed to sell, and it is later established that the property truly belonged to the principal, the sale can remain effective even if the agent violated instructions, this is because we presume the agent intended the principal's sale to stand and intended personally to correct the deviation. This can be proved from the buyer-agent case. If an agent appointed to buy deviates from instructions and then seeks to keep the purchase for himself while returning the principal's money, he cannot do so. He must give the purchase to the principal and correct the deviation, as stated in seif 6. The Sages therefore assessed the agent's intent as being to perform the principal's agency and personally repair any deficiency, so as not to become a robber. Accordingly, even where the principal proves that the agent violated instructions, the sale can remain effective so long as the agent is able to cure the defect. If the agent cannot cure it, one might initially think the principal can void the sale, because the principal consented only to the terms he specified. An agent who knowingly departed from those terms and cannot make the principal whole resembles one who attempted to sell property not his own. Similarly, where a buyer's agent deviates from instructions, if the agent cannot cure the defect and the principal proves that the money was his, the transaction may resemble kiddushin made with stolen money. If the only acquisition was the money itself, the principal could recover his money and the purchase would be void. If, however, another independent kinyan occurred, such as meshichah, the seller's transaction with the agent remains effective, and the seller may compel the agent to provide substitute money, while the agent must reimburse the principal. Where the agent is able to repair the deviation, however, even a money-only acquisition remains effective despite proof that the agent violated instructions. We presume the agent intended to complete the principal's mission and correct the defect rather than become a robber. Nevertheless, as I will explain in siman 187, there are grounds to hold that where the principal was not disclosed, the transaction remains effective in all cases. Maharshal on Ketubot 99 writes that where one was appointed to sell, the sale is void when he violated instructions even if he did not reveal that he was an agent. In my view the rule is as stated above.
