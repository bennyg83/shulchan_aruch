Seif 6. Note: I was uncertain where an agent informed the seller for whom he was purchasing, but deviated from his instructions so that the sale was initially void, and afterward the seller agreed to assume the relevant responsibility. Does the transaction revive because the defect has now been cured, or is the agency already void? From Eruvin 75b it appears that even a temporary deviation that can later be corrected invokes the principle, “I sent you to improve matters, not to impair them.” Accordingly, from the moment of sale until the seller later assumes responsibility there was a deviation, and the agency was already void. I also saw in Yad Malachi, citing Ritva, that where agency operates to a person's detriment we do not say that his agent is like himself. This is difficult from Bava Metzia 10a. The Gemara derives acquisition by one's courtyard through agency and asks whether this would create agency for transgression in the case of theft. Why is that necessary? The liability for double payment is itself detrimental. Similarly, the Gemara's case of a man who says to a woman, “Round the hair of this minor for me,” treats her act as agency even though he did not expressly appoint her as an agent and the consequence is detrimental. This suggests that even where formal agency is not required, ordinary language may create agency, and even to one's detriment. The matter requires study. In the same seif I was also uncertain whether one who hires an agent to perform a Torah or rabbinic prohibition must pay the agreed wage. From Bekhorot 59 it appears that he does not. The Gemara's discussion of a kohen entering a beit haperas does not answer simply that the kohen knowingly sinned or was ignorant, which would have resolved the case. This implies that work involving a transgression does not earn a wage, as a rabbinic penalty. The Gemara's continuation shows that this applies even to a rabbinic prohibition. Although the agent thereby loses his wage, he receives nothing. This is a novel ruling and still requires study.
