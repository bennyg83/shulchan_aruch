In my view Rif and Rambam are primary, and their approach resolves numerous difficulties in Ketubot 99. The Gemara asks: if a principal told an agent, "Sell a kor," and he sold half a kor, do we say, "You improved matters for me," or does the principal say, "I do not want numerous documents against me"? The Gemara first attempts proof from the Mishnah in Me'ilah where an agent was given a gold dinar to buy a cloak and bought a cloak for only part of the money and another item with the rest. The Mishnah says the owner commits me'ilah. If partial performance were a deviation, why should the owner be liable? The Gemara answers that the agent obtained goods worth the full amount for a reduced price. It then brings the Mishnah concerning a widow whose ketubah was four hundred zuz and who sold separate parcels worth one hundred, with only the last sale containing overcharge, and says the earlier sales stand. The Gemara ultimately distinguishes small parcels and then records the dispute whether an instruction to sell to "one" means exactly one buyer or allows several. The difficulty is that the initial formulation itself seems contradictory. If the only issue were whether partial sale benefits the principal, then absent benefit it should be a deviation. Yet the alternative formulation suggests that only an actual disadvantage, namely many deeds, makes it a deviation, implying that absent disadvantage the agent would not be treated as deviating. Mishneh LaMelech explains a general dispute in several places: where an agent departs from the principal's wording but the departure is neither beneficial nor harmful, is the wording merely descriptive or strictly limiting? If the change is beneficial, everyone agrees there is no insistence. If the change is harmful, everyone agrees the principal is particular. Accordingly, the Gemara's question is posed according to both views. According to the strict-wording view, perhaps selling gradually is nevertheless a benefit and therefore allowed. According to the descriptive-language view, perhaps the burden of multiple deeds is an actual disadvantage and therefore the principal is particular. The Gemara first resolves the first branch from Me'ilah. There, proliferation of documents is irrelevant, yet partial purchase counts as performance. This proves that partial execution can be considered beneficial. It also fits the general halachah that wording is strict where no benefit exists. The Gemara then turns to the second branch and tests whether multiple deeds are itself a genuine disadvantage. It cites the widow's sales. Ritva explains that if multiple deeds were considered a real disadvantage, a widow who needs to sell everything on one day should not be allowed to divide the sale among multiple deeds. The Mishnah therefore shows that multiple deeds are not regarded as a legal disadvantage. When the Gemara then says "this too indicates," it concludes that the agent who sold only half a kor is nevertheless treated as deviating, because although multiple deeds are not a legal disadvantage, selling half is also not an actual benefit sufficient to override the principal's strict wording. Thus the principal's statement "a kor" is treated as limiting. This explains Rif and Rambam. They rule like Rav Chisda that an instruction "to one" can include several buyers, because multiple deeds are not considered a legal disadvantage. Yet they also rule that if one said "sell a kor" and the agent sold half, the agent deviated. There is no contradiction. The latter rule does not depend on the number of deeds. It depends on the principal's specific quantity instruction, which is controlling where the agent cannot show a genuine benefit. This also explains why they do not distinguish whether the first half was already transferred by a separate document or whether both halves might ultimately be included in one deed. Once "a kor" is treated as an exact instruction, the principal can retract from the partial transaction, just as in siman 200 one can retract even from the last se'ah where the unit price was stated for the entire kor. The halachah thus follows the strict-wording approach. Wherever the agent materially changes the principal's stated instruction, such as selling half when instructed to sell a whole kor, or selling to two when explicitly told "to one," the transaction is void unless the change is shown to be an actual benefit. But if the principal merely said "sell" without specifying one buyer, a sale to several buyers stands, because there is no departure from the instruction and the multiplication of deeds is not itself treated as harm. This also resolves the question from Even HaEzer, siman 103, concerning the widow. A widow selling to satisfy her ketubah is not an ordinary agent subject to the principal's exact language. Therefore, once multiple deeds are shown not to be harmful, her prior sales stand even without limiting the Mishnah to unusually small parcels. The "small parcels" answer was necessary only under the intermediate assumption that multiple deeds might itself be harmful. Accordingly, in my view the halachah follows Rif and Rambam rather than Mechaber's distinction between one deed and several. The decisive factor is not the number of documents, but whether the agent changed an express instruction of the principal. When the principal said "a kor" and the agent sold half, or said "to one" and the agent sold to several, the sale is void because of the change. When the principal spoke without such limitation, the sale stands.
