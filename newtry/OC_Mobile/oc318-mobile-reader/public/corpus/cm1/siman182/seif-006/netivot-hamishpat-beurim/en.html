Some say this applies only when the agent lacks funds. See Shach and Taz, who write that the opinions do not actually disagree. Taz adds that the case also assumes the seller cannot return the purchase money. Otherwise, because the transaction is void, the agent could force the seller to return the money. This requires explanation. If the sale is void and the seller owes the agent money but cannot pay, why does the Gemara advise the agent to take the field without warranty and transfer it to the principal with his own warranty? He should instead have the field collected through beit din as payment of the seller's debt, in which case ordinary collection rules would impose the appropriate warranty on the seller. Moreover, why does the Gemara give this elaborate advice at all? The agent cannot force the principal to accept that field with the agent's warranty, and the principal cannot force the agent to satisfy the debt specifically with that field. Like any debtor, the agent should be able to pay with other property at appraised value. It therefore appears that the agent may satisfy the principal only with cash or with this particular field accompanied by the agent's warranty, but not with unrelated property at beit din appraisal. Rama's wording supports this. He says that the agent "does not have the money to return," rather than merely that he cannot pay. The reason is that we do not presume the agent to have intended robbery. We presume he intended from the outset to correct his deviation in a legally effective way. Because he was disclosed as agent, one valid way to cure the problem is simply to return the principal's money. But if he lacks cash and the principal is willing to accept the field with the agent's warranty, the agent must deliver that field with warranty. That too corrects the deviation. He cannot instead give unrelated appraised property, because we do not presume that his original intent involved an impermissible appropriation of the principal's money followed by forced substitution of other assets. This also answers why he cannot simply compel the seller to return the money through collection proceedings. The case can be one where the field with warranty is worth more than the amount of cash advanced. If beit din collected the field from the seller for the agent's debt, only value equal to the debt would be transferred, leaving the agent unable to satisfy the principal's demand for the entire field with warranty. The agent therefore purchases the field from the seller without warranty and transfers the whole field to the principal with his own warranty. Alternatively, the seller may have movable property with which he could discharge his debt, while the agent's obligation to the principal is specifically cash or this field. In that case too the agent must buy the field and personally assume the warranty if he has no cash.
