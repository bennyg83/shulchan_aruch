This applies specifically where the principal is willing to accept reimbursement, etc. Because I saw confusion here, I must explain at some length. The Gemara relates that a woman gave an agent money to purchase land for her. He bought it without warranty. Rav Nachman ruled: Go purchase it from the seller without warranty, and then sell it to the woman with warranty. The Rosh explains that at first sight it appears the agent told the seller he was buying for the woman. He concludes, however, that the agent expressly stated that he purchased for her. Since he deviated from his agency, there was no purchase on her behalf. The agent could return her money, but the case concerns one who lacked funds to do so; therefore he had to assume warranty liability. Ran, in HaIsh Mekadesh, asks why this case is not simply a void transaction. He answers that the agent purchased without disclosing the agency, so the seller dealt only with him. His later claim that he bought for the woman is irrelevant to the seller. The agent must therefore pay the seller, while the woman may say, 'Return my money.' Rav Nachman's ruling means that if she wants the land, the agent must assume warranty. Apart from such a case, if he failed to carry out the agency, the transaction is void as to the principal. It appears to me that the Rosh and Ran do not disagree in law, but only in explaining the Gemara. Had the Rosh been able to interpret the case as an undisclosed purchase, he would have explained it like Ran and would not have been troubled by the passage in Kiddushin. An undisclosed purchase is different. If the woman wants the land, the agent must convey it to her with warranty even if he has funds, because the purchase remains valid toward the seller. The Rosh, however, proves that the Gemara cannot be speaking of an undisclosed purchase. He therefore explains that the agent expressly identified the woman, which raises Ran's question why the purchase is not void. The Rosh answers that the agent lacks money to reimburse her; if he had money, he could simply return it. Ran did not understand the Gemara to be limited to an insolvent agent, so he explains it as an undisclosed purchase where the woman wants the land. If the agency had been disclosed, then even if she wanted the land, the agent could choose to return her money. This reconciles the Rosh and Ran. According to this, Rama's words are imprecise. He first writes that the law applies specifically where the principal is willing to accept reimbursement. That must refer only to an undisclosed agency, for where the agency was disclosed, the agent may cancel his action and return the money even if the principal is willing to accept the land. Ran clearly proves this. Rama then writes that some say the rule applies only where the agent lacks funds, but if he wishes to return the money he may do so. These are the Rosh's words, and the Rosh said this only where the agency was disclosed. Where it was undisclosed, since the purchase is binding toward the seller, it is likewise binding toward the principal if the principal wants it, and the agent must convey it with warranty, as I explained. Examine carefully. This too was written in my youth.
