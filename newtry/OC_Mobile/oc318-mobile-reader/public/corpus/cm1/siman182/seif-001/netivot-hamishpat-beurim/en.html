Even for a transgression. See Shach, seif katan 1, who says Mechaber did not need to cite the law of a courtyard because its practical relevance would be only double payment. This is difficult, because there are many other consequences to being legally treated as a thief, including liability for ones and acquisition through change. Shach also questions Nimukei Yosef, who writes that if the principal sends a minor who has no independent legal agency, the principal can be liable, even though a minor ordinarily cannot serve as an agent. The same question could have been asked against Tosafot in Shevuot, who infer from the wording of a baraita that one who tells a minor or a non Jew to cut another's hair can be liable. Evidently, agency for a transgression follows different principles. In me'ilah, slaughter and sale, and shelichut yad, Scripture expressly includes agency for transgression. Liability there can arise even where the act was carried out through a minor. These cases are not learned from "you also," the source for ordinary agency, and therefore they do not require the agent to have the same legal status as the principal. The qualification of "you also" applies only to forms of agency derived from terumah. On this basis, where the principal's liability for a transgression is learned from the law of a courtyard, such as where the agent is not himself subject to the prohibition according to Ravina, or where the object is placed against his will according to Rav Sama, the principal can be liable even if the act is performed through a minor, mentally incompetent person, deaf mute, or non Jew. Ritva in Kiddushin writes that the rule that there is no agency for a transgression is not fundamentally based on "the words of the Master versus the words of the student." Rather, ordinary agency learned from terumah establishes that the legal act is effective, but does not automatically impose punishment upon the principal as though he personally violated a prohibition involving an act. This explains why many poskim hold that the underlying transaction remains effective even when accomplished through an agent for a transgression, while the principal is exempt from punishment. Thus effectiveness of the act and punishment of the principal are separate issues. Liability for punishment in the exceptional cases comes from independent verses, and where those verses apply, the principal can be liable even through someone who lacks ordinary agency status. This also resolves the case of one who sends fire. Shach apparently overlooked Ritva, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, who explains that the exemption there concerns one who did not instruct the minor to cause damage; if he expressly told him to damage, the principal can be liable. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 10, however, support Shach's approach, because they understand the rule through the reason that the agent should obey Hashem rather than the principal. Their position is consistent with that premise. This also explains Shach's first point. For legal effectiveness of the act itself, ordinary agency principles can still apply even when the act is a transgression, so it was unnecessary to derive from a courtyard that theft committed through an agent takes effect. The courtyard derivation is needed for imposing the punitive consequence on the principal, such as double payment. Nevertheless, if so, where one appoints an agent to steal, the principal should acquire the stolen property sufficiently to become liable for ones, because the legal act is effective. We must therefore say that liability for ones is itself included in the punitive liability imposed on a thief and does not attach to the principal unless the exceptional agency-for-transgression rule applies.
And all his acts are effective. This applies specifically where the act is performed through someone capable of ordinary agency. If done through a non Jew, minor, deaf mute, or mentally incompetent person, who are outside ordinary agency law, a sale or purchase is not effective for the principal, whether he is seller or buyer, as explained in siman 188 and derived from the verse requiring an agent comparable to the principal. Nevertheless, in terumah there is a distinction. If one merely tells a minor to separate terumah and the minor designates it, the designation is ineffective. But if the owner instructs the minor to set aside produce and the owner himself declares in advance, "Whatever this minor separates and calls terumah shall be terumah for the remaining produce," the designation can be effective. Terumah can take effect through thought. If one says, "When the rains fall, this shall be terumah for that," it is effective once the condition occurs. The same applies where he says that whatever the minor or even an animal separates shall become terumah when separated. There is no problem of bereirah where the effect is expressly deferred until the future event becomes known. Likewise in sale and purchase through a person incapable of agency. If a buyer's agent who lacks agency capacity performs meshichah on the buyer's behalf, nothing is acquired. Even if that person later hands the item to the buyer and the buyer performs meshichah privately, the act may still be ineffective because the seller never authorized the buyer himself to acquire through that meshichah. But if the seller gives the object to a minor or non Jew merely to carry it to the buyer, and by that messenger sends the instruction, "Go, perform meshichah and acquire," the buyer's own meshichah is effective. The messenger is only performing a physical delivery, like a trained animal. Even in the law of gittin there are circumstances where a non Jew may merely carry the document while the operative instruction comes from the Jewish principal. All the more so in monetary acquisition. The seller must, however, use language that itself effects acquisition, such as "Go, perform meshichah and acquire." If he says merely, "Perform meshichah and you will acquire," which can imply a future rather than present transfer, the language may be insufficient. Likewise, if a buyer gives money to a messenger incapable of agency merely to deliver the purchase money to the seller, the buyer can acquire land with that money. The messenger is only conveying the physical funds.
He cannot retract. The same applies if he says, "Buy ordinary merchandise with your own money and I will join with you," even without identifying a particular commodity. As soon as the agent purchases, a partnership arises. If, however, he buys an unusually large amount beyond ordinary commercial expectations, so that the principal plainly did not contemplate advancing so much money, the agent cannot compel him to participate, similar to Rama's rule in seif 3. See Sema, who explains that the party who cannot retract is the principal. At first glance this is difficult. Before the agent buys, the principal can certainly cancel the agency, because no money has yet been delivered. After the agent purchases in the principal's name, the agent likewise cannot retract, for the principal has already acquired, as explained in siman 183. Sema's intent appears to concern only cancellation outside the other's presence. An agent can revoke his agency before purchase by declaring before witnesses, even outside the principal's presence, that he is no longer acting as agent and will buy for himself. But if the principal privately cancels the agency before witnesses without informing the agent, and the agent, unaware of the cancellation, then purchases with his own money in the principal's name pursuant to the original instruction, the principal must reimburse him. The agent expended money on the principal's word. This resembles one who says, "Give one hundred to so and so," or, "Throw one hundred into the sea," and thereby becomes liable under guarantor principles.
