This applies specifically where the agent is personally subject to the prohibition, etc. The Shulchan Aruch and Sema cite Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 5 of the Laws of Agency. This is an error, for nothing of the sort appears there; it should read chapter 2, as in Darkei Moshe and in Hagahot Maimoniyot at the beginning of that chapter. Nevertheless, Rama's ruling requires study. One who examines Hagahot Maimoniyot will see that it may hold that the law follows Rav Sama, under whom everything depends upon whether the agent acts voluntarily or is compelled. It concludes that because the agent did not know the animal was stolen, he is comparable to a courtyard into which the property is placed against its will, even though an ordinary person could choose whether to act, in accordance with R. Yochanan. Its opening statement, that where the agent is himself subject to liability the principal is not liable, merely records the Gemara's ruling according to Ravina. Likewise, at the end of siman 348 Rama writes that where the agent is not subject to the prohibition, some say the principal is liable. This follows Darkei Moshe, which cites the Mordechai in the name of Maharam: where the agent is not personally liable, the principal is liable; if one told another, 'Take for me an ox from so-and-so's house, for it is mine,' and it was later discovered not to be his and that he intended theft, the principal becomes liable for the ox when the agent draws it into his possession, because the agent did not know it was stolen. Darkei Moshe cites no dissent. In my humble opinion, however, the Mordechai itself rules according to Rav Sama, for it concludes that because the agent did not know it was stolen, he is like a courtyard into which property is placed involuntarily, although a person could choose whether to act. Its opening words, 'where the agent is not liable, the principal is liable,' merely state the Gemara's formulation according to Ravina. This is even clearer from the Mordechai itself, which marks that language as a citation from the Gemara. Rama understood it as Maharam's own ruling because the immediately preceding law begins, 'From here Rabbeinu Meir ruled.' In truth, Maharam is not mentioned in this law at all. Hagahot Maimoniyot plainly derives from the Mordechai, and both intend the same ruling. Proof appears from Agudah, which cites R. Yitzchak that because the custodian does not know the object is stolen, he is like a courtyard into which it is placed against his will, and the principal is liable. Most of Agudah is drawn from the Mordechai. Rashba, at the end of siman 571, also speaks according to Rav Sama, implying that the law follows him. I later saw that the Rosh, at the beginning of Bava Metzia, cites Ramah as ruling like Rav Sama, because Ravina himself accepted his distinction: there is no agency for a transgression unless the instrument is a courtyard into which the object is placed against its will. The Rosh clearly agrees. Mahari Weil, siman 28, rules this way without qualification, as do Ran and Nimukei Yosef. This also appears to be Rif's view at the beginning of HaIsh Mekadesh. Rif states generally that agency does not apply to a transgression. Had he followed Ravina, he should have stated that agency does apply where the agent is not personally subject to liability. Rather, Rif follows Rav Sama, and the only exception is a courtyard. He omitted that exception because it is relevant chiefly to double payment, which is not practiced, and because liability through one's courtyard is obvious where the object is placed there against its will. The Tur also appears to rule this way, and this is the primary law. R. Yerucham, at the end of Netiv 28, reads that only a courtyard creates agency for a transgression, because an ordinary agent who is personally liable exempts the principal, but a courtyard is not personally liable. In my opinion, his text contains a scribal error. Since R. Yerucham generally follows the Rosh, he cannot here oppose the Rosh without noting it. His words should read: an agent who can choose whether to act exempts the principal, but a courtyard, which cannot choose, obligates the principal. This is also proved by his opening words that agency for a transgression exists only through a courtyard. Had he followed Ravina, many other cases would exist, such as a priest who tells an Israelite to betroth a divorcee for him, or a man who tells a woman to shave the sidelocks of a minor. Under Ravina the principal is liable; under Rav Sama he is exempt. Since R. Yerucham says the exception is only a courtyard, he follows Rav Sama. Even were his words retained literally, the practical ruling remains as I wrote. Although Ramah, the Rosh, and their colleagues inferred Rav Sama's authority from the Gemara's phrase that Rav Sama spoke before Ravina, implying that Ravina accepted his statement, our printed Gemara lacks that wording. The printed text is evidently abbreviated, for an ancient parchment manuscript contains the reading of the Rosh and Ran, and that is the correct text. Nimukei Yosef writes that when the agent is a minor with no legal capacity at all, the principal is liable. This appears entirely incorrect. Although such a minor lacks understanding, the established rule is that a minor cannot serve as an agent. He is unlike a courtyard, which is the principal's domain and therefore functions as his legal instrument. A minor does not. A minor without legal capacity is certainly no better than a mentally incompetent person, yet the Mishna teaches that one who sends fire through a deaf-mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is exempt, whereas if he sends it through a competent person, that person is liable. The Gemara explains that the competent person is liable because there is no agency for a transgression. Why then is the sender exempt when using a mentally incompetent person? Necessarily, because such a person lacks agency. The same applies to a minor without legal capacity. Tosafot and Agudah explicitly write that the sender is exempt when using these persons because they are not legally capable of agency. Rav Sama is nowhere shown to disagree with Ravina on this point. Moreover, the Mishna's term 'minor' includes every minor, whether or not he has some legal capacity, and nevertheless the sender is exempt. This is the primary law. See below, siman 32, seif 2, regarding liability before Heaven where there is no agency for a transgression, and siman 28, seif 1, citing Maharshal that even where one is liable before Heaven, seizure is ineffective and the property is recovered. Rivash, siman 392, likewise writes that seizure is ineffective where the claimant has no enforceable right and there is only liability before Heaven.
