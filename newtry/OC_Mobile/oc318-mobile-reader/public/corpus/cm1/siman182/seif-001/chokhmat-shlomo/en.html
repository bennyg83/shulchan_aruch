Seif 1, gloss: In every matter a person's agent is like himself, except for a transgression. Note: I record here several points regarding whether the halacha follows Ravina or Rav Sama. Elsewhere I proved from Rashi in Sanhedrin that Rashi rules like Rav Sama, making the matter depend on whether the act itself is one the agent can perform, rather than whether the agent is personally subject to the prohibition. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if one appointed an agent to give a divorce in a certain manner and the agent delivered it forcibly, all agree it is effective. In a responsum I argued the opposite, that all agree it is ineffective and the get is void by Torah law. I also proposed a novel rule: just as an entity that ordinarily lacks agency can nevertheless function as one's domain when it acts involuntarily, so too where agency is generally impossible because the principal himself cannot perform the act, or because the agent is a non-Jew, if the principal compels the agent and the agent is forced to act, the act can acquire the character of agency. I further distinguished between a principal who cannot perform the act and an agent disqualified from agency. Where the deficiency lies with the principal, one who cannot do an act himself cannot normally appoint an unpaid agent, but he may be able to hire someone to act for him. Where the deficiency lies with the agent, such as a non-Jew or agency for a transgression, payment does not cure the defect. Ibid. I have elsewhere cited Machaneh Ephraim's view that a non-Jew can act as an agent when hired. A proof may be brought from Zevachim 116b, where Rabbi Yose says that it is forbidden to assist non-Jews and to perform their agency. If there were never agency for a non-Jew, the statement that one may not perform their agency would be obvious. This supports the distinction where the agent is hired.
