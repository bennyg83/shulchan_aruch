Except for a matter involving a transgression. Shaar Mishpat writes that even if the principal hired the agent to commit the transgression, we nevertheless apply the rule that there is no agency for a transgression. With regard to one who seizes property for a creditor where the seizure harms other creditors, the appointment of an agent does not help, yet if the person is hired the seizure is effective because the hand of a worker is like the hand of the employer, as the Shach writes at the beginning of siman 105. That principle does not apply, however, to remove the rule of no agency for a transgression. The same applies to all who are legally incapable of agency, such as a non-Jew, a minor, or an incompetent person. Even if hired, the act does not become the principal's act through the rule that the hand of a worker is like the hand of the employer. See there.
Because the agent is himself subject to liability. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Noda BiYehuda, Even HaEzer 75 and the responsa that follow it. Responsa Shevut Yaakov I:164 writes that the Shach's position is not entirely clear. The Shach himself rules in Yoreh Deah 160:22 that when the agent is not subject to liability for the particular prohibition, the sender is liable, citing Mordechai. This also appears from Tosafot, Bava Kamma 79a, from the Agudah in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, from Mordechai there, Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sheluchin, and from Rabbeinu Yerucham as cited by the Shach here. This also appears to be Maharshal's view in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma 7:33, and the Sema in siman 292, seif katan 10. In Shevut Yaakov II:165 he discusses a Jew known to be fraudulent who owned merchandise with a serious hidden defect. He was afraid to sell it himself, so he gave it to a non-Jewish acquaintance. Another Jew innocently purchased it from the non-Jew. After discovering the defect, the buyer could not recover his money from the non-Jew, and investigation revealed that the fraudulent Jew had delivered the merchandise to the non-Jew as his agent to sell it to a Jew. Must the first Jew reimburse the purchaser? Shevut Yaakov answers that had a Jew appointed another Jew to make such a fraudulent sale, the purchaser would have no monetary claim against the sender because there is no agency for a transgression. Here, however, the agent was a non-Jew, who is not subject to the Torah law of commercial onaah. The Rama at the beginning of siman 182 rules that where the agent is not personally subject to liability, the sender is liable. Shevut Yaakov states that he had already ruled in I:164 that this is the primary halachah, against those who disagree. Moreover, even according to the dissenters, if the agent is habitually engaged in such misconduct we do not apply the rule of no agency for a transgression, as explained in siman 388:15. All this applies if the Jew expressly instructed the non-Jew to sell the defective merchandise to a Jew. If he merely gave it to him to sell to someone, the Jew cannot be charged, because his conduct is only indirect causation. He can say that he expected the non-Jew to sell it to someone for whom the defect would not be considered significant. He is believed regarding this upon oath. See there, and see Nachalat Tzvi on this matter.
And all his acts remain valid. Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat 18, discusses a case in which Reuven gave an object to Shimon to sell for twenty-seven gold coins. Shimon went to the market and sold it to Levi for exactly that amount, told Levi that the object belonged to Reuven, received the money from Levi, and delivered the object to him. It was later established by witnesses that before Shimon's sale to Levi had been completed, Reuven himself had already sold that same object, while it was still in Shimon's possession, to Yehudah, by kinyan sudar or by situmta. Reuven's own sale plainly amounted to revocation of Shimon's agency. The scholars therefore questioned whether such revocation is effective, in which case Shimon's later sale to Levi is void and Levi must return the object to Yehudah, or whether the revocation is ineffective as against Levi, so that Shimon's act remains valid and Reuven's earlier sale cannot defeat it. The responsum rules that if Levi did not know of the revocation and Reuven's retraction, Levi need not return the purchased object to Yehudah. Although Reuven could revoke Shimon's authority, revocation is ineffective if he notified neither Levi nor Shimon, analogous to the rule in siman 122:3. According to the Shach, seif katan 11, in this case the revocation would be ineffective even if Shimon the agent knew of it, so long as Levi did not know. See there.
