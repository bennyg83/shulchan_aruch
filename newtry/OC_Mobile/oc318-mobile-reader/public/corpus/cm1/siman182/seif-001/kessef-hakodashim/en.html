Buy for me. One tells an agent to purchase for him, and the agent buys from a non-Jew, after which the item is stolen before the principal is informed that it was purchased. There is room to compare Tosafot in Bava Metzia 71b, who explain that because a Jew cannot act as the non-Jew's agent, the Jewish intermediary does not necessarily acquire from the non-Jew directly for another Jew. Perhaps, however, when the principal expressly appointed the Jew as his agent, that agency governs the Jewish side of the acquisition even if the seller turns out to be a non-Jew, especially when at the time of appointment the principal did not know from whom the agent would buy. Just as the agency would operate if the seller were Jewish, perhaps it remains operative here. Alternatively, the principal may become obligated under the law of guaranty because the agent paid the purchase money at his direction. If so, the principal's obligation may arise immediately when the agent buys for him, even if the agent later negligently loses the item. According to the Tosafot principle, if no one acquired the object at the moment of sale it might effectively be ownerless, in which case ordinary custodial liability would be difficult to apply. Yet guarantor liability may still be uncertain because such liability ordinarily depends on the benefit of reliance, whereas here the agent received merchandise worth the money he paid, especially where the item has an obvious market value and could immediately be resold for the full price. The effectiveness of the agency and acquisition may therefore depend on whether this is a readily available, specifically identified purchase, as discussed by Bach. This requires study.
