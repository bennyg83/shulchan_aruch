The rule that "a person's agent is like himself" does not mean that agency can perform every mitzvah that rests upon a person's body. Tosafot Rid asks why, if agency is so broad, one cannot tell another person to sit in a sukkah or put on tefillin in his place. He answers that bodily mitzvot must be performed by the person obligated. Agency works for divorce, kiddushin, terumah, and the Paschal offering because the legal act can be performed through another while the resulting status or sacrifice belongs to the principal. The deeper distinction is that agency applies to acts. The principal's agent can perform an act and the act is legally attributed to the principal. It does not make the agent's body into the principal's body. Rosh makes this distinction in Nedarim concerning an agent appointed to annul a wife's vows. Hearing is a passive fact, not an act susceptible to agency. The agent's hearing is not treated as the husband's hearing. Accordingly, if an agent places tefillin on his own head, the placement act can conceptually be attributed to the principal, but the tefillin are still physically upon the agent's head. The agent's body is not the principal's body. The mitzvah therefore remains unfulfilled. The same is true of sukkah and tzitzit. Divorce, kiddushin, sacrifice, and even transgression according to the opinion recognizing agency for transgression concern legal acts. Giving the get, delivering kiddushin, slaughtering the offering, or performing the prohibited act can be treated as the principal's act. Once that act is legally attributed to him, nothing further requires the principal's own body. This is the clear boundary of the doctrine.
Noda BiYehudah ruled that a get delivered by an agent against the wife's will is wholly ineffective because, where there is no agency for a transgression, the agency itself is void, and delivering against her will violates the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom. In my view that is correct only if the husband appointed the agent specifically to divorce her against her will. If he appointed him simply to deliver a get, and the agent on his own initiative forced it upon her, the principal agency for divorce remains valid. The wrongful compulsion is the agent's independent transgression. Mishneh LaMelech makes the same distinction regarding slaughter. If one appoints an agent specifically to slaughter on Shabbat, the sinful agency fails. If he appoints him simply to slaughter and the agent chooses to do it on Shabbat, the original agency to slaughter is not thereby annulled. This distinction also resolves a major difficulty from the accepted rule that an agent for divorce can appoint another agent, even through many successive agents. By contrast, an agent for kiddushin generally cannot appoint another because "words are not transmitted to an agent." Mordechai explains that a divorce agent receives the get itself and can effect divorce even against the woman's will, so his agency is substantive rather than mere words. Today, after the ban of Rabbeinu Gershom, if any forced delivery were simply void because of agency for transgression, divorce agency would become like kiddushin and the traditional rule permitting successive agents would be undermined. Once we distinguish ordinary divorce agency from a special appointment to force the wife, the rule remains coherent. A further debate concerns whether one who receives a bill of emancipation for a slave is agent of the slave or also of the master. Noda BiYehudah dismissed a proof from the Mishnah, "Give this bill of emancipation to my slave," arguing that the recipient is merely the slave's agent for acquisition. But Tosafot in Bava Metzia concerning a Jew receiving money from a gentile shows that where the giver has not abandoned the property, a recipient who acquires for another can also require legal power from the giver to transfer the giver's property. If the giver is a gentile, the lack of agency creates a problem. Thus the acquisition agent can in a meaningful sense stand on both sides of the transfer. The same reasoning supports the proof from women accepting kiddushin for one another with produce of Shemittah. Still, the core rule that agency for a transgression can invalidate the principal act remains sound where the transgression was built into the appointment itself. The emancipation case does not refute it because a master who says, "Give this emancipation document to my slave," is not presumed to violate the positive command concerning retaining Canaanite slaves gratuitously. Rashba explains that when a master directs emancipation we presume there is some benefit or debt owed to the slave, making the emancipation analogous to a compensated transfer rather than a gratuitous violation. That is also why "give" can operate like "acquire" in the emancipation context. Thus no agency for a transgression problem necessarily arises.
