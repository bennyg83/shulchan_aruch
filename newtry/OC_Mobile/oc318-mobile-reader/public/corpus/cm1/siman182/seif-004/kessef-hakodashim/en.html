Several days later, before leaving town, he appointed another person generally to sell the oxen. The agent sold eight, allowing the buyers to choose the eight they preferred, at four hundred apiece. When the owner learned of the sale he objected. It appears that the earlier discussion constituted a clear disclosure that the agency was only to sell all the oxen, or at least not to sell selected superior animals for four hundred each. Since only a few days had passed and the market had not changed, the owner's prior statement is significant. Still, the matter requires study because the agent said that he understood the owner to mean that he should sell as best he could. The owner denied this and claimed that at appointment he expressly instructed him not to accept less than four hundred for each of all eleven. I ruled that the owner should swear that his account was correct. Because the matter came before me on Purim, when it was impractical to convene judges and an oath is not administered on the day of judgment, this was not intended as an irrevocable final ruling. When the parties complete the matter, it may be examined again. I also said that the oath should include whether the owner's wife customarily handled transactions of this magnitude, because she had agreed to the sale. The agent's own commercial assessment gave some basis for requiring the oath, since he was more familiar with the circumstances.
