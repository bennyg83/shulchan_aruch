Nevertheless, the buyer may retract. At first glance this appears inconsistent with siman 203, where one who says, "A cow and lamb for a kor of wheat," and performs meshichah only on the cow, acquires wheat corresponding to the cow's value, and neither party can retract from that part. The rule is learned from siman 192 concerning ten fields where partial payment acquires only the corresponding portion. The distinction is that in those cases both seller and buyer know that the kinyan has taken effect only on part. They therefore intend each partial acquisition to stand independently. Here, by contrast, the seller knows he can transfer only one half, while the buyer does not know this and performs a kinyan intended to acquire the whole kor. Because the buyer expected an entire purchase, he may say, "I want the complete transaction," while the seller, who knew the limitation, cannot use that argument. Nimukei Yosef in Yesh Nochalin makes the same distinction concerning one who gives property to his son from today and after death, and the father later sells the land without qualification. The buyer, who did not know the limitation, may void the transaction, while the seller may not. Mishneh LaMelech questions Rashba, who rules concerning a husband who sells his wife's land without qualification that even the husband can void the transaction, apparently contrary to Nimukei Yosef. This is not difficult. Rashba explains that the husband sold the body and fruits as one integrated sale. Since the body sale is void and the fruits exist only by virtue of the land, the entire transaction falls. This differs from a case where only part of a field is stolen, because one parcel does not derive legally from the other. Even Rashba agrees that if two independent items are sold together and one transfer fails, the other may remain effective, as in "you and the donkey," or where existing property and a davar shelo ba la'olam are transferred together. Rashba goes even further: even if the buyer did not know and the seller cannot complete the missing portion, the existing partial transfer can stand. Ran disagrees and holds that where completion is impossible the buyer may void everything and say, "I need the whole transaction." If the seller can complete the missing portion, however, even Ran agrees the partial acquisition remains. Shulchan Aruch in siman 232 follows Ran. This explains Rambam's rule in siman 216 regarding "land and palm trees." If the seller does not provide the palm trees, the buyer may retract from the land because he did not know the seller lacked palms and can say, "I want the entire purchase." But if the seller later acquires palms and supplies them, the land purchase stands. Taz's objection that failure of the transfer of the future palms should itself invalidate the land is difficult, because we rule that transfer of existing and nonexisting property together acquires the existing half. Likewise here, although only half a kor was transferred, if the principal-seller can supply the remaining half, the transaction can be completed. Siman 232, concerning one who sold one hundred nuts and delivered fewer, follows the same pattern: the seller may later complete the amount; if he cannot, the buyer may void the transaction. Where separate prices were fixed for separate items, they are treated as separate sales, and failure of one does not necessarily void the other. But where everything was included in one global price, the buyer can say that the missing portion was essential to him and refuse appraisal of the remainder. Accordingly, in Taz's case in siman 216, where two synagogue seats were sold together and one was later found not to belong to the seller, if both were included in one price the buyer can void the entire sale rather than accept half at expert appraisal. This assumes the kinyan extended to both seats and failed regarding one only because it was not the seller's. If, however, the buyer performed chazakah only on the seat that did belong to the seller and did not pay for the whole, then even had both seats belonged to the seller the kinyan would have covered only the amount corresponding to payment. The valid seat is therefore acquired and the buyer cannot insist on the whole. Rama in siman 200, seif 7, says that where a seller said "a kor for thirty," he can retract even in the last se'ah because he may say, "I needed the full purchase price." This seems to conflict with the rules just discussed. The distinction is that there the seller's wording itself shows a special insistence on selling the whole kor as one unit. The customary pricing was by se'ah, yet he deliberately priced by the kor. That reveals intent that no partial sale stand. In ordinary cases such as siman 203, there is no comparable indication, and the seller cannot say he needed the whole price. Nevertheless, the stronger view appears to be Rashba's, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 200, that once the buyer has paid for the portion he actually drew, even in a "kor for thirty" sale the seller cannot retract from that portion. Ritva in Shitah Mekubetzet also rules this way. Finally, Ran's conclusion that when part of the field is found stolen the entire purchase may be void should, in my view, be limited. Once the buyer has already used the property, Rashba's view should be followed. A robber-seller cannot himself void the buyer's acquisition merely because the true owner may later dispossess him. Until the true owner actually does so, the buyer can compel the seller to give him possession, because if the seller later acquires title from the owner the original sale becomes effective. The money is therefore purchase money, not a loan. Likewise the buyer cannot demand repayment merely by proving that the field is stolen; he is told to produce an actual dispossession document. Even after dispossession, the buyer's recovery from the seller is ordinarily through warranty, not because the original sale was void. If there was no warranty, he may have no recourse. All the more so, where only part was stolen, the purchase of the valid portion need not be canceled. It is possible that Ran speaks only before the buyer took possession, or follows the view that stolen property creates a fundamental voiding defect.
