This applies specifically where the seller combined the sale of the entire field into one total price, the seller knew the relevant limitation, and the purchaser did not know and assumed that the kinyan took effect on the entire kor. The purchaser may therefore retract and say, "I wanted a complete purchase," since he lacked knowledge. The seller, who knew, cannot retract from the portion on which the kinyan did take effect. But where both seller and purchaser know that the kinyan can take effect only partially, such as siman 192, seif 12, where ten fields in ten different provinces are sold, or siman 203, seif 2, where he says, "a cow and a lamb for a kor of wheat," and both understand that the kinyan applies only to part, neither can retract from the acquired portion. Likewise, if two items were sold with a separate price fixed for each and one was not in the seller's possession, so that the kinyan took effect only on the other, the purchaser cannot say, "I wanted a complete purchase," even if he did not know the first item was unavailable. If two distinct types of property were combined into one total price, such as land and palm trees in siman 216, seif 5, the Rambam and Rashbam disagree. According to the Rambam, if the seller cannot supply the palm trees, the purchaser who did not know may say, "I wanted a complete purchase." According to the Rashbam, the land sale remains valid even if the palm trees cannot be supplied, because they are separate types, and this is so even though the purchaser lacked knowledge. Where the sale concerns one type and the entire transaction was combined into one price, as in siman 232, seif 1, all agree that the purchaser who lacked knowledge can rescind and say, "I wanted a complete purchase." This applies specifically where the seller cannot complete delivery. If the seller is willing and able to complete delivery later with property that did not yet exist at the time of sale, the purchaser cannot necessarily void the kinyan of the portion that did exist. Likewise, if he sold ten fields and one was not in his possession, even if the purchaser did not know this, if the purchaser made chazakah on only one field he cannot void the purchase of that acquired portion, because he knew that the kinyan would initially operate only on part. Where a father gives his property to his sons effective from now and after death, retaining only the produce, and then sells the entire field as though it were fully his while the purchaser does not know the limitation, or where a husband sells his wife's property though he owns only the produce, the Rashba and Nimukei Yosef disagree. According to the Rashba, even the seller can void the transaction despite knowing the facts, because he did not separately sell the land for its produce. He sold the body of the land generally, expecting the produce rights to follow from the sale of the body. Once the sale of the body is void, the sale of produce rights also falls away. Therefore even the knowing seller may rescind. According to Nimukei Yosef, the seller who knew cannot rescind, but the purchaser who did not know can. The rule that the seller cannot retract in the cases above applies only where there was no indication that he needed the entire transaction. Where there is such an indication, for example a sale of a kor for thirty in siman 200, where ordinary practice is to price by the se'ah and his saying "a kor" shows that he needs payment for the entire kor, even the seller may retract. Yet even there the Rashba holds that if he received partial payment corresponding to the portion the purchaser drew into his possession, he cannot retract and cannot say that he needed payment for the entire quantity. If one sold a field and part was later found to have been stolen property, the Ran holds that the purchaser can rescind the entire field because the seller cannot complete delivery. The Rashba holds that the sale remains valid as to the portion not stolen. See the Beurim. If the purchaser already used the property, the principal ruling follows the Rashba, because all authorities agree that when a purchaser used land later discovered to have been stolen, his claim against the seller is not one of voiding the sale but one of warranty. Therefore the sale remains valid as to the valid portion.
