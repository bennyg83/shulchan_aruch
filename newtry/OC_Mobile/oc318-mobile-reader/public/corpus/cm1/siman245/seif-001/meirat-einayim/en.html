Even if a kinyan was made, it is merely a kinyan on words, etc., and therefore the custom is to make new conditions at the time of the wedding. Some ask what the second set of conditions accomplishes that the first did not. If at the wedding they now formulate the obligations as present personal liabilities, which can bind even regarding something not yet in existence, or make a kinyan sudar effective from now, why not use the same formulation in the first engagement document and avoid the need for new conditions? There is no difficulty. At the time of engagement neither side ordinarily wishes to bind himself absolutely from that moment to every promised item, such as giving his daughter in marriage, providing a stated dowry, support, residence, and similar obligations. Each side fears that some circumstance may later cause the match to be broken. Therefore they use formulations such as "I will give," which do not create an immediate proprietary obligation, and rely mainly upon the penalty each side accepts if it breaks the engagement. This is why the common practice is to collect from the breaching party only the penalty, even where the father himself stated the entire dowry and made a kinyan concerning it. The kinyan is upon a future promise and does not itself obligate payment of the dowry. The binding part is the separately accepted penalty. This also explains why engagement agreements need a penalty at all, and why the authorities had to say that such a penalty is not an unenforceable asmakhta because of the embarrassment caused to the other side. If the original kinyan had itself created a complete debt, no penalty would be needed. At the time of the wedding, however, the parties no longer ordinarily fear that either side will retract. They therefore make new conditions in which the prior promises are converted into full present obligations, through personal liability and a kinyan effective from now. The same explains why obligations whose due time is after the wedding, such as support or residence, are not enforced solely by the original penalty clause if they were never incorporated into new binding conditions. People commonly say that the reason is that after the marriage the other side is presumed to have forgiven whatever was not performed beforehand out of affection created by the marriage. In my view, the more fundamental reason is that the original obligations were only future promises, enforceable through the penalty. The penalty avoids asmakhta only because of the embarrassment caused by breaking the match. That rationale applies before the wedding, when a failure to perform may prevent the wedding and directly cause embarrassment. Once the marriage has taken place, that embarrassment is past. A later failure to provide promised support is no longer the same kind of injury. Therefore, before the wedding each side can compel the other, by virtue of the penalty, to convert the earlier promises into new binding obligations. Rama writes similarly in Even HaEzer siman 51, that the agreed penalty can compel the husband to write the larger ketubah promised at the engagement. But if he failed to do so before the wedding, we cannot afterward seize his assets merely on the basis of the original engagement kinyan. Mahari Weil writes that even if one accepted under penalty at the engagement to create a larger ketubah, if no such document was made at the wedding, the wife did not acquire the additional amount. His later statement that because a kinyan was made he must fulfill what he accepted is according to the opinion that a kinyan on "I will give" is effective, and even then the wife has no immediate property right until performance. At most, coercive measures may be used to compel him during life. If he dies before performance, she cannot collect the additional amount from the estate solely by virtue of the earlier kinyan. This also explains the custom not to impose a ban upon a party who breaks an engagement even where the agreement mentions both a ban and a penalty and states that the penalty does not exempt the ban. The parties ordinarily do not expressly accept a ban not to retract. They merely make a kinyan upon what is written in the conditions, whose principal language remains a future promise. Therefore the ban itself does not take effect unless it is expressly accepted, or unless the community imposes such a ban by enactment.
