This remains a doubt. In the Derishah I cited the baraita in which the Tannaim disagree. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel holds that the recipient's final protest proves what his initial silence meant, so the property remains with the donor. The Sages hold that by remaining silent initially the recipient acquired the gift, and when he later says that he does not accept it, it then becomes ownerless. The Rambam and Mechaber rule that the halachah is doubtful because the Gemara itself expresses uncertainty. The Rosh simply records the dispute. From his unqualified presentation it appears that he follows the Sages, who are the majority, because the rule that the halachah follows Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel applies only to disputes recorded in the Mishnah, not in a baraita. This is why Rama writes at the end of the siman that according to the Rosh the recipient acquires in every case. The Tur likewise writes so in the Rosh's name.

And some say that if it was the gift of a dangerously ill person, etc., he acquired it. Meaning that it does not return to the donor. The recipient acquired it, and when he later says, "I do not accept it" or "I do not want it," it becomes ownerless property. According to the Rambam this applies even to land, while according to Rashi and the Rosh the resulting ownerless status applies specifically to movable property.
I have not seen, etc. In the Derishah I defended the Mechaber. The alternative opinion he cites is indeed the view of the Rosh, who rules like the first Tanna that the recipient acquires. It is possible, however, that the Sages said this specifically regarding the gift of a dangerously ill person, because two factors are present. First, the words of a dangerously ill person are treated as written and delivered. Second, another person may already have acquired on the recipient's behalf while the recipient remained silent. Therefore he acquires. With a healthy donor, even if another person acquires for him and he remains silent at that time, perhaps the Sages would agree with Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel that the recipient may later say, "Why should I have protested then?" The Mechaber therefore cites in the name of the alternative view only what is clearly compelled according to the Sages, namely the case of a dangerously ill person. Rama, who questions him, may have known this explanation but regarded it as strained, because the Sages, whose words are arranged first in the baraita, do not themselves mention a dangerously ill person, although Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's case clearly does. Rama therefore assumes that the Sages speak more generally. Examine carefully.
