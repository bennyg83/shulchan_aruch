And after it came into his possession while he remained silent. See Sema, seif katan 16, who writes that with a shechiv mera, if the recipient remained silent when the gift was first declared and afterward protested, the law differs. This is difficult because Tur, cited by Rama, holds that the language "I do not want it" makes movable property ownerless specifically because it is already in the recipient's possession, whereas land is different. Rashi explains similarly. Rashba in his novellae to Gittin gives the reason: with movable property already in his hand, if he says he does not want it, we presume he means to abandon it, for otherwise he should return it to the owner. With land still standing in the donor's domain, he can mean only, "I do not want this gift to remain effective; let it remain in its prior ownership." Accordingly, what difference does it make that the donor is a shechiv mera and that his words are treated as delivered? The physical reason, that the recipient should return the object to its owner, still does not apply. We must say that once Chazal treated a shechiv mera's words as though the gift had been delivered, the gift receives every legal consequence of actual delivery. There is a proof. Otherwise the Gemara in Keritot 24 could not challenge Reish Lakish from the case of one who wrote all his property to another and there were slaves among it. Rashi and Rashbam explain that the case involved delivery of the deed through another person. Surely delivering a deed through another is no stronger than the donor himself handing over the deed while the actual property has not yet reached the recipient. In an ordinary case, saying "I do not want it" would not create abandonment merely because the deed reached him. The Gemara's challenge therefore makes sense only because the case was a shechiv mera, as Rif and Rosh explain. In such a case the property is treated as though actually delivered even for this rule. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel nevertheless holds that if he protested immediately when the deed reached him, his end reveals his beginning and he never accepted the gift.
He has said nothing. See Sema, seif katan 17, who writes that according to Rambam, with the other formulations the property remains with the recipient. Derishah explains that Rambam follows Rabbeinu Yitzchak in Tosafot to Gittin 32. This is very difficult. According to Rabbeinu Yitzchak there, where the recipient says, "It is void," the gift remains his because this is past-tense language and his admission that the gift was void cannot overcome a known fact that the property came to him as a gift and he already acquired it. But Rabbeinu Yitzchak agrees that language clearly directed to the future is abandonment. Rambam, cited by Mechaber in seif 6, writes explicitly that if the recipient says, "The gift was void from the beginning," it returns to the owners. This plainly disagrees with Rabbeinu Yitzchak. Moreover, "it is void" is clearly present-future language, like "it is consecrated" or "it is forbidden," and the Gemara compares such formulations. Lechem Mishneh and Mishneh LaMelech say similarly in Rambam, Hilchot Zechiyah, chapter 4. Beit Yosef answers that Rambam omitted the specific rule of "it is void" because it can be learned from his ruling concerning one who says, "I wrote and gave it to him," while the recipient says, "He did not write or give it to me." Lechem Mishneh similarly derives it from Rambam's law cited by Mechaber in seif 8. In my view those derivations are still difficult, because seif 8 deals with one who says explicitly that the gift was void from the outset, and the case "he did not write and did not give me" likewise concerns a denial of the original acquisition. Rambam should still have stated the special rule concerning the ambiguous words "it is void," because the Gemara says the word can have two meanings, present-future and past. The rule depends on the principle that a person uses the formulation that produces legal effect. That is itself a distinct halachah Rambam should have taught.
If he has a creditor. See Sema, seif katan 18, and Taz, who rule that even an oral creditor can collect from ownerless property. Ketzot HaChoshen brings proof from siman 271, seif 31, and he is correct. Sema also cites Nimukei Yosef that if the creditor acquires the object, the recipient is thereby discharged from his debt. Ketzot correctly objects that this cannot mean merely that the creditor independently acquired the ownerless object. Why should the creditor be worse than any stranger who can acquire ownerless property without affecting the former owner's debt? Nimukei Yosef must mean that another person first acquired the object, and the creditor then collected it from that new owner by way of debt collection. In that case the original recipient is certainly discharged, because the creditor chose to satisfy his debt through that collection. It further appears that even before anyone else acquires the property, if the creditor did not know that the recipient had said "I do not want it" and therefore collected the property specifically as payment of the debt, the recipient is discharged. By collecting it as payment, the creditor has removed his lien through that collection. There is, however, room to say the opposite. Once the object became ownerless it is legally like property belonging to another person. If the creditor mistakenly seized someone else's property believing it belonged to the debtor, and on that basis mistakenly waived the debt, perhaps the debtor is not discharged. The matter requires consideration.
