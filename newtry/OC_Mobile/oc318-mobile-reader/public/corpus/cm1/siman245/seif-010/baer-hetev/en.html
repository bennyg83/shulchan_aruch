Silent. The Tur writes that in the case of a dangerously ill person, if the recipient was silent initially when the donor ordered that the gift be given to him, this is considered as though he were silent when the gift actually came into his possession, because the words of a dangerously ill person are treated as though written and delivered. The Rif and Rosh likewise rule this way. The Mechaber copied the Rambam's formulation, and the Sema writes that the Rambam did not state this rule. The Shach responds that the Rambam states it explicitly in chapter 9 of Hilchot Zechiyah, halachot 13 and 14. See Responsa Rashach, part II, siman 122; Responsa Rama MiPano, siman 72; and Responsa Rabbi Yosef Beit HaLevi, siman 54.
From it. The recipient is thereby released from his debt, and the creditor cannot say, "I acquire the property from ownerless status, while my debt remains outstanding." So writes the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, as cited by the Darkei Moshe. The Taz writes that it appears to him that the same rule applies to an oral loan. Since the debtor has no other means of payment, he has no right to declare this property ownerless and thereby actively injure his creditor. There is support for this from the rule that a person is not believed to say that property in his possession belongs to others, as explained in siman 99. If he had the power to render it ownerless, he should be believed through a migo that he could declare it ownerless immediately and allow those others to acquire it. Necessarily, he lacks the power to declare it ownerless in this manner.
I do not want it. The Sema understands the Rama to distinguish between saying, "I do not want it," and saying, "I have no desire for it." This is not compelling. Certainly the Rama regards the two expressions as equivalent and merely adopted the wording of the Rosh. Shach.
