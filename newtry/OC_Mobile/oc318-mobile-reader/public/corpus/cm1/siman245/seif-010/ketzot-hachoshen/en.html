If the recipient has a creditor. Sema limits the creditor's right to a documented debt, reasoning that the recipient cannot abandon property already subjected to the creditor's lien. Taz says the same should apply to an oral loan where the debtor has no other property, because he cannot deliberately abandon his only assets and harm the creditor. Taz's proof from the rule that a possessor cannot say property in his hands belongs to someone else is not compelling, since he could equally have given away movable property and yet the migo is rejected for other reasons, such as the presumption that what a person holds is his or because he does not wish actually to transfer it. The substantive rule, however, supports Taz and is even broader. When a convert dies without heirs and others seize his ownerless property, any loan collectible from heirs can also be collected from the property that became ownerless. Maggid Mishneh and Rashba say an existing lien is not extinguished by ownerless status, which is no stronger than consecration. This includes an oral loan in the circumstances in which it would be collectible from heirs. Rashba goes further. Even movables that by Talmudic law would not ordinarily be collected from heirs can remain subject to the creditor when they become ownerless. Ownerless property is in this respect weaker protection than inheritance. The Shulchan Aruch's phrase "any loan collectible from heirs" primarily addresses the evidentiary rules for fear of repayment, not an absolute limitation of the lien to property collectable from heirs. Shiltei Gibborim raises a doubt where a living debtor voluntarily abandons all his assets. In my view the creditor certainly retains his lien. If even consecration does not destroy the creditor's underlying right, voluntary abandonment cannot. The debtor's future acquired assets remain subject to him as well. The creditor therefore can collect from the abandoned property according to the ordinary priority rules.
A creditor who himself seizes the abandoned property may choose to take it as ownerless property rather than as payment of his debt. Sema understood Nimukei Yosef to say otherwise, that the creditor's taking necessarily discharges the debt. That is not the meaning of the source. Nimukei Yosef says that when the recipient of a gift first remained silent and only later protested, the gift is ownerless because the donor already withdrew. If the recipient's creditors then come and collect that property by virtue of their lien, the recipient's debt is discharged even though he himself had abandoned it. The creditors are collecting, not acquiring from ownerless status. If the creditor himself is the first person who physically acquires the ownerless property, he is like any other person. He can elect either to take it as payment or to acquire it independently from ownerless status, just as a creditor of a deathly ill person who was also given a gift may choose whether to take as creditor or as donee. Where strangers acquired the abandoned property, the creditor collects from them only if the debtor has no other property from which to collect. They can otherwise say, "I left you a place from which to collect." Priority among successive acquirers follows the ordinary rule that the last acquirer is first exposed to collection, and only if insufficient does the creditor proceed backward.
