If they made a kinyan from him, the language "I will give" is effective. Maggid Mishneh cites Rashba's dispute concerning a sudar kinyan on the words "I will give you." He wonders how such language can acquire, since a document saying "I will give it" does not itself transfer the property, and suggests perhaps kinyan is stronger than a document. The distinction need not be that a kinyan immediately transfers the promised object. Even according to those who validate a kinyan on "I will give," the beneficiary does not presently own the object. The kinyan creates a personal obligation upon the promisor to perform the gift. Mahari Weil says explicitly that once one accepted such an undertaking by sudar, beit din compels him to give, but until he actually gives, the recipient has no proprietary right. Beit Shmuel similarly says that if the promisor dies before performance, one may not necessarily collect the promised object from the heirs. This explains the baraita in Gittin. Rabbi says that language such as "I will make him free" effects acquisition, while the Sages say he has not acquired. Their dispute can concern present acquisition. The Sages can still agree that the writer has obligated himself to perform what he wrote. The passage about a slave does not disprove this. A slave's hand is his master's hand. If the master writes an obligation to free him but does not presently free him, the slave has no independent hand capable of receiving the instrument that creates the personal obligation. Therefore the special clause compelling heirs can depend on the law of fulfilling the words of the deceased rather than a direct present acquisition by the slave. Thus the distinction between present title and enforceable obligation resolves Maggid Mishneh's difficulty.
