(Seif 4: the donor's creditor does not collect.) It is difficult why the Tur did not state the practical consequence that if the recipient now wishes to receive the field and says, “you already admitted that you gave it to me,” while the donor refuses to give it, the law follows the recipient, as Rashi writes. It is also difficult that the Tur says this applies only while enough property remains in the donor's hands, implying that the admission has significance for the creditor. Yet even without any prior admission, the creditor could not take this field while the debtor had other property from which to pay. One cannot say that the field had been designated as an apotiki for this creditor, for then the donor's admission could certainly not impair the creditor's lien. It appears to me that the case is where the due date arrived and the debtor then admitted that he had given this average-quality field, which was subject to the creditor's collection, to another. He is plainly not believed. Rather, the case is where he admitted the gift before the due date. There is then a distinction. If he presently has other assets from which to pay, even if they are inferior land, his earlier admission is effective and the creditor does not collect from the average land. At the time of the admission, had the debtor sold the field, the creditor would have been compelled to accept the inferior land, since one does not collect from encumbered property while unencumbered property remains. The same is true of the admission. If, however, he presently has no other assets, the earlier admission is ineffective against the creditor, for even had the debtor sold the field, the creditor could now seize it from the purchaser. The Tur chose this practical consequence because it teaches this novelty, rather than Rashi's simpler consequence. Below, in seif 7, it will be explained that if the recipient refuses to take title to the gift it is void. Here we are not discussing such refusal. The essential question is whether he ever acquired. If he truly acquired, his present refusal does not undo the earlier acquisition in the manner discussed here.
