New. At the time the first engagement conditions are written, neither side wishes to bind himself fully or effect a present kinyan for everything that was promised, because each fears that one side may later regret the match for some reason and the engagement will be broken. Therefore, each accepts only a kinyan phrased as "I will give," which does not itself take effect. He is obligated to fulfill the conditions only by virtue of the penalty accepted by the parties. This explains the custom that one who withdraws is charged only the stipulated penalty, even though he made a kinyan and obligated himself to give the entire dowry. Necessarily, the reason is that the kinyan was merely a kinyan to give in the future. At the time of the wedding, however, there is no longer the same concern that the parties will withdraw, and therefore it is customary to write new conditions and create a present, complete obligation and an effective kinyan. This also explains why, without new conditions, the father of the bride or groom would not be obligated after the marriage to provide support, lodging, or the other matters he had accepted upon himself merely because of the penalty undertaken at the engagement. In my view, all that was promised at the engagement was accepted only through a kinyan to give, which creates no direct obligation except through the penalty. That penalty is ordinarily an asmachta, except that the Sages held that the penalty for breaking an engagement is not invalid as asmachta because of the humiliation caused. That reasoning applies only to obligations whose time for performance precedes the marriage. It does not apply to matters becoming due only after the marriage, for by then the humiliation has already passed because the marriage occurred, and the penalty cannot create liability because it is an asmachta. Nevertheless, the party can be compelled to enter into new conditions, for refusal at that point would itself still fall within the matter of humiliation. It further appears that for this reason the custom is not to impose cherem upon one who breaks an engagement even when the written conditions state that he accepted both cherem and a penalty, and even when they state that payment of the penalty does not release the cherem. Since the parties did not expressly accept a cherem never to withdraw, but merely made a kinyan on what was written in the conditions, and the essential kinyan was only a kinyan to give, the cherem likewise does not take effect unless they expressly accept it or the community itself decrees the matter under cherem.
