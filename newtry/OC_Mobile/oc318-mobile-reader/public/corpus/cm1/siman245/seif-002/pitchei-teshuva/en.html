New conditions. See Baer Heitev, through the statement that the conditions also record that the penalty does not release the parties, and see Taz, Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 236, together with Pitchei Teshuva there; also Baer Heitev, Even HaEzer siman 20 seif katan 23 and siman 51 seif katan 11, and Pitchei Teshuva there seif katan 8. Responsa Chut HaShani, siman 36, discusses Yaakov's claim against his son Reuven. The father alleged that while the son was unmarried he urged him to arrange a marriage and to obligate himself by kinyan to provide a large dowry, assuring him that he did not truly demand any money and wanted only to obtain a respectable match. The father did so. At the wedding he refused to hand over anything unless the son gave him a written undertaking to return it all. The son answered, “I am your son. Do you not trust my word that I will return everything?” The father relied on him, gave him a chest of articles and jewelry worth about two thousand as security, and made a kinyan obligating himself for seven hundred, with authority to sell or pledge the security if he did not pay by the appointed time. He claimed that all this was done before the other family merely for appearances and demanded his deposit back. The son answered that before the original conditions they had never discussed any such arrangement; the father had independently bound himself in the match without his knowledge. Only on the wedding day did the father ask that he return the property, and he refused. Even if he then appeared to consent, it was under duress, since otherwise the father would have departed and the match would have collapsed; he never intended to return anything. Chut HaShani rules that if the father's version is correct, so that the original undertaking was made at the son's request and upon the son's assurance that he sought nothing for himself and wanted only the appearance of a large dowry, and the condition was repeated on the wedding day, the son must return the property. Although the father handed it over without repeating the condition at the moment of delivery, even a prior disclosure of intent can defeat a gift, as is evident from Nedarim at the end of HaShutafin and from Chazakat HaBatim. If, however, there was no such agreement before the first conditions and the father simply bound himself to give the stated sum, as the son alleges, then even if the son later promised before delivery to return everything, he need not do so. Once the father obligated himself by kinyan in the original conditions, a complete debt arose in the son's favor, because one may acquire a benefit for another without his knowledge, and the father had to fulfill his undertaking. A later promise by the son to return it is mere words and has no legal force. Although the Gemara teaches that “I will give” does not itself effect a gift, the early authorities dispute whether a kinyan is effective with such future-tense language. We therefore treat the matter as an unresolved monetary question, in which the burden of proof lies on the claimant. Since the son is in possession, the father cannot extract the property, for perhaps the law follows those authorities who hold the kinyan effective; if so, the obligation arose at the original conditions and the son's later promise to return it could be withdrawn as mere words.
