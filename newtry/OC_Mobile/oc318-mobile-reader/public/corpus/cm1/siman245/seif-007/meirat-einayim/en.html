He takes a hesset oath. This applies when the creditor makes a definite claim: "I know that this property is a gift in your hand." If the creditor has only a doubtful claim, there is merely a general ban. The Beit Yosef writes this according to the Maggid Mishneh's explanation of the Rambam, namely that the required oath is one taken to the creditor. The Maggid Mishneh himself, however, leaves the matter in difficulty, and the Tur challenges the Rambam: If the recipient says that the property is not a gift, let him simply withdraw his claim to it and leave it. In the Perishah I explained that the Tur and Maggid Mishneh found it difficult to say that the alleged recipient can defeat a definite creditor's claim merely by swearing that he holds the property as a custodian, especially when the original owner asserts nothing and the property is found in his possession. This resembles, though not conclusively, the rule that Reuven, who holds a promissory note against Shimon, cannot defeat his own creditor by swearing that Shimon already paid the debt. Rama in Darkei Moshe likewise writes that the recipient would ordinarily not be believed even by oath when his assertion harms a creditor. Yet he adds that perhaps this case is different because we know the property was not originally his. That is the point to which Rama alludes here by referring to seif 10, where the same principle appears.
