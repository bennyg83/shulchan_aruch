And he became liable through it. This applies even if beit din did not yet formally obligate him, so long as according to his first claim he would be liable. This is evident from the rule below concerning a change after the period of immediate speech and from the Rashba and other authorities.
Even if there are witnesses, etc. If the circumstances allow him to say that he later remembered that he had paid, the second claim is effective when he brings witnesses supporting it, as explained in siman 79, seif 3.
But if he left beit din, etc., perhaps they taught him, etc. In my view this refers to a case where beit din already told him that he was liable. Otherwise there is no reason to suspect that others taught him a false claim, because on the face of matters his first claim would already have won. In the Gemara we find the concern that others taught him a false argument primarily after witnesses contradicted him, when he sees that he will lose. Therefore, where no witnesses contradicted him and beit din did not declare him liable, if after leaving he clarifies his first statement in a manner that its language reasonably supports, beit din should hear the clarification.
He may, etc., contradict his first claim. This is where his original words were not a complete admission before witnesses with a valid declaration of you are my witnesses, or another form of binding admission described in siman 81.
From exemption to exemption. This excludes changing from a claim that creates liability to one that creates exemption. Such a change is not allowed even before leaving beit din unless the second statement genuinely clarifies the first. It appears that even where the first claim imposed only an oath, he cannot later change his claim merely to escape that oath. Hagahot Maimoniyot states that if the first words create liability for money or oath and the later words create exemption, or the reverse, we follow the formulation that creates liability, because a litigant’s admission is like one hundred witnesses. Maharshal’s view cited earlier may imply that where the first claim would merely enable the opponent to swear and collect, the defendant may still change to a claim under which he himself swears and is exempt, since the opponent has not yet actually sworn. This requires study. At least once beit din has ruled that the opponent shall swear and collect, the defendant can no longer switch to a defense under which he would swear and be exempt.
The first. Some versions of the Tur read the latter, and that is also the reading in the Rambam itself. It is more accurate.
And all this applies only after the period of immediate speech, etc. The Rashba discusses one who admitted a debt in beit din, where the creditor then said, do not repay me except before witnesses, and the defendant immediately responded, I have collateral of yours. The Rashba rules that the period of immediate speech following another person’s statement is not treated as continuation of one’s own first statement. This must be where the first words implied that he held no collateral. Otherwise he could plainly clarify that he meant, I owe you money and you in turn must return my collateral. Everything depends on how beit din reasonably understands his language.
And not like those who disagree, etc. The Darkei Moshe cites Mahariv that a person cannot later raise a claim he omitted initially, because the omission is like an admission that no such defense exists. Hagahot Maimoniyot, in the name of Maharam, says that mere initial silence does not prevent a later defense. In my view there is no dispute. Mahariv and the Rosh speak of one who initially advanced a different claim that itself created liability, whereas Hagahot Maimoniyot speaks of simple silence regarding one among several claims. Silence alone is not an admission unless it occurs in a setting that constitutes formal testimony or admission. The Darkei Moshe also attributes to the Rosh a statement that the defendant may later say his first claim was conditional upon performance of some act, but the cited responsum actually indicates the opposite.
