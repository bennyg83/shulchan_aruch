And became liable. This means even if beit din did not formally pronounce him liable, but his own first claim necessarily leads to liability. The preceding gloss concerning retraction only within the time of immediate speech proves this, and Responsa Rashba and other authorities state it explicitly. The Taz rules likewise. See Responsa Maharam Alshakar 39. Shach.
Witnesses. If the circumstances are such that he can credibly say, "I remembered that I paid," then witnesses supporting the second claim are effective, as stated in siman 79, seif 3. Shach.
As above. There, the main reason is that witnesses established him as a false denier when he said, "I never borrowed." Here no witnesses appeared. The Sema explains that in the earlier case, without the witnesses his first claim would not have made him liable, and the witnesses themselves also did not directly impose liability because they testified that he had paid. Therefore we needed the special rule that his denial established him as a false denier. Here, by contrast, his first claim itself created liability. We learn from the earlier case that once his own denial has produced liability, he cannot later free himself even with supporting witnesses.
Went out. The Shach understands this as a case where beit din had already ruled against him and told him, "You are liable." Although the simple wording of Tur and Shulchan Aruch might not appear to require that, otherwise there is no reason to presume that people coached him to lie after he left. If his first claim would have won, why would they teach him a false claim? The Gemara uses such a concern where witnesses have contradicted him and he sees that he will lose. But if no witnesses contradicted him and beit din did not tell him that he was liable, then when he later clarifies his original wording in a manner that the language can reasonably bear, beit din should hear him even after he left.
To contradict. This refers to a case where his initial admission was not made before witnesses with the formula "you are my witnesses," nor in another fully binding form of admission under siman 81. Shach.
And to prevail. The reason is a migo: why would he lie, when he could have remained with his first claim? See Responsa Mahari Weil 28 concerning one who admitted informing on another and later sought to reinterpret his words; he was not believed. Sema.
To exempt. This excludes a change from a liable claim to an exempting one, which is not permitted even before leaving beit din, if it truly contradicts the first claim rather than merely clarifying it. It also appears that once a litigant has become obligated in an oath, he cannot switch claims merely to avoid that oath. Hagahot Maimoniyot says so. Maharshal may imply that where the first claim would allow the opponent to swear and collect, the defendant can change to a claim under which he himself swears and is exempt, because liability has not yet become final until the opponent actually swears. The Shach leaves this requiring study and suggests Maharshal may have been explaining another authority rather than ruling so. At minimum, once beit din has already ruled that the opponent shall swear and collect, the defendant can no longer change to a defense by which he would swear and be exempt. See siman 146 and Responsa Maharshdam 180.
Speech. Responsa Rashba discusses one who admitted a debt in beit din, whereupon the creditor said, "Do not repay me except before witnesses." Within the interval normally considered immediate speech, the debtor added, "I have a pledge of yours." Rashba ruled that the latter statement is not automatically treated as part of the first. The case must be where the first words implied that no pledge existed. Otherwise he could simply clarify that he meant, "I owe you money, but you must return my pledge." Everything depends on whether beit din finds the original wording capable of that explanation. See Responsa Mahari ibn Lev and Mabit II, 160.
Was silent. The Sema cites Mahari Weil's proof from the law of two brothers or partners litigating with another person: if the absent partner is in town, he should have appeared, and his silence can be treated as agreement that his colleague argued properly. All the more so, when a litigant himself could have made a claim and remained silent, that silence indicates that he conceded the claim was unavailable. There is also a policy reason: if later changes were freely allowed, every losing litigant would alter his claim once he saw he was about to lose, undermining beit din. The Rama's phrase "even if he was initially silent" follows this reasoning. The Shach disagrees; see his discussion.
