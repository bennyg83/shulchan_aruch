And he became liable through it. At the end of this siman it is clear that even if beit din did not expressly issue a liability ruling, but his own claim made it evident that he would be liable, the same rule applies.As above at the beginning of siman 79.
There it was explained that even if witnesses later come and say he borrowed and repaid, we do not listen to them in a way that would rescue him from the consequences of his own earlier denial. There the principal reason is that witnesses established him as a false denier after he said, 'I did not borrow.' Without the witnesses his first claim alone would not have made him liable, and the witnesses themselves would not have made him liable because they also say he repaid. Therefore we need the rule that his false denial bars him.
Here, however, his own first claim itself generated liability. We learn from the earlier case that once a person became liable through his own claim, we do not later listen to a new claim seeking exemption, even where he has witnesses.But if he comes to explain, etc.
See Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 28, regarding one who admitted that he informed against his fellow and afterward sought to reinterpret his words; he was not believed.To return and argue and thereby prevail, etc. We say: why would he lie? He could have remained with his first claim.See above siman 79, etc.
There it was explained that even if witnesses arrive only after he changes his claim, we do not say he anticipated their arrival and therefore changed his story; the ordinary migo remains.Provided that he did not leave beit din. If he left, we suspect that others taught him how to reframe his first statement.All this applies only after the time of immediate speech, etc.
Rashba writes that where one admitted in beit din, the other party said, 'Do not repay me except before witnesses,' and the defendant replied within the time of immediate speech, 'I have collateral of yours,' the defendant cannot treat his response as part of his own earlier speech merely because it followed immediately after the other litigant's words. The immediate-speech period is measured relative to his own statement, not the other's. Regarding testimony, however, one witness may retract within the immediate-speech period of the second witness because the two testimonies are treated as one unit.But within the time of immediate speech, etc.
This refers back to one who first stated a claim that would make him liable and then retracted within the immediate-speech period, even where no witnesses were involved. It cannot refer to a case where witnesses contradicted him, because the relevant immediate-speech period is from his own claim, not from the witnesses' testimony.
The wording 'and he became liable through it' does not mean that beit din already issued a formal judgment, for then the immediate-speech period would likely have elapsed. Rather, it means that everyone could see from the claim itself that liability would result. Mahari Weil further proves that silence can operate like an admission.
If one party could have asserted a point and remained silent when the matter arose, that silence may show he conceded that the argument was unavailable. Were a litigant always permitted to replace his claim after seeing that he would lose, judicial proceedings would have no final force.
