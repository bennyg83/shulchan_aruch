One who pleads in beit din. Responsa Maharash Halevi discusses whether a litigant may retract his first plea by giving an explanation, analogous to a woman who said, "I am married," and later gives a valid explanation for her earlier statement. Maharash Halevi holds that such an explanation works in monetary law, all the more so if it works in the stringent laws of forbidden relationships, and he cites Maharshal. Maharav Aharon Chasson disagrees. He asks: if a person made a complete admission that cannot be explained as joking or self impoverishment, may he later retract merely by supplying some excuse or saying he was coerced? Even in prohibitory law, Raah, cited by Ran, says that if a woman says, "I am betrothed to so and so," she is not believed through any explanation, because after admitting a legal bond to him she cannot injure his rights by withdrawing herself. Maharash Halevi replies from Rambam in Hilchot To'en, who says a litigant may retract an earlier plea and advance another one that contradicts it if the later plea can be read into the first through a satisfactory explanation. But this is no proof. Rambam speaks where the later plea is a clarification or reinterpretation already included within the possible meaning of the first. The classic case is one who said, "It belonged to my fathers," and later explains, "I meant that I rely upon the seller as I would upon my fathers." Rambam does not say that a person may abandon an unequivocal first plea and replace it with a wholly different one merely by offering an excuse for why he previously lied. The same answers the proof from joking and from saying, "I spoke so as not to make myself appear wealthy." Those are special explanations recognized by the Gemara because people commonly speak in those ways. Indeed, with the self impoverishment explanation some authorities even advance it for heirs without their pleading it. Ordinary explanations are never advanced by beit din; even in prohibitory law the person must articulate a concrete and credible reason. The Mechaber in siman 81 carefully distinguishes when joking and self impoverishment apply and does not mention a general power to escape admissions through other excuses. Therefore the correct rule is that in monetary law there is no general explanation that permits withdrawal from an unequivocal plea. One may rely only on the recognized defenses of joking or self impoverishment where they fit, or on an explanation that actually reconciles the later plea with the earlier one.
And he became liable through it. Knesset HaGedolah cites a responsum saying that a litigant is bound by an earlier plea only if he himself knew that the plea made him liable. If he thought it exempted him, though the law actually imposes liability, he may retract and plead differently. I do not understand the basis. A litigant's admission is accepted like one hundred witnesses. Why should it matter whether he understood its legal consequence? One might distinguish if the foundation of admission were a new undertaking or gift, as Mahari ibn Lev suggests, because one who did not know he was obligating himself may lack intent to create a new obligation. But as I explained in siman 34, a litigant's admission works through special credibility granted by the Torah to a person concerning himself, in monetary law as in prohibitory law. Under that approach his lack of legal knowledge does not diminish the factual credibility of his words. A responsum of Shav Yaakov makes a similar distinction concerning the rule that one renders something forbidden upon himself, arguing that if he did not know the legal prohibition his statement does not create the prohibition. In my view that too is incorrect for the same reason. The Torah accepts his statement as testimony concerning himself. There is clear proof from the rule that one who says, "I never borrowed," is treated as also saying, "I never repaid." He plainly did not intend to create liability by conceding nonpayment, yet his words are received against him. If the permission to change from one exempting plea to another were based on migo, one might argue that where he did not know the first plea was legally harmful he still has the practical position of one who could have remained with it. But as I explain in the next seif katan, the rule is not based on migo. Therefore even ignorance of the legal consequence does not permit withdrawal from an admission that actually created liability.
From one exempting plea to another. The authorities often phrase the rule as though the litigant may change because he has a migo that he could have remained with the first plea. The Tur says this in the name of Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash, and the Shach uses the same language. Yet this creates a difficulty. If later witnesses arrive and refute the first plea, why should the revised plea remain credible? In the law of a woman who says, "I am married and divorced," she is believed because her mouth created the prohibition and removed it, but if witnesses later establish that she is married, she is no longer believed even if the witnesses came only afterward. Likewise, one who admits a document but says, "I paid," is believed through migo of forgery only until witnesses later validate the document. Once they do, the migo disappears. If the revised plea here were credible only by migo of standing by the first, later refutation should destroy it. The correct explanation is that a retraction from one exempting plea to another is legally valid in itself. So long as the first plea did not create liability, it does not yet have the character of a binding admission against the litigant. It resembles testimony not yet fully completed, which can still be corrected. The authorities' phrase, "He could have remained with his first plea," is not meant as a technical migo of "why would he lie." It means that because he still had the legal option to stand by the first plea and had not yet become liable, the first plea had not become fixed as a binding self admission. This is proven by Maharshal, who writes that if the claimant has incurred an oath but has not yet sworn, the defendant can still change his plea. This is not a migo, because if he stood by the first plea he would lose once the claimant swore, while his new plea seeks full exemption. Rather, the point is that until the first plea has actually produced a final liability, it remains retractable. Therefore later arrival of witnesses does not necessarily destroy a revised plea that was validly made while the first plea was still legally open.
After witnesses come. If a defendant first says, "It never happened," then changes to, "I paid," and only afterward the claimant produces a written instrument, the change should be treated as a retraction from liability to exemption. This is so even though the document was produced only after the change. Signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din. Rivash rules that even testimony received in another beit din can establish the defendant as a false denier. A written instrument is no weaker. The same is true of the defendant's own handwriting, which stands as written evidence of the obligation. Once such existing evidence contradicts the original total denial, the original plea was legally one of liability, and the later payment plea is an impermissible change from liability to exemption.
After the time of an utterance. Darkei Moshe cites Rashba concerning one who admitted before beit din and the creditor said, "Do not repay me except before witnesses." Within the time of an utterance the defendant added, "I have collateral of mine in your hands." Rashba ruled that one person's speech is not joined to another person's statement as though it were the same utterance. The Sema cites this. The Shach writes that the case must be one where the first admission implied that no collateral existed, for otherwise the defendant should be able simply to clarify, "I meant that I owe you money, but you must return my collateral," and everything depends on whether his first language can bear that meaning. In my view judicial discretion is unnecessary. Once he admitted the debt, the new claim, "I have collateral in your hands," is simply a counterclaim that ordinarily works only through migo, as explained in siman 24. If a person says, "I owe you a maneh," and days later says, "You hold my collateral," he is believed only through a migo of payment. If at the time of the admission the creditor said, "Do not repay me except before specified witnesses," eliminating the payment migo, the counterclaim is not believed. A statement made after the time of an utterance is no different from one made days later. One cannot use a retroactive migo that existed only before the admission. This is straightforward.
