But if he left beit din. The Shach writes in seif katan 3 that this applies specifically where beit din had already ruled that he was liable on the basis of his original claim. If beit din had not yet ruled, however, we do not say that someone taught him a false argument, because he still believed that he could prevail with the argument he initially made. These words have no proof at all. The language of the Tur and Mechaber indicates that this clause refers back to the preceding case, namely one who made a claim that would result in liability. The Shach himself explains there that this does not mean that beit din had already formally adjudged him liable, but only that he stood to become liable. Yet the distinction here between leaving beit din and remaining there is stated with reference to that very case. This proves that even where judgment had not yet been formally entered, if he left beit din his revised claim is ineffective. This also follows from Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and the Rambam, who write that if even under his first claim he could have prevailed, he may return and make a different claim even after leaving beit din and returning. According to the Shach, the phrase "even after leaving beit din and returning" is difficult. If judgment had not yet been rendered, leaving beit din would make no difference even where he could not have prevailed under his first claim, because beit din had not yet ruled against him. Why then is it relevant that he could have prevailed? See Rivash, responsum 327, concerning a widow who claimed that a house belonged to her from her first husband, and afterward, when she was shown a deed of admission from the first husband, changed her claim and said that her second husband had purchased it. The questioner wrote that she could not change her claim because she had already left beit din. Rivash answered that the questioner had spoken correctly, except that the point was unnecessary because in any event she was changing from a position of liability to one of exemption. Yet there, as is clear from the responsum, beit din had not issued any ruling at all. See there. Likewise, Rivash responsum 392 concerns a person who admitted that Reuven had given him a cup to pawn and later claimed that he had indeed pawned it and given the money to Reuven. Rivash writes that although we rule that once a litigant leaves beit din he may not make a new claim, that applies where he explains his earlier words in a strained manner. Here, however, his explanation is not strained, because he said that Reuven gave him the cup to pawn, and that is exactly what occurred. It is evident from the lengthy responsum that, absent this feature, he could not have changed his claim even though no ruling had yet been rendered on the first claim. The matter is therefore clear, contrary to the Shach.
We do not listen to him. In the preceding seif katan I cited Rivash, who distinguishes that the difference between leaving beit din and not leaving applies specifically where the litigant explains his words in a strange and strained manner. If his explanation is straightforward, he is believed even after leaving beit din. What Rivash regarded as obvious was uncertain to Tosafot and Ri in Bava Batra 31a. There, one litigant said, "The land belonged to my ancestors," and the other brought witnesses that he had established three years of chazakah. The first then explained, "I meant that my ancestors purchased it from you." This is a close and natural explanation, for even the Nehardeans, who hold that one may not abandon one claim and make another, agree in this case, as the Rashbam writes, because he is not uprooting his earlier words at all and both statements can be true. Nevertheless, Tosafot, s.v. u'modeh, write that Ri was uncertain whether he is believed if he already left beit din. It is true that the Rif and Rosh, who cite the rule that the Nehardeans agree where he says, "It belonged to my ancestors who purchased it from you," appear to support Rivash. Otherwise, why did they cite that qualification when we rule against the Nehardeans and hold that generally a litigant may make a new claim, even where he says that he relied upon the property as belonging to his ancestors? Necessarily, they cite it to teach that because even the Nehardeans agree in such a case, since the explanation is close and natural, he may make the explanation even after leaving beit din. If it were considered a true change of claim, the Nehardeans would not agree.
However, the Tur and Mechaber below in siman 146, seif 24, explicitly rule that even with the claim, "My ancestors purchased it from you," we require that he not have left beit din. There is not even the uncertainty expressed by Ri. There the litigant is the one presently in possession of the land, and this is clear.
And not in accordance with those who disagree. The dissenting view is Maharam, cited in Hagahot Maimoniyot to Hilchot To'en VeNitan, chapter 7, who holds that if a person initially remained silent in response to his fellow's claim, he may later make a claim. Mahari Weil writes that a person cannot later make a claim that he did not raise previously, because his failure to raise it initially is treated as though he admitted the other party's position. The Rama rules like Mahari Weil because the Rosh, in responsum 64, ruling 3, agrees. The Shach objects that the Rosh and Mahari Weil agree with Maharam regarding mere silence. They discuss only a case where the litigant initially made an argument that rendered him liable and now seeks to add a new argument that he did not previously make. In a case of silence, the Shach says, they agree with Maharam. The Shach is correct regarding the responsum of the Rosh. There the person initially said that he had promised to give the other party one quarter of the barrels of wine, and afterward sought to say that the condition was that the other party also acquire one quarter share in the wine. See there. The Rosh properly writes that this is a change of claim according to all opinions. But the Shach's reading cannot be fitted into Mahari Weil's words. Mahari Weil brings proof from the case of two partners where one made a claim and the other partner was in town but did not make that claim. This shows that he accepted his partner's position. Mahari Weil concludes: "Thus, since he remained silent, he waived it and it is as though he admitted that his fellow argued correctly. All the more so concerning a matter that he himself could have claimed and did not claim," etc. He is plainly discussing silence, not a case where the litigant expressly made an argument that rendered him liable, as the Shach writes. Such a case has no connection at all to the rule concerning a partner who was in town, and Mahari Weil would have had no reason to seek proof elsewhere. The matter could have been learned directly from the discussion in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim concerning whether one may make a second claim. It is therefore clear that Mahari Weil indeed discusses silence. At first glance, the dispute between Mahari Weil and Maharam appears to depend upon the dispute among the Amoraim in Bava Metzia 37a concerning one who robbed one of five people but does not know which one. Rabbi Tarfon holds that he places the stolen item among them and departs. The Gemara asks: What do the claimants say? Rabbi Yochanan said in the name of Rav that they remain silent, and their silence is not an admission because each can say, "The reason I remained silent before each claimant was that I thought perhaps this is the one," while considering the matter carefully. Rav Masna said in the name of Rav that each claimant protests, because if he remained silent his silence would be treated as an admission. According to that opinion, even though the silent person has a strong explanation, namely that he was thinking carefully in order to determine the truth, his silence is nevertheless treated as an admission. The Ritva to Bava Metzia writes that it is uncertain which of these Amoraic opinions is the halachah. However, according to Hagahot HaRosh in the name of Or Zarua, and especially according to what Or Zarua writes in the name of Tosafot Shantz and a student of the Rav, the halachah follows the opinion that silence is not an admission. He writes that whenever a person's silence can reasonably be attributed to the fact that he is considering what to say, we do not treat silence as an admission. Otherwise every person would be trapped simply because he could not immediately respond. Even in the very case there, if he himself says, "I remained silent because I was considering whether you were the person I robbed," he is believed. But if he does not give that explanation, we do not advance it for him. See there. Their view therefore follows the Amora who holds that silence is not an admission, and these are precisely Maharam's words. In my opinion, Maharam learned his rule from here. It is true that Hagahot HaRosh's wording can be read as though even according to the opinion that the claimant protests, one may still give such an explanation, but that is not the implication of the Gemara. The Gemara itself attributes the explanation, "I remained silent because I was considering," specifically to the opinion that the claimant remains silent. The Shitah Mekubetzet likewise explains that this leniency follows the opinion that even a silent claimant is not deemed to admit. According to the Shitah Mekubetzet, then, this matter is subject to the Amoraic dispute. Nevertheless, since the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omit the dispute entirely, even though there is a practical difference not only in the case of robbery, where we rule like Rabbi Akiva that the robber must pay each claimant, but also in the case of one who purchased from one of five people, where we rule that Rabbi Akiva agrees with Rabbi Tarfon, a question already raised by the Ritva without answer, we must say that they hold that in ordinary cases it is obvious that silence is not an admission because the person may be considering how to respond. The robbery case is different, because a person does not willingly make himself appear to have robbed each claimant. He should not remain silent but should say, "I do not know whether I robbed you, but I robbed only one person." His silence can therefore be treated as an admission in that special context. According to this, there is no general practical difference between the Amoraic opinions. In ordinary cases all agree that silence is not an admission. Their dispute concerns only robbery, and there we rule that the robber must pay each claimant, so the omission by the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh is understandable. Maharam's rule is therefore, in my opinion, compelled by this analysis. I further think that Maharam and Mahari Weil do not actually disagree. Maharam discusses a case before the litigant has left beit din. In that situation he can give the above explanation, namely, "I remained silent because I was considering carefully how to respond." Maharam himself gives the reason that even an earlier affirmative claim may sometimes be reconciled with a later one despite appearing somewhat inconsistent, all the more so can mere silence be explained and not treated as an admission. This very reasoning teaches its own limitation: it applies specifically where the earlier position can still be reconciled, namely where the person has not left beit din. Once he has left beit din, he can no longer reconcile a prior argument, and likewise can no longer explain his silence. The entire Gemara discussion above concerns one continuous appearance before beit din, as its language indicates. Mahari Weil, by contrast, discusses a case where the person had already left beit din, as appears from his proof concerning the partner, where judgment had already been rendered between the other partner and the claimant. In that situation we may indeed say that someone taught him a false claim, as Mahari Weil explains, for otherwise anyone could do this after seeing that he would lose. If so, what force would a beit din judgment have? Thus the case necessarily concerns one in which judgment had already been rendered and he left beit din. Just as he can no longer reconcile his first claim, he can no longer explain away his silence. There is therefore no difficulty. Both authorities are correct and their rulings stand together.
