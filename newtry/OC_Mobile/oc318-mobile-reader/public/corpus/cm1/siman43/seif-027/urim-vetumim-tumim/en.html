Because in ordinary speech, "until" excludes the endpoint. The commentators ask from the immediately preceding ruling, which is also a responsum of Rosh. There, Reuven produced a note showing Shimon owed him one hundred, and Shimon produced a receipt written that same day saying Reuven had waived all claims against him "until this day." Rosh ruled that although same-day payment is uncommon, it does occur, and the receipt can cancel the note. Shach distinguishes between "until this day" and merely "until the fifth of Nisan." He argues that "until this very day" resembles biblical expressions in which the endpoint is included. But the second responsum also says the waiver extended "until that day." It does not use Shach's exact contrast. More fundamentally, I do not understand how the word "this" should change the ordinary rule of speech. In Torah language, "until" can include the endpoint, as many verses show. But our question concerns ordinary human speech. If a person forbids produce picked "until today," the produce picked today is ordinarily excluded. Why should adding the word "this" automatically reverse the meaning? The correct distinction is not linguistic alone but depends on the underlying legal claim. I explained above, in the laws of seizure and siman 42, that when a party seeks to extinguish an established debt through a gratuitous waiver, the waiver must be clear. If the language is doubtful, we interpret it narrowly and preserve the debt. This is also the rule below in siman 65: where the cancellation depends entirely upon the language of a waiver, we give the waiver its narrower scope. By contrast, where the debtor claims actual payment and holds a receipt, the case is different. Then the uncertainty is factual: did payment occur before or after the note arose? The debtor already possesses the money, and the creditor seeks to extract. If the receipt can reasonably cover the debt, the burden rests on the creditor to prove otherwise. This explains the two Rosh responsa. In the first case, the debtor's position is fundamentally, "I paid." Rosh therefore discusses whether same-day payment can occur. If payment is possible, and the receipt can be read as covering all claims through that day, we do not extract money from the debtor out of doubt. The witnesses may have used "until" in the broader Torah sense, and uncertainty favors the possessor. In the second responsum, however, the debt is known not to have been paid. The debtor relies only on a waiver. Now the question is purely interpretive: does the language of waiver clearly include the debt arising that very day? Since ordinary human speech treats "until" as excluding the endpoint, and the waiver is not clear, the established note remains in force. This is analogous to the rule that a writing saying "your document is forgiven" is construed narrowly. The same expression can therefore receive different legal treatment depending on whether the issue is payment or pure waiver. There is also no contradiction concerning the phrase "until now." There the uncertainty is not over the semantic scope of "until" but over factual sequence. If the waiver says "until now" and we do not know whether the note was written before or after the waiver, the debtor can say perhaps the waiver came later. That is a factual uncertainty affecting possession, not merely ambiguity in waiver language. Accordingly, we do not extract money. The two responsa of Rosh are therefore consistent without inventing a special rule that the word "this" makes the endpoint included.
