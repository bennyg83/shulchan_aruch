According to ordinary language. See Tumim. Many authorities note that this responsum seems to contradict the immediately preceding rule, because there too the receipt said the creditor waived all claims through that day. Shach distinguishes between “through this day,” which includes the day, and “through the fifth of Nisan,” which may exclude it. I reject that distinction. I instead distinguish according to the debtor's claim. In the previous seif, the debtor says he actually paid and the receipt reflects that payment. Where the uncertainty concerns payment, it resembles the case of one who says one of several notes was paid, where the burden of proof lies on the creditor and the larger claim may be treated as discharged. Here, however, the debtor does not claim actual payment. He relies only on a deed of waiver. Where the doubt lies in the language of a waiver, the person relying on the waiver has the weaker hand, as in the rule that saying “my deed is waived to you” waives only the operative right actually conveyed. If the document says “until now,” the uncertainty is not in the language of the deed but in the factual sequence, whether the waiver was written before the debt document and therefore did not cancel it, or afterward and did. Since the doubt concerns historical fact rather than wording, the burden of proof governs. This resolves the cases.
