We give it to the one whose document says simply "Adar." Shach in Yoreh Deah siman 220 asks from Mechaber's ruling there following Rambam and R. Meir. R. Meir holds that when writing dates in a leap year, the first Adar is called "Adar Rishon" and the second may be written simply "Adar." Yet regarding vows, if someone vowed "until Adar," the law can depend on whether he knew the year had been intercalated. If he knew, the term can extend to the second Adar. Rosh explains that scribes ordinarily know whether the year was intercalated. How then can Mechaber rule here that a document saying simply "Adar" means first Adar? The answer may lie in the practical possibility that leap-year status is not always known when the document is written. We rule in Eduyot that the year can be intercalated throughout Adar until Rosh Chodesh Nisan. Suppose witnesses write a sale document on the twenty-eighth of Adar without knowing the year will be intercalated. They naturally write "Adar" without qualification. On the twenty-ninth, beit din declares a second Adar. Later, in the second Adar, the seller reacquires the field or the borrower acquires new property. Years afterward, a court sees the old document saying "Adar" and, if it automatically interprets that as second Adar, the paper becomes postdated. That could cause improper title or lien consequences. It is unrealistic to say that decades later every court will know this particular leap year was declared only at the very end of Adar and will therefore reinterpret the old writing. For that reason, documentary practice must be uniform. Whenever a document is written during the Adar period itself, the first Adar is written simply "Adar," while the second is written "Adar Sheni." This avoids later confusion regardless of when intercalation became known. The Talmudic discussion of R. Meir and R. Yehudah can be understood differently when the writing occurs after the calendar status is certainly known, such as from Nisan onward in a document referring back to Adar. At that point everyone knows there were two Adars. R. Meir can call the first "Adar Rishon" and the second simply "Adar," while R. Yehudah uses the opposite convention. That is the meaning of the distinction between a case where the intercalation is known and one where it is not. Before Nisan, one cannot always know which situation one is in, so scribal practice must use the safer uniform convention. From Nisan onward, where the leap year is certain, the Tannaitic naming rules can apply. This reconciles Mechaber's ruling here with Yoreh Deah. Kenesset HaGedolah adds that if a document says payment is due "at the end of Adar," that can mean the end of the second Adar. This fits Mechaber's rule in Yoreh Deah that "the end of Adar" means the second Adar. Ran questions that reading because if the speaker did not know the year was intercalated, why assume he meant the second month? One can answer that the phrase may mean, "at the end of whichever Adar is ultimately established as the concluding Adar of the year." If beit din intercalates the year, that becomes the second Adar. Because the phrase is inherently uncertain, laws of vows are stringent. For the same reason, one who says "until half of Adar" should, in my view, remain bound until the middle of the second Adar where the context is uncertain. This differs from Bach's lenient ruling. Where there is also an oath to pay by the end of Adar, however, the prohibitory doubt requires payment by the end of the first Adar to avoid a possible violation of the oath. Thus documentary dating and vow interpretation follow different practical pressures, and Mechaber's rulings are consistent.
