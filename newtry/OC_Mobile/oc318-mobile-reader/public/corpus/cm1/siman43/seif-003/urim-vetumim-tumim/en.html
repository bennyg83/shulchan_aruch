Handwriting, etc. Shach disputes Maharam Lublin concerning priority among creditors where two handwritten obligations bear different dates. Mechaber below, siman 104, rules that an earlier oral loan has priority over a later oral loan as to free assets, and even over a later written loan according to his view. Suppose there are two handwritten acknowledgments, one dated earlier than the other, or one handwritten obligation dated earlier than a later formal document. Maharam Lublin says the earlier handwritten obligation has priority. Since the writing was never impaired by being lost, we do not suspect collusion. Shach disagrees, apparently because the debtor could write the handwritten paper today and backdate it by many years in collusion with one creditor. He relies on Rashba's responsum cited at the end of siman 46 and on the fact that our law does not require dates in handwritten obligations. If dates had priority effect, Shach argues, we should require dating them. In my view, one must distinguish. Where the competition is between an earlier handwritten obligation and a later oral creditor, there is generally no reason to suspect collusion. If the debtor wants to deprive the later oral creditor, he can simply sell or give away the assets, or create many other valid transfers. There is no need for a fraudulent backdated writing. Rashba himself makes this distinction. A man wrote an acknowledgment that half his assets belonged to another person, dated before his wife's ketubah. Rashba feared collusion against the wife and refused to let the handwritten paper prejudice her ketubah lien. But regarding movables not subject to her enduring lien, Rashba says there is no reason to fear collusion. The husband could in any event dispose of them by sale or gift. Thus, for free movables and similar assets, the mere possibility of collusion is not enough. Maharam Lublin's position is therefore persuasive at least against a later oral creditor. The harder question is priority against a later written creditor, because according to Mechaber an earlier oral loan can sometimes take priority even over a later written loan as to free assets. Here backdating a handwritten paper could accomplish something the debtor cannot easily accomplish after the later formal lien exists. On that issue, Rashba's responsum gives Shach substantial support. Even so, Rashba's case may be distinguishable because the handwritten admission contradicted a strong presumption that assets in a person's possession belong to him. He claimed half his present assets were actually another person's. Such a writing is inherently suspicious against a powerful chazakah. Our ordinary handwritten debt does not contradict such a presumption. Tosafot and Rosh also hold that a judicial ruling can collect from purchasers despite allowing a personal repayment claim, because where the document has not been impaired by loss we do not automatically suspect collusion. That supports Maharam's general principle. But Tosafot in Gittin 3b provide a direct argument for Shach. They discuss a get written entirely in the husband's own handwriting without a date. They say that even if it had a date, the woman could not use it to recover fruits sold by the husband from that date, because purchasers could say the husband colluded with her after the divorce and antedated the handwritten get. This is very close to our case and indicates that handwritten instruments can indeed be suspected of backdating against third parties. Ramban, responsum 22, likewise remains uncertain whether to suspect collusion in handwritten obligations and leaves the burden on the claimant. Thus, against a later formal creditor, Shach's concern carries substantial weight. There is, however, an apparent proof for Maharam from Bava Metzia concerning a found loan document. Where a document contains no property warranty, the Gemara says it can be returned because it cannot harm purchasers. According to the view that an earlier oral loan has priority over a later written loan as to free assets, why is there no concern that the borrower wrote the document earlier but never borrowed, then incurred a later debt, and now colludes to revive the older paper and gain priority? The answer differs by Talmudic approach. According to Rav Asi and Rosh's explanation, even before delivery to the creditor, the signatures can create the substantive debt lien as between free assets; only purchaser publicity is lacking. Once the debtor admits the debt and the document reaches the creditor, the underlying priority can relate back in the relevant sense. Rashi and Rif understand differently, which is why Rav Asi needs another explanation for the found-document rule. According to Abaye, who is concerned about repayment and collusion, one can answer that the priority of an earlier oral debt over a later written debt depends on shi'abuda d'oraisa. Tosafot in Gittin indicate Abaye holds the lien is not Torah law. On his view the earlier oral debt may not have such priority at all. Alternatively, where the document cannot collect from encumbered property and the debtor can claim repayment, the later written creditor can himself argue that perhaps the earlier debt was already paid. Thus returning the paper does not necessarily give the earlier creditor an unjust advantage. There is also a broader Tannaitic point. The found-document discussion concerning no-warranty papers follows R. Meir. In the Bechorot framework, R. Meir may not accept the priority of an earlier oral loan over later creditors in the same way. Thus the Gemara is not a decisive proof for Maharam. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 13 sharpen the matter. They ask why the Sages' refusal to return even a no-warranty document is explained through concern for collusion rather than a simpler concern that the debtor repaid and now wants to borrow again on the old paper to save scribal fees. Their question assumes that if the old debt is revived, it may gain an earlier priority improperly even without a purchaser lien. They answer that such renewed borrowing is less likely than collusion. This suggests that priority among creditors can indeed be affected by the apparent earlier date of a document even where purchaser collection is absent. Still, the precise effect depends on whether the earlier debt is oral, written, and whether shi'abuda d'oraisa applies. The practical conclusion should therefore distinguish: An earlier handwritten obligation has priority over a later oral creditor as to free assets. There is ordinarily no meaningful collusion concern because the debtor had many lawful ways to prefer or transfer assets before the later debt. Against a later formal written creditor, however, the earlier handwritten date is not enough by itself. Because handwritten papers can be easily backdated and major authorities fear collusion, the handwritten creditor does not gain priority merely from the date. This is especially so because many authorities hold an earlier oral loan has no priority over a later written loan in any event. Thus, combining both considerations, Shach is correct in the latter case. This also explains why Chazal did not require dates in handwritten obligations. Dates are not needed for purchaser collection because handwritten obligations lack the necessary public notice. For free assets and competition with later oral creditors, the holder can show the paper to beit din or witnesses immediately and thereby establish practical priority without a statutory date requirement. The same reason Tosafot give for not requiring dates in certain gittin applies here: where third-party publicity is not the operative issue, the party can establish timing through outside proof. Accordingly, the absence of a mandatory date does not prove handwritten obligations never have priority. It proves only that their priority does not function like the public lien of a formal document. There is another conceptual distinction between collusion against purchasers and priority among unsecured creditors. A purchaser has given value for property and relies upon the public state of title and liens. Halachah therefore gives special weight to concerns that the debtor and creditor can fabricate an earlier handwritten date after the sale. An unsecured creditor competing only for free assets has no comparable reliance upon public records. His claim is simply that his debt arose at a particular time. If the debtor had validly preferred another creditor beforehand, sold the property, or given it away, that later creditor would have had no complaint. This is why the "what would he gain by lying?" argument is much stronger against later oral creditors. The debtor had many ordinary lawful methods to place the assets beyond them. There is no special incentive to forge an old handwritten obligation. Against a later written creditor, however, the question is more serious because the later document creates a formal lien and priority structure. Backdating a handwritten paper may then be the only way to insert an earlier claimant ahead of that formal lien as to free assets under Mechaber's system. Therefore the collusion concern becomes meaningful. Rashba's ketubah case belongs to this latter category. The wife held a preexisting formal lien whose priority could be damaged by a supposed earlier handwritten admission that half the husband's assets belonged to someone else. The husband had a motive to fabricate the earlier date specifically to defeat her lien. Thus Rashba's suspicion is not a general distrust of handwriting. It is tied to the third-party priority effect the handwriting is being asked to create. This also explains Tosafot's handwritten get. The husband could collude with the woman after the divorce and antedate the get to defeat purchasers of fruits. The date is being used directly against third parties who relied upon the husband's apparent ownership. That is precisely the setting where handwriting is least trustworthy for retroactive priority. By contrast, if the only dispute is between the debtor and the handwriting holder, and the debtor admits the writing is genuine, there is no reason to disregard the date absent another defect. The concern is not that handwriting has no evidentiary value. It is that its date lacks sufficient public reliability to bind strangers retroactively. This leads to a useful practical rule. A handwritten obligation can prove the debt and can establish its date between the immediate parties where the writing is admitted or authenticated. It can also establish priority against a later oral creditor in free assets where no special collusion motive exists. But it should not displace the priority of a later formal lienholder or purchaser solely through its written date, because the date can be fabricated privately without public notice. Independent outside proof of when the handwriting was actually delivered or acknowledged can alter the result. If witnesses saw the obligor hand the dated writing to the creditor years before the later formal debt arose, the concern of backdating disappears. Then the earlier chronology is independently proven. The dispute discussed by Shach and Maharam concerns reliance on the date in the handwriting itself, not a case with external proof of delivery. This distinction also answers the question why we do not simply omit all dates from handwritten obligations. A date remains useful between the parties and as corroborative evidence. It can also help where outside evidence confirms the writing existed at that time. What it lacks is the automatic public-lien effect of a formal document. Thus the fact that Chazal did not require a date does not mean a written date is meaningless. It means the date was not instituted as a mechanism for purchaser protection. Finally, this analysis explains the found-document sugya more simply. A no-warranty document found and returned to its admitted creditor does not itself create a retroactive purchaser lien. Its possible priority among free creditors depends on the underlying debt and the applicable rules of oral priority. Where the debtor could have achieved the same preference by lawful transfer, collusion is not presumed without a concrete defect. Once the paper has fallen, however, that defect can trigger concerns of repayment or collusion under the special laws of found documents. An ordinary handwritten obligation that has remained safely in the creditor's possession lacks that impairment. Maharam Lublin is therefore correct not to import the found-document suspicion automatically. The final halachic balance remains the distinction stated above: earlier handwritten priority against later oral debt can stand; priority against a later formal written lien should not be granted solely on the private handwritten date.
