Seif 28. Priority is given to the holder of the document that states simply “Adar.” See Shach here and in Yoreh Deah 220, where he concludes that the parties divide because Rambam holds that an unspecified Adar means Adar II. At first glance this is difficult. The holder of the document stating simply “Adar” is at least equal to the holder of a document explicitly dated Adar II if “Adar” means Adar II, in which case they divide; whereas if “Adar” means Adar I, the first holder receives everything. He is therefore certainly entitled to half and possibly to the other half. Why not say that a doubt cannot displace a certainty, as Urim VeTumim writes in siman 104? This may support what I wrote there, that where the uncertainty is not about a person's intent but about the governing law itself, the rule that a doubt cannot displace a certainty does not decide the matter. Even if the document writer personally intended Adar I, if the legal meaning of an unspecified Adar is Adar II, we follow the objective meaning of the document. This is similar to a document delivered in the middle of a day, which takes effect only from the end of that day because documents generally do not record hours. Thus the doubt is in the law itself, and the certainty principle does not apply. Alternatively, the case may be one where the document saying merely “Adar” contains a day of the month later than the day stated in the document expressly dated Adar II. If unspecified Adar means Adar II, the expressly dated document is earlier. If unspecified Adar means Adar I, the other document is earlier. There is therefore genuine doubt which document has priority, and they divide. If neither document states a day of the month, however, then even according to Shach the holder of the document saying simply “Adar” should prevail under Urim VeTumim's approach. Yet according to what I wrote in Choshen Mishpat 39, that the certainty principle does not apply to a doubt in law, they divide in every case. This resolves Shach's position. The Shulchan Aruch's statement that documents are interpreted like vows does not necessarily apply in our countries. It applies where people speak the language of the Torah. Where the spoken language is another language but the document is written in Hebrew, we follow Hebrew legal usage, as Shach writes in Yoreh Deah 217 in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham. The same should apply to documents. Seif 28. Two documents made in a leap year.
