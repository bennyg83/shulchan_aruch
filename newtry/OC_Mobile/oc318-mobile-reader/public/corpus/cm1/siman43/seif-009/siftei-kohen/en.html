None of this, etc. One might have thought that there is no concern that collection will be made from the first date, because in the year 57 the grantor was not yet described as his father in law, so the year 57 should be understood as 58 and the deed should take effect from the latter date. The ruling teaches instead that the deed is treated as though the identifying description were absent, making it antedated and invalid. The Mechaber then explains the practical consequences. If the witnesses' signatures are not otherwise authenticated, the matter depends on them: they are believed to say the transaction occurred in 58 and may write a replacement deed dated from the second date, not the first. If their signatures are independently authenticated, they are not believed that it occurred in 58. If the recipient says the kinyan occurred in 57, he is believed; if he concedes that it occurred in 58, the original deed is invalid, though he is believed to say the discrepancy was an error by virtue of the migo that he could have maintained the deed was entirely valid. The Ir Shushan and Sema understood that when the signatures are independently authenticated the witnesses cannot write another deed, but that distinction is unpersuasive. They may write a deed from the second date even then; the Rashba and Mechaber mean as explained here. The Ir Shushan's alternative formulation also appears erroneous and likely contains a textual error: it should read 'if their signatures are authenticated from another source.' See the responsa of Maharam ibn Chaviv.
They may sign another deed dated from the second date. The Beit Yosef, citing Rashba, explains that they have not yet fulfilled their agency, since they were instructed to write in the year 58 and wrote 57. This reason seems unnecessary in an ordinary gift deed, for several gift deeds may be written. Nor can the case involve a gift with a warranty, because a replacement deed written only now could improperly enable collection from purchasers from the second date. Even where the witnesses possess a migo, an invalid first deed cannot justify creating retroactive lien rights. The authorities' treatment of antedated deeds proves that witnesses who admit a life threatening coercion or an error in the regnal year cannot simply issue a deed from the later date and thereby authorize collection. The same applies to a gift with a warranty. Rashba's conclusion that other witnesses who saw the kinyan in 58 may write a gift deed likewise concerns an ordinary gift, not one carrying warranty liability. In an ordinary gift, however, the witnesses may write from the second date because the deed merely confirms that the property belonged to the recipient from then, and the ability to issue another deed follows from the ordinary law of gift deeds, not from failure to complete their agency.
