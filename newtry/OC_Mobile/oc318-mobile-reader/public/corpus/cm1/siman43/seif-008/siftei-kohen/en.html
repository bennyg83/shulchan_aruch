When does this apply, and so forth. It applies when the date was not intentionally advanced, such as where the witnesses wrote a note for the borrower in the absence of the creditor and the loan was not actually made until later. Although ordinarily such a note may be written only when it contains an immediate act of acquisition, an unlearned person who did not know Rav Assi's rule is not thereby disqualified. So explain the Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Ribash, Ramban, Rashba, and Maggid Mishneh. This establishes that a note written for a loan in Nisan but not funded until Tishrei is an antedated note and, according to the authorities who reject Abaye's position, is entirely invalid even for collection from the later date. This follows the Tur against the Ramah, who allows collection from Tishrei where the note could lawfully have been written in Nisan. The Gemara, Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah, Baal HaItur, and Rashi support the Tur. The Rambam includes three cases as antedated: a paid note later reused for another loan, a note written in Nisan but funded only in Tishrei without an acquisition, and a loan made in Nisan whose date was advanced to Adar. In each case it does not collect even from the later date. Gidulei Terumah and Lechem Mishneh objected that a note whose lien was extinguished should be mere scrap and collect nothing at all. There is no difficulty. The Rambam's point was that the instrument is antedated. He may be discussing a case in which the note was newly delivered at the later time, or a new acquisition was made, before the original signed witnesses. Their delivery can legally recreate the obligation, yet the instrument remains invalid because its written date is earlier. This is why the Mordechai says that reuse is effective only on the original date. It also explains why the Tur cites and rejects the Ramah despite having ruled like Abaye elsewhere. The practical difference concerns a repaid note that is newly delivered before its original witnesses at a later time. According to the Ramah it is not antedated because it was originally fit to be written; according to the Tur it is antedated. The Tur's ruling is primary.
When they did not antedate it, etc. This applies in the circumstances I explain below in seif katan 19, or where, as stated immediately above in the name of the authorities, a deed was written for the borrower without the lender being present and the loan was not made until later, in which case the witnesses remain qualified. The Rivash and Maggid Mishneh indicate that, whether the deed was written for the borrower in the lender's absence or the error concerned the intercalation of the month, the witnesses themselves are believed even when their signatures are authenticated from another source, according even to Ramban. Although the Nimukei Yosef appears to imply that where a deed was written in Nisan for a loan not made until Tishrei they are believed according to Ramban only when their signatures are not otherwise authenticated, that passage can be reconciled by explaining that his later citation of Ramban does not address that case. The Ran likewise distinguishes an antedating caused by a common error, such as writing a deed for the borrower without the lender, and states after citing Ramban that an error witnesses commonly make is not barred by the rule against retracting testimony.
Some say that the witnesses are believed regarding this, etc. Meaning, even without claiming an error in the intercalation of the month or the like, they may say that the deed was antedated and that they did not notice, because they signed only as to the substance of the deed and not its date. They are therefore believed to say, 'We signed regarding this, but not regarding that.' The Sema cites the Rivash concerning witnesses who wrote four years when they should have written thirty four and says they are not believed because such an error is uncommon. This citation is difficult, for the Rivash does not say that, and the Beit Yosef expressly validates such a case. The text should apparently read that they wrote 104 when they should have written 134, as in the Rivash. Nor is this merely the ruling already stated above, because there the Rama teaches only that such a deed is invalid, perhaps where its invalid execution is otherwise known or the witnesses are unavailable, whereas here the point is that even witnesses who are present and claim error are not believed. Yet the Darkei Moshe itself first records the Rivash accurately and later records the version cited by the Sema. Their words therefore require study.
The same applies to every error, etc. The Rivash records that the Baal HaMaor concluded: the matter requires a great beit din and deliberate examination of the law. The Beit Yosef quotes this, and it also appears in the Ran and in the Baal HaMaor himself.
Some disagree. The Rama's words require study, for they imply that according to the dissenting opinion the witnesses are not believed even regarding an error they commonly make. That is incorrect. The dissenters are Ramban, Ri, and the Rosh, and they concede that witnesses are believed concerning a common error. The Ran, Maggid Mishneh, and Rivash all show that even Ramban accepts their testimony concerning an error in the intercalation of the month or another ordinary mistake. The Tur's contrast is narrower. The first opinion, that of the Baal HaMaor and Baal HaTerumot, believes the witnesses even when their signatures are independently authenticated and they merely say that they intended to sign the substantive transaction but did not attend to the date. Ri and the Rosh reject that broad proposition. Their discussion of coercion, an error in the regnal year, or intercalation explains how witnesses whose deed is shown by other testimony to be antedated may avoid being disqualified. It does not establish that the witnesses themselves can prove the deed antedated whenever their signatures are authenticated. An error in the regnal year is not common, so they themselves are believed only when their signatures are not otherwise authenticated; absent a claim of mistake they are not believed at all, because they would brand themselves wicked, as with a claim of a trust deed or coercion for money. By contrast, in a matter witnesses commonly mistake, reason indicates that Ri and the Rosh agree that the witnesses themselves are believed, since they follow Ramban and Ramban expressly concedes this. The Mechaber also rules generally in siman 29 that witnesses are believed concerning every common error and that this is not a forbidden retraction. Accordingly, the Rama's phrase 'some disagree' must be read as referring only to the opening part of his statement.
But if they antedated it intentionally, etc. The Beit Yosef cites this in the name of the Rivash, but the ruling is explicit in Tosafot, the Rosh, Hagahot Mordechai, Nimukei Yosef, the Ran, Maggid Mishneh in the name of Ramban and Rashba, Baal HaTerumot, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and many other authorities.
