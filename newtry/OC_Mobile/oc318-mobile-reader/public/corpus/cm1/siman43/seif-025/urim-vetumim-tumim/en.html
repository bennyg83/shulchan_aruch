They are found to have testified falsely, and the document is invalid. Shach, seif katan 41, asks from Mechaber's ruling below in seif 21 in the name of Ramah. There, if the currency differs between the place where the parties instructed the witnesses and the place where the document is written, the witnesses are told to write the place of the original instruction. If an incorrect place would invalidate the document after the fact, how could the witnesses ever be instructed to write the earlier place when they are physically executing the document somewhere else? Shach therefore argues at length for the view of Rashi, Tosafot, and their school in Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot, that an incorrect place does not invalidate ordinary civil documents after the fact and that the strict invalidity applies only to gittin. With due respect, the matter appears otherwise. The Sanhedrin sugya itself strongly indicates that a true place falsehood can invalidate. The Gemara discusses a document dated on the first of Nisan in the shemittah year whose witnesses are later contradicted about being at the stated place and time. The Gemara answers that perhaps the document was actually written earlier and merely postdated. Tosafot ask why we need that explanation. Why not say the kinyan itself occurred on the first of Nisan, as the date states, but the witnesses did not write the paper until Av, and when they wrote it they used their current place of writing? Then the date would be the true kinyan date and the place would be the later writing place. Tosafot answer that in a loan document one does not ordinarily write from the kinyan date in the manner under discussion, whereas in sale and gift documents that possibility exists. Tosafot follow their own view, discussed above at length, that a loan kinyan does not always permit writing from the kinyan date. But according to our accepted halachah, which follows the authorities who do permit a loan document to be written from the kinyan date, Tosafot's question returns with full force. Why does the Gemara need the complex explanation of postdating, which itself depends on additional conditions, such as the rules concerning receipts and, according to some authorities, after-acquired property? Why not simply say the transaction occurred in Nisan, the witnesses wrote in Av, and they used the place where they wrote? The unavoidable answer is that this would be an invalid combination. If the witnesses write the original kinyan date, they must use the original kinyan place. Writing the old date together with a later place means that the document, on its face, attributes the relevant legal act to a place where it did not occur. That is a direct falsehood. By contrast, there is a different permissible structure. Suppose the kinyan occurred in Nisan in Jerusalem. The witnesses later write the document in Tishrei in Bavel. They may preserve Jerusalem as the place of the kinyan and delay the date to Tishrei. This is not the same kind of falsehood. The place stated in the document remains the true place of the transaction. Only the date is postdated. Ran distinguishes exactly this way, and Nimukei Yosef appears to follow him. Nimukei Yosef's rationale is that people do not scrutinize dates with the same precision because lawful postdating is common, whereas a false place is much more serious. The witnesses always know where the relevant transaction occurred and where they themselves are writing. Accordingly, one may write the transaction place and delay the date. But one may not preserve the transaction date while substituting a false place. This also explains the Sanhedrin sugya precisely. When the Gemara says the document may have been postdated, the proper reconstruction is: the underlying transaction occurred earlier in the place named in the document; the witnesses wrote later and delayed the date; they retained the original transaction place. The later contradiction regarding where they physically stood on the written date does not prove false testimony, because the date was merely delayed. But if the document uses the original transaction date and names a place where the transaction did not occur, there is no comparable excuse. The witnesses have written an actual falsehood regarding place. This resolves Tosafot's question without requiring a broad distinction between gittin and other documents. The same emerges from Tosafot in Gittin 80a. At first they consider differentiating between a get and ordinary documents because a get that says the husband was in the east when he was in the west is invalid, whereas Sanhedrin seems to tolerate a place-time inconsistency in civil documents. But Tosafot's later answer removes the need for such a distinction. A postdated get is valid where the document truthfully states the relevant place and merely bears a later date. Even though the parties may no longer be in that place on the face date, readers can attribute the discrepancy to lawful postdating. But if the husband was in the east at the relevant act and the get says west, the document directly falsifies the place. That can be invalid. This is exactly the distinction stated above. Maharsha's comments likewise show that Tosafot's question from Sanhedrin is tied to their question concerning a postdated get and changed place. Once the second question is resolved through the distinction between true place with delayed date and false place with true date, the first question is resolved as well. Now Ramah and Mechaber below in seif 21 present no difficulty. Where currency varies by location, they instruct the witnesses to write the place of the original transaction or instruction even though the document is being written later elsewhere. That is precisely the permitted arrangement: preserve the true transaction place and use the later writing date. For example, a kinyan occurs in Nisan in Jerusalem, and the document is written in Tishrei in Bavel. The witnesses may write Tishrei and Jerusalem where the legal meaning depends on Jerusalem's currency. There is no direct falsehood about the transaction place, and the date is lawfully postdated. What is forbidden is the reverse: writing Nisan, the true kinyan date, but substituting Bavel, the later writing place. That falsely combines the old date with a place where the kinyan never occurred. Mechaber's phrase here, "they are found to have testified falsely, and it is invalid," therefore refers specifically to that first case: they preserve the kinyan date but change the place. This is invalid. Even Rashi need not disagree. Rashi's Sanhedrin explanation can be read as follows. The relevant transaction occurred in Adar in Bavel. The witnesses wrote the document only in Nisan. Although they were no longer in Bavel in Nisan, the document retains Bavel because it was the transaction place and merely delays the date. That is fully valid under Ran's framework. Rashi nowhere permits the opposite case of writing the original transaction date while changing the transaction place to the later writing location. Accordingly, there is no necessary dispute between Rashi and the stricter ruling here. Tosafot themselves ultimately support the same distinction. Thus the law is compelled: if witnesses use the original kinyan date, they must preserve the actual kinyan place; if they write a later date, they may retain the transaction place where that place is legally relevant; but they may not combine the original date with a substituted false place. A direct falsehood concerning place is invalid even in ordinary civil documents.
