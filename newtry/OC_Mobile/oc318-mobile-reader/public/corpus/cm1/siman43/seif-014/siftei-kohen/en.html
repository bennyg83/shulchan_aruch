If he says, 'Swear to me,' etc. The Sema and Bach understand this as a demand that the claimant swear the deed is not antedated. That reading is difficult according to the Mechaber, who rules above that an antedated deed may still collect from unencumbered property. It is also strained to confine the ruling to purchasers, because against purchasers an oath is required in any event. Rather, the debtor claims that the witnesses intentionally antedated the deed and thereby became disqualified for signing falsely. This accords with the Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot: after a deed has been authenticated we do not presume the witnesses disqualified, but if the debtor demands an oath the claimant must swear; if the deed has not been authenticated, the holder must first establish it.
'Swear to me.' He must swear that the deed is not antedated even though the debtor does not demand the usual oath that the debt was not repaid. A claim that the instrument is a trust deed or was written for a contemplated loan never made does not ordinarily require an oath, but here the date falls on Shabbat and thus creates an evident defect suggesting antedating. Absent such an apparent defect, the debtor cannot require an oath that the deed is not antedated. The case may be one where the debtor says he repaid but deliberately avoids asking for the ordinary repayment oath, because he fears the claimant may rationalize swearing falsely, while he believes the claimant will not swear that the deed is not antedated. Alternatively, the debtor denies borrowing; with a valid deed he could not require the claimant to swear that the loan occurred, but the Shabbat date creates a specific defect requiring an oath. The Tur's further rule that every deed found must be shown to have reached the holder from its written date has troubled later authorities because the Tur elsewhere accepts Abaye's rule that a signed deed creates rights from signing. The Bach's proposed distinction, that a fallen deed may have been ordered delivered to the borrower, conflicts with numerous passages proving that according to Abaye the rule applies even when the deed is delivered to the borrower. More fundamentally, proof of delivery would not remove the separate concern of repayment and collusion. The Tur's language is copied from the Rosh's discussion of bills of divorce. It therefore concerns a document analogous to a get, where neither repayment and collusion nor the doctrine that signatures create prior rights applies. Thus, whenever a document of that type is impaired by having fallen, proof is needed as to when it reached the holder. This also explains the Tur's earlier disagreement with Ramah concerning a get written in Nisan and delivered in Tishrei.
