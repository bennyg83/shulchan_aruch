Sale transactions, etc. Shach writes that the same rule should apply to a gift document because the reason is identical. Although this is plausible, there is room to distinguish. Tosafot in Bava Batra 164b explain the concern with postdating as harm to purchasers. As to harm suffered by the original debtor or transferor himself, they say he caused his own loss by using or accepting that document. If so, one might say the special invalidity of a postdated transfer document applies only to a sale, where third-party purchasers can be harmed. Suppose the original owner sells the field, later buys it back, and then the first buyer produces an earlier postdated deed. If the first buyer had conveyed the property or incurred liabilities meanwhile, innocent purchasers can be affected. The Sages were concerned for those outsiders. A gift without warranty is different. If the donor later reacquires the field and then sells it to someone else, the original donee may produce the postdated gift document and claim his gift preceded the later purchaser. But here the later purchaser could have investigated. A gift of land is itself a public matter, as Beit Yosef writes below in siman 239. If the purchaser chose to buy from the original donor without verifying whether the donee still held a document, he may be treated as having caused his own loss. Tosafot explicitly focus on harm to purchasers in a sale context, and therefore it is not obvious that every gift document shares the same rule. This distinction helps explain why the sources repeatedly mention sale documents and not gift documents. The issue becomes clearer through Tosafot later in Bava Batra 172b. They discuss the rule that if witnesses know the date of the kinyan they write that date, and if not they write the day of writing. Rashbam limits the rule to a gift. Ri says it applies to both sale and gift, and even in a sale the witnesses can write from the kinyan date where the document is prepared with the knowledge of both parties, because there is public notice and no purchaser is harmed. Maharsha explains that "with the knowledge of both" is intended to distinguish the rule from the Mishnah allowing a document to be written for the seller even when the purchaser is absent. That explanation is difficult. Where a kinyan occurred, the kinyan itself generates publicity. Why should the purchaser's presence when the document is written matter? Maharsha himself elsewhere recognizes that acquisition documents can be written even without the other party because the kinyan has a voice. And if the writing occurs today but is dated from the earlier kinyan day, present joint consent cannot create publicity retroactively. Mordechai states Ri's position more clearly: whether sale or gift, the witnesses can use the kinyan date because a standard kinyan stands for writing and witnesses publicize it from that day. That applies even if only the seller was present at the kinyan. So Maharsha's explanation cannot be the whole point. There is also a question why Ri mentions only sale and gift. If kinyan itself creates publicity, the same should apply to a loan. Many commentators struggled with this. The correct approach, in my view, distinguishes two separate problems: antedating and postdating. Rashbam's limitation to a gift is not necessarily because an earlier kinyan date cannot work in a sale. Rather, the difficulty lies in the second half of the rule: if the witnesses do not know the kinyan day, they write the day of writing. In a gift without warranty, a later date may not create the same harm as in a sale, and the recipient's own later conduct can often be treated as having accepted that risk. In a sale, however, a postdated document can create purchaser harm, as Beit Yosef and Sema discuss below in siman 239. Ri therefore must explain why writing the later day can still be valid in a sale. The phrase "written with the knowledge of both parties" supplies that explanation. When both parties participate, the document can state or reflect the true payment situation. Tur and Mechaber below in siman 238 distinguish a sale document written at the seller's request alone from one written with both parties' knowledge. If only the seller requests the document, it must state that the purchaser already paid the price. But if both parties are present and consent, the paper can state that the price remains unpaid. That matters greatly here. The danger from a postdated sale document arises where the first purchaser later returns the field or where the original seller reacquires it, and the first buyer can then use the delayed deed to assert a later title. But if the document itself shows that the purchase price was still outstanding, then the original seller who later reacquires must account for that unpaid price. The feared hidden second title becomes far less damaging. At minimum, the relevant monetary adjustment remains visible. Thus Ri can permit a postdated sale document where it was written with both parties' knowledge and in a form that does not conceal completed payment. This explains Tosafot's wording better than Maharsha's account. Their conclusion, "there is no loss to purchasers because sale transactions have publicity," addresses the antedating side: from the kinyan date the sale has a voice. Their phrase "written with the knowledge of both" addresses the postdating side: the later writing can be framed so it does not create the usual hidden harm. This also explains why the same discussion does not extend readily to an ordinary loan. Before R. Safra's enactment requiring undated receipts, a postdated loan document was invalid because repayment might occur before the face date and the debtor could later be harmed. There was no general solution merely through joint knowledge at writing. Therefore the Gemara's rule, stated by an Amora from the period before R. Safra's later receipt practice, naturally concerns sales and gifts rather than loans. Mordechai's statement that a kinyan has publicity even in a loan remains true for the earlier-date question. But the later-date alternative can still fail in a loan for the separate receipt problem. This resolves the apparent contradiction. One can also understand Tosafot in Gittin 18a. They say that if a kinyan was made on an additional ketubah obligation before Shabbat and the document was signed after Shabbat, the earlier kinyan can support the lien because the arrangement has publicity comparable to acquisition documents. Although Tosafot elsewhere say a loan kinyan may not always create the same immediate documentary publicity under Abaye's theory, an additional ketubah obligation is more like a fixed public undertaking. Tosafot in Gittin 51a treat a fixed obligation of that sort as comparable to a sale before witnesses, especially once reinforced by kinyan. Thus there is no contradiction. A deeper way to state the dispute is this. R. Asi's approach treats kinyan itself as generating the creditor's public lien. Abaye can hold that, in an ordinary loan, even numerous kinyanim do not produce the same public lien until a document is written, because his theory is that the witnesses acquire for the creditor through their signatures. According to Abaye, the writing is what fixes the public documentary lien. This explains Tosafot in Sanhedrin 32, where they say a loan document written later from the earlier kinyan date can still be antedated under Abaye. By contrast, a sale before witnesses can create publicity independently of the document because the field itself changes ownership in a public transaction. Thus Tosafot can accept an earlier sale kinyan date under Abaye while rejecting the same move for an ordinary loan. Rashbam's apparently contradictory comments can be reconciled similarly. Here, in the passage about acquisition documents, Rashbam limits the use of the kinyan date in loan documents and follows Abaye's practical framework. Elsewhere, Bava Batra 157b, he explains the law according to R. Asi, where a loan kinyan itself can generate public lien force. He expressly closes that discussion by contrasting it with Abaye. Thus the two comments address different Amoraic structures. Accordingly, even though my own initial inclination was to distinguish gifts from sales more sharply, for practical law one should not depart from Shach's ruling. A postdated gift document should be treated with the same caution as a postdated sale document. The sources concerning postdating, publicity, and later conflicting transfers are broad enough that there is no safe basis to validate gifts categorically. Shach's conclusion therefore stands, even though the conceptual route is more complex than simply saying "the same reason applies." A further point clarifies why the sale case is more severe than the gift case. Where the first transaction was a sale, the purchaser may have a warranty claim against the seller if the field is later taken from him. That warranty itself can travel with the transaction and affect later purchasers. A postdated sale document can therefore distort not only title chronology but also the order of warranty claims. If the seller reacquires the field and then transfers it again, a delayed earlier deed can make it appear that the first purchaser's title, and therefore his warranty rights, arose later than they truly did. That can change which assets are reachable and which purchaser bears the loss. This is exactly the type of third-party harm for which Chazal invalidated postdated instruments. In a pure gift without warranty, those secondary consequences are weaker. This supports the initial distinction. Nevertheless, practical halachah cannot rest on that distinction alone because title itself can still be harmed through a postdated gift. Suppose the donor gave the field in Nisan, took it back in Tammuz, and then sold it to a purchaser in Elul. If the donee holds a gift document falsely dated in Tishrei, the later purchaser can be displaced even though the gift had actually ended before his purchase. The lack of warranty does not eliminate the basic title distortion. This is why Shach's conclusion has force even if the sale rationale is broader. The reason Tosafot emphasize purchasers may simply be that their sugya deals with a sale context, not that gift documents are categorically exempt. There is also a useful distinction between publicity created by the transaction and publicity created by the paper. A sale of land before witnesses can itself generate a voice because people know ownership changed. A gift likewise can generate publicity, though perhaps less strongly depending on circumstances. A loan, by contrast, remains a private personal obligation unless there is a kinyan or document. Thus when Tosafot say that sale transactions have publicity, they are not necessarily attributing every aspect of public notice to the kinyan. They may be relying on the underlying transfer of the field itself. This helps explain why the same language is harder to apply to loans. It also explains why Maharsha's reading, though difficult as a complete explanation, captures part of Tosafot's point. Where the sale document is written with both parties' knowledge, the purchaser's agreement confirms that the transfer is complete and public. Where only the seller orders the writing, the purchaser may not yet have accepted or completed the transaction. There is then less reason for witnesses to spread a definite voice that the field was sold. Thus "with the knowledge of both" can matter to publicity not because the writing itself retroactively creates a voice, but because it proves the transaction had already become final enough to generate one. This can be combined with the explanation above concerning unpaid purchase money. Both considerations point in the same direction. It is also possible to resolve the Mordechai-Tosafot tension through the difference between R. Asi and Abaye more sharply. R. Asi understands the kinyan itself as a completed public act. Therefore, once witnesses see a kinyan, they may later write from that day. Abaye says the witnesses' signatures acquire for the creditor. According to him, before the writing there may be no public creditor lien at all. Yet in a sale, title can still pass through another acquisition mechanism such as money, chazakah, or kinyan, and the sale itself can be public. Therefore Abaye's narrower theory concerning loan liens does not prevent writing the sale document from the actual acquisition day. In other words, "a kinyan has a voice" is not one universal rule operating identically in every legal category. Its effect depends on what legal relationship the kinyan is creating. A land transfer has inherent public consequences. A loan lien is more abstract and may require a formal document to bind outsiders under Abaye. This distinction resolves several passages that otherwise appear inconsistent. The same reasoning helps with the additional ketubah obligation in Gittin. A fixed support or ketubah obligation undertaken by kinyan is closer to a public family obligation than to an ordinary private loan. Once the husband publicly undertakes it by kinyan, the witnesses can spread the matter even before the formal writing. Thus Tosafot can compare it to sale documents without adopting the full R. Asi model for every loan. Accordingly, the practical rule should be stated with care. For a sale, a postdated document is generally problematic because it can distort title and warranty priority. For a gift, even without warranty, similar title distortion can occur and therefore the same caution should apply. Where all affected parties knowingly agree to the later date and the document itself makes the true transaction structure clear, there can be room for validation. But absent such protective circumstances, we should follow Shach and not distinguish gifts from sales merely because warranty is absent.
