They are no longer believed. Sema and Ir Shushan explain that the witnesses are no longer believed to write a second document. Shach, seif katan 21, objects: why should independently authenticated signatures prevent them from writing a new document from the true later date if they never completed their original agency? And if they are not believed at all, why should their explanation invalidate the first document? Shach therefore interprets the ruling in the opposite direction, that the witnesses are not believed to invalidate the original document. That reading cannot be sustained. Rashba's responsum and Mechaber's language begin by saying that nothing saves the original document from invalidity. Mechaber then says, "Nevertheless, the recipient of the gift is believed..." That "nevertheless" makes no sense if the document remains valid because the witnesses are not believed. The correct explanation is as follows. Through the recipient's own admission, it is known that the actual kinyan and gift occurred in year 58. Therefore the document dated year 57 is certainly antedated and invalid. It does not matter whether the witnesses say they made a mistake. Even an innocent mistake does not make the false earlier date operative. The remaining question is the personal status of the witnesses. Rashba holds that confusing year 58 with year 57 is not an ordinary mistake witnesses commonly make. The witnesses themselves admit that the kinyan occurred in 58. If their signatures are independently authenticated, we cannot simply assume a different transaction occurred in 57 to preserve them. They signed a false year. They also cannot save themselves by saying, "We merely made an uncommon mistake," because once their signed testimony is independently fixed, they have no migo and are not believed to retract through an implausible explanation. Nor do they have a migo that they could have said, "The kinyan really occurred in 57." That would be a migo requiring two witnesses to coordinate a common false account, a migo by two people, which we do not use. A migo from silence does not help either. If one witness remained silent while the other said the kinyan occurred in 58, that would still leave testimony contradicting the year 57 document. In addition, the recipient himself knows the kinyan occurred in 58 and has no reason to lie about a fact that harms the witnesses. Thus the witnesses become disqualified. Once they are disqualified, they cannot write a replacement instrument even though their first agency was not completed. The obstacle is no longer exhausted agency. It is their personal invalidity as witnesses. If, however, their handwriting is not independently authenticated, they have a different migo: they could have denied that these were their signatures at all. In that situation, their statement, "We signed but mistakenly wrote the wrong year," can be believed through migo to preserve their own fitness. The original document still remains invalid because its date is wrong. But the witnesses themselves remain valid, and because they never completed the original agency they may write a corrected document. This is the meaning of Rashba's distinction. There is then a further case. Suppose the recipient himself says with certainty, "I know they wrote the earlier year only by mistake." Even if the witnesses' signatures are independently authenticated and they would otherwise be unable to save themselves, the recipient has a migo concerning his own claim. He could have supported the original document by saying the kinyan truly occurred in year 57, especially where the document itself gives him that apparent right. Therefore, as between him and the donor, he can be believed sufficiently to receive a corrected document from these witnesses, because his own claim supplies the basis for treating their false date as mistake rather than deliberate false testimony. Mechaber abbreviates Rashba here. In the full responsum, Rashba first gives the recipient a migo to say the entire matter occurred in year 57. He then discusses the harder variation where there were witnesses who saw the signing in year 58 and the recipient instead says the earlier date was merely a mistake. This is why Mechaber's wording can appear compressed. The core structure is nonetheless clear: the first document is invalid because it is objectively antedated; if the signatures are not independently authenticated, the signatories can preserve their own fitness through migo and then write a new document because their agency was never properly completed; if the signatures are independently authenticated, they lack that migo, are not believed concerning an uncommon error, and become disqualified from writing another document; but a recipient who himself has an adequate migo can sometimes rely upon his own definite claim that the witnesses merely erred and obtain a corrected instrument. This is the correct reading of Rashba and Mechaber, and it fully vindicates Sema against Shach's reversal of the ruling.
