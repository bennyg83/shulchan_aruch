Any document, etc. The case is where the document fell and was found. Sema and Bach ask why Tur requires the claimant to prove that the document actually reached him on the day it was written. Tur follows Abaye, who holds that once witnesses sign at the borrower's request, they acquire for the creditor through their signatures. If so, actual delivery that day should not be necessary. Bach answers that in a lost-document case we fear the borrower may have expressly instructed the witnesses to sign and deliver the paper back to him rather than to the creditor. Abaye's rule would then not apply. Shach objects that this contradicts the whole sugya in Bava Metzia concerning a found loan document. According to Abaye, the reason the document is not returned is concern for payment and collusion. If Bach's concern were valid, the Gemara should instead say perhaps the borrower instructed the witnesses to give him the paper and no lien ever arose for the creditor. In my view, Bach's explanation is essentially Tosafot's. Tosafot ask why Abaye needed his theory that the witnesses acquire for the creditor. Even if he agreed with Rav Asi that there can be documents written for a contemplated future loan, the found document could still be withheld because of payment and collusion. Tosafot answer that the statement, "we do not say the witnesses happened to write improperly," is an independent point. The two principles are not the same. Abaye can hold that a normal document written at the borrower's request gives the creditor rights through the signatures, while separately holding that we do not suspect witnesses of violating the rules and writing in a form they were not permitted to use. This explains the found-loan sugya. If the borrower had expressly told the witnesses, "Sign and give the paper back to me," Abaye's acquisition-for-the-creditor mechanism would not necessarily apply in the same way. But witnesses ordinarily should not create a loan document that can later become antedated without the proper conditions. Why then should a found paper create that concern? Because if such improper writing were required to explain the case, Abaye refuses to assume "perhaps they happened to write improperly." He therefore needs the separate concern of repayment and collusion. The fact that Abaye accepts his acquisition-through-signatures principle does not itself eliminate the conceptual possibility of a paper the borrower instructed the witnesses to keep or return to him. The Gemara's point is that we do not ordinarily suspect valid witnesses of having created such a problematic paper contrary to law. Thus Bach and Tosafot can be reconciled. The two statements attributed to Abaye are independent: one, a properly created document can benefit the creditor through the witnesses' signatures; two, we do not normally suspect witnesses of having written contrary to law merely because a paper was found. Tur can accept the first principle and reject or limit the second in a case where the document has actually fallen and a concrete defect is before us. Then a found paper creates enough suspicion to consider the possibility that the borrower had ordered it written and kept in his own possession. Accordingly, proof of actual timely delivery can be required. Shach tries instead to establish Tur's rule specifically in a woman's get, where delivery is a detriment to the woman and Abaye's creditor-benefit theory does not apply. He then says this also solves Tur's objection to Ramah concerning a paper written in Nisan and delivered only in Tishrei. This does not fit Tur's language. Tur says "any document," not merely a get. If Shach means other instruments analogous to a get, the same original question returns: if Abaye's theory does not apply, why were the witnesses allowed to write the paper in advance at all? Shach also cannot comfortably establish Tur's dispute with Ramah in a get, because Tur himself validates certain get procedures through agency in Even HaEzer. Thus this is not the correct reading. The explanation I gave above concerning two people with the same name can solve Tur's dispute with Ramah without limiting it to gittin. Suppose the borrower had the document prepared in Nisan for one Yosef ben Shimon but did not borrow from him, and in Tishrei borrowed from another Yosef ben Shimon. The witnesses' Nisan signatures did not acquire for the second creditor, whom they did not have in mind. The paper can therefore be antedated as to the actual lender even under Abaye. Another possibility is that the borrower died before the paper reached any lender, and his heir later used it. Abaye's original signature mechanism cannot simply transfer to the heir's later transaction. These cases preserve Tur's dispute. Still, Sema's original question has another side. Even if the claimant proves the paper reached his hands originally, why return it now? We ordinarily fear that a lost debt document may have been paid and then lost from the debtor. Sema answers by establishing the case where it is known the document fell from the creditor. That is strained. If we know it fell from the creditor, then the concern that it was written for a loan that never occurred is equally reduced. The better answer is that Tur here is speaking not about a loan document but about a sale or gift document whose function is to maintain title to land. In those instruments, repayment is not the ordinary concern. The question is only whether the seller or donor wrote the paper but never actually delivered it, as the Mishnah says concerning a gift document, "Perhaps they were written but never given." Once the claimant proves actual delivery, that concern disappears. This also solves the Abaye problem elegantly. Even according to Abaye, we can fear that a seller or donor ordered a document written, then changed his mind and delivered the property to someone else on the same day. Abaye's acquisition-through-signatures principle does not necessarily give the first intended recipient full title from the beginning of the day. Tosafot and Rosh explain that even where the signatures operate, the first recipient can acquire only from the end of the day for certain priority purposes. If the donor actually delivered another sale or gift deed to a second person during that same day, the second recipient may acquire immediately and have the superior right. If we return the lost first document to the originally intended recipient, beit din might later see two documents bearing the same day and divide the property or use judicial discretion. That could give the first person some or all of a field he never truly acquired. This is a real concern. Tosafot and Rosh raise an analogous problem concerning a receipt under Abaye. A woman could write a receipt to her husband and then sell her ketubah that same day. The later purchaser could acquire before the receipt's effect reaches the husband at the end of the day. They answer that it is unusual for her both to receive payment from her husband and sell the ketubah the very same day, and sale of a ketubah is itself uncommon and risky. No such improbability exists with ordinary sales and gifts of land. People regularly negotiate with one person and then give or sell to another on the same day. The whole sugya of two same-day deeds exists precisely because such events happen. Therefore, in a found sale or gift document, we can fear that the paper was prepared but never delivered, while another person acquired the property that day. Returning the first paper would distort the law. This is why proof that it actually reached the claimant is required. The concern exists even though ordinary witnesses were entitled to prepare the paper initially. At the writing stage, we do not require witnesses to guard against every remote sequence. Once the document has fallen, however, there is a concrete defect and we consider possibilities that would otherwise be too remote to prevent writing. This distinction is necessary in many found-document sugyot. It also explains Maharsha's question about Abaye. Maharsha asks how witnesses can write a loan document for a borrower before the lender is present. Perhaps the borrower will delay the loan until the next day while selling property on the written day. Tosafot answer that even then the creditor does not necessarily collect ahead of the same-day purchaser. The concern in a loan is therefore limited. But in sale and gift documents, a same-day actual transfer to another person can directly determine title. Accordingly, if such a transfer document is found, it should not be returned without proof of delivery. This is the most natural reading of Tur. It fits the language "any document" insofar as the rule includes title instruments generally, while the practical concern in the present discussion is especially sale and gift documents. It also avoids the payment difficulty and reconciles the rule with Abaye. Thus Tur's ruling is sound. There is another point in Tosafot that supports this approach. The Gemara distinguishes between a document that was merely prepared for a contemplated transaction and one whose transaction already took effect. Abaye's theory that the witnesses acquire for the creditor through their signatures works only where the writing was executed in the legally authorized manner. If the borrower specifically instructed, "Prepare this and leave it with me," the signatures may be only preparatory. They do not necessarily create a present creditor right. That is why the possibility mentioned by Bach is conceptually real even if we do not usually suspect it. The found-paper defect changes the evidentiary posture. Before any paper is lost, we assume witnesses followed ordinary procedure. Once a document is found detached from its expected holder, the very fact of loss creates a reason to revisit possibilities that would otherwise be ignored. This principle appears repeatedly in the laws of found documents. A paper that would be fully enforceable in the creditor's hand can become unreturnable when found because the loss introduces concerns of payment, non-delivery, or collusion. The legal defect is not in the original writing itself. It is in the uncertainty created by the document's current status. This also answers why Tur can require proof of delivery even if the original witnesses were permitted to write. The permission to write says nothing about whether the document was in fact delivered. A seller can instruct witnesses to prepare a deed and then decide not to complete the sale. A donor can prepare a gift instrument and then retract before delivery where the transfer mechanism permits. If the paper later falls, returning it without proof of delivery can create a title that never existed. Abaye's doctrine does not eliminate this across all document types. Indeed, even within loans, the acquisition-through-signatures principle depends on the witnesses being authorized to act for the creditor. If the borrower set conditions limiting their authority, those conditions matter. One can also explain Tur through the difference between substantive acquisition and purchaser priority. Suppose the first recipient acquires some right through the signatures at the end of the day, as Tosafot explain. A second recipient who actually receives a deed earlier that same day may acquire immediately. If the first paper never reached its intended recipient at all, returning it later can make two same-day documents appear equal when in truth they were not. Beit din may then use shuda d'dayanei or another same-day allocation rule and award property incorrectly. Thus proof of actual possession on the day of writing is not redundant even if some abstract right arose through the signatures. This is especially important under Rabbenu Tam, who can give same-day documentary effects more immediate force. A later-found sale deed can interfere with a creditor whose lien arose that same day. The court therefore needs reliable evidence of when the paper actually entered the claimant's hands. There is also a practical distinction between a loan paper and a title document in terms of repayment. In a loan, once the document leaves the creditor's possession, the ordinary first suspicion is that the debt was paid and the paper was returned. In a gift or sale, there is no analogous "payment" extinguishing the title document in the same ordinary way. Therefore the principal suspicion shifts to non-delivery or superseding transfer. That is why the same found-document framework can generate different specific concerns in different instruments. This supports reading Tur's language broadly while applying the operative concern according to the type of document. A further difficulty arises from the rule that witnesses may write a document for a seller even though the purchaser is absent. If same-day competing transfers are a real danger, why did Chazal permit such writing at all? The answer is the familiar distinction between ordinary ex ante procedure and a paper already found out of place. Witnesses need not assume every seller who requests a deed will immediately change his mind and sell to another person the same day. That would make ordinary commerce impossible. But once the paper is found rather than presented by the expected holder, there is a concrete irregularity. The threshold for concern changes. This is exactly how Chazal treat found loan documents. Ordinary writing is permitted even though later repayment is possible. Once the paper falls, repayment becomes a real enough concern to block return. Thus there is no inconsistency. The same logic explains Tosafot's treatment of a receipt. Ordinarily we do not fear the woman will write a receipt and sell her ketubah the very same day. But if the receipt itself is found, the abnormal circumstance justifies greater caution. Where the underlying transaction, like sale of a field, is common and same-day changes are realistic, the concern is correspondingly stronger. This is why Tur's proof requirement is particularly sensible in sale and gift papers. Accordingly, the correct synthesis is: Abaye's rule validates ordinary authorized writing and can create a creditor benefit through signatures. It does not prove that every prepared document was actually delivered or that every intended recipient acquired immediate exclusive priority against same-day competitors. A found paper introduces a new defect. For loans, that defect usually raises payment and collusion. For sale and gift papers, it raises non-delivery and competing title. Therefore Tur can require proof that the paper truly reached the claimant on the relevant day without contradicting Abaye at all.
