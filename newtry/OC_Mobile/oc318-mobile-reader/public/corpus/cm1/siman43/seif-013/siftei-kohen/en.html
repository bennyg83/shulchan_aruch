But with deeds of sale. The same applies to a gift deed, as follows from the underlying reason. See the responsa cited in Mishpatim, siman 65, which discusses the Baal HaIttur at length.
Even postdated ones are invalid. The Gemara and the authorities explain the concern: the buyer may later resell the property to the original seller, then produce the postdated first deed and claim that he purchased it from him a second time. Ordinarily the original sale deed will not be surrendered, because returning the document does not reverse the sale or gift, and the document belongs to the buyer who paid the scribe. If the seller later wishes to repurchase, he must preserve his new deed like any other buyer, and the first buyer may refuse to surrender his own deed. This differs from a postdated loan deed, where the debtor receives the discharged deed and the instrument itself belongs to him. The Rashbam's forced explanation that the sale deed was lost and a receipt written is therefore unnecessary; the Gemara mentions loss and a receipt only regarding a loan deed. The Bach proposes additional reasons, such as prejudice to the first buyer against a creditor or an improper division between two lenders where the property was acquired between their loans. These reasons are untenable. A deed is not invalidated merely because its late date weakens the holder, who may accept that loss; invalidity is imposed where the deed gives its holder an improper advantage. Nor does the later date prove that the buyer first acquired the field then, since he may have acquired, resold, and reacquired it. The Gemara and all authorities give only the concern that the original seller may repurchase and then face the postdated deed. This also shows that a postdated sale deed knowingly retained by the seller is valid, for he will not repurchase without taking back the deed. The Bach's reasons would invalidate it even in the seller's possession, which is plainly contrary to the sources.
