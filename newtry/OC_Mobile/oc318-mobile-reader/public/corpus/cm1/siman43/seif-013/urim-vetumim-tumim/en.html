But with a sale document, etc. Shach explains Rashbam's case as follows. A person sold his field in Nisan on condition that if he later returned the money, the buyer would return the field. The seller then returned the money in Tammuz. When he asked the buyer to return the acquisition document, the buyer said, "I lost it." If the original sale document had been written with a later date than the real sale, the buyer could later claim, "I purchased the field again from you at that later time; here is my document." Shach asks why Rashbam needed to say the original document was lost. Even if it still existed, once the field is being transferred back, the buyer would need to execute a new sale to the original seller. Why could he not simply give back the original paper? This is no question against Rashbam. Rashbam rules that documents can be acquired by mere delivery. Therefore returning the original sale document could itself transfer the field back, like Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's rule that return of the document can effect return of the gift. Rashbam follows that rule in Perek HaSefinah. The more difficult question is why Rashbam established the case specifically as a conditional sale, where the original seller had a right to redeem upon returning the money, rather than a simple absolute sale followed by a later repurchase. Most authorities explain the problem that latter way. The answer is connected to the law of postdated sale documents. We say a postdated loan document can be valid because, if the debtor pays, he can take an undated receipt. If he fails to protect himself in that way, he caused his own loss. One might then ask the same about sales. When the original seller later buys the field back, why not require him to take an undated sale document, so the former buyer can never later rely upon an older-looking postdated deed? One cannot answer that the later seller may refuse to write an undated document. A person selling a field generally needs the money and is therefore under pressure to complete the transaction on the buyer's terms, just as a creditor must provide a receipt if he wants payment. Rashbam therefore deliberately establishes a different case. The first seller did not later decide voluntarily to repurchase. He had originally sold only on a condition that when he repaid the money, the field had to be returned. The buyer is therefore not a willing seller who needs cash. He is compelled by the original condition to return the field and naturally wishes to preserve every possible obstacle to giving it back. No one can force him to issue an undated sale document that would permanently deprive him of any future claim based on the original paper. The original seller, eager to recover his field, may therefore accept a document bearing the current date. That creates the danger from the earlier postdated document. Ramban asks generally why the returning seller cannot protect himself with an undated receipt. He answers that in an ordinary repurchase, the second buyer may need a dated instrument for purposes of warranty. Rashbam may hold, like Tosafot in Bava Batra 46, that a purchaser is not primarily concerned about later creditors in this setting, and the main warranty concern is that the property be stolen. That issue does not arise here because the same person had sold the field originally. Thus Rashbam did not rely on Ramban's warranty answer. There is another, and perhaps stronger, explanation of Rashbam. Rashbam elsewhere says that where witnesses know the day of a kinyan in a sale or gift, they may write that day, and if not, they write the day of writing. But a sale or gift document that is postdated can create a serious defect, as discussed above and below in siman 239. How then can Rashbam permit writing the later day? The answer is that in ordinary absolute sales and gifts there is usually little fear that the original seller will later reacquire the property and then be harmed by a delayed original document. Since the law allows delayed documents, a prudent repurchaser will suspect that the original buyer may possess one and will not reacquire without first seeing the old instrument or otherwise securing himself. If the original buyer says, "I lost the document," that itself should awaken suspicion. People normally guard title documents carefully. Moreover, an ordinary purchaser who wants the property can buy elsewhere. He need not accept a risky transaction from this seller. The conditional-redemption case is different. The original seller is entitled to recover this particular field once he returns the money. He cannot simply go buy another field instead. The buyer, meanwhile, has no incentive to cooperate beyond the strict terms of the condition. Most importantly, the buyer cannot safely postdate the first sale document to extend the redemption period. The original arrangement necessarily had a fixed term. Otherwise the buyer's enjoyment of the fruits could create a ribbit problem, since the first seller could redeem at any time. The transaction must therefore be structured like a time-limited mortgage or conditional sale, as discussed below in siman 207. If the buyer artificially extended the original date, he would extend the period during which the seller could redeem and thereby harm himself. The original seller therefore has no reason to suspect the first deed was deliberately postdated. He may repurchase or redeem in reliance on the apparent date, and the buyer could later produce the delayed original deed to his detriment. That is why Rashbam chose this special case. After R. Safra instituted the use of undated receipts, however, the analysis changes. With loans, postdated documents become acceptable because repayment can be protected through an undated receipt. In this conditional-sale case, the seller who redeems can likewise receive an undated writing stating that the field was redeemed and returned to him. There is no purchaser-warranty concern. Once the original buyer's conditional interest is extinguished by redemption, his creditors or successors have no claim to the field beyond the debt or security interest itself. Thus the same receipt mechanism can protect the returning owner. It follows that under our later law, postdated instruments can be valid not only in loans but, under the proper conditions, also in sale and gift settings. This helps explain why below we sometimes allow witnesses who do not remember the kinyan date to write the present date, even though it is later. The ordinary concern is no longer enough by itself to invalidate every postdated transfer document. This also fits the opening sugya of Perek Get Pashut. A folded document written as a plain document can become postdated. The Gemara's concern is that the debtor may borrow and repay and later the old paper will be used against him. It answers through the rule that receipts are not written according to that opinion. The discussion speaks broadly of documents, including the sale and transfer models from which folded and plain document forms are derived. Rashbam's conditional-sale case explains how the same postdating concern can arise in transactions other than an ordinary loan. Accordingly, his interpretation is precise and internally consistent.
