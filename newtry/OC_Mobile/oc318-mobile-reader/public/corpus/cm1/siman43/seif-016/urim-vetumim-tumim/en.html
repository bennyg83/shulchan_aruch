If he says, "Swear," etc. Shach asks: even if the document is antedated, Mechaber holds it still collects from free assets. If the claim is against purchasers, the creditor must swear anyway because one does not collect from purchasers without an oath. Shach answers that if the document is antedated, the witnesses become disqualified and the entire document collapses. The question can be answered even without that point. The oath ordinarily imposed when collecting from purchasers concerns payment and similar ordinary defenses. Beit din does not automatically raise every unusual defect in a document on behalf of purchasers. An allegation that the document is antedated resembles a claim that it was a trust document or another unusual invalidating circumstance. Unless the purchaser himself raises that claim or there is some visible defect, beit din does not necessarily plead it for him. Accordingly, it is enough to say that if the purchaser alleges antedating, the creditor must swear to answer that claim. There is no reason for beit din to impose such an oath sua sponte. Nevertheless, the entire discussion ultimately must revolve around witness disqualification. A document dated on Shabbat contains the chronological defect on its face. There is no realistic concern that the creditor will actually collect from an earlier improper date without the court noticing. The same Gemara says that according to the authority who invalidates postdated documents because receipts are written, even he agrees to validate a paper where the later date is self-evident from the writing. Similarly, Tosafot ask why a document bearing a Shabbat date should not simply be explained by calendar error. They assume that aside from witness status, the document can be preserved because the date problem is apparent. Thus the remaining concern is whether the signatories themselves are invalid. If the date could reflect a common calendar mistake, the witnesses retain their fitness. If it could not, their false dating can disqualify them and thereby destroy the paper entirely. This helps identify the scope of the oath mentioned by Baal HaItur. The creditor is being required to swear that the document is not antedated in a manner that would establish the witnesses as false signatories. Accordingly, Tosafot's resolution that the case occurs after most of the month is important. Before most of the month, a one-day calendar error is common enough that the witnesses would not be disqualified. In that setting, what substantive defect remains that requires the special oath? This is inconsistent with Sema's broader view above that even in writing witnesses can make such calendar mistakes after most of the month. It is also inconsistent with Shach's broad reading of Razah, under which witnesses can always say that regarding the date they did not sign carefully, even in matters not commonly mistaken. If the witnesses could always escape personal disqualification by such an explanation, the oath here would lose its entire function. Baal HaItur himself follows Razah in the earlier discussion, yet this law shows that his position cannot be as broad as Shach describes it. Even before most of the month, however, the oath can have a role if the creditor alleges a chronology involving more than one day. Suppose the written date falls on Shabbat. A one-day difference could be explained as an ordinary calendar mistake. But perhaps the loan actually occurred on Monday, two days later, and the witnesses deliberately or negligently antedated it to the Shabbat date. A two-day error is not an ordinary calendrical mistake. The witnesses could then be disqualified. This explains Tur's wording. Tur says we do not fear that the loan was actually made on Monday. Rambam speaks of Sunday. Perishah already noted the difference and Sema gives a strained explanation. The better answer is that Tur is citing Baal HaItur's additional oath rule. If the only concern were that the loan occurred on Sunday, a one-day calendar mistake would preserve the witnesses. To create the special concern that justifies the oath, Tur chooses Monday, where the date is two days early and ordinary mistake is no longer plausible. Rambam, who omits Baal HaItur's oath law, can state the simpler Sunday possibility. Thus the language is exact. Accordingly, the oath is directed toward the possibility of a true antedating substantial enough to disqualify the witnesses, not merely the obvious one-day calendar discrepancy.
