His own handwriting need not contain a date. See Shach, who says that even against another creditor we are concerned for collusion if an earlier date was inserted intentionally. Urim VeTumim argues that against a later oral creditor there should be no concern, because the debtor could in any event remove his property through gift or sale, and even against a later written creditor some authorities do not suspect collusion absent an independent defect in the instrument. In my view the Shach is correct. Ritva distinguishes between collusion used actively to collect from purchasers, which we do not readily suspect absent a defect, and collusion used to prevent another creditor from collecting from the debtor's own assets, which is much more plausible and resembles one who admits an accommodation document. The same applies even against an oral creditor. The debtor's theoretical ability to give property away does not make him believed in court to say he already obligated himself to someone else, because creating a false obligation to defeat a creditor has the appearance of wicked evasion. This is explicit in siman 99. If the handwritten acknowledgment was established before the other property was placed under judicial control, different considerations apply. But once the assets are before beit din, a newly produced handwritten obligation can be suspected as collusive. The Shach's analogy is therefore sound.
