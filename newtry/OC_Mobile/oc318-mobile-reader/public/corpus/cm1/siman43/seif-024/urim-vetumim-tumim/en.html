Therefore it is written from the beginning of Cheshvan, etc. The source is the Gemara's rule: if the witnesses know the day of the kinyan, they write that day; if they do not, they write the day of writing, because otherwise the document appears false. The commentators ask: if they do not know the true kinyan date, of course they cannot simply invent another date. Perhaps the invented date would precede the kinyan and create an antedated document. Rashba and Baal HaTerumot therefore explain that even where the witnesses know a day that is certainly safe, the rule of "appearing false" can still prohibit using it. For example, they know the kinyan occurred sometime during Tishrei but do not remember which day. They are certain it did not occur in Cheshvan. One might think they can write the first of Cheshvan, because that date is certainly later than the transaction and therefore creates no antedating. The Gemara says they should not. Neither the kinyan nor the writing occurred on that day. Writing it as the document date creates the appearance of false testimony. Shach, seif katan 40, disagrees. He says the Gemara merely prohibits witnesses from preparing a document with an uncertain date and holding it until they later discover the true date. If the date is never clarified, they should destroy the draft. According to Shach, authorities who generally do not fear "appearing false" need not accept the present rule. He says this is why Rosh omits it in Perek Get Pashut. That was apparently Shach's principal basis for disagreeing with Rashba and Baal HaTerumot. But Shach did not then have before him Rosh's words in Perek HaMerubeh. There Rosh discusses a power of attorney and says that although some forms of "appearing false" are not a concern, everyone agrees to the type in Perek Get Pashut: "If you know the day of the kinyan, write the day of the kinyan," precisely so that the paper not appear false. Rosh adds that the two cases differ. In an ordinary case, the problem is merely that the writing looks inaccurate. In Perek Get Pashut, the witnesses can suffer a far more serious appearance: they may later be shown not to have been in the stated place on the invented date and will appear to be false witnesses. This shows that Rosh accepts the Gemara as practical halachah and necessarily interprets it like Rashba and Baal HaTerumot. If Shach were correct, and the witnesses merely wrote a draft while waiting to learn the true kinyan date, there is no concern of hazamah or even apparent hazamah. If the correct date is later clarified, they insert the truth. If it is not clarified, they destroy the paper. No one can ever confront them with being elsewhere on a false completed date. Rosh's concern makes sense only if the proposed procedure is to write a day known not to be the kinyan day or the writing day, such as the beginning of Cheshvan merely because it is safely later. Then outsiders may later say, "You were with us elsewhere on that day." In beit din the witnesses may be able to answer that the document was intentionally postdated. As Perek Echad Dinei Mamonot explains, we can sometimes plead that explanation for them. But ordinary people may not know the legal explanation. They may view the witnesses as liars or zomemim. That public appearance of falsehood is itself the concern. Pilfelei Charifta on Bava Kamma supports this understanding. It also reconciles another apparent contradiction in Rosh. In Perek HaZorek, Rosh says that if judges sit in Hini while the matter was given to them in Shili, they must write the place where they actually sit, lest people later say to the witnesses, "You were with us elsewhere that day." In Perek Get Pashut he speaks of "appearing false." These are the same concern. The fear is not that beit din will actually convict them of hazamah, because the judges can understand the document was postdated. The fear is that the public will see a document bearing a date and place inconsistent with the witnesses' actual presence and consider them false witnesses. That is the type of appearance everyone agrees must be avoided. Rosh therefore fully supports Rashba and Baal HaTerumot. Shach also cites Baal HaItur, who writes that since elsewhere we rule that we do not fear appearance of falsehood, the witnesses can use a day they know is later, provided they write that the document was intentionally postdated. This citation actually supports the same interpretation. If Shach were correct that the issue is simply whether witnesses may prepare an uncertain draft, there would be no "postdating" once the true date becomes known. Baal HaItur's phrase "write and postdate it" shows he understands the proposed procedure as using a day that is known not to be the actual kinyan day. His only disagreement is whether an explicit notation that the document was postdated removes the appearance of falsehood. Baal HaItur's position itself needs explanation. If the paper expressly says, "We postdated this document," why would there ever be an appearance of falsehood? Anyone reading it sees the date is not the event date. Responsa Maimoniyot raises this. The answer appears to be that the concern is not only what an expert reader of the paper knows. Ordinary people may see witnesses repeatedly using dates disconnected from the actual transaction and become suspicious. A truthful witness normally remembers the event date or at least writes the day he is actually executing the document. Someone who invents an intermediate date can look as though he deliberately chose a day on which he cannot be contradicted. That creates suspicion even if the document formally notes that it is postdated. Baal HaItur says that because the general halachah does not always prohibit such appearances, an explicit postdating clause is enough. Rosh, Rashba, and Baal HaTerumot are stricter in the specific case where the false day could expose the signatories to public accusation of being elsewhere. Accordingly, Mechaber's ruling is correct and should not be questioned. There is further support from Rashi and Tosafot at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah. Rashi explains that the calendar rule for kings helps determine whether a document dated Tammuz relative to a Kislev loan is antedated or postdated. Tosafot ask how this works according to Abaye, who holds the witnesses acquire for the creditor through their signatures. One might have offered a simple case under Shach's reading: a kinyan occurred in Tammuz, years later the witnesses came to write, did not remember the exact kinyan day, and chose a Kislev date known to be later. Then the problem would have nothing to do with Abaye's theory. The fact that Rashi and Tosafot do not explain the case that way strongly indicates they consider such an invented intermediate date impermissible because it appears false. For Tosafot, one could answer that they follow a theory under which a loan kinyan does not itself authorize writing from the kinyan date. But Rashi is commonly understood, as Or Zarua and many authorities state, to follow Rif concerning acquisition documents and to treat the kinyan as meaningful. Yet Rashi still does not use the invented-safe-date case. That is a strong indication he views it as invalid. One should not ask: if such a false-looking date is prohibited, why do we need the separate decree invalidating antedated documents because they may collect from purchasers from the wrong time? The answer is that the two rules cover different cases. The appearance-of-falsehood rule applies where the witnesses intentionally choose a date they know is neither the transaction date nor the writing date. But an antedated document can arise through ordinary mistake. If the witnesses accidentally write the wrong year or wrong calendar day, they did not intentionally create a false-looking instrument. The special decree against antedated documents invalidates it because of the substantive danger to purchasers even though there was no deliberate appearance problem. Alternatively, according to Rosh's explanation that the concern is apparent hazamah tied to place, if a document does not state a place there may be little risk of that public accusation. Yet antedating is still invalid because of purchaser harm. Thus the two doctrines remain distinct. The first distinction, between deliberate invented dating and innocent error, is the stronger one. Accordingly, the correct practical law is: If the witnesses know the exact kinyan day, they use it. If they do not, they write the actual day on which they prepare the document. They should not select a convenient intermediate day merely because they know it is safely later than the kinyan. Doing so creates the appearance that they are testifying to an event on a day when no relevant act occurred and may expose them to the public appearance of falsehood. Rashba, Baal HaTerumot, and Rosh all support this rule. Shach's alternative interpretation does not fit Rosh's explicit language.
