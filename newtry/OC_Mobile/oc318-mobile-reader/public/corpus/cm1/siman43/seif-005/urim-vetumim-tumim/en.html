And some say it is completely invalid. Shach, seif katan 12, disagrees with Sema and says that according to this stricter opinion the debtor may even claim, "This never happened." That is difficult. Tur describes a known antedated document. How do we know it was antedated? The witnesses may themselves say, "We mistakenly or under compulsion wrote an earlier date," while maintaining that the underlying loan truly occurred. Or other witnesses may prove that the signatories were forced specifically regarding the date. If the testimony establishes the loan but only invalidates the earlier date, how could the debtor possibly say the loan never occurred? Shach must therefore establish a narrower case. According to Abaye, witnesses may prepare a document for a borrower before the lender is present. They could say, "On the earlier date we prepared this paper and gave it to the borrower, but we do not know whether a loan was ever made." Then the debtor can still say, "I never borrowed; the paper fell from me or was deposited with you." Under our law, which does not accept Abaye's rule broadly, one could say the witnesses improperly gave the prewritten paper to the borrower before any loan occurred. That misconduct does not necessarily disqualify them, as Ran explains elsewhere, but it leaves open whether the actual loan ever happened. Only in such a case is there room for Shach's position that the debtor can deny the transaction entirely. As a matter of practical halachah, however, Sema is clearly correct. Tur below, siman 45, explicitly cites Rabbenu Yonah that a document with two blank lines between text and signatures, although invalid because it can be forged, nevertheless prevents the debtor from claiming that the underlying transaction never occurred. Rabbenu Yonah says the same should apply to an antedated document. If Tur cited him merely for the blank-line case but rejected him concerning antedating, that would be highly forced. There is also strong internal logic. A blank-line document is in some respects worse than an antedated document. It gives the holder the ability to insert additional liabilities or alter the date and amount. Yet even there Rabbenu Yonah preserves the evidentiary effect against total denial. Why should an antedated document, whose defect is principally a decree against improper collection from purchasers, lose even that basic evidentiary force? Many authorities hold an antedated document remains fully valid against free assets and is invalid only against encumbered property. Even according to the stricter view, one should not enlarge the dispute unnecessarily by allowing denial of the loan itself. There is further support from the law below, siman 70, concerning a coerced sale. Where the sale document's recital of payment does not have full document force, the seller may still say he repaid the purchase money later, but he cannot ordinarily say, "I never received money at all and simply handed over a sale document without consideration." This shows that a document can lose its special written-loan strength while retaining evidentiary force against total denial. The same principle applies here. One might object from the Gemara's rule that an antedated document is invalid because of the decree lest the creditor collect from the earlier date. If it remains useful against denial, perhaps the holder will still preserve it carefully and later misuse it. That concern justifies stripping its purchaser-lien effect. It does not require treating the paper as though no witnesses ever signed it. Indeed, Tosafot in Bava Metzia indicate that where a document has some remaining utility, its continued preservation explains why finding it is not necessarily suspicious. This actually supports Sema. Tosafot distinguish a document that was never used for a loan from one that was used but later became defective. Even if the latter loses purchaser collection, the holder may still preserve it because it has evidentiary value against the debtor. That explains why an antedated loan document can still matter. The comparison to a woman's ketubah is particularly instructive. If a ketubah is antedated, the woman can still have an underlying ketubah right by operation of marriage. She need not preserve the defective paper merely to prove the basic obligation. Therefore, if such a paper is found, failure to guard it can be more suspicious than in an ordinary loan where even an invalid antedated document may still prevent total denial. This resolves Tosafot's apparently inconsistent passages about found documents. Shach also argues from the Gemara's question concerning a paid document reused for a second loan: "Why invoke extinguished lien? It is antedated." He infers that an antedated document is mere parchment. But even a document whose original lien was extinguished may still prevent total denial of the second transaction under some circumstances. The Gemara's point is only that it cannot function as the operative written lien for the second loan, not that it has zero evidentiary value. Thus that passage does not prove Shach's broader claim. There is an additional proof from Tur and Mechaber's treatment of the two-line blank. Rabbenu Yonah says the document remains useful against total denial because it could have been cured on the same day by filling the space with relatives. One might ask: after that day passes, filling the space would create an antedated document, so the original cure is no longer available. Does the paper suddenly become worthless the next day? That cannot be. Either Rabbenu Yonah holds that even an antedated document retains evidentiary force against total denial, or he would need to say the paper's status changes after one day. The latter is implausible and nowhere stated. Therefore his reasoning itself presupposes that the antedating defect does not erase the document's basic evidentiary force. Alternatively, as explained above, the date can be corrected prospectively before witnesses. Either way, the paper remains more than meaningless parchment. Accordingly, the practical law is clear. Even according to the stricter authorities who invalidate an antedated document for collection, the debtor cannot ordinarily claim that the underlying loan never occurred merely because of the defective date. At minimum, the document remains evidence of the transaction unless independent evidence shows the witnesses never witnessed a loan at all. Sema's position is therefore correct. All the more so because the majority of major authorities hold an antedated document is still a valid document against free assets and is disqualified only from collection from purchasers. We should not expand the minority invalidation view beyond what its sources require.
