Postdated documents are valid. There is an apparent difficulty from the rule below, siman 85. If Reuven produces a debt document against Shimon, and Shimon produces a later-maturing debt against Reuven, Reuven can sometimes say: "If I really owed you, why did you later borrow from me? You should have collected your debt instead." How, then, can postdated documents be universally valid? Suppose Reuven owed Shimon from Adar, payable in Iyar. In Nisan, before Shimon's debt was due, Shimon needed cash and borrowed from Reuven. At the true time of that second loan, Reuven had no argument. Shimon could not yet demand payment of the first debt because its due date had not arrived. But if the witnesses postdate Reuven's loan document until Sivan, after Shimon's debt had already matured, Reuven can now argue: "Your own document says you borrowed from me in Sivan. If I still owed you then, why did you not demand your money instead of borrowing?" Shimon could unjustly lose his earlier claim solely because the witnesses delayed the date. The answer is that because we independently rule that postdated documents are valid, Shimon is believed to explain this very circumstance. He can say, "I actually borrowed from you in Nisan; the witnesses merely postdated your document to Sivan." We do everything reasonably possible to preserve valid documents rather than destroy one through an inference that can be reconciled with lawful postdating. Accordingly, the rule in siman 85 must concern a later document known to have been written at its actual time, either because it lacks an after-acquired-property clause that would allow ordinary postdating, or because witnesses testify that it was not delayed. Where postdating is genuinely possible and the party claims that explanation, one should not invalidate an otherwise valid earlier debt merely from the appearance that he later borrowed. The same general principle appears regarding a document contradicted by a circumstance that could be explained through postdating. We prefer to preserve two valid instruments rather than destroy one through a circumstantial inference. There is also a broader answer. Chazal did not invalidate postdated documents merely because unusual later events could make the delayed date disadvantageous. Otherwise, since we rule that receipts may be written, every postdated document would be suspect. Suppose a loan truly occurred in Nisan and was repaid in Iyar. The debtor received an undated receipt before witnesses. The witnesses to that receipt then died in Iyar. If the creditor's loan document was postdated to Sivan, the debtor might later be unable to prove that the earlier receipt relates to a document whose face date is later than the receipt. Yet the law still permits postdated instruments. Evidently Chazal did not legislate against every remote combination of a delayed date, intervening payment, later death of witnesses, or other unusual event. Ramban's novellae make the same point in a related question and answer either that such a sequence is uncommon or that the debtor can explain that the document was postdated. Both answers apply here. There is a possible refinement in the receipt example. The debtor could have demanded a receipt directly from the creditor rather than rely only on outside witnesses. If the creditor himself signs the receipt, the later relationship between the receipt and the postdated document may be clearer. Thus the debtor who accepts only a fragile proof of payment may have contributed to his own risk. In any event, the central rule remains: postdated documents are valid because the law does not fear remote subsequent complications, and where the opposing inference arises only from the apparent later date, the party may explain that the document was lawfully postdated.
