Shach writes that if the lender himself, before the instrument was seen in beit din, wrote on it that it should collect only from a later date, the document is valid from that date. This must involve witnesses who saw the document in his hands from the second date, because the lender himself cannot establish when the new effective date begins. The original instrument is antedated, so without independent proof the timeline is unproven. It is enough, however, that witnesses saw the document in his possession; they need not witness a fresh delivery. The lender may then write that later date and afterward bring those witnesses to beit din. If he has a migo because the fact of antedating is known only through him, he can also be believed concerning the second date. Tosafot ask why the Gemara treats a paid document reused for another loan as having a discharged lien rather than simply an antedated instrument that might be corrected by notation. The answer is that a repaid document reused for a second loan can be invalid even where the second loan was delivered before witnesses, because the signed document's original obligation was already extinguished and it becomes internally false. If one writes openly on it that a new loan occurred, the defect can be corrected because no one will rely on the old signatures for the new event. The Gemara therefore discusses where no such correction was made. The concern about writing a receipt directly on a partially paid document is different: a notation may be erased or altered, and where erasures create uncertainty we should not establish a correction whose later manipulation could strengthen improper seizure. The proper practical method is to strike through the original date in a visible non-erasing manner and add the later effective date in a place supported by witnesses, so that the first date can no longer be relied upon.

Urim VeTumim correctly disagrees and cites the same proof Ramban gives from a document that was not permitted to be written. Even a deed whose lien was extinguished can still serve against a total denial. A document not written in accordance with rabbinic documentary form may be unusable against a plea of payment, but it is not necessarily disqualified as written testimony. Where it was written at the borrower's instruction, the witnesses' writing is not barred as testimony from writing. In an ordinary loan deed the signed witnesses do not directly attest the loan itself in the same way as oral testimony; their proof that it was written at the borrower's instruction, together with the fact that it reached the lender, gives rise to the presumption of delivery. That structure can remain effective against a total denial even though the instrument lost some of its collection force. By contrast, where the document was neither written nor delivered at the borrower's instruction and the writers purport to testify directly to the underlying event, it is ordinary testimony from writing and cannot be used.
