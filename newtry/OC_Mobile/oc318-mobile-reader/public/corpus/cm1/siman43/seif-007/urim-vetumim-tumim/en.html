And there are those who say, etc. The basic dispute here is whether witnesses who signed a document may later say that the date written in it was mistaken, when their handwriting is independently authenticated. Shach wishes to prove that wherever the mistake is one witnesses commonly make, they are believed even though their signatures are independently established. In my view this is incorrect. Ramban, Rashba, and the authorities who follow them require that the witnesses' handwriting not be independently authenticated. Only then do they have the migo that they could have denied the signatures and are believed to explain that they erred. The authorities who disagree with Ramban do not necessarily say witnesses are always believed. Their leniency is limited to mistakes that are genuinely common and natural. This is explicit in Shitah Mekubetzet in Perek Eizehu Neshech. R. Shmuel Vidal challenges Ramban from the case of a receipt whose date or terminology was mistaken, and he explains that Ramban holds that once the signatures are independently authenticated the witnesses are not believed even concerning something in which error is possible and common. Likewise Rashba in Gittin 26a writes that witnesses do not ordinarily scrutinize the date of a document carefully and cites the Yerushalmi allowing them to say, "We signed regarding the principal matter, not regarding the date." Nevertheless Rashba follows Ramban, as Maggid Mishneh records in his name and as Rashba himself writes at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah. Thus even though a date error is a common type of mistake, Rashba still requires that the handwriting not be authenticated from elsewhere. Shach attempts to prove otherwise from Tur's language. Tur, when explaining how we can know a document is antedated, gives the case where the witnesses themselves say they erred in the regnal year, and he specifically says this where their handwriting is not independently authenticated. Shach argues that Tur could instead have given the easier case of a calendrical error, which according to him should be believed even with authenticated signatures. That argument reverses the proof. If Tur agreed with Shach, why did Tur limit the regnal-year case to handwriting not independently authenticated? A regnal-year error can itself arise from a calendar mistake. A king may have begun his reign on the thirtieth of Adar when Adar was full, while the witnesses thought Adar had been deficient and therefore treated the next day as still within the first regnal year. Baal HaTerumot gives precisely such an explanation. Thus the regnal-year mistake can be one of the ordinary calendar mistakes people make. Yet Tur still requires that the signatures not be independently authenticated. This is strong evidence that Tur follows Ramban even in a common mistake. The same emerges from Sanhedrin 32. The Gemara asks how postdated documents can be valid if monetary testimony required full interrogation. Rashi explains the question: perhaps the witnesses are lying about the date and the loan testified to by other witnesses was a different loan. One could have answered that the document was postdated only by a single day and that the witnesses merely erred in the calendar, especially before most of the month had passed. The Gemara does not do so. Evidently it did not wish to establish the entire law of antedated and postdated documents in such a narrow and strained case. The same is true of Tur. He preferred the broader regnal-year example rather than limit the law to a one-day mistake before most of the month. The questions raised from Maggid Mishneh and Rivash prove nothing to the contrary. Maggid Mishneh and Rivash discuss cases where it was independently established that the document was antedated. For example, the date in a chalitzah document might precede a time when the husband was still alive, or the witnesses may have been minors or overseas at the stated date, or the lender and borrower may have been known to be elsewhere. In such a case, the question is not whether the witnesses' statement can invalidate an otherwise valid document. The document is already known to be chronologically impossible. The only question is whether the witnesses themselves become disqualified for having signed a false date. There, if a plausible ordinary mistake can explain what happened, we certainly do not disqualify them. A person is not removed from his presumption of fitness unless the wrongdoing is clear. That is very different from a case where the only reason to call the document antedated is the witnesses' own later statement, "We made a mistake." In that case, once their signatures are independently authenticated, Ramban and Rashba say they cannot use an oral explanation to weaken the document. Shach treats these two questions as identical: how do we know the document is antedated, and whether the witnesses become personally disqualified. They are not identical. A plausible mistake is enough to preserve the witnesses' personal fitness after an antedating has been independently established. It is not necessarily enough to let them invalidate the document itself once their signed testimony has become independently established. This distinction explains Tosafot and Rosh in Perek Eizehu Neshech. They ask why the witnesses to an antedated document are not themselves disqualified. They answer either that other witnesses say they were forced under threat to their lives, or that the original witnesses say they made a mistake in the regnal year or calendar. They do not mention that the handwriting was not independently authenticated. Shach infers that they are believed even with authenticated signatures. That inference is not justified. Tosafot and Rosh there are not asking how the court knows the document is antedated. They assume that fact is independently established. Their question is only why the signatories should not become invalid witnesses. To preserve the witnesses' fitness, a plausible mistake is enough even though the document itself is already invalid for other reasons. This is why Tosafot and Rosh discuss threat to life and calendar mistake. Tur asks a different question: how is the antedating itself established? There he properly requires the migo created by handwriting not independently authenticated if the source of the antedating is the witnesses' own explanation. This resolves the apparent contradiction completely. It also explains why Tosafot and Rosh use witnesses to coercion in one formulation but Tur uses the signatories' own mistake. Outside witnesses can testify directly that the signatories were under life-threatening coercion, because that is an objective fact they observed. Outside witnesses cannot meaningfully testify, "The signatories made a mental mistake." They do not sit inside another person's mind. At most they can establish facts from which mistake is inferred. Bach makes this point correctly. The original witnesses themselves, however, can say they made a calendrical mistake, provided the legal conditions for believing that later explanation are met. There is another important proof from the Yerushalmi's question, "Who informs us that it is antedated?" According to Ramban, the answer is that the witnesses themselves can say they erred only if their handwriting is not independently authenticated. Some ask: how can this fit Reish Lakish's principle that witnesses signed in a document are treated as though their testimony was examined in beit din? The Mishnah in Ketubot already says that where signatures are not independently authenticated, witnesses can say, "We were under duress," because their statement both authenticates and limits the document. There is no contradiction. One answer is that the Yerushalmi's question is particularly sharp according to R. Meir. R. Meir holds that even where the signatures are not independently authenticated, the witnesses are not believed to say they signed under threat to their lives. Tosafot and Rashi explain that R. Meir effectively treats admission of the signed document as sufficient and does not require ordinary reauthentication in the same way. Reish Lakish himself establishes the Mishnah of antedated documents according to R. Meir. Thus the Yerushalmi can ask: according to R. Meir, if the signed testimony is already treated as investigated testimony, how can the witnesses now undo its date? The answer is that where the alleged error is of the type witnesses commonly make, and they have the migo connected to authenticating their own signatures, even R. Meir can concede more than in an extraordinary claim of coercion. A second answer follows Ran's treatment of amana and moda'ah. Ran holds that even where the signatures are not independently authenticated, witnesses are not believed to say, "It was a trust document," or "There was a prior declaration nullifying it," because the document was validly written and they now seek orally to impair it. Tosafot explain the difference from ordinary duress: where they say, "We were coerced," the document was never validly constituted. It is mere parchment. But an amana or moda'ah document was written in a legally recognizable form, and the later oral statement would weaken a writing that came into existence lawfully. An antedated document has significant legal effect even according to those who allow a repayment defense. At minimum, as I explained above following Sema, it can prevent the debtor from denying the underlying loan. It therefore resembles a moda'ah document more than a document that never existed legally. Why then should witnesses be believed even where their handwriting is not independently authenticated? The Yerushalmi's answer is that the date is a field in which ordinary witness error is common. Their migo therefore joins a naturally plausible explanation and allows them to say, "We signed regarding the transaction but were mistaken as to the date." This is not comparable to amana or moda'ah, which are unusual and directly contradict the ordinary meaning of a valid document. Thus the Yerushalmi's structure itself points to Ramban's rule: even a common mistake still needs the migo where the later explanation would impair the written testimony. This also explains Tur's formulation. Tur says that other witnesses can testify the signatories were under life-threatening compulsion, or the signatories themselves can say they erred in the regnal year when their handwriting is not independently authenticated. Why does Tur not say the signatories themselves can say they were under life-threatening compulsion? Because Tur is explaining the law in a manner that works according to the stricter authorities as well. According to Ran's analogy to moda'ah, where the document was otherwise properly written and only the date was forced, saying afterward, "We were coerced only concerning the date," resembles a later oral impairment of an otherwise valid writing. That is more doubtful than a common calendar mistake. Therefore Tur chooses the mistake example for the signatories and objective coercion testimony for outsiders. This is precise. The same analysis answers Tosafot in Bava Kamma 30b. They ask why, according to R. Meir, antedated loan witnesses are not disqualified and why the Gemara needs a special decree that the document cannot collect from the earlier date. One could have asked the same under the Sages. But under the Sages it is easier to establish the case where the signatures are not independently authenticated and the witnesses themselves explain that they were forced under threat of death. R. Meir is stricter about such explanations. Therefore Tosafot emphasize R. Meir and seek a different way to preserve the witnesses' fitness. Alternatively, the Sages may follow R. Yose in Zeh Borer, who requires a wickedness of monetary greed for certain disqualifications. A witness who negligently antedates without personal gain may not become invalid in the same way. R. Meir, following the stricter approach, presents the sharper problem. Thus Tosafot's question is not proof that witnesses may always retract from a signed date. Now consider Shach's particular ruling: if the witnesses say, "We wrote the document in Nisan for a contemplated loan, but the loan did not occur until Tishrei, in violation of Rav Asi," Shach says they are believed even though their signatures are independently authenticated. In my view they may be unable to make this statement even when the signatures are not independently authenticated. Ran at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah first asks why antedated witnesses are not disqualified. He answers that perhaps they wrote in Nisan for a loan to be made later and the loan did not occur until Tishrei. Although they violated Rav Asi's rule, a person who does not know Rav Asi's law is not necessarily a wicked witness. Ran then cites the Yerushalmi asking, "Who tells us the document is antedated?" and records the dispute of Razah and Ramban whether the witnesses can say they made a mistake. Beit Yaakov correctly asks: if the witnesses can say they wrote in Nisan for a future loan, why does the Yerushalmi need other explanations of how the antedating becomes known? The answer is that the Rav Asi explanation is enough to save the witnesses from personal disqualification once the antedating is independently established. It is not necessarily enough to let them invalidate the document. Where the document itself is otherwise valid, the witnesses cannot simply say, "We did not know that writing for a future loan was prohibited," and thereby destroy a signed instrument. Consider witnesses who say, "We signed falsely because we were coerced by money and did not know monetary coercion was forbidden." Would we believe them merely because they claim ignorance of the law? Certainly not. Likewise, R. Meir says witnesses under threat to life should have allowed themselves to be killed rather than sign falsely. The Gemara asks whether that can really be demanded of them, but it does not say simply, "They did not know the law, so believe them." Ignorance may save a person's status from definite disqualification. It does not automatically authorize him to annul a document he signed. Accordingly, Ran's first answer concerns only witness fitness. The Yerushalmi's question concerns document validity. This is why the two answers are not interchangeable. Nimukei Yosef's wording likewise points in this direction. He discusses preserving the witnesses' presumption of fitness through the possibility that they misunderstood Rav Asi, not giving them affirmative power to void the instrument. All the statements of Rivash and Maggid Mishneh can be read the same way. They are about not disqualifying witnesses where an innocent misunderstanding can explain an already established chronological defect. They do not say the witnesses' unsupported explanation overrides an independently authenticated document. There is also substantial support from Tosafot at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah. Rashi explains a case where the witnesses say they saw the loan in Kislev while the document bears a Tammuz date, but we do not know which Tammuz relative to Kislev is meant. Tosafot ask why the document should be invalid out of doubt. Elsewhere we preserve documents where the date fell on Shabbat or where witnesses were later hazemed, rather than invalidate from uncertainty. If witnesses who wrote for a future loan were not personally blameworthy, then there would be no presumption of fitness helping the document. The court should simply say the holder bears the burden and the instrument is doubtful. Tosafot's comparison works best if even writing for a future loan is treated as wrongdoing by the witnesses, so their presumption of fitness pushes us to interpret the chronology in the document's favor. This suggests Tosafot do not fully accept Ran's lenient personal-status analysis either. At minimum, one should not extend Ran's answer into an independent power to invalidate the document. One may ask how Ran's example works according to Abaye, who holds that the witnesses acquire for the creditor through their signatures and therefore permits writing a loan document for the borrower before the lender is present. There are several possibilities. One is a city containing two people with the same name, Yosef ben Shimon. The borrower tells the witnesses in Nisan to write a document for a contemplated loan from one Yosef ben Shimon. They prepare it. In Tishrei he changes his mind and actually borrows from the other Yosef ben Shimon. Abaye's mechanism does not relate the original signatures to this second creditor because at the time of writing the witnesses did not intend to acquire for him. If the same writing is later delivered to the second man, the document becomes antedated as to him. Ramah, cited by Tur, says that if the paper was written in Nisan and the actual loan occurred in Tishrei, the document may still collect only from the later date because the witnesses did not lie regarding the underlying writing act. Tur disagrees and says it is still an antedated document because it creates an earlier apparent lien than the real creditor relationship. This example makes the dispute intelligible even under Abaye. One might object that ordinary monetary documents do not require lishmah for the named creditor the way a get does. That is true as to the paper's general formal validity. But Abaye's theory that the witnesses acquire for the creditor through their signatures still depends on whom they intended to benefit at the time of signing. A lien cannot arise for a person they did not have in mind at all. Later delivery before witnesses can perhaps validate the paper prospectively, like a paid loan document reused through a new delivery according to the authorities discussed below in siman 48. But it cannot create retroactive priority from Nisan for a different intended creditor. There is still a question against Abaye. How can the Mishnah allow writing a document for the borrower without the lender? Perhaps the borrower asks in Nisan for one Yosef ben Shimon and in Tishrei borrows from another man of the same name. Abaye elsewhere is concerned about two people with identical names even when only one is known. Why does he not fear this scenario? The answer must be that such a chain is extraordinarily remote: the borrower would have to prepare the document months earlier, fail to borrow, another same-named creditor would need to exist or appear, and the borrower would then borrow from that second man and use the old paper. Even Abaye does not legislate for so remote a possibility. Another case is where the borrower had the document prepared in Nisan but died, and his heir later borrowed in Tishrei using the same paper. The witnesses' signatures cannot acquire retroactively for the heir's creditor because the person on whose behalf they acted is no longer the borrower. Tosafot distinguish a father's transaction from a son's in related discussions. This can also generate an antedated paper even under Abaye. The existence of such cases removes the alleged contradiction. Now return to the central legal question. Razah, as cited above in siman 34, holds that witnesses may say they erred in a regnal year or similar date even when their signatures are independently authenticated. But this approach creates a severe problem with hazamah in documents. If witnesses can always say, "We were mistaken about the year," then when later witnesses say, "You were with us elsewhere on the stated date," the first witnesses can answer, "We never testified to that exact date. We erred." How can any document witnesses ever become zomemim based upon the date? It is no answer to say the document says, "We wrote it at its proper time." If they are allowed to explain that they were mistaken about the date, they can say the same after hazamah. Worse, if their theory is that regarding the date they can say, "We did not sign upon this exact detail," then their signed date was never treated as testimony fully investigated in beit din. Hazamah cannot attach to a detail that was never legally part of their investigated testimony. Mechaber above cites Rif and Rambam that hazamah can apply to document witnesses. That strongly suggests he does not accept Razah's broad position. Ramban's narrower rule avoids the problem. Where signatures are independently authenticated, the date in the document stands as binding written testimony. The witnesses cannot later say they erred merely because calendrical mistakes are possible. Then hazamah can operate. Where the handwriting is not otherwise established, their own authentication accompanied by a plausible limiting explanation can be believed through migo. In that special case the document never became fixed against their explanation. This is fully coherent. One can answer that even under Ramban, once an antedating is independently established we may preserve the witnesses' fitness by assuming they made an ordinary calendrical mistake. How then can hazamah ever apply? The answer is that hazamah concerns a contradiction that cannot readily be explained by ordinary mistake, or a date and factual setting where no plausible calendar error exists. There are many such cases. Razah's position is more problematic because he allows the witnesses themselves to strip the date from their signed testimony even after independent authentication. There is another indication from Razah's explanation in Perek HaCholeitz. Razah explains why documentary testimony is not disqualified as "from their writing" by saying the witnesses wrote as agents of the borrower or husband. Ramban asks: if so, in the case of witnesses to kiddushin who no longer remember the date, why not simply submit the original document to beit din? The court would have the document itself and would not need the witnesses' oral recollection. Razah can answer according to his own theory. The date is not treated as a fully investigated component of the signed testimony. The witnesses may say they did not focus on it or that they erred. In ordinary monetary law, Chazal can still allow collection so long as no one raises a defect. But one cannot execute a person for adultery based solely on a date that never had the status of fully investigated testimony. For capital consequences, the witnesses must orally establish the date. Ba'al HaItur states essentially this after citing the Yerushalmi: witnesses can say, regarding the date, "we did not sign upon that detail," and therefore a document date is not automatically treated as testimony investigated in beit din for the strictest purposes. This explains Razah well. But it also shows why Ramban rejected the approach. Rif, Tosafot, and Rambam give other answers to the kiddushin-date problem. Their refusal to use Razah's solution indicates they do not accept the idea that an authenticated document date remains outside the status of investigated testimony. The halachic majority therefore favors Ramban. There is a possible rejoinder from the opening of Perek Admon, where the Gemara asks about postdated documents and whether we carefully verify the signatories. Rashi explains that even if other witnesses testify about the actual loan date, perhaps the signed witnesses are lying. Maharam Lublin asks: why not establish the case where the signed witnesses themselves say, "We postdated it"? Then they should be believed and there is no reason to suspect false testimony. He concludes Rashi must hold the signatories themselves are not believed like outside witnesses. But that conclusion is difficult because Rashi at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah expressly explains a case where the document witnesses themselves say, "We wrote in Tammuz and saw the loan in Kislev," and treats their account as relevant. Maharam Lublin's solution that the Gemara teaches nothing if the signatories themselves explain the postdating is likewise strained. There were authorities who invalidated postdated documents because receipts are written, and there is also a question of after-acquired property. The law is not obvious. A better answer is found by looking at the preceding clause concerning a prozbul. The baraita says an antedated prozbul is valid and a postdated one invalid, while an antedated loan document is invalid and a postdated one valid. A prozbul generally does not require ordinary signature authentication because a creditor is believed to say, "I had a prozbul and lost it," based on the presumption that he would not abandon a permitted method and violate shemittah law. If the prozbul is physically before us and the creditor says it is genuine, certainly we do not need ordinary authentication. Therefore the question of how the prozbul's chronological defect is established cannot rest only on the signatories' migo. It must involve outside proof or another independent basis. The baraita pairs the loan document with the prozbul in the same factual style. Accordingly, Rashi reasonably explains the loan case through outside witnesses as well. This avoids Maharam Lublin's question without adopting Razah. The opening Gemara's question then makes sense: if full interrogation applied to monetary cases, how could we accept the signed date when outside witnesses establish a different chronology and the signatories themselves are not available to explain? The conclusion that civil cases do not require full interrogation changes the status of the date and allows the law of ordinary documents. Still, if one follows Razah, one can offer a more strained reconciliation. Perhaps the ability of witnesses to say, "We did not focus on the date," arises only after the conclusion that monetary cases do not require full interrogation. At the Gemara's initial stage, where it assumes even monetary testimony requires full interrogation, the date is part of the very core of testimony. Witnesses would be expected to scrutinize it meticulously, and they could not later say, "We did not sign regarding that." Thus the initial question necessarily treats the chronological problem as testimony from outsiders. After the Gemara concludes that civil cases do not require full interrogation, the Yerushalmi can allow witnesses to say the date was not a detail they carefully verified. This saves Razah somewhat. But Ramban's approach remains much cleaner and fits the mainstream Bavli authorities. I would therefore rule like the dissenting opinion recorded here: once the witnesses' signatures are independently authenticated, they are not believed to say that the document's date was mistaken merely because the mistake was one witnesses can make. Where their handwriting is not independently authenticated, they can be believed through migo concerning a plausible ordinary mistake. If the document is already independently proven to be antedated, however, we may still preserve the witnesses' personal fitness by attributing the false date to such a mistake. That last rule concerns their status as witnesses, not the validity of the document. This distinction resolves nearly all the apparent contradictions among Tur, Tosafot, Rosh, Ran, Ramban, Rashba, Rivash, and the Yerushalmi. There remains one further point regarding other witnesses who say the signatories were under life-threatening duress specifically to antedate the document. If the original document itself, through its independently authenticated signatures, implies that the witnesses signed voluntarily and lawfully, while two other witnesses testify that they were coerced, this can be treated as two against two. Ordinary documentary testimony carries the presumption that the witnesses were not under such coercion. Therefore the new pair may not simply erase all force of the document as though no counter-testimony exists. See below, siman 46, seif 37. This too confirms that the matter should not be simplified into a blanket rule that any plausible explanation offered after signing can defeat an authenticated document.
