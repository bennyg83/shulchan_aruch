He shall swear, etc. Shach asks why the creditor needs this special oath at all. In any event he must swear that the debt was not repaid. Shach gives two answers. First, perhaps the creditor could rationalize swearing falsely about repayment while being less willing to swear falsely that the document is not antedated. Second, if the document is antedated, the debtor may even be believed to say, "I never borrowed," whereas if it is not antedated the document blocks that claim. The difference between the two answers appears where the creditor says: "I do not know whether the document is antedated or postdated, but I am prepared to swear that I lent you the money and was not repaid." According to the first answer that is insufficient because the special oath concerns the dating itself. According to the second answer, it can suffice because the creditor establishes the underlying loan and nonpayment, which is all that matters once total denial is removed. The second explanation is preferable. The first is extremely strained. More fundamentally, Baal HaItur's entire oath rule requires study because I have not found a clear source for it. At first glance Tosafot at the beginning of Rosh Hashanah appear not to accept such a rule. Rashi explains a case where witnesses do not know whether Tammuz preceded Kislev or vice versa, and out of doubt the document is invalid. Tosafot ask: elsewhere, where a document bears a Shabbat date, we preserve it from doubt rather than invalidate it. Why should this case be different? Tosafot say that under Rashi's explanation the lender and borrower must acknowledge that the loan and document relate to the same transaction, for otherwise how would we know the chronological relation. If the creditor is the one admitting the transactions are the same, why should he not be believed to say the document is merely postdated through a migo that he could have denied that they were the same loan at all? A migo used to support a written claim is not necessarily classified as migo to extract, particularly according to Rashi, who holds even a truly antedated document can still collect from free assets. One might therefore think the creditor should prevail. The necessary answer is that the case is one where the creditor himself also does not know which month came first. If he does not know whether the paper is antedated or postdated, there is no migo because migo does not operate for an uncertain claimant. Now apply Baal HaItur's law. If a creditor must swear that the document is not antedated, this creditor cannot swear. Since he does not know, he should lose as one who must take an oath in order to collect and cannot do so. Then Rashi's conclusion that the document is invalid out of doubt is obvious, and Tosafot's question from the Shabbat-date document should never arise. This seems to contradict Baal HaItur. The better reading of Tosafot is different. The lender and borrower did not newly come before beit din and say, "This is the same loan." Rather, the witnesses who testify about the separate dates know that the document and loan are one transaction because at the time the parties themselves identified them as such. The witnesses can therefore testify to that relationship. Now, even if the creditor presently insists the paper was postdated, his claim is not supported by a current migo. He cannot now say, "It was a different loan," because that would contradict the earlier established testimony connecting the two. A past migo is not a migo. Tosafot likely found the alternative, that the creditor himself has no idea when his own loan occurred, too unusual to use as the standard case underlying the entire calendrical enactment. This removes the contradiction. There is another subtle point in Tosafot's wording. They say the lender and borrower acknowledged that it was one loan. Why mention the borrower? The creditor's admission alone should suffice against himself. The answer is that Tosafot wish to remove the creditor's possible migo. If only the creditor had acknowledged the transaction while the borrower was absent, the creditor could perhaps now claim the document is postdated through the migo that he could have denied the identification. But when the borrower also appears and says, "Yes, this is the same transaction, though I do not know which month came first," then if the creditor were to claim two separate loans, he would be contradicting the borrower's testimony. A migo that requires contradicting an opposing witness is not accepted in the same manner. The borrower has no financial bias concerning whether collection ultimately comes from him or from a purchaser if his own liability is fixed; he is not simply giving the creditor a friendly admission. Thus Tosafot's language is exact. Accordingly, Tosafot do not necessarily contradict Baal HaItur's special oath rule. Still, the rule itself remains without a strong direct source and should not be expanded beyond its stated case. Where the creditor can swear to the substantive facts, the second explanation of Shach is the more convincing basis for understanding its effect.
