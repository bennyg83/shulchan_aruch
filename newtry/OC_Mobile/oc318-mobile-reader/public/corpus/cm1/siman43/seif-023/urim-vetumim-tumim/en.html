But not where he becomes obligated through kinyan or document. Sema, seif katan 32, explains that "obligated through kinyan" means the kinyan was made at night. If the kinyan occurred during the day, it immediately creates publicity and a property lien from that daytime moment. Ran's question, "What was encumbered before the money was received or the document completed?" then has no force, because the kinyan itself created the lien. At first glance Sema's explanation is sound. But Ran's question against Rambam is difficult for another reason. Even without a kinyan, if a person says before witnesses, "I obligate myself to so-and-so for one hundred," Rambam holds he becomes liable even though he never previously owed anything. Once he has a complete personal obligation, why should his property not become subject to the creditor just as though he had received loan money? It is difficult to say Ran simply rejects Rambam's rule of self-obligation. In Perek HaNose, Ran appears to accept that law. And if Ran did reject it, Mechaber, who rules like Rambam above in siman 40, should not cite Ran's view here without qualification. Bnei Yaakov raises this and leaves it requiring study. One possibility is that Ran distinguishes the time while the parties remain engaged in the same transaction. A statement such as, "I hereby obligate myself to you," creates a binding liability once the matter is complete. But while the parties are still dealing with the same transaction, even an obligation made through kinyan can sometimes be retracted under the relevant rules. All the more so a bare verbal self-obligation can still be withdrawn while they remain engaged in the matter. If the entire transaction begins during the day and continues into night, perhaps no final fixed obligation existed before night. Then no property lien could arise during the day, because the obligor still had the power to retract. This would avoid Sema's need to establish the kinyan specifically at night. Still, the answer is not fully satisfactory. Even where a party retains a right to retract during the transaction, if he ultimately does not retract, one might say the obligation becomes effective retroactively from the original kinyan. If so, the lien problem returns. A stronger answer is possible. The general rule that one can obligate himself before witnesses applies where he says simply, "I owe you," or otherwise creates an immediate obligation. But if he says, "Write a document for him stating that I obligate myself," then his language shows he does not intend the obligation to become complete until the document reaches the creditor. The writing is not merely evidence of an already completed oral obligation. It is part of the act by which he wishes to become bound. Consider Shmuel's rule that one who finds a shtar aknayata may return it because payment is not feared. If every borrower who told witnesses, "I obligate myself; write a document," became fully bound at once by his oral words even without kinyan, then even a no-warranty document should often be returnable despite the borrower's denial. The oral self-obligation would already exist. Yet below in siman 65 the law is that if the alleged obligor does not admit the debt, the found paper is not simply returned. The necessary conclusion is that when the person makes the writing itself part of his undertaking, no liability arises until the document is properly delivered. His words are conditional upon completion of the written instrument. Ran's question then becomes clear. Before the document was completed and delivered, what created the property lien? A mere incomplete instruction to write did not. This also applies to a kinyan if the kinyan was framed specifically as part of an obligation that would take effect through the document rather than as an independent present kinyan. Accordingly, Sema's day-night distinction is not necessary. The central issue is whether the obligation was independently completed before night or whether the document itself was an essential part of creating it. The latter is the more natural reading of Ran.
