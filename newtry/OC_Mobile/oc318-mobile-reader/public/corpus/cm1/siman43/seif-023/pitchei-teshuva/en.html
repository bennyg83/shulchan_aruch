They give it to Reuven. Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes in his glosses that this is because Reuven’s date is definite while Shimon’s is not. The Raah writes that the same rule applies where a lender’s document is dated the fifth of Nisan and a purchaser’s deed of sale merely says “Nisan”: the creditor collects from that purchaser. In my view this ruling requires great study. Perhaps the rule applies specifically to two debt instruments, where in any event the land is certainly subject to both claims and Reuven unquestionably has a lien, so we award it to him because his date is more precisely established and neither party is in possession. But where one seeks to take property from a purchaser on the basis of a doubtful lien, what authority do we have to extract it? The Ran and Maggid Mishneh cite the Rashba regarding two purchasers, one whose deed is dated the second of Nisan and the other whose deed merely says Nisan. A creditor may collect from the purchaser whose deed bears the unspecified date, and that purchaser cannot say, “Perhaps I purchased on the first of Nisan and left the later property for you to collect from,” because the rule that creditors do not collect from purchasers in such a case is itself an enactment, and the purchaser must establish that his acquisition was earlier. This too implies that only where the creditor’s lien on the property is certain and the obstacle is the protective rule against collection does the burden fall on the purchaser. In the Raah’s case, however, we are uncertain about the very existence of the lien against the purchaser, and one should not be able to extract property on a doubt. This requires study.
