They are invalid, etc. Shach writes that if the witnesses informed the seller that they were postdating the document, the instrument is valid. He further suggests that when a postdated sale document comes before us, we should presume they informed the seller because witnesses are presumed to act properly. This practical conclusion requires study. If that were correct, the same should apply to postdated loan documents according to the view that receipts are written. Once the borrower knows the paper was postdated, he can protect himself by refusing to pay without taking back the document or receiving an adequate receipt. We should then presume the witnesses informed him and validate every postdated loan document. That is not the implication of the Gemara. Regarding a document whose date falls on Shabbat, the Gemara validates it because the postdating is evident from the face of the instrument. If we could simply presume the witnesses privately informed the debtor whenever they postdated, there would be no need for the visible-date rationale. It is also strained to establish all those discussions as cases where we specifically know the witnesses failed to notify the party. Likewise, Beit Yosef below in siman 239 labors to explain how Tur can permit writing the current date where the kinyan day is unknown. He could have answered simply that the witnesses inform the seller of the delayed date. He does not. Therefore, although Shach's first proposition is reasonable, his presumption is not. If it is actually known that the seller was informed of the postdating and accepted the risk, there is room to validate the document because the concern of a hidden later date is greatly reduced. But when a postdated document comes before us without such proof, we cannot assume notice. Witnesses may have forgotten to explain it, or they may themselves not know the detailed law well enough to appreciate the need. The general silence of the poskim indicates that a postdated transfer document remains invalid unless the protective circumstances are established. Thus Shach's leniency should be limited to a case where notice to the seller is known, not merely presumed.
