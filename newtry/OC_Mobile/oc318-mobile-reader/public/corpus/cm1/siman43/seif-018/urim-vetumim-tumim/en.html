The holder of the document must bring proof. Meaning, even if the debtor does not raise the objection, beit din raises it on his behalf. This is difficult. What exactly is the debtor claiming? If he says, "This never happened," or "I repaid," why does it matter that the document is dated on Shabbat? Even where there is no defect in the date, we rule below, siman 82, that he is believed to assert nonoccurrence or repayment by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed forgery. If he admits that he owes the money, what difference does it make that the document is antedated? If he admits the debt, everyone agrees that he must pay even if the witnesses are disqualified. If the rule concerns claims made for purchasers, the Shach concludes below, siman 106, that beit din claims forgery for purchasers even where the debtor admits that the document is authentic. If so, remove the issue of the antedated date entirely, for beit din already claims forgery on their behalf. The Baal HaIttur is not difficult, because he follows his own view that where a document contains a credibility clause, even if it is not authenticated and the debtor admits writing it, he cannot claim repayment or nonoccurrence, as explained below in siman 46 in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch. Where the document contains a defect suggesting that it may be antedated, however, Baal HaIttur may concede that despite the credibility clause the debtor is believed by virtue of migo, because the document itself has been weakened. Bnei Yaakov, page 28, writes that Baal HaIttur agrees in such a case. But according to our ruling below in siman 82, which rejects Baal HaIttur, the difficulty remains. It therefore appears that one must read the exact wording of Baal HaIttur, also quoted by Baal HaTerumot in gate 40: "But if it was not authenticated, the holder of the document must bring proof, and we say to him, authenticate your document. Even though there is no challenger and witnesses are available, we ourselves make the claim for him." The phrase "witnesses are available" indicates that there are witnesses presently available who could authenticate the document, but the document had not yet been authenticated before coming to beit din. Because the document contains an apparent defect and may be antedated, beit din does not itself proceed to authenticate it. Ramban writes similarly in Bava Batra regarding the ruling that the halachah follows Rabbah with respect to land. There too the document is not authenticated and witnesses are available to authenticate it, but because the party has admitted that it is forged, beit din does not undertake to authenticate it and he does not receive a migo based on an authentication that has not yet occurred. The same applies here. Since the defect is before us and the document has not yet been authenticated, beit din does not undertake to validate it, and raises the claim that it is invalid. This is why the source emphasizes that even though no party objects and witnesses are available, beit din raises the issue on its own. This resolves the difficulty and teaches an important point: once the document is weakened in this manner, later authentication does not automatically cure the problem.
