They shall write from the day of writing. I am uncertain whether such a document must expressly include an after-acquired-property clause, and whether omission of that clause makes the document invalid like other postdated loan documents. One possibility follows Beit Yosef below in siman 239. There, concerning sale documents, Beit Yosef says witnesses can write the present day even though the actual transaction occurred earlier because if the seller had reacquired the field from the purchaser in the interim, that reacquisition would be a public event. If the witnesses heard nothing of the sort, they need not fear it. The same might apply here. If the borrower acquired new land between the kinyan date and the writing date, such an acquisition could have sufficient publicity that the witnesses would hear of it. If they heard nothing, perhaps there is no concern that a postdated document will improperly reach property acquired in the interim. Still, one can distinguish. A seller reacquiring the very field he just sold is an unusual and conspicuous event and is more likely to become public. A borrower acquiring some new field during the interval is not comparably remarkable. Therefore absence of rumor may not be enough to exclude after-acquired-property concerns. Moreover, Beit Yosef's own ruling below requires study. His question against Tur, that writing the day of writing makes the document postdated, can perhaps be answered differently. The case may be where the sale document records that the field was sold but does not say the purchaser already paid the price. Then the usual harm associated with a postdated completed-sale document is reduced for the reasons explained above. If so, we need not build a broad rule that witnesses may rely on the absence of rumor concerning reacquisition. Accordingly, the present loan question remains unresolved. It requires study whether an express after-acquired-property clause is necessary when the witnesses write the later day rather than the remembered kinyan date.
