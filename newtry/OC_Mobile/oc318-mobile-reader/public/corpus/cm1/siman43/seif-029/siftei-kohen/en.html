Meaning, until most of the intervening days have passed, etc. This ruling requires study, for such a distant date is implausible. It would mean that a promise payable 'after Shavuot' or 'after Sukkot' is not due until most of the period before the next festival has passed; the Ran expressly notes that after Sukkot this could allow three months. I therefore favor Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation, cited by the Ran, Rashba, and Rosh, that 'after the festival' means fifteen days after any festival. Although the Ran and Rashba say his basis was unclear, Rabbeinu Chananel's traditions are authoritative, and a source appears in the rule that rain was requested on the seventh of MarCheshvan, fifteen days after Sukkot, to allow the last pilgrim to reach the Euphrates. As long as pilgrims had not crossed that boundary they were still regarded as occupied with the festival journey, so common usage fixed 'after the festival' at fifteen days. The Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham appear to accept this. Rabbeinu Yerucham's other statement, that 'until Pesach' extends through most of the interval to Shavuot, may distinguish Pesach because the counting of the Omer connects the two festivals, as the Ir Shushan says. If so, the Rif's ruling may concern Pesach alone, and perhaps the Mechaber likewise meant only Pesach, although Ramah's formulation applies the midpoint calculation to every festival. The Shiltei Giborim gives yet another short period. In any event, several early authorities omitted the law altogether, apparently because after rejecting Rabbi Meir's thirty day rule the Gemara never determines the replacement period; it may therefore remain an unresolved legal doubt.
Most, etc. The Sema says 'most' is not exact and that one half suffices, as in the Ran. In my view 'most' is exact, as Rabbeinu Yerucham states. Where the interval contains an odd number of days, one waits until a majority has passed; the Ran may discuss an even interval. Alternatively, the Mechaber and Rabbeinu Yerucham concern a date written in a deed, where the holder bears the disadvantage of ambiguity and therefore requires a majority, while the Ran concerns an oath to pay, where Torah doubt is treated stringently. The Sema's arithmetic is also inaccurate. From the end of Pesach until Shavuot there are forty three days in Eretz Yisrael and forty two outside it, so twenty one days is not a majority for either and is not even half in Eretz Yisrael.
