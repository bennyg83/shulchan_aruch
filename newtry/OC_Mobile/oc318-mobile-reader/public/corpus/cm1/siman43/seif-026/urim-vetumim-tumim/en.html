And likewise Shimon cannot collect, etc. Mechaber states this law without qualification, but in my view it depends on the dispute at the beginning of siman 43 concerning an undated or partially dated document. According to the authorities who hold that even if witnesses saw the document in the creditor's possession early in the month, the creditor still cannot collect from purchasers until the end of that month, our present case must involve a kinyan document. Otherwise, how can purchasers who bought after the first creditor's uncertain Nisan date say, "We left property available for you with those who bought earlier in Nisan"? If the document merely says Nisan without a day, and its lien does not begin until the end of Nisan, then throughout Nisan the creditor effectively has only an oral obligation as against purchasers. There is no earlier encumbered property that the later purchaser can point to as property left available for collection. The analogy is a postdated document. We rule that a creditor cannot collect from purchasers until the date written in the document arrives. Until then, the earlier obligation is treated, for purchaser-lien purposes, as an oral loan. Suppose Reuven lent orally to Shimon and only later converted the debt into a written loan. Could purchasers who bought during the oral-loan period tell the creditor, "We left you property among those who bought before the written instrument arose"? Certainly not. The creditor had no written lien against those earlier purchasers. The same applies to a document that merely says Nisan. According to Raah, Ran, and Ritva, even if witnesses saw the document on the first of Nisan, purchaser collection begins only from the end of the month because only then is the lien date certain from the face of the instrument. Until then, the earlier purchaser-lien force is waived or absent. Therefore the present law has no basis in an ordinary non-kinyan document. It must be speaking of a document containing a kinyan. In a kinyan document, everyone agrees the substantive lien and publicity can begin from the kinyan itself. The uncertain monthly dating does not erase that underlying lien. Purchasers who bought later can therefore argue that property remained available among purchasers whose acquisition was earlier within that same period. I searched and found this stated explicitly by Raah in Ketubot. After discussing two loan documents, one dated merely Nisan and another dated the fifth of Nisan, he writes that an ordinary loan document cannot be the case, because its lien does not begin until the time evident from the document. Even if witnesses saw the paper earlier, purchaser collection waits until Iyar. He therefore establishes the sugya in shtarei aknayata. That is exactly the distinction stated here. Ran and Ritva, who use similar language, should likewise be understood as speaking of kinyan documents. One might ask: if so, why did Mechaber not say so expressly? The answer is that the kinyan framework is embedded in the underlying Rishonim and Mechaber abbreviates their case. But the limitation is necessary. There is also a possible question from the rule above that where witnesses do not remember which day in Tishrei the kinyan occurred, they should not write from the beginning of Cheshvan because of the appearance of falsehood. Why is the problem there only appearance of falsehood? Would writing Cheshvan not also harm purchasers? Suppose one creditor's kinyan occurred sometime in Tishrei and another's later in Tishrei. If the first document is written as Cheshvan, a purchaser who bought in Cheshvan might say, "You had no earlier lien against me." But the actual land was in any event encumbered from the kinyan in Tishrei. The purchasers after Tishrei bought property already subject to one of the liens. The uncertainty concerns which creditor has priority among themselves, not whether the purchaser acquired unencumbered property. Thus there is no independent injustice to the purchaser. Each purchaser after the kinyanim bought land that was already subject to at least one creditor. At most, each creditor can deflect the other toward different property until they clarify priority. This is not the kind of wrongful purchaser loss that invalidates an antedated document. It is particularly clear according to Kenesset HaGedolah, who holds the creditors can be compelled to authorize one another and ultimately collect through proper coordination. Accordingly, the central obstacle to writing the invented Cheshvan date remains the appearance of falsehood, not substantive purchaser harm. Thus the present law is sound only when understood in the kinyan-document framework.
