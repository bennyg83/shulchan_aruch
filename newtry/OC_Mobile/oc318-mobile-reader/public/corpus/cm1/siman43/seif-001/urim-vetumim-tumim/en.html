And some say that if they made a kinyan, etc. Shach, seif katan 2, accepts this ruling as primary and brings proofs for it. In my view the law requires substantial reconsideration. Tosafot in Ketubot 94 write that two loan documents dated the same day create equal liens even if one was written and delivered earlier. Since the priority is not evident from the face of either document, neither lien is treated as earlier until the next day, when both documents are equally public. Raah, Ritva, and Ran extend this: if a document states only the year, even witnesses who prove it was written at the beginning or middle of the year do not allow collection from purchasers until the end of that year, because before then the earlier lien is not evident from the document and does not generate public notice. A document that gives only the year is effectively like a document with no precise date. If outside proof of the writing date cannot create purchaser priority there, all the more so should outside witnesses not create purchaser priority for a document containing no date at all. There is also a comparison to a postdated document. Avodah Zarah says one does not collect until the date stated in the document even if the paper reached beit din earlier. One can distinguish: a postdated document is worse because by intentionally delaying the date the parties are treated as having conditioned that the lien should not begin until that later date. A document with no date contains no such condition. This distinction exists elsewhere. Tosafot hold that a postdated get does not take effect until its date, whereas a dateless get can be valid from delivery. Still, the comparison remains difficult, especially where the postdating was not authorized clearly by the debtor. More significantly, the year-only document is hard to distinguish. One cannot say that writing the year but not the day itself means, "the lien shall begin only at the end of the year." A get that says merely "in such-and-such seven-year cycle" can take effect from delivery and is not treated as postponed until the cycle ends. Thus partial dating does not itself create a delayed condition. If a dateless document allowed purchaser collection from the day witnesses later prove they saw it, the year-only document should work the same way. Rabbenu Tam might disagree. He explains equal-priority same-day loan documents through a special enactment against closing the door to borrowers; by strict law, if one delivery was proved earlier, that creditor might have earlier priority. On his theory, a dateless document could perhaps gain priority through outside proof. But Rabbenu Yerucham, who is the source of the present ruling, himself writes elsewhere that same-day loan documents do not acquire priority until the time evident from within the document, following Rif and Tosafot rather than Rabbenu Tam. So his ruling cannot simply be explained by Rabbenu Tam. The Yerushalmi in Perek HaMegareish is even more explicit. It discusses one of the three invalid gittin, a document lacking a date, and says it does not collect from encumbered property. R. Bunah explains because purchasers can claim collusion: the borrower may now say the debt preceded them even though it did not. R. Ila says the reason is that the document itself is defective for public-lien purposes. The Yerushalmi then asks: suppose the father borrowed under an undated document and died, and only afterward the son sold the inherited property. In that case there is no possibility that the purchasers preceded the debt, because the father necessarily borrowed before his death and the son's sale occurred later. Nevertheless the undated document still does not collect from purchasers. This proves the defect is lack of public document status, not factual uncertainty about which event occurred first. Rashba to Gittin cites the Yerushalmi expressly and says the undated writing is a valid document against free property but not against encumbered property because it lacks publicity. This is a direct refutation of the broad rule that later witnesses can establish a date and restore purchaser collection. Therefore Rabbenu Yerucham's language must be limited. The correct case is where the document contains a kinyan. Then even without a written document at all, witnesses to the kinyan create public notice and collection from encumbered property. If later witnesses see the undated document, they can establish at least that by that day the kinyan had already occurred. The absence of a written date does not weaken a lien whose publicity comes independently from the kinyan. This also fits Raah, Ritva, and Ran, who agree that where a kinyan itself is proved, the creditor can collect from the time the kinyan is established. Thus the present ruling should be read as a kinyan document, not an ordinary undated loan document. Shach's proof from a dateless get does not apply. When a divorced woman proves when the get reached her, she is not collecting because of a creditor lien that requires publicity. From the moment of divorce, the husband's subsequent sale of her property is simply a sale of something he no longer owns. The same is true of a gift or sale document: once ownership passed, a later transfer by the former owner is ineffective regardless of public creditor notice. Loan liens are different. Likewise, the rule concerning a found get is irrelevant. Once the woman proves the get was in her possession before it was lost, the get returns to ordinary validity from its written date or delivery. The issue is not creation of a creditor lien through an undated loan document. This also explains why Mechaber says initially that loan documents require dates because of collection from purchasers, rather than because an earlier oral creditor may have priority over a later creditor from free assets. As to priority among free assets, a creditor can show his undated document to beit din or witnesses immediately and thereby establish factual priority between creditors. Public notice is irrelevant there. But for purchasers, mere later proof that the document once existed does not supply the publicity needed for a creditor lien. Therefore written dating is essential. The practical conclusion is clear. A dateless loan document without kinyan does not collect from purchasers merely because witnesses later prove when they saw it. A dateless document containing a valid kinyan can collect from the time the kinyan is independently established, because the kinyan itself supplies publicity. The present ruling should be limited accordingly. There is a further proof from the very nature of publicity. If an undated loan document is shown privately to two witnesses today, that does not create the kind of public notice purchasers are expected to investigate. The witnesses know the document exists, but the marketplace does not. A kinyan is different because the act itself is ordinarily performed before witnesses as a formal transaction and is treated by halachah as generating public notice. This is why Rishonim repeatedly say "witnesses to a kinyan create a voice." The legal significance lies in the transaction, not merely in the existence of people who happen to have seen a paper. Therefore, later proof that witnesses saw an undated document cannot replace the missing date for purchaser collection. If it could, every undated document could be converted into a retroactively public lien merely by finding people who happened to see it early. That would defeat the entire purpose of requiring a date. The Yerushalmi's father-and-son case is decisive precisely for this reason. No factual uncertainty remains about sequence: the father's debt necessarily preceded the son's sale. Yet the purchasers are protected because there was no public date-bearing lien to warn them. Thus the defect is institutional, not evidentiary. This also explains the distinction between free assets and encumbered assets. For free assets, priority between creditors can turn on the actual temporal order of their obligations. If the first creditor can prove through witnesses that his debt arose earlier, that can matter even though no public document existed. There are no outside purchasers whose reliance must be protected. For purchasers, however, actual chronology alone is insufficient. The earlier lien must also have the public form required to bind outsiders. A dateless paper does not provide that form. This is why Mechaber's introductory reason for writing dates focuses on purchasers and not merely on contests among creditors. There is also an important consequence where the undated document itself expressly contains a kinyan clause. If the court can establish that the kinyan occurred no later than a certain day, collection from purchasers can begin from that day even though the paper has no date. The proof does not work because the paper was seen. It works because the kinyan, once established, independently created public lien force. This is the only coherent reading of Rabbenu Yerucham. It also harmonizes his ruling with his separate acceptance of the rule that same-day loan documents share equal priority until their order is evident from the face of the instruments. There is therefore no contradiction. One should also distinguish a dateless document from one whose date was accidentally erased after originally being valid. If witnesses establish that a valid dated document once existed and can reconstruct its date under the laws of erased documents, the creditor can regain the original priority because the public lien once truly existed. An instrument that was created without a date never had that publicity in the first place. Later witnesses cannot manufacture it. This distinction is essential. Likewise, a document bearing only a year may create some public notice that an obligation exists during that year, but it still does not identify priority within the year. Therefore the authorities delay purchaser collection until the latest possible point within the stated period. That is not because the lien literally begins then. It is because only then can one say with certainty that every purchaser during the stated period preceded no validly identifiable lien date. The creditor bears the burden against purchasers. This reinforces the same principle. Accordingly, an ordinary undated loan document cannot be given retroactive purchaser force through witnesses who later testify when they first saw it. The exception is a separately established kinyan or another transaction that independently creates the necessary publicity.
