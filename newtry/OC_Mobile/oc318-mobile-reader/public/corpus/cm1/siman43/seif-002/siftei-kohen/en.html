He omitted the hundreds, and so forth. I do not know why the Mechaber ruled this way without qualification, since the Beit Yosef left the matter requiring study and suggested that Terumat HaDeshen may have been stringent only for bills of divorce because of the severity of forbidden relationships, not for monetary documents. Terumat HaDeshen's first proof is not compelling. The Rosh's statement that omission of the era is attributed to scribal abbreviation prevents us from saying that the scribe counted according to another, later era, which would allow purchasers to claim that their acquisitions preceded the note. That is the issue in Avodah Zarah and Sefer HaTerumah. It does not establish that every written date must be treated literally despite an obvious omission. His second proof from the Mordechai is likewise inconclusive. The word “thousands” can denote two or more. Therefore, when it is written without the grammatical form that means precisely two thousand, it can be interpreted consistently with the true date. Had it expressly said “two thousand,” no omission could repair it and the document would necessarily be antedated. Here, by contrast, the hundreds may simply have been omitted. Since either explanation is possible, it is more reasonable to attribute the discrepancy to omission than to regard the note as antedated. The Ribash appears to invalidate only omission of the tens, not omission of the hundreds. That distinction is readily understood. Rabbi Moshe Mintz likewise validates after the fact a date omitting two hundred years, reasoning that no one would deliberately antedate a document by so extraordinary a period; it is plainly forgetfulness. This is the primary ruling. It also appears that Terumat HaDeshen was stringent only for bills of divorce, and even there grounds for leniency may exist.
