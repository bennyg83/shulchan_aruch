It was on the twenty-fourth day, etc. Sema, seif katan 8, cites Mordechai that where the weekday and the date of the month conflict, we can say the witnesses erred regarding even two successive month determinations. Sema adds that this can apply even though after most of a month has passed we ordinarily do not attribute such an error, and even in writing where the contradiction itself proves a mistake. His words are obscure. Tosafot in Perek Get Pashut, concerning a document dated on Shabbat, imply that even written testimony is not ordinarily treated as mistaken about the calendar after most of the month has passed. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 57, however, discusses an antedated document where the witnesses thought Adar had been deficient rather than full and therefore wrote the first regnal year when technically the second had begun. That seems to allow written calendar error even after much of the month. In truth, I already explained above, siman 30, that where the weekday and date necessarily contradict, one of them must be wrong. People are far less likely to err about the weekday than about calendrical calculations. Therefore, even if calendar error is unusual, it is still preferable to attributing the error to the weekday. In civil cases either saying they erred or saying they corrected themselves can preserve the testimony. Capital cases are different because formal interrogation requires a consistent answer to both day-of-week and day-of-month questions. If one answer must be discarded, the required interrogations are incomplete. There is, however, another difficulty. Why does Mordechai need to explain exactly how the witnesses erred at all? If the contradiction is evident from the document, there seems no danger of collecting from an improper earlier date. Beit din can see the inconsistency, and the burden of proof rests on the creditor. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 72 likewise say an antedated document can sometimes be found valid where the error is obvious. Perhaps Mordechai is concerned not about improper collection but about the witnesses' personal validity. If there is no plausible innocent error, they signed a false date and become disqualified, which can invalidate the document independently. That is possible but somewhat strained. A better explanation is that Mordechai, like Rivash, worries that the contradiction may not remain obvious years later. Judges may not remember which weekday corresponded to which date in that old calendar year. Then an apparently antedated document could be used improperly. If so, merely saying the witnesses made a calendar mistake does not solve the collection concern. The document would still appear to allow collection from the earlier date. The answer is that the document is actually postdated. We assume it was prepared months earlier, before the relevant month arrived, and the witnesses wrote a later date. At the earlier time, before the month's calendar was publicly fixed, they calculated in advance and erred about whether the intervening months would be full or deficient. This explains how they could err even about two successive month determinations and even after what would later become most of the month: at the time they wrote, the month had not yet begun at all. It also eliminates improper collection. Since the document was actually postdated to Tishrei, the creditor is not collecting from an earlier false date; he is delaying collection. After writing this, I found that Shev Yaakov, Even HaEzer 15, cites a similar explanation in the name of the author of Shevut Yaakov. I was pleased to have reached the same understanding. Shev Yaakov objects that the rule "people do not err in two month determinations" remains unanswered. That objection is incorrect. Sanhedrin 41 explains the reason they do not err in two determinations because by the second discrepancy most of the earlier month has already passed. But here the witnesses calculated before the month even began. The ordinary safeguard of public calendar knowledge did not yet exist. The alternative Gemara explanation concerning two shofar sounds likewise applies when witnesses actually heard the public signals. Here they wrote in advance and heard no shofar at all. Tosafot in Bava Batra 171 say that if the document says a certain weekday during Tishrei but that day was Yom Kippur, we do not say the witnesses merely erred in the calendar because the sanctity of the day was publicly known. This too supports the advance-writing explanation: the document must have been written earlier, before the actual festival date arrived. Shach asks why Rama here validates the document without mentioning dissent, while in Even HaEzer 126 Rama cites Rivash as dissenting regarding a get whose weekday and month-date conflict. There is no contradiction. In monetary documents, if we understand the paper as postdated, the calendar error can be innocent and there is no improper earlier collection. In a get, however, Rambam's view that a postdated get is invalid creates a separate problem. Therefore Rama cites a dissent there. One might object that a postdated monetary document lacking an after-acquired-property clause can also be invalid in some respects. That would be significant if the only basis for validity were the advance-writing theory. But Rosh, responsum 45, gives a broader explanation. Even though witnesses ordinarily do not err after most of the month, here they necessarily erred somewhere because their own statements conflict. It is more reasonable to attribute the error to the calendar date than to the weekday. This does not depend on saying the document was postdated. Rivash can agree that one of the two dates is wrong yet remain uncertain which. In civil law, that uncertainty itself can prevent improper collection because the ambiguity is apparent to beit din. In a get, however, uncertainty can create the very concern of shielding adultery. If the woman commits adultery on the twentieth, she can say perhaps the month-date is correct and she was already divorced, and capital punishment cannot be imposed out of doubt. Rosh, by contrast, treats the weekday as certainly correct and the month-date as the mistaken part. Therefore he sees no such shielding. This explains why Rama omits Rivash's dissent here but cites it in gittin. There is an additional possibility. If a get comes before us stating, for example, "Wednesday, the twentieth of the month," and we do not know on which day it was actually written, one could treat the later-written conflicting clause as a retraction rather than an error. According to authorities who allow retraction before the completion of formal examination, that could preserve the get differently. Rivash's actual responsum involved a get known to have been written and delivered on the nineteenth while the document stated the seventeenth. There the month-date was objectively antedated and could not be rescued by uncertainty. Thus the factual cases are not identical. The practical rule in civil documents remains that an internal weekday-date contradiction does not automatically invalidate the witnesses. We attribute the discrepancy to a plausible calendar mistake or postdating, especially where the document itself reveals the conflict. In gittin, because of the additional concerns of date, shielding, and postdating, the law is stricter and disputed. There is also a distinction between civil testimony and testimony in gittin concerning the legal effect of uncertainty. In civil law, if the document itself contains two inconsistent dates, beit din sees the contradiction when the paper is produced. Even if decades have passed and the old calendar is not immediately known, the court can investigate. If uncertainty remains, the creditor cannot extract from purchasers on the basis of whichever date benefits him. Thus the internal contradiction itself protects against wrongful collection. In a get, uncertainty is more dangerous because the issue can be whether the woman was married or divorced at the time of adultery or another severe prohibition. A reasonable doubt can prevent punishment or alter personal status. That is why Rivash can be lenient enough to preserve a monetary document while still invalidating a get. The practical consequences of doubt differ. This also explains why the rule that witnesses do not err after most of the month is not absolute in the present setting. That rule describes ordinary oral recollection after the calendar has become public. Here the document itself proves that one of two calendar designations cannot be correct. Once we know some error occurred, we choose the more plausible type of error. Weekdays are part of everyone's continuous weekly cycle and are much harder to miscalculate. Month lengths, intercalations, and future festival dates require calendrical computation and are more susceptible to mistake, especially when written in advance. Thus even an otherwise unlikely calendar error becomes the preferred explanation over a weekday error. This is not an abandonment of the ordinary rule. It is comparative evidentiary reasoning in a case where error is certain. One can formulate Rosh's view in exactly this way. He does not say people commonly err after most of the month. He says that because the witnesses' two statements contradict, and because one must be wrong, we assign the error to the calendrical side. Rivash stops one step earlier. He agrees there is an error but treats it as unresolved which side contains it. That is why their disagreement matters so much in gittin but much less in monetary documents. The advance-writing explanation gives an independent route to validation even without choosing between them. If the paper was actually prepared in Iyar but dated for Tishrei, the witnesses were not speaking from an already public calendar. They were projecting future month lengths. In that setting, even two consecutive calendrical mistakes are readily understandable. There was no public Yom Tov, shofar, or majority-of-the-month information yet available to correct them. The later reader sees the resulting mismatch only because history eventually fixed the calendar differently. This also answers the concern that the witnesses would thereby be false witnesses. They did not knowingly sign falsehood. They made a good-faith advance calculation that turned out wrong. The document therefore need not be rejected because of witness disqualification. At the same time, because the paper was postdated, no purchaser is harmed by an earlier false priority. Thus both witness validity and monetary safety are preserved. This is why the interpretation is so compelling.
