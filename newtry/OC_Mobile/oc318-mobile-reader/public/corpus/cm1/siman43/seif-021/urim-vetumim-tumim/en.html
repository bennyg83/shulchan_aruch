If they remember the date of the kinyan, etc. One should ask that Rashbam disagrees. Rashbam understands the rule, "If you know the day of the kinyan, write the day of the kinyan," as applying to sale and gift documents without warranty. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 56, part 1, law 7, explains that Rashbam follows his own view that although an ordinary kinyan stands for writing, the obligor can retract from the writing until the paper is actually prepared. If so, the kinyan does not create full public notice. Since the obligor can still stop the writing at any time, people do not assume a document will necessarily issue. Baal HaTerumot apparently disagrees and treats the kinyan as sufficiently public. How can he disagree with the Talmudic rule that a person can retract from the document while unable to retract from the land? Bnei Yaakov raises this. The answer is that Baal HaTerumot follows Rashba, responsum 1034, who limits that retraction rule to a transaction without kinyan. Where a kinyan occurred, the party cannot necessarily stop the writing. Then the kinyan remains fully public and the difficulty disappears. One may still ask according to Rashbam himself. Rashbam holds that even after a kinyan in a sale or gift, the transferor can retract from having a document written. Yet Rashbam agrees that in no-warranty sales and gifts, if the kinyan date is known, the witnesses may write from that date. If the transferor's ability to revoke writing destroys publicity, why does the kinyan date help there? The answer is that those instruments do not need publicity to create a creditor lien. Their principal role is to maintain title to the transferred field. The property transfer itself already occurred through the kinyan. Whether the kinyan generated a public "voice" is irrelevant to the recipient's ownership. Therefore the ability to retract from writing does not undermine use of the kinyan date in a no-warranty transfer document. Baal HaTerumot's statement that the obligor's right to retract prevents the lien from arising means only that the kinyan lacks enough public force for creditor collection from purchasers. That is not needed in a simple gift or sale title instrument. This resolves the difficulty against Baal HaTerumot. Still, a problem remains for Mechaber. Above in siman 39, Mechaber cites two opinions whether a party who made a kinyan can retract from the writing. If Baal HaTerumot is correct that this dispute determines whether the kinyan itself has publicity, Mechaber should not rule here unconditionally that a loan can use the kinyan date. The answer is that Rosh does not accept Baal HaTerumot's dependency. Rosh rules in Perek Get Pashut that even in a loan, a kinyan document can be written from the date of the kinyan. Yet Rosh also holds, as explained at the beginning of siman 39, that even after kinyan the party can in some circumstances retract from the writing. Therefore Rosh necessarily holds the two questions are independent. Even if the obligor theoretically retains some power to object to the paper, the witnesses ordinarily publicize the kinyan on the assumption that he will not object. So long as no protest occurs, the kinyan has a voice sufficient to support writing from its date. Mechaber follows Rosh on this point rather than Baal HaTerumot. That is why he can cite the dispute about retraction above yet rule here that the kinyan date is used in a loan.
