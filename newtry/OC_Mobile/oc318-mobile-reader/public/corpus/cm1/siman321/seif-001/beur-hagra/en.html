One who leases for a fixed rent or, etc. The Gemara there: Rav Pappa said, "these are two separate rules, etc."
But in a sharecropping arrangement, etc. See Rashi there, s.v. "let him say to him, etc.," and "the Tanna, etc.," and Rashi 105b to the Mishnah, s.v. "a regional calamity, etc." Necessarily it must be understood this way, as shown by the Gemara's statement that from then onward there is, etc. Although the Mishnah can refer to both types of arrangements, the Gemara's question concerns only fixed-rent leasing. This is also implied by Tosafos there, s.v. "makah, etc.": although, etc., nevertheless here in the latter clause, etc., since, etc. This implies specifically the latter clause, because he said, etc. The Tur, however, writes that the law applies both to fixed-rent leasing and sharecropping. Nimukei Yosef writes that Rambam also holds this view. Shulchan Aruch here begins with "one who leases and one who accepts as a sharecropper," and then says "if, etc." Although it then says "his lease payment," this is not exclusive; it merely uses the Mishnah's language, just as in the first clause it says "he does not deduct, etc." This accords with Tosafos's second answer 104a, s.v. "de'efshar," "alternatively, etc." Tosafos's language elsewhere reflects their first answer. The Tur holds that both answers are correct as law, and Hagahos Ashri there writes likewise. Perishah answers that the present case is not exactly like a locust plague, because the lessee can refrain from drawing water and say he will not irrigate, etc. See Sema, seif katan 5, based on Tosafos's first answer. This differs from the Mishnah's case where the small river dried up, where the sharecropper is obligated to continue working, as Tosafos write. Yet even there the latter clause says that he deducts even in sharecropping. Tosafos therefore had to explain that this is because he specified "an irrigated field, etc."
The same applies to every, etc. As stated there 105b regarding a locust plague, where in fixed-rent leasing or rental one always deducts, according to the first Tanna. The Gemara there says, "there is no one who takes this into account, etc.," excluding sharecropping. This is why the text says "from his rent."
(Collected note) Where the matter was entirely lost. See seif 2 in the gloss.
And if it is possible, etc. This follows Tosafos's first answer cited above. Certainly according to Rambam's approach, which rejects the second answer, the first answer must be accepted.
Maharam likewise ruled, etc. Mordechai, siman 470, brings proof that a regional calamity applies also prospectively, unlike Maharam of Padua.
And every, etc. Maharam of Padua likewise writes this in siman 86, and so does Hagahos Ashri. This requires study, for on the contrary, because a regional calamity causes a deduction from the rent, as above. Hagahos Ashri in chapter HaOmanin likewise raises this question concerning a garment: "if the cancellation is because of the ruler's decree, etc.; this requires study." Darkei Moshe answers that rental of land differs because land remains in its owner's possession and the lessor also suffers the loss, unlike other hired property. But it remains unclear why this distinction is made specifically because it is a regional calamity. The gloss's statement "the same applies to every similar case, etc.; and Maharam ruled, etc." appears to imply that even with other rental arrangements he always deducts. Maharam of Padua, siman 39, likewise implies this. He explains that the reason rent is reduced while in a sale a loss that arises afterward falls on the buyer is that in a sale the property is entirely in the buyer's domain, whereas in rental it remains the lessor's property, as the Ran writes in chapter HaMekabel. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes there that the lessor benefits from the fruits, and similarly in any rental the lessor benefits from the rent, because if the object is destroyed he loses the rent from that point forward, as stated above in siman 310 regarding a donkey and siman 312 where a house fell or burned. Thus the lessor is benefiting throughout the rental period. This also requires study from siman 334:1 in the gloss, "and likewise with every unavoidable event, etc." Sema likewise raises this difficulty. The Rama was careful there to write, "and if it is a regional calamity, etc.," referring to siman 321. He holds that even where both parties knew or neither knew and ordinarily the worker bears the loss, in a regional calamity the employer bears the loss. This is the opposite of regional calamity regarding land, where it operates as a leniency for the landowner. This is why he refers to siman 321 and not siman 322. Nevertheless his words are difficult and overturn the usual order.
Others disagree, etc., as stated above siman 310, etc. Meaning, he wrote there that if the proceeds are insufficient either to purchase or rent another animal, the lessor pays only for half the journey. Specifically if the animal merely became ill or mentally unfit and remains fit for the contracted work can he say, "here is yours before you." But not where it died and the matter was entirely lost. Likewise where "this house" fell, as stated above siman 312:17, the lessor is not obligated to rebuild it. If so, the renter should have retracted when he saw the loss; if he did not, he knowingly accepted it. This is like siman 232:3: one who sells to his fellow, etc.; but if he used it, etc. See there seif 4: "one may not, etc., and he cannot say, etc.; and likewise if, etc." There it is written that this differs from a locust plague, because there the past cannot be undone, and similarly where the tree was cut down or the spring dried up, for if they now retract all the prior labor was in vain. Darkei Moshe writes: I am surprised that he brings proof from a donkey that died, which is not a regional calamity, to a case that is a regional calamity. Perhaps the cited ruling speaks only of deduction and not of a right to withdraw. The law of a teacher, etc., likewise appears to show this. These distinctions regarding a right to withdraw depending on whether there is a regional calamity are unsupported. See Sema, who also challenges him. Nevertheless, the Rama's view appears correct from our sugya. See seif 2 in the gloss, "others say specifically, etc.; but if he said, etc." They prove this from the Tosefta cited by the Rosh: "I rent from you an irrigated field; I rent from you a field with trees. If the spring dried up or the tree was cut down, he must provide another spring or tree. If he said, 'this irrigated field I rent from you,' or 'this field with trees,' and the spring dried up or the tree was cut down, he deducts from the rent." The Rosh writes that even in the first clause, where he did not say "this," the specification is binding because the lessee said it to the lessor. This creates a difficulty for Maharam of Padua. According to him, in the first clause the lessee could withdraw, yet the Tosefta says only that he deducts from the rent. Nor can it be said that his past work would have been in vain, because the lessor must dig another spring. One must therefore say that Maharam of Padua follows Tosafos and the Rosh there, s.v. "idi, etc.," that in the first clause there is no deduction even where the lessee said the description to the lessor, because the Mishnah concerns such a case. This resolves the question of Bach and Derishah, who note that the Tosefta appears to imply the opposite and ask how the Rosh could cite it immediately afterward. According to what I wrote, the Tosefta supports the Rosh: there is no deduction because the lessee can withdraw. According to this, the Rama is correct that this rule does not apply to a regional calamity, because the Mishnah itself concerns a case where the lessee described the field to the lessor, as above. What, then, is the Gemara's question, "what are the circumstances? If, etc."? Yet it appears to me, on the contrary, that Maharam of Padua does not accept Tosafos's approach, for Tosafos struggle whether the description is binding, whereas according to his view even if it is binding he does not deduct, as above. Rather, he follows Nimukei Yosef and Maggid Mishneh. Their statement that it is "binding" means only that he may withdraw, as in the Tosefta, not that he receives a deduction. This is why Nimukei Yosef and Maggid Mishneh do not say that he deducts, only that the specification is binding, unlike what is stated in seif 2 in the gloss. The gloss there is difficult, for in a place without a regional calamity Maharam of Padua concedes, so why should he deduct? According to this, there is no practical disagreement between Nimukei Yosef and the Rosh.
