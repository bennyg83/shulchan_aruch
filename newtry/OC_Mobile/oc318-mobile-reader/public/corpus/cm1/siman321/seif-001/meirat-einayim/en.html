And it is an irrigated field. Meaning, a dry field for which rainfall is insufficient and which therefore requires irrigation. It is called a beit hashalchin from the verse, "who sends water upon the open places." To avoid the great labor of bringing water from a river, people ordinarily dig a spring in such a field and draw water from it for irrigation. The Mechaber and Tur's phrase, "and it is an irrigated field," means that the particular field he leased already had that status at the time. It does not mean that the tenant said, "Lease me this irrigated field," as in the following seif.
Or an orchard field. Meaning, a grain field in which trees also grow. The fruits of the trees come to the sharecropper without additional labor, as explained at the end of siman 320.
And the large river did not dry up. Had the large river dried up, it would be a regional calamity and the tenant would deduct from the rent, as explained afterward in siman 322.
But it is possible to bring water with a bucket. Otherwise, the neighboring farmers presumably could not bring water either, because their small channels would likewise have dried up, and the event would be a regional calamity for which the fixed-rent tenant deducts from the rent, as Tosafos explain in one answer. Tosafos give additional answers with practical differences regarding a sharecropper. In the Derishah at the beginning of this siman I recorded their wording. I did not reproduce it here because Rama concludes that in a sharecropping arrangement there is no deduction even in a regional calamity, and according to that view Tosafos' other distinctions are not relevant. Examine this carefully.
But with a sharecropping arrangement, etc. Rama rules without qualification like Nimukei Yosef, even though Tosafos, the Rosh, the Tur, and those who follow them imply that even a sharecropper may deduct. According to the Tur, one must distinguish this case from one where locusts ate the crop or it was blighted, which is also a regional calamity and will be discussed in siman 322, where a sharecropper does not make a deduction but each party simply takes the agreed share of whatever actually grew. Here, where the large river dried up, the sharecropper can withdraw before the grain and fruit develop and say, "I did not accept the field on the understanding that I would have to draw water from distant rivers." This follows Tosafos' language cited in the Derishah at the beginning of the siman. When locusts consume or blight the crop, by contrast, the event has already happened and does not allow the sharecropper to withhold his labor on that account. See the Derishah.
And wherever he may deduct, there is no distinction between what has already passed and the future. Rama writes here, as he also wrote in Darkei Moshe to this siman, that Maharam ruled accordingly, while others disagree and hold that prospectively the matter stands under the law of retraction. The dissenting view cited here is the position of Maharam Padua in responsum 39. Rama challenges it in Darkei Moshe. In my eyes Rama's challenge is astonishing, because one who studies Maharam Padua's actual responsum will see that his words are clear. Maharam Padua was asked about Reuven, who rented to Shimon the right to a shop for six years at a fixed annual rent. The local ruler then ordered his judge not to enforce loans made by Jews to non-Jews on personal credit, but only loans secured by collateral. After this decree remained in force for about nine months and Shimon saw that it could not be rescinded, he demanded a retroactive reduction in rent for those nine months during which he could not use the shop for unsecured lending. Reuven refused. Maharam Padua answered that where a defect or deterioration affects the rented object prospectively, as here, the matter is governed by the right to retract rather than by a continuing deduction. This resembles one who rents an animal or house and a defect subsequently develops. There is no distinction in this respect between a regional calamity and an individual calamity. Once the rented thing changes and becomes defective, the renter can withdraw. If he does not withdraw, he is deemed to desire the rental despite the defect. If he later seeks to withdraw, he cannot retroactively deduct for the period during which he continued without objection, because his failure to withdraw demonstrates waiver of the defect. The source is the law in chapter HaOmanin concerning one who rents "this donkey" and it becomes sick or deranged. If it was rented for riding or to carry glassware and there is insufficient value in the animal to purchase or rent another, the renter may withdraw and pays only for the distance traveled before the defect arose. Although where an animal merely becomes impaired but remains fit to carry an ordinary load the owner can say, "Here is yours before you," that is because it remains capable of the precise work for which it was rented, albeit with greater effort and delay. In Maharam Padua's case, the inability to conduct unsecured lending destroys part of the profit-producing function, which is more like partial destruction. Where the defect can no longer be remedied, there is no reason to force a valuation and deduction that one or both parties might not have accepted at the outset. He further proves from siman 232 concerning a sale in which a hidden defect is discovered. If the buyer did not know of the defect, he may rescind even after many days; but if he used the item after learning of the defect, he has waived it and may no longer rescind. The same applies to a rental defect: once the defect arises, the renter should withdraw immediately, and if he does not, he waives the complaint. In a sale, a defect that arises only after the purchase ordinarily falls entirely on the buyer because the object is wholly his. A rental, however, although it is a sale for a day, remains connected to the owner because the underlying property remains his, as the Ran explains. Therefore a subsequent defect cannot simply be attributed to the renter's bad fortune; perhaps it arose from the owner's fortune. Both sides may therefore terminate the arrangement: the renter may refuse to continue at the full price, and the owner may refuse to continue at a reduced price. This is the substance of Maharam Padua's responsum, with some shortening and clarification. He later repeats the same reasoning and answers the objections of Maharar K. and Maharar Z. Their challenges were not directed against this fundamental logic or proof but against other aspects of the case. Rama, after citing Maharam Padua's reasoning, writes in Darkei Moshe that he is surprised: what proof can be brought from a donkey that dies, which is not a regional calamity, to a case that is a regional calamity? Perhaps in a regional calamity the law always remains one of proportional deduction rather than retraction. He cites a responsum of Maharam in Mordechai, chapter HaOmanin, concerning a teacher prevented from teaching by a governmental decree. Maharam ruled that it is a regional calamity and that the loss is upon the employer, implying that regional calamity also governs the future and the employer cannot simply cancel. Rama further distinguishes this from a leased field struck by locusts, where the tenant deducts from the rent rather than bearing the entire loss. Land remains in the owner's possession, and therefore the owner also bears part of the loss; with other rentals the full loss may fall upon the renter. He cites Responsa Maimoniyot siman 27 concerning a burned house. I do not understand Rama's objection from beginning to end. Maharam Padua explicitly prefaced his ruling by stating that, for a defect affecting the future, there is no distinction between a regional and an individual calamity. According to Rama, why should the death of a rented donkey, precisely because it is not a regional calamity and there is no deduction, necessarily fall under rescission? One could argue the reverse: in a fixed-rent lease struck by an individual mishap there is ordinarily no deduction because we attribute the loss to the renter's fortune. Then perhaps with a donkey too the renter should be unable to withdraw and should pay the full rent because the donkey died while in his possession through his own bad fortune. Responsa Maimoniyot siman 27 itself says concerning a rented house that burned that there is reason to require the renter to pay the entire agreed rent without deduction, because the house burned while in his possession and his fortune caused it, comparable to one who rents "this house" and it collapses. Its wording suggests that this may apply even if the renter has not yet paid. But if other houses in the city also burned, it is a regional calamity, and then the law resembles a donkey rented for a journey that dies halfway, where the renter pays proportionately for the part used. Thus, in the non-regional case that source actually suggests full payment, not rescission. Maharam, in another responsum cited in Maimoniyot siman 47, disagrees with that view, as I noted in siman 312, gloss to seif 17. In any event, the source shows that one cannot explain the donkey case as rescission simply because it is not a regional calamity, since regarding a burned or collapsed house some authorities hold that the renter cannot rescind at all. Rama's proof from Mordechai's case of a teacher prevented from teaching by governmental decree also does not establish that the employer must bear the entire loss. Since the decree was unusual and neither the employer nor teacher should have anticipated it, the ordinary rule would place the loss upon the teacher or worker, who seeks to extract payment, as explained in the Gemara, the Tur, and the Mechaber in simanim 333 and 334. Those sources do not distinguish between a regional calamity and another unavoidable event. Although Rama in siman 334, seif 1, refers the reader to siman 321 if it is a regional calamity, that reflects his own approach here, and I have not found this distinction in an earlier source. On the contrary, logic suggests that because a regional calamity affects owner and renter equally, one cannot attribute it to either party's fortune. We should therefore return to the general rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant, or at least divide the loss between them, just as the Gemara, Tur, and Mechaber rule above in siman 311 concerning an unspecified ship hired for unspecified wine when the ship sank and neither party can perform. There too they divide the loss unless one party offers to perform his side of the condition. The same reasoning would indicate that in the teacher case the loss should be divided, for if either party says he wants to perform, the other is equally willing but the ruler's decree makes performance impossible. Alternatively, it should resemble "this wine in this ship," where the party in possession has the advantage. Why then should Mordechai's simple statement that "the loss is upon the employer" mean that the employer must bear all of it even where he still holds the wages? In my opinion, Mordechai's wording does not say, "the entire loss is upon the employer." It says merely that if the teacher's interruption results from the ruler's decree and he cannot teach, it is a regional calamity "and there will be loss to the employer." The phrase can naturally mean that the employer too bears part of the loss, namely that they divide it, like the ship case. This gives force to Mordechai's reason: because it is a regional calamity, both are equally affected and neither one's fortune is blamed, so the rent is reduced or the loss divided in a manner that burdens both sides. Although Mordechai did not spell out that both parties bear the loss, his words are at least compatible with that reading, whereas reading them as placing the entire loss upon the employer generates the difficulties just noted. Rama's proof from Responsa Maimoniyot siman 27 concerning a burned house likewise provides no support for him; if anything, it supports my approach, as explained above. According to Rama's own position, his general rule must be that in a regional calamity one makes a prospective deduction rather than saying that the renter should have rescinded and, by failing to do so, waived the defect. The view he cites afterward as dissenting holds that the rule of waiver through failure to rescind applies only where there is no regional calamity and the loss falls on the worker or renter. Since the loss is his, he should withdraw promptly if he wishes to avoid it. In a regional calamity, however, the worker does not bear the loss, and therefore his failure to withdraw is not treated as waiver. Thus, a teacher, who has the status of a worker, places the loss on the employer in a regional calamity. Regarding the employer, there is no principle that he should have withdrawn, and in any event an employer cannot simply retract in every circumstance, as explained below in siman 333, seif 4. According to Rama, one must also say that he deliberately omitted the dispute concerning a burned house in siman 312, seif 17, because he ruled in favor of this view and inferred it from Mordechai's concise wording and from Responsa Maimoniyot siman 27, which compared a regional burning to the donkey case. Examine this carefully.
