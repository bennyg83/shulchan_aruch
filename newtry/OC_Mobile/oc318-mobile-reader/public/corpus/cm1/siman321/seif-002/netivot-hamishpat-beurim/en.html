I rent you this irrigated field. In my view the case cannot mean that he said merely "an irrigated field" or "an orchard" without identifying a specific parcel. Shitah Mekubetzet says that where one uses only the generic description, we do not say he sold or rented merely the field's name. Beit Yosef in siman 218 and Shulchan Aruch rule that if one says simply "a vineyard," we do not assume the designation is merely a name of a vineyard that no longer has vines. The Gemara's difficulty arises because he said "this." The true distinction is between a parcel being physically before the parties or pointed out, and a parcel merely identified by location. If the parties stand in the field, see the spring, and one says, "this irrigated field," the words contain deliberate extra specificity. He could have said simply "this." By adding "irrigated field" while the spring is visibly present, he shows insistence that the present condition remain. The same is true if he points to the parcel from outside and says, "this irrigated field." But if the property is not before them and he says, "the irrigated field I own in such-and-such place," there is no redundant wording. He must call the parcel by some identifying name so the other person knows which field he means. The description can therefore function merely as its name. This also explains the sale rules. If one says, "the vineyard I have in such-and-such place," even if there are no vines, the buyer can receive it because the word "vineyard" may be only the field's name. If the buyer is actually standing there and sees that there are no vines, one need not invoke the name-rule at all. The buyer sees the facts. Likewise, if a field visibly measures only half the stated amount and people do not make such a mistake, the transaction can still stand because the language is understood as praising its quality rather than guaranteeing the stated measure. Accordingly: If one rents merely "an irrigated field," whether or not the parcel is before them, and the spring later dries, the landlord must provide an irrigated field with a spring. If he says, "this irrigated field," while they stand in it or he points it out, the wording is a condition of quality. If the spring dries, the tenant reduces the rent. If they are not present and he says, "the irrigated field I have in place X," and the landlord is the one who used that description, it can be treated only as a name and the tenant does not reduce. If the tenant was the one who demanded "the irrigated field in place X," the poskim dispute whether the wording expresses insistence. This also reconciles Rosh. The abbreviated rulings of Rosh contain a textual reversal: where the printed text says "this" it should read "generic," and where it says "generic" it should read "this."
