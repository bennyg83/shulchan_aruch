(And that which we say, etc.) The Sema cites a dispute between Maharam of Padua and the Rama. Both positions require clarification. Maharam of Padua holds that when a defect affects the future, regional calamity does not produce a proportional deduction; the remedy is cancellation, except where the condition can be repaired through effort. This is untenable. If a sharecropper no longer desires a field because its trees or irrigation are gone, why could he not withdraw prospectively according to that theory? Tosafot nevertheless say that, absent a regional calamity, he must continue working. Similarly, when authorities search houses and seize animals, the Tur cites Rabbeinu Chananel that the donkey owner bears the loss and must supply another animal, even though the defect concerns future performance. Nor can every governmental decree be treated as a defect permitting cancellation, for when a donkey becomes weak but remains fit for its load, the renter must continue with it despite the added burden. The Rama's explanation is also difficult. Regional calamity determines whose fortune caused the loss and therefore who bears the reduction, but it does not itself prevent cancellation whenever deduction is available. The correct framework is as follows. When the subject of the lease is destroyed or wholly unfit for the contracted purpose, such as an animal that dies or breaks, or one hired for riding or glassware that becomes unfit, the lease cannot continue, and the result depends on whether a particular animal or an unspecified animal was hired. When the leased object remains in existence and usable but has suffered a partial impairment, neither party may cancel. This is not the ordinary law of a preexisting defect, which would constitute a mistaken transaction; the impairment arose later, and the question is whose fortune caused it. If it is a regional calamity, the landlord bears the loss and the rent is reduced proportionately. This applies to leased land and also to an animal when the loss affects the community generally. If it is not a regional calamity, the tenant bears the partial loss, as with a weakened animal still fit for the original work. Accordingly, in the shop case discussed by Maharam of Padua, where a decree impaired the lending business conducted there, the shop still existed for that activity but its usefulness was reduced. If the decree affected the community and could not be overcome even by effort, the rent should be reduced according to the impaired portion; if it was not a regional calamity, the tenant bears the loss. In neither case is there automatic cancellation. Thus the Rama's practical conclusion that the issue is deduction rather than cancellation is correct, although his comparison to a dead donkey and his statement distinguishing locust-damaged land are not properly explained. Maharam's ruling concerning a ruler's decree also shows that where there is a regional calamity the entire loss falls upon the employer or owner, regardless of whether the event was common or uncommon. The Sema's attempt to divide the loss in half is mistaken; Hagahot Ashri expressly says that the worker receives his entire wage. The distinction in siman 334 concerns an isolated mishap, not a regional calamity.
