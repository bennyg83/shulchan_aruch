Where the lessor said it to the lessee. The Mishnah says that where one rents "this irrigated field" or "this orchard" and the spring dries or the tree is cut, the rent is reduced. Shmuel distinguishes whether the description came from the lessor or lessee. Ravina says even where the lessor said it, the word "this" shows the field was already identified, so the unnecessary description "irrigated field" must express insistence. Rif and Rosh rule like Ravina. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, Ramban, Rashba, and Nimukei Yosef maintain that Ravina did not reject Shmuel completely: if the lessee himself says, "I rent an irrigated field," that alone is a specification even without "this." The Tosefta supports them. Rosh appears contradictory because he rejects that reading yet says that if the tenant says simply, "I rent an irrigated field," the lessor must provide another irrigated field if the spring dries. The resolution follows Ritva and Raavad's text in the parallel sale case. The conventional-name defense exists when a specific tract is identified, such as "this beit kor" or "the beit kor in such and such place." If the seller simply promises "a beit kor" without identifying a tract, he must provide a true beit kor and cannot answer that some smaller parcel bears that name. So too in rental. If the parties simply say "an irrigated field," the lessor must provide an actual irrigated field. Once a specific field is identified, the conventional-name defense becomes possible. Ravina then says that in rental the extra description is nevertheless significant because the field was already identified. In sale, by contrast, "this beit kor" can still be merely a conventional name. This reconciles Rosh with the Tosefta and explains the differing authorities.
Accordingly, the practical difference among the rental authorities is small. If a particular field is identified, the extra words create a specification under Ravina. If no particular field is identified, the lessor cannot satisfy the agreement with a field that merely bears the name; he must provide the actual type promised. The principal dispute therefore matters more in sale, where even a particular parcel called "this beit kor" can sometimes be sold according to its conventional name. See siman 219.
