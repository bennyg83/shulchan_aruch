If the purchaser acquired the land and it later rose in market value, Sema and Bach treat the increase as ordinary passive appreciation and therefore say the creditor takes only half. I do not see why a mere market increase should be treated like new appreciation. The body of the land itself was already encumbered. When the very same land rises in value, the creditor's existing lien rises with it. No new property came into existence and no after acquired clause is needed. The uncertainty about when the market value changed does not alter this. In many areas the present value is treated as the current status unless witnesses establish otherwise. More fundamentally, a market increase is not a separate asset. It is simply the increased value of the already encumbered body of the land. The rules of dividing appreciation apply to new growth or improvement added to the land, not to a simple rise in price. Therefore the creditor should be able to collect according to the increased value of the encumbered land.
Where the land was worth one thousand when sold and later depreciated to five hundred before foreclosure, Baal HaTerumot and Raavad indicate that the purchaser's warranty is measured so that he does not suffer a loss. The Shach says the seller need reimburse only the value at the time of foreclosure. Maggid Mishneh appears to say the same in his discussion of guaranty. Tosafot, however, points in the opposite direction. In the case where a purchaser encounters a challenge before taking possession, Tosafot asks why, after possession, he cannot simply say, "Take back your land for the money I owe you," and answers that the land may have depreciated. This answer works only if the seller's warranty would reimburse the original purchase price rather than the lower current value. Otherwise there would be no special difference between a sale with responsibility and one without it. Therefore the stronger rule is that when property is taken from a purchaser under the seller's warranty, reimbursement is according to the price the purchaser paid, so that the purchaser does not lose through the foreclosure. Since Tosafot and Raavad both support this, their approach should not be rejected.
Even if responsibility was not expressly written, a sale ordinarily carries responsibility because "responsibility is a scribal error." Some responsa argued that this does not apply to an exchange of houses, relying on the sugya of brothers who divide an estate and are treated like purchasers without responsibility. Their logic is that each party receives value instead of cash and, just as the brothers accept mutual uncertainty in the inherited shares, exchangers likewise accept the risks. In my view this is incorrect. Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash states explicitly that exchange is like sale and each party accepts responsibility to maintain the property given in exchange. A person does not throw away property given in exchange any more than he throws away cash. If he exchanges a sound house for another house, he naturally expects the other party to warrant that his house is not stolen and will not be taken by a creditor. The case of brothers is exceptional. Neither brother knows which portion was intrinsically his, and each inherited share is subject to the same possible claims arising from their father's estate. Each therefore agrees to take his allotted share without placing responsibility on the other. Ordinary exchangers do not stand in that relationship. Accordingly, responsibility is presumed in an exchange just as in a sale.
