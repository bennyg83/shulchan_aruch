Likewise, if it had been worth one thousand, etc. The Shach and Knesset HaGedolah both cite the Maggid Mishneh, who writes expressly that if the property declined in value, the purchaser can recover only according to the amount of the decline. It makes no difference that he paid a higher price when the land was then worth more; at the time of collection it is worth less. I initially thought to support this from the Rosh in Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui concerning the two mansion cases. Maharam writes that if the purchaser seeks to increase the appraisal, we do not listen to him, because the creditor can say, "What do I care if you add money? You will simply go back and collect it all from the seller." Suppose, for example, the property is appraised at 500 and the creditor is still owed another 200. If the purchaser adds 100 and pays 600, the creditor can later collect only 100 more, while the purchaser can recover only 500 because the added 100 is enhancement and cannot be collected from encumbered property. If the purchaser does not add, the creditor takes the property at 500 and later collects another 200, while the purchaser collects his 500 from other purchasers. Those purchasers therefore lose an additional 100. Apparently Maharam assumes the purchaser ordinarily does not add beyond his original purchase price; he deals with a case where the appraisal is below the price he paid. But that would seem to create an even greater loss for the later purchasers. If the purchaser originally bought for 600 and the property is now appraised at 500, then without his addition he should recover his full 600 principal from later purchasers and the creditor another 200. If he adds 100 to retain the field, the creditor later collects only 100. The later purchasers and the debtor benefit. This points toward the Maggid Mishneh's view: when the property has declined, the purchaser's collectible principal is limited to the current judicial appraisal, so his voluntary addition does not alter what he may collect. Admittedly it is strained to say Maharam stated his rule only in that particular factual configuration without explaining it. It may instead be that he follows Tosafot's answer that encumbered property in these cases is ordinarily found only through an apotiki. Otherwise later purchasers can say, "We left you property from which to collect." Property overseas is uncommon, and an after-acquired clause is ordinarily implied rather than expressly written. Maharam's case concerns no apotiki, so there may simply be no later encumbered property in the background. Even so, the prior argument remains forceful: Maharam's unqualified language should have distinguished a judicial appraisal below the original purchase price, where allowing the purchaser to add would benefit the debtor because the creditor's remaining claim would shrink. The omission strongly supports the Maggid Mishneh. On the other hand, Tosafot in Ketubot 50, s.v. "what claim does he have," write that an obligation can be considered unliquidated even if capped above, whenever it has no fixed lower amount and may continually decrease. This seems to support the Tur. Why does a purchaser recover principal from encumbered property while enhancement is collected only from unencumbered property, where one opinion in Gittin explains the distinction by saying enhancement is not fixed? The purchaser's principal would also be unfixed if a later decline in market value reduces the amount he may recover. Just as one cannot know whether the land will appreciate, one cannot know whether it will decline, and real estate prices vary from day to day. The only way principal is fully fixed is if the purchaser recovers the full amount he paid despite subsequent decline. Thus the Tur's position is compelling according to those Tosafot. But this very principle is disputed among the poskim. The practical rule therefore requires study. At minimum, the Tur and Mechaber, who above in siman 114 quote Maharam's rule without qualification that a purchaser cannot increase the appraisal where the creditor's entire debt remains unpaid, should have explained that if the judicial appraisal is below the original purchase price the purchaser may increase it, because doing so benefits the debtor, for whose sake the appraisal process operates. This remains difficult.
Or it was worth one thousand and he sold it for five hundred, etc. This means that at the time of collection it is still worth one thousand. But if it has now declined and is worth only five hundred, even the Tur, who disagrees with the Maggid Mishneh and holds that later depreciation does not reduce recovery where the purchaser originally paid one thousand, would agree here. This purchaser paid only five hundred, and the property is now worth no more than five hundred, so he has no claim beyond five hundred. This resolves Responsa of the Geonim, responsum 160. Reuven owed Shimon one thousand and owned two mansions, each initially worth one thousand. He sold one to Levi and one to Yehudah, each for five hundred. Shimon first seized Levi's mansion at an appraisal of five hundred. When he came to seize Yehudah's, Yehudah said, "If you wish to retain the first mansion at a value of one thousand, retain it; otherwise here is one thousand and withdraw." The Geonim ruled that although the creditor retained the first mansion at one thousand, the writ of seizure is written for only five hundred. At first glance this contradicts the Tur and Shulchan Aruch here, who say that where land worth one thousand was sold cheaply for five hundred, the purchaser's principal for purposes of recourse is nevertheless one thousand. There is no contradiction. The creditor certainly could not take the mansion except through a judicial appraisal, and beit din appraised it lawfully at five hundred. The property had therefore declined to that value. Since Levi paid five hundred and at the time of seizure it was worth five hundred, everyone agrees that the seizure document is only for five hundred. At first sight, the Geonim's responsum appears to conflict with the Rama above in siman 114, seif 3, citing Rabbenu Yerucham, that where there are two purchasers the second purchaser cannot say, "Take one thousand and withdraw." The distinction is what I wrote there: before completion of the public appraisal there is no difference between the parties, because each may increase the bid. The Geonim's incident occurred in that setting. It therefore accords with the Rama. Indeed, the responsum supports the Tur's broader position, because otherwise even if the purchaser had originally paid one thousand, the later decline would independently reduce his right. Examine this carefully.
Rather, merely with witnesses. Bava Batra 41b states that one who sells a field before witnesses alone creates a right of collection from encumbered property. The Ramban explains in his novellae that this means the purchaser's field was taken from him through beit din, and before he leaves beit din they write him a writ of seizure dating from the sale. Otherwise later purchasers could claim that perhaps the seller already repaid this purchaser. The Mechaber does not quote this qualification. The Ramban's position requires explanation. If we suspect repayment in a sale witnessed only orally, why do we not suspect the same where there was a written sale? If the answer is that in a written sale the seller should take back the sale document upon repayment, then in an oral sale he should similarly take the purchaser's loan or debt instrument, which would prevent further collection, as explained in Bava Batra 169a. If one says that a seller may fail to insist on receiving the underlying debt instrument, then the Gemara there becomes difficult. It asks why one cannot write two sale documents for the same field even with warranty, and answers that otherwise the purchaser could collect twice. But if beit din tears the debt instrument after the first collection, how could he collect again? The answer would have to be that on the first occasion he goes to the seller, who fails to insist on taking the debt instrument and takes back only one sale document. The purchaser could then use the second sale document while still holding the debt instrument, and the seller would no longer be believed to say he paid. The Gemara's concern therefore assumes that the seller does insist on taking the debt instrument. It must be that the Ramban meant a case where beit din removed the purchaser because the land was stolen, not because of the seller's debt. In that case there is no underlying loan instrument available to prove that reimbursement remains unpaid, so later purchasers could indeed claim that the seller paid the dispossessed purchaser. But where the field is seized because of a creditor's loan, the Ramban agrees that the seller cannot claim repayment while the creditor's debt instrument remains outstanding. The Tur and Mechaber therefore omit this qualification here, because our present case concerns property seized for the seller's debt, as the Mechaber expressly states. Examine this carefully.
Even if he did not expressly stipulate warranty. The Baal HaTerumot disagrees and writes at the beginning of Shaar 47 that one who sells before witnesses must expressly stipulate warranty, for where no document was written we do not say that warranty was omitted only by scribal error. Giddulei Terumah writes that he found no other authority who says this and, after searching, found no proof for the rule. The Shach notes that the Rashbam on Bava Batra 42, explaining Rav's statement that one who sells a field before witnesses may collect from encumbered property, writes explicitly that the seller "accepted warranty upon himself." Thus, says the Shach, the Baal HaTerumot does have support. That proof is not decisive. Giddulei Terumah's entire difficulty was that if lien liability is d'oraisa and a sale has publicity, why should an express warranty clause be needed? The Rashbam is explaining Rav, and Rav holds lien liability is not d'oraisa, so Giddulei Terumah's question does not arise there. More importantly, Hagahot HaRosh in Perek Get Pashut cites the Rashbam and Rabbenu Baruch of Greece as holding that in any sale we do not say warranty is automatically implied. Thus the Rashbam supplies no proof for a special distinction between an oral sale before witnesses and a written sale. The source of the Baal HaTerumot's rule can nevertheless be established. Tosafot in Bava Batra 141 discuss the rule that once witnesses have completed their agency they may not repeat it, because a second document may create the appearance of an antedated instrument. Yet the Gemara permits rewriting a sale document that contains no warranty. As I explained above in siman 41, we do not fear that witnesses will intentionally falsify the date and write an Adar date for a transaction that actually occurred in Nisan. Otherwise even a non-warranty sale document could not be rewritten, because an artificially earlier sale date might allow the purchaser to defeat a later purchaser. Rather, the concern is a more subtle error: the loan really occurred in Nisan, but the document is written only in Iyar, and the witnesses may mistakenly date it from Nisan even though that would be legally antedated. A non-warranty sale document causes no such collection problem. If an oral sale before witnesses automatically carried warranty, however, why would a warranty sale document ever be forbidden to rewrite once the first was known lost? Suppose the sale occurred in Nisan and the replacement is written in Iyar. Dating it from Nisan would be entirely proper, because the lien would already have arisen with the witnessed sale, just as a kinyan document is dated from the time of the kinyan. The very prohibition therefore indicates that an oral sale without an express warranty creates no lien. Sometimes the sale occurred before witnesses in Nisan without a warranty undertaking, and only in Iyar was a written warranty instrument executed. If the witnesses then date the document from Nisan, it is genuinely antedated and invalid. This strongly supports the Baal HaTerumot. I wrote above in siman 49 that a responsum of the Rashba appears to imply the same. Giddulei Terumah asks that if lien liability is d'oraisa and a sale is public, why is any warranty stipulation needed? The Shach answers correctly: before warranty is accepted there is no debt at all to which a Torah lien can attach. A person may buy land even for a single day. The purchaser paid and received possession of the land. Unless the seller undertook to repay him if the land is seized, what debt exists? The entire question is whether such warranty is implied or must be express. There is therefore no difficulty. However, a difficulty remains from Bava Metzia 72, where the Gemara asks why, when the true owner takes stolen land from the purchaser, the purchaser may collect his principal from encumbered property, since the sale instrument was not legally authorized to be written and should be treated as worthless. In truth the sale was void, so the purchaser's money remains with the purported seller as a loan. By Torah law an ordinary loan carries a lien; the only reason an oral loan ordinarily lacks recourse against purchasers is its lack of publicity. Here the transaction was publicly presented as a sale and therefore had publicity. Why should the invalidity of the document matter? Remove the document and the public transaction still exists. One cannot answer that stolen land is sold secretly. A document was in fact written, and Bava Batra itself derives publicity from the rule that one who sells a field before witnesses collects from encumbered property. The seller cannot now say that because the land was stolen he sold it in secret. The difficulty therefore remains. Tosafot there may have avoided it by explaining that the Gemara's concern is not the existence of the lien but that the seller should be believed to say he already paid because the document is legally worthless. According to Rashi and the other commentators, however, who understand the question as one of lien priority, it must be that the Gemara asks according to the authority who holds lien liability is not d'oraisa. One might object that even under that view, the Sages permit collection from purchasers where there is publicity, as Rav himself holds regarding a sale before witnesses. But the answer is clear. Since collection from encumbered property is only a rabbinic enactment, we do not extend it beyond its stated scope. The Sages enacted that a written loan may collect from purchasers, not an oral loan. The usual loan is private, and rare unusual transactions do not justify expanding an enactment that uproots the Torah default. A purported public sale of stolen property that later turns into a loan claim is an uncommon circumstance and was not the subject of the enactment designed to prevent the closing of the door before lenders. This also resolves the powerful question raised by Maharam Grato in Chavot Yair. The Rambam, followed by the Shulchan Aruch below in siman 373, seif 1, rules that if one stole land and sold it, even where the purchaser knew it was not the seller's, the purchaser may recover his principal from encumbered property. The Gemara's question that the instrument was not authorized to be written seems to apply, while its answer that the seller wishes to preserve his reputation cannot apply where the purchaser knew the land was stolen, as the Maggid Mishneh writes and the Rama records in siman 374, seif 2. Chavot Yair is forced into strained answers. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The entire question and answer concern the view that lien liability is not d'oraisa. We rule that it is d'oraisa. Since the public sale gives the transaction publicity, the purchaser collects from encumbered property independently of the invalid instrument. Where the supposed seller transferred the property to his minor son, by contrast, the claim is treated like an oral loan and does not collect from purchasers because the transaction itself was coercive or improper. Just as no valid sale arose because of the coercive circumstance, as Shitah Mekubetzet explains, no lien is thereby created. Why should the Sages impose a lien in favor of one who acted improperly? This also answers Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia 16, s.v. "what I will inherit," against Rav Hai Gaon, who ruled that Rav's rule concerning one who later acquires the stolen field from the true owner is not accepted because Rav follows his own view that a person can transfer something not yet in existence. Tosafot ask that the baraita concerning enhancement of land is accepted halachah and seems to rely on Rav's rationale that the seller wishes to maintain his credibility. According to what I have written, the baraita's difficulty according to Rav arises because Rav holds lien liability is not d'oraisa. Shmuel, who denies enhancement to a purchaser from a robber, cannot establish it in that case in the first place. It must concern a creditor or a case where the seller expressly made a kinyan. If there was an express kinyan that covered even the possibility of the sale proving stolen, the principal obligation is public and collectible. The distinctive question therefore concerns Rav. For us, who rule that lien liability is d'oraisa, there is no difficulty even without the credibility rationale. Tosafot follow their own interpretation that the issue is the plea of repayment; Rav Hai Gaon may follow Rashi. Examine this carefully.
For a gift has no publicity. The Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Malveh, chapter 11, writes this in the name of the Rashba. I do not understand it. If a donor gives property and accepts warranty over his assets for the gift, by what mechanism do his assets become obligated? The recipient gave him nothing in exchange through which the obligation could arise. A seller can subject his assets in consideration of the purchase money, but can a donor create a lien by mere words? It must be a case where a kinyan was made, and the kinyan itself subjects the donor's property. But a kinyan ordinarily has publicity, and a standard kinyan stands to be reduced to writing. The Ran in Perek HaNose cites the Rashba expressly as holding that a person cannot obligate himself by mere words, even by saying, "I owe you one hundred," contrary to the Rambam; see above in siman 40. All the more so he cannot assume warranty for nothing by mere speech without a kinyan. It is strained to say that the Rashba follows the Rosh's view that a kinyan not ultimately written has no publicity, since I have not found such a position stated by the Rashba. I therefore think the principal explanation is what Knesset HaGedolah cites in siman 43 in the name of a sage: where the kinyan is itself necessary to effect the underlying transaction, so that without it the transaction would not be legally complete, we do not say that an ordinary kinyan necessarily stands for writing. This resolves many difficulties, including Shmuel's answer that the parties made a kinyan and yet the scribe must still consult before writing a lien clause, as Tosafot state in Bava Metzia 15. The same is true of a guarantor whose obligation is effective through kinyan, yet if the kinyan occurred only after the signatures on the instrument he collects only from unencumbered property. Although Knesset HaGedolah disputes this distinction, he brings no decisive proof against it. In my view it is nearly compelled. This requires study.
Specifically when the witnesses testify, etc. Rabbenu Yonah, cited by the Rosh in Bava Batra chapter 3, requires that the purchaser prove the sale through the particular witnesses who were present, in order to prevent repeated collection. Bava Batra 169 states that any writ of seizure that does not say that the creditor's original loan document was torn is invalid. It is therefore possible that Rabbenu Yonah primarily speaks of a dispossessed purchaser whose land was taken as stolen property. There, later purchasers may face a genuine risk that he will collect twice. But when an ordinary creditor seizes the purchaser's field, the creditor's debt instrument must be torn at collection. If he later tries to seize again, he no longer has the debt instrument. Why then would the purchaser need a unique set of sale witnesses? The better explanation is that Rabbenu Yonah sees a more elaborate possibility of collusion. Ordinarily, how can the original creditor seize this purchaser's field if other property remains? The purchaser could say, "I left you property from which to collect." Tosafot therefore establish the case as one where the creditor lacks an after-acquired-property clause and cannot reach property the debtor acquired later, while the purchaser's warranty claim can reach it. Now suppose the creditor holds two notes, neither containing an after-acquired clause, and the field in the first purchaser's hands is the only property subject to both notes and is insufficient for both debts. The creditor could collect under the first note from that field and have the note torn. The purchaser would then properly receive a writ of seizure and collect from later purchasers whose property is subject to his warranty claim. Afterward the purchaser and creditor could collude by returning possession of the field to the purchaser. The creditor would then present the second note, which likewise lacks an after-acquired clause, and seize the same field again, because that original field remains subject to the debt. The second note would be torn, and the purchaser would again use a writ of seizure to collect from later purchasers. Those purchasers would thus pay twice, although the same field should have satisfied only one of the creditor's notes. Requiring a sale document or a single identified set of witnesses prevents repeated use in this fashion, because the document is controlled and the witnesses can testify whether they already appeared in beit din. A similar concern exists where the creditor lacks an after-acquired clause and can seize only this purchaser's field. He first seizes it. Later the true owner appears and proves that the field was stolen, thereby defeating the creditor's collection; because the creditor lacks an after-acquired clause, he cannot turn to property the debtor later acquired and sold. The purchaser could then collude with the true owner, obtain possession again, allow the true owner to seize it from him, and use his own sale witnesses to collect from later purchasers. He would thereby recover a principal that had already been effectively paid while shifting the creditor's unrelated loss onto those purchasers. Similar schemes can be constructed with two apotiki debts or where the debtor inherited or acquired this one field from a predecessor against whom both debts were valid but the later purchasers' property was not subject to them. Why then does the Gemara itself focus on the case of stolen property rather than these creditor scenarios? According to the Mechaber, one can say that in an ordinary creditor case the purchaser might later receive money from the seller, and because an appraisal can revert, he will ultimately turn back to the seller. With true stolen property, that route is different, and the Gemara therefore needed that case to show why a general discharge clause cannot solve everything. Once the Sages declined to rely on written receipts in the stolen-property setting, related creditor collusion concerns also remain. According to Rabbenu Yonah and the Tur, who hold that an appraisal does not revert to a purchaser, a further explanation is needed. Tosafot ask why loan instruments cannot simply include a clause cancelling all prior copies. One might answer that the creditor could collect once, have that note torn, collude with the purchaser to restore the field, and then collect again under another note. Even if the debtor has a receipt, the colluding purchaser can avoid bringing the debtor into the proceeding. Tosafot must therefore assume that beit din does not collect from purchasers without first notifying the debtor and determining whether the debt has been paid. If so, the debtor's receipt should generally prevent repeated collection. That observation explains why the Sages nevertheless required the original note to be torn when issuing a writ of seizure. Ordinarily they could perhaps have relied on a receipt in the debtor's hands. But the Gemara shows that such a system still does not eliminate the special risk of stolen property. Since they could not safely adopt a universal receipt solution, the other collusion scenarios likewise remain relevant. This also explains why, when a sale document is lost, a replacement cannot simply say that warranty applies if a creditor seizes the field but not if the true owner takes it as stolen. At first glance creditor seizure presents no repeated-collection concern. According to what I have written, however, creditor cases can also generate collusion in the ways described above. The statement that beit din gives the purchaser a written proof that the field was seized so that he may later claim against the seller was difficult for the Sema and Perishah, who understood it as allowing immediate collection first from unencumbered and then from encumbered property. Rather, the paper serves as evidence to be used when circumstances later make collection safe. For example, if the debtor admits that he owns no other relevant property and the purchaser lacks an after-acquired clause, there is no risk of another improper seizure from later purchasers; against the debtor himself any receipt remains available. Or the creditor may be the debtor's father and die, leaving the purchaser to claim from the debtor who is himself the creditor's heir, so collusion is impossible. Likewise, if after the seizure the seizing creditor sold the field back to the debtor, the purchaser may have a safe claim. Thus beit din gives him proof of the seizure so he can collect when an appropriate situation arises. If the Sema's immediate-reading were correct, the Sages could simply have allowed recovery from unencumbered property whenever a sale document was lost rather than barring collection entirely. Therefore, the proper meaning is as stated.
