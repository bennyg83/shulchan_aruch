And it increased in value, if it had been worth one thousand. The Sema asks why this implies that the creditor takes the entire increase, while in siman 115 the Tur and Mechaber say that he takes only half the appreciation. He answers that this is a case where the field was specifically designated to the creditor. Alternatively, the phrase means that the half share of the appreciation itself came to one thousand. Likewise, if it had been worth one thousand. The Tur writes this, but Baal HaTerumot speaks only where the purchaser paid one thousand although the field was worth five hundred. In that case the purchaser may say, “Return my field or pay me one thousand, for it was worth one thousand to me from the outset, as shown by what I paid.” If the field was initially worth one thousand and later declined to five hundred, however, he should receive only its value at the time of seizure. The Maggid Mishneh states this plainly: whether the field increased or decreased in value, the purchaser is credited according to its value when the creditor seized it, not according to the purchase price. The Bach argues that the Maggid Mishneh erred concerning depreciation because this siman says that the purchaser’s authorization for collection records the earlier value. That objection is unpersuasive. The Maggid Mishneh disagrees with the Tur, and his view is more reasonable.
But merely before witnesses. The reason is that one who sells land ordinarily sells publicly so that many purchasers will gather. This is stated explicitly in the Gemara in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim.
Even if he did not expressly undertake warranty. The Tur states this from the Rosh in the name of Rabbenu Yonah, and Rabbenu Yerucham records it likewise. Baal HaTerumot, however, says that when land is sold before witnesses without a written instrument, the seller must expressly accept warranty; omission of warranty is not treated as a scribal error where there was no document. Gidulei Terumah could not locate the source, but Rashbam’s comments in Chezkas HaBatim imply it. Gidulei Terumah further asks that since a land sale is public, there should be no distinction because liens are biblical. The question is unfounded. The biblical lien principle applies to a loan, under the rule that a borrower is subject to the lender, not to a seller’s warranty obligation. Mabit writes that if the purchaser has not paid the purchase price, the seller may collect that debt from property sold by the purchaser from the date of the sale, even without a written instrument, just as a seller without a deed collects from encumbered property. In my view this is not compelled. The sale itself is public so that purchasers will assemble, but nonpayment is not public; ordinarily payment is immediate. Even according to Mabit, the case must be within the payment term or supported by witnesses who know that payment was not made, for otherwise the purchaser could claim that he paid.
