And the creditor need not write, etc. The seller cannot say, 'Perhaps you did not pay the creditor. Bring me a receipt from him or an admission.' The purchaser need not obtain one. Once beit din issues a writ of seizure, it is certain that the creditor actually collected from him. Likewise, if the purchaser wants a receipt from the creditor, the creditor need not provide it and cannot be compelled by beit din. The judicial writ is sufficient.
And it rose in value to one thousand. According to the Mechaber in the preceding siman, natural price appreciation is automatic improvement and the creditor takes all of it, so this is straightforward. According to Rama, who cites the dissenting view that even price appreciation yields only half the improvement, one must say either that the field was an apotiki, or that 'rose to one thousand' is not literal and merely means that it appreciated enough that the creditor's collectible share itself equals one thousand. See Sema and Shach.
They write him a writ of seizure for one thousand. The writ must specify how much was the original purchase price and how much resulted from appreciation. The purchase price may be recovered from encumbered property, while the appreciation is only an improvement claim and can ordinarily be collected only from free property. The distinction must therefore appear in the writ. Even according to Tosafos in Bava Metzia, who hold that purchasers who bought after the seizure writ may be liable for the improvement as well, the distinction still matters with respect to purchasers who bought earlier and are presently overseas. The principal may be collected from them, but not the improvement.
At the time of seizure it was worth only five hundred. See Tumim. This rule is disputed, and Maggid Mishneh disagrees. The practical law requires study. But if the field was worth only five hundred both initially and at the time of seizure, and the purchaser nevertheless paid one thousand for it, everyone agrees that his writ is for one thousand, because he can say, 'For me it was worth one thousand from beginning to end.' Shach writes this explicitly.
Or it was worth one thousand and he bought it for five hundred. If it later fell in value to five hundred, everyone agrees that the purchaser receives a writ for only five hundred, because he paid five hundred and it is presently worth only five hundred. I proved this in Tumim from a responsum of the Geonim.
And he comes to seize, etc. This refers to recovery of the principal. Improvement is collected only from free property, not from purchasers, as explained.
Rather, merely through witnesses. A loan is ordinarily made privately and therefore has no publicity without a note. A sale, however, is ordinarily public and has publicity even when made only before witnesses. This is from the Gemara. Concerning a loan, even where there is no concern of repayment, such as during the term or where repayment was restricted to named witnesses, the absence of publicity still prevents collection from purchasers unless the loan was documented.
Through witnesses. See Tumim. If the purchaser was dispossessed by a creditor, he cannot later say to the seller, 'You already repaid me,' because he should have taken the original loan note from the purchaser if reimbursement was made. But if he was dispossessed because the field was stolen property, purchasers may plead repayment on behalf of the seller unless beit din wrote the seizure writ for the purchaser immediately at the time of dispossession. If the writ is in his possession, there is no reason to assume repayment. By contrast, where the original purchase was documented by deed, we never plead repayment, because the purchaser's continuing possession of the purchase deed itself shows that the obligation remains. See Tumim.
Even if he did not expressly state warranty. General warranty is treated here too as a scribal omission. See Tumim. Baal HaTerumos disagrees and has support from the Gemara, and therefore this law requires study.
For a gift has no publicity. Even if the donor says, 'Write it publicly,' which is necessary for the gift itself to take effect in some contexts, a gift is still usually given privately and lacks the same degree of publicity. See Tumim. I wrote that this ruling requires study.
Only through a deed. If one sold a field before witnesses and the purchaser still owed the purchase money, Mabit holds that if the seller is later dispossessed the seller can recover the purchase money from encumbered property, because a sale is public and therefore the unpaid price has publicity. Shach disagrees. Although the sale itself is public, the fact that the purchase price remained unpaid is not. This is correct. I have an opposite doubt: if a field was sold before witnesses with the price still unpaid, and the purchaser later pays without witnesses, does the purchaser acquire any lien at all for warranty? When would the lien attach? At the time of sale before witnesses, the purchase money was still in the purchaser's own hands, so there was no reason for the seller's property to secure repayment. Later, when the purchaser paid, through what public act would the lien then attach, since no witnesses were present? The matter therefore requires study.
That they did not sign any deed, etc. See Tumim, where I discussed at length what concern exists if they did sign a deed. Examine there carefully.
So that he may claim against the borrower who sold it to him. Sema and Derishah remain uncertain. According to one answer, the purchaser may recover from the seller only if the seller admits that no deed was written. Once he admits that, there is no danger of double recovery. But if the seller claims that a deed was written, there is concern that the purchaser will first recover from the seller through the witnesses and later recover again from purchasers through the deed. On that approach he cannot presently collect, although perhaps an oath can be imposed on the seller. A second answer is that the Sages were not concerned for the seller's possible loss. According to that answer, even if it is known that a deed was written, the purchaser may recover. In Tumim I agreed with this practical conclusion, though for a different reason. See there.
