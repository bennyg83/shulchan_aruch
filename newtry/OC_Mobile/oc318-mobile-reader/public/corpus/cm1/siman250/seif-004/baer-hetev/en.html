With a kinyan. See below, siman 253, seifim 18 and 20.
Seizure. The Tur and Mechaber write likewise at the end of siman 125. This requires explanation because the passage opens with delivery and ends with seizure. There are two kinds of delivery. Ordinary delivery means placing the object in the recipient's hand so that he acquire it through an act of kinyan, but not necessarily taking it away to his home. A second type of delivery gives the object to him to take home and acquire immediately and absolutely. With a partial deathbed gift, ordinary delivery is effective because such a gift has the law of a healthy person's gift and cannot be retracted or given to another. It merely requires a kinyan, and delivery or pulling can serve as that kinyan just as exchange can. With a gift of all the property by a person dangerously ill, ordinary delivery is ineffective because it is no stronger than making a kinyan with him, and we rule that even then he may retract. But if he delivered it as an absolute present transfer so the recipient should acquire immediately, he certainly cannot retract and give it to someone else, unless he recovers from his illness, in which case he may take it back for himself. The Tur writes an even stronger rule in the name of the Ramah at the end of siman 125: If a person dangerously ill delivered property to another and said, 'Take this to so-and-so,' he cannot retract and give it to another person even before it reaches the recipient, unless he recovers, because we presume he did not give it with recovery in mind. The Shulchan Aruch rules likewise there. The statement that the words of a person dangerously ill are treated as written and delivered does not mean they are equivalent to absolute delivery for present acquisition, because he may still retract and give the property elsewhere. Rather, they are like delivery for purposes of pulling and kinyan, so that if he dies the recipient acquires retroactively. The Rama's words are therefore clear. He means that a partial gift does not specifically require a sudar kinyan. If the giver delivered the property to the recipient so he could acquire through that act, it is also an effective kinyan. But with a gift of all the property, where a kinyan does not prevent retraction, such ordinary delivery likewise does not prevent retraction. Even if the recipient physically takes it home, possession alone does nothing unless the person dangerously ill delivered it to him as an absolute present acquisition. The end of siman 125 likewise discusses such a case. Sema.
That he has. Although we follow an estimation of intent here, because a person does not ordinarily give away everything and leave himself to beg, sometimes even a very small income can provide his needs. The Sages similarly say concerning two brothers who divided equally but one later possesses great wealth that we may say he saved it from his food, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 62. Ibid. His livelihood. Why did the Rama not also write 'and the livelihood of his household,' as the Rosh does? It appears that the Rosh said this specifically when the giver retained land. In that case he cannot himself plow, sow, harvest, thresh, fallow, and perform all the strenuous labor of agriculture, so household members must assist him, and the retained property must suffice for their livelihood as well. This does not necessarily apply when his livelihood comes from other kinds of business, which is the Rama's case. Ibid.
