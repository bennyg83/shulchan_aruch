Possession does not help. See Sema, seif katan 15, who writes that if the donor delivered the object to him so that he should acquire it immediately, the donor cannot retract during his illness. It appears that this is specifically where the donor now hands over the object. If the object was already in the recipient's possession and the donor merely transferred ownership to him, the donor may retract even during his illness.
Sufficient for his livelihood. Responsa Rosh, rule 78, siman 3, implies that if the donor owes others an amount equal to what he retained, this is not considered a valid retention. Nimukei Yosef, page 220 at the end of the first side, implies that even if he owes others against the amount retained, it is still considered a retention. It appears that each follows his own view. The Rosh holds that the donor must retain enough for his livelihood. See Sema, seif katan 17, explaining that this means enough to live from the profits for the rest of his life. If creditors will take the retained assets, nothing remains from which he can support himself through the profits. Nimukei Yosef follows the Rif, who holds that any retention, however small, suffices. See Sema, seif katan 16, explaining that sometimes a person can profit even from a tiny amount. Therefore, even if he owes others an amount equal to the retention, certainly where the debt has not yet matured the fact that the money may remain in his hands for some time itself has monetary value, for a debt payable later is appraised according to its present value, as explained in Makkot 3. Thus some minimal value remains in his hands. Even if the debt is already due, Nimukei Yosef maintains that there is no compelling presumption that the donor intended to give everything, because he may have expected that payment of the debt would still be delayed long enough for him to earn some profit from the retained amount, and then use that profit to provide for his livelihood.
