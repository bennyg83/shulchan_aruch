Seizure is of no effect. The Tur and Mechaber likewise rule at the end of siman 125. The terminology needs clarification, because the discussion begins with delivery and ends with seizure. There are two kinds of delivery. Ordinary delivery means handing an object to the recipient so that the recipient should acquire it through that act. Complete delivery means handing it over for immediate and absolute vesting, so that the recipient takes it away as his own. In a partial gift of a dangerously ill person, which has the law of a healthy person's gift, ordinary delivery suffices because such a gift merely requires a valid kinyan. Delivery can serve as that kinyan just as exchange, meshichah, or another valid act can. But in a gift of all the property of a dangerously ill person, ordinary delivery does not prevent retraction. It is no stronger than an ordinary kinyan, and such a donor may still retract while ill. If, however, the donor delivers the property for complete immediate vesting, so that the recipient should acquire it absolutely at once, the donor cannot retract and give it to someone else, although if he recovers from the illness he may reclaim it because the gift was made in contemplation of death. The Tur at the end of siman 125 goes even further: if a dangerously ill person gives an item to another and says, “Take this to so and so,” he cannot retract and give it to someone else even before it reaches the recipient, unless he later recovers. The statement that the words of a dangerously ill person are like matters written and delivered does not mean that they are equivalent to complete immediate vesting, for he can still retract. Rather, they are treated like an ordinary completed act of acquisition for purposes of giving effect to the disposition upon death. Thus Rama means that it is not only a kinyan sudar that suffices for a partial gift. Ordinary delivery also serves as a kinyan. But for a revocable gift of all the property, neither ordinary delivery nor the recipient's later unilateral seizure helps unless the donor himself delivered it for complete present vesting. This is also the case discussed below in seif 22 and at the end of siman 125.

Enough for his livelihood. The Tur quotes the Rosh that if the donor is a farmer he must retain enough land to support himself and his household, while if he supports himself through commerce or interest income he must retain enough capital to live from its profits. The Baal HaItur likewise requires enough for livelihood, but doubts the measure and suggests twelve months. The Rosh does not appear to accept a mere twelve month measure. If enough capital remains to generate one's livelihood from its profits for twelve months, it can ordinarily continue supporting him afterward as well. Nor is the Baal HaItur's proof decisive, because in the law of a large estate we do not rule that support is limited to twelve months, but until the daughters mature or become betrothed. Rama's unqualified statement that he must leave enough for his livelihood likewise implies livelihood for the remainder of his life, like the Rosh, not merely twelve months. Apart from these reasons, the general rule that the burden of proof is upon the claimant would also leave the property in the donor's possession. Rama does not mention support of the donor's household. It appears that even the Rosh required that specifically where the donor retained agricultural land, because he ordinarily cannot plow, sow, reap, thresh, and perform all the difficult agricultural labor alone and needs his household's assistance. For other forms of income this does not necessarily apply.
