Regarding a will made by a deaf mute through gestures, there is room to invoke the principle that Chazal do not make one rabbinic enactment upon another. A deaf mute's ability to acquire and transfer property is itself rabbinic, and the rule that the words of a dying person are treated as written and delivered is likewise a rabbinic enactment. This concern is especially strong regarding property he inherited, where his ownership is from the Torah. Thus, even though Chazal empowered him to buy and sell movables, perhaps his deathbed will is ineffective. On the other hand, the reason Chazal validated the directions of a dying person was to prevent mental distress. Presumably that concern applies to a deaf mute as well. If so, perhaps his will can even affect land, despite his ordinary lack of power to buy or sell land. At minimum, this rationale may validate a will concerning land that he inherited. One cannot prove the matter from a competent person who became mute during illness and can make a will by gestures. Such a person is fully competent, and his gestures may have Torah force, even to effect matters such as divorce, through the special rules applicable to one who has lost speech. The gestures of a congenital deaf mute have only rabbinic force. It may also be that the special examination required for a sick person who became mute is unnecessary for a congenital deaf mute. The former has a new impairment that may reflect confused mental state, whereas congenital inability to speak does not itself indicate confusion. Yet if physicians or experienced persons say that the deaf mute was suffering from mental confusion during the illness, his gestured will should perhaps require the same examination as that of any sick person who became mute. All ordinary ketubah conditions apply to a deaf mute as to a competent person. If he married off one daughter during his lifetime, the unmarried daughter may receive a dowry according to her sister's measure, and the other rabbinic enactments governing inheritance and estates apply to him. The same is true of a mentally incompetent person to the extent applicable, although because such a person has no legal power of acquisition at all, he cannot make a will in any manner.
