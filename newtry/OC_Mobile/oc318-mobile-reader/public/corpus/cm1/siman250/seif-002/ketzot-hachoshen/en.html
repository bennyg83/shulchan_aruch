Although waiver ordinarily requires no kinyan, it is no stronger than the other dispositions in the same deathbed gift. If the donor recovers, he can retract. The rule that property physically removed from the donor's possession may become irrevocable is different. Where the donor personally takes the trouble to place movables immediately into the recipient's hands, that conduct can show that he intended a present and generous transfer even if he later recovers. Waiving a debt involves no comparable act because the debt is already in the debtor's hands. There is therefore no evidence of a present absolute transfer. Maharam of Rothenburg likewise ruled concerning a creditor who became deathly ill and waived a borrower's debt as part of his deathbed arrangements, then recovered, that he could retract. Maharam frames the case either as an ordinary partial deathbed gift, which would require a kinyan, or as a disposition motivated by imminent death, from which the donor can retract upon recovery even though a kinyan was made. His mention of a kinyan is difficult because waiver itself normally needs no kinyan, and the precise formulation requires study.
The gift is automatically void. Shach writes that if the deathly ill donor recovers, the gift is void even if the recipient already took possession before the recovery. He therefore interpreted Rashba's discussion of a deathbed consecration placed in the treasurer's hands as meaning that the donor had expressly made it a present gift from that day. I do not see the difficulty. Rashba's case is one where the deathly ill person himself delivered the property to the treasurer. In such a case the donor can no longer retract if he later dies, because delivery to the recipient or to an agent with the force of acquisition gives the transaction present legal effect. According to the Rashba discussed earlier, even recovery need not permit retraction where the donor himself deliberately removed the property from his possession, because that conduct proves an intention to transfer generously and presently. There is an important distinction between that case and one where the recipient simply seizes the property on his own. If the donor did not remove the gift from his own possession and the recipient later grabbed it, the seizure has no special force. If the donor recovers, or if the gift is otherwise one from which he could retract, the recipient cannot improve his position by unilateral seizure. Thus Rashba's case, where the donor himself placed the property into the treasurer's hands for consecrated use, is fully consistent with the ordinary rule. Intentional delivery by the donor creates a present transfer; unilateral seizure by the recipient does not.
