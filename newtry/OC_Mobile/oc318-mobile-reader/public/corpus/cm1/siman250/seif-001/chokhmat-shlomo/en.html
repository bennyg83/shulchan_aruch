Seif 1, gloss. Immediately upon death his property falls before the heirs. Note: From this I ruled concerning a dying person who wrote that when his house would be sold, if his grandchildren wished to purchase it, it should be sold to them for less than a specified amount. I ruled that this provision has no legal effect. He did not give the grandchildren a defined property right; he merely instructed the heirs that if they later sold the house they should sell it to them at a discount. Once he dies, the property belongs to the heirs and he has no power to impose such a direction upon them. Moreover, Bava Batra 148 teaches that anything ineffective for a healthy person is ineffective for a dying person, and this is codified in siman 253, seif 21. Since he did not specify the amount of the discount, the undertaking would be ineffective for a healthy person because the purchaser lacks sufficient reliance, as explained in siman 206. The principle that a dying person's words are treated as written and delivered does not transform an ineffective formulation into a different, effective formulation. It merely supplies the missing formal kinyan where the language itself can create a valid transaction. Likewise, the recipient must still have sufficient reliance. The Sages dispensed with a formal kinyan because of concern that the dying person's mind become distressed, but they did not remove defects on the recipient's side. Since a healthy purchaser would not acquire under such an indefinite arrangement, neither do the grandchildren acquire priority under a dying person's gift. I therefore ruled that they have no preferential right. See my fifth edition on Choshen Mishpat, siman 43, where I added further proofs and answered objections sent to me by the rabbi of Dubno.
