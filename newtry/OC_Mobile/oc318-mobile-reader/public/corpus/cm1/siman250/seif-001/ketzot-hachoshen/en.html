The words of a deathly ill person are as though written and delivered. Tur writes that whether he wrote or orally stated, "My property to so and so," the recipient acquires when the donor dies, retroactively from the time of the gift, even without a kinyan. Beit Yosef objected that a deathbed gift is generally said to acquire only after death. Tur follows Tosafot and Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Gittin. If a deathly ill donor said, "Take a maneh to so and so," and the recipient died before the donor, the money is nevertheless given to the recipient's heirs. Rosh explains that because the words of a deathly ill person are treated as written and delivered, once the recipient was alive at the time of the original instruction it is clarified retroactively that he acquired from that time and transmitted the right to his heirs. This retroactivity matters specifically where the recipient dies before the donor. The rule that an "after you" recipient defeats a later deathbed gift made by the first recipient is not contradictory. Until the deathbed donor actually dies, he can retract. Therefore his continuing retained power counts as a remainder in himself, and the "after you" recipient can precede the attempted deathbed disposition. See my discussion in siman 248.
Rashba and Ran appear to disagree with this retroactive theory. They understand a deathbed gift as taking effect only after death. In the case where the donor sent a maneh through an agent and the recipient died first, the heirs acquire because, for a deathly ill donor, the instruction "take" or "bring" has the effect of "acquire" through the agent. The practical difference is where no property was delivered to an agent and the donor merely commanded that it later be given. If the recipient dies before the donor, Rashba's approach gives the recipient's heirs nothing, because the recipient never acquired. According to Tosafot, Rosh, and Tur, the recipient's right can be treated as having arisen retroactively from the original command and therefore passes to his heirs.
Rosh's responsum, however, seems to say that a deathbed gift does not acquire retroactively. He explains that an "after you" gift can take effect even though the donor is no longer alive when the later recipient acquires, because every deathbed gift takes effect when the donor is no longer legally present, whether one says at the completion of death or after death. This language appears inconsistent with retroactive acquisition. For practical law, Rosh's rulings in his halachic decisions govern over his responsa, and there he expressly adopts retroactivity. Still, the better synthesis is that the special deathbed enactment can operate even after death where retroactivity is impossible, while where retroactivity is legally possible the law can treat the recipient as having acquired from the earlier instruction. This distinction matters where the recipient dies during the donor's lifetime. Accordingly, a later recipient under "after you" can acquire after the first recipient's death even though the original donor is gone, while an original deathbed recipient who was alive at the command can, where necessary, be regarded as having acquired retroactively and transmit the right to his own heirs.
Bach explains Tur's statement that a deathbed gift is "as though written and delivered" as meaning that upon death the acquisition relates back to the original time of giving. Bach wishes thereby to avoid saying that the document is delivered only after death, because "there is no document after death." He cites the case where a man appointed guardians during his lifetime and told them to divide his estate however they wished; Rabbeinu Gershom ruled that this was ineffective because it was as though he had directed a document to be written and delivered only after death. I do not agree that a deathbed gift must always operate retroactively. A deathbed gift can take effect after death even though the donor is no longer alive. The rule that there is no deed after death applies to an ordinary gift of a healthy person, not to the special rabbinic institution of a deathbed gift. This is proven by the law of "after you," where the later recipient does not acquire from now but only when the first recipient dies. Rosh states expressly that the original donor need not be alive then, because deathbed acquisition itself was instituted to operate when the donor is no longer present. The same is proven in Bava Batra concerning a gift to a fetus: if the donor says that the child should acquire when born, the gift can work in the special deathbed setting, even though an unborn fetus cannot acquire now and the donor may already have died before birth. One cannot explain that result as retroactive acquisition because the fetus did not exist as a capable recipient earlier. Nimukei Yosef's explanation that a delayed deathbed gift is treated as delivered from now, with the later date only a condition, was intended to avoid the rule that inheritance, once vested, has no interruption. Ramban instead explains that the Torah and the rabbinic deathbed enactment allow a testator to direct a later devolution without requiring the heirs first to acquire irrevocably. That approach also accommodates the fetus case. The guardian case cited by Bach is different. There the testator did not himself make a gift. He merely authorized the guardians to decide later how to distribute. There was no present language of gift at all, so once the estate fell to the heirs the guardians could not create a new transfer after death. But where the deathly ill person himself gives by the recognized language of a deathbed gift, the gift can take effect after death. Where retroactive acquisition is possible, there can still be practical consequences of treating the gift as related back, such as where the recipient dies before the donor. But retroactivity is not an indispensable basis of every deathbed gift.
