They do not write and deliver it. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 121, discusses a testamentary document opening, “A record of testimony that occurred before us, the undersigned witnesses,” and saying that the testator asked them to hear and receive his will and to write and sign it as set forth in the instrument. It was later alleged that the witnesses did not sign until after his death. Chatam Sofer rules that if so, the instrument is invalid, because the testator may have intended the transfer to take effect only through a document bearing witnesses, and there is no acquisition after death. The fact that he himself also signed does not cure the defect, since he explicitly made the witnesses' signatures part of the requested instrument. If witnesses knowingly signed after death, delivered the paper to the beneficiary, and failed to disclose that the signature was late, they acted improperly, for the instrument falsely appears to confer rights that could not arise after death. This is especially serious where one witness is a city judge who cannot plausibly claim ignorance of the law. Therefore, if the holder of the document denies that it was signed after death, the witnesses cannot ordinarily disqualify their own prior signatures by later testimony, particularly where their handwriting is independently authenticated. But if the beneficiary admits that the document was signed only after death, the document is worthless as an instrument of transfer. Chatam Sofer nevertheless leaves some room to distinguish provisions that normally need a kinyan from a provision merely exempting someone from an oath. Where the testator expressly declared that he trusted his wife and son, perhaps that waiver remains effective even if the transfer provisions do not. The point requires some study.
