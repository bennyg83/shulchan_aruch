Here they were found, and here they are presumed to have been. See Baer Heitev above, siman 60 seif katan 19, and what I wrote there. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 43, discusses Reuven, who wrote his son Chetzron a gift document with ordinary credibility clauses, giving him half his house from today and after death and “all the books that I have, from now and henceforth.” After Reuven died, another son, Chanoch, held many of his father's books, with no witnesses who had seen their prior possession. Chetzron claimed that these were the books the father owned when the gift was executed and therefore belonged to him. Chanoch answered that perhaps the father had already delivered all those books to Chetzron and later bought these books back from Chetzron or from someone else, or perhaps these were entirely different books. He added that the surviving document might remain only because it also included half the house. Chanoch further claimed that his father had told him Chetzron had taken many books not included in the gift and demanded their return. At first glance one might invoke the rule in siman 250 seif 26 concerning unspecified movable property and the presumption “here they were found and here they were,” especially where the heir asserts only a doubt. If the original books had already been delivered to Chetzron, saying that the present books are the same would seem contrary to that presumption; even if they are physically the same volumes, once the father returned them and later reacquired them, the original presumption of continuous possession would have been broken. Noda BiYehudah rejects that argument. Suppose witnesses had known that the books originally belonged to Chetzron and certainly reached the father only as a loan, and they were items ordinarily lent. If there are no present witnesses seeing them in the heir's possession, the court can still plead for the deceased that he later bought them, as Shach explains at the end of siman 297. A gift “from today and after death” is different from ordinary ownership: the donor is entitled to hold the property during his life, so mere possession does not prove that it is his, and a claim of later purchase is less natural because there was no reason for him to transfer it to the beneficiary before the appointed time and then buy it back. But here the father himself could have claimed purchase with a migo that he had returned the property, and the court advances such a claim for the heirs. The document in Chetzron's hand does not overcome this. It is not a complete instrument identifying the individual books; moreover, the father could have claimed he returned them with a migo of accidental loss, and the document does not specify which volumes were covered. As to Chanoch's counterclaim that his father told him Chetzron had taken additional books, the questioner suggested that because Chanoch holds inheritance money he might swear that his father told him this and deduct the value. Noda BiYehudah rejects that. Chanoch does not know the underlying facts; he knows only what his father said. Even two witnesses that the father made such a statement would not make Chanoch's possession effective on the strength of the father's allegation, and a person may swear only to facts clear to himself. Even according to Raavad, cited as the alternative opinion in siman 75 seif 21, that “my father told me” can constitute a sufficiently certain claim to impose a Torah oath where the defendant partially admits, it is not a sufficiently certain personal claim to allow the son who seized without witnesses to swear and retain property. Accordingly, Chetzron must swear that he took no books from his father other than those given to him, and Chanoch must return Chetzron's share of the inheritance that he holds. See there.
