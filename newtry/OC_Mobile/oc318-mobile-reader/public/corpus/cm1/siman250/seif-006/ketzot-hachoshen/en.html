A moribund person's gift is a valid gift. Kiddushin derives the father's special credibility concerning the firstborn from the verse of recognition and asks why it is needed when the father could simply give the favored son additional property. The Gemara answers that the verse is needed for property that fell to the father when he was moribund. Rashi explains that a moribund person's gift is ineffective. Tosafot challenges this from the law that a gravely wounded person can signal that a get be written, and from the rule that a get left with a sick person can sometimes be delivered unless he is presumed already dead. Tosafot therefore distinguishes a moribund person who is unable to speak from one who still has legal mental capacity. If he can speak, his acts can be valid. If he is unable to speak, he is presumptively not of sound mind. A merely ill person who became mute can recover, and if he answers questions by nodding his instructions can still be valid. Rosh distinguishes the case of a get entrusted while the husband was still healthy. Once a valid agency was created earlier, the later moribund state need not invalidate the agent's already existing authority. Maharit questions whether an agent can do what the principal presently could not do, but the issue depends on how the moribund person's incapacity is conceptualized. The practical dispute is therefore sharp. Under Tosafot, a moribund person who can communicate and is of sound mind can make a valid disposition. Under Rashi, the moribund state itself prevents effective legal acts even if he can speak. See my following discussion for the consequences of these approaches.
Get Pashut explains Tosafot as following Rashbam. The verse authorizing a father to identify his firstborn is needed where property fell to him only after he became moribund and unable to speak. Although a mute but mentally competent person can sometimes make a gift by answering questions through nodding, the father himself cannot independently formulate the gift, and therefore there is no ordinary migo that he could simply give the property to the son. The explanation is attractive but does not fit the Gemara's wording. The Gemara says specifically that the property fell to him when he was moribund, implying that he had already identified the firstborn while still healthy. The question was that at that earlier time he might have given away even future acquired property according to Rabbi Meir. The answer is that when the property later fell to him he was no longer legally capable of completing that transfer. Thus the point is not that he identifies the son only while moribund and needs others to ask him questions. The recognition occurred earlier. The missing migo concerns his inability to make the later property legally subject to the earlier gift once it comes into existence. Maharit therefore explains Tosafot more convincingly: one who cannot speak is presumed not mentally competent, and Tosafot asks why the Gemara did not instead use an ordinary sick person who became mute. They answer that such a patient commonly recovers, whereas a moribund person does not. A mute sick person who can answer by nodding can still act; a moribund person incapable even of that cannot. Under Rashi's stricter approach, every moribund person's acts are ineffective, even if he can speak. This raises a difficulty from Bava Batra concerning when a deathbed gift takes effect. One view says at the completion of death. The Gemara compares this with a get stated to take effect upon death and infers that if acquisition took effect during the final stage of death the get should work. But if, according to Rashi, a moribund person lacks legal capacity at that very stage, even a transfer intended to take effect then should fail. The difficulty is heightened by Rosh's responsum, which says that under either view of the timing of a deathbed gift, the donor is not legally present at the moment of acquisition. This suggests that "completion of death" is itself treated as a stage at which the donor can no longer create an ordinary transfer. Rashi's theory therefore remains difficult against that sugya and requires study.
