If the document says that he transfers from today, it has the law of a healthy person's gift and requires a kinyan. Nimukei Yosef states this in Ramban's name, and Rivash similarly rules that where a will expressly says it should remain effective even if the sick person recovers, the special deathbed enactment does not apply. By choosing a disposition effective from now and independent of death, the donor places himself outside the special rule enacted to calm a deathly ill person's mind. The transaction must therefore satisfy the ordinary modes of acquisition. This resolves Tosafot's question in Bava Batra concerning a deathly ill person who says, "I owe a maneh to so and so. Give it to him." Why do the Sages not treat that command as written and delivered? The answer is that he is not giving by way of a deathbed gift. He is directing repayment of what he says is an existing debt, which he means to be due immediately even if he recovers. Once the act is understood as a present healthy person's obligation, the special deathbed enactment does not apply. The same principle illuminates the Talmudic document that contained both a kinyan and language of illness. Rav said the donor had placed the recipient "on two mounts": for property capable of present acquisition, the kinyan gives the gift the strength of a healthy person's transfer from now; for rights such as an outstanding loan that cannot be transferred by that kinyan, the deathbed language gives them effect after death. That result necessarily rests on an estimate of the donor's intent. If one applied a single literal time to the whole instrument, the kinyan and "from now" would force everything into the present and the loan would fail. The law therefore interprets the instrument as though the donor intended present acquisition where possible and deathbed acquisition where present kinyan cannot work. This is why the sugya is evidence that Rav follows legal presumptions of intent.
