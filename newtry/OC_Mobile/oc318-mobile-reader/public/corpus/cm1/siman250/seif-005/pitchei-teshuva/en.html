For he may retract until he dies. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 49, discusses a dying man who made a will and established a permanent charity fund. Later his brother-in-law reminded him of several relatives, and wrote an additional paper containing five points. One point directed that the testator's brother receive four gold coins each week from the permanent fund, with an additional two if necessary. The dying man did not sign the paper; only his wife and adult son did. Two witnesses later certified their signatures and recorded that the signatories acknowledged having consented to the provisions and having heard them from the dying man himself. The testator died, the paper remained with the brother-in-law, and the beneficiary learned of it only about three years later. He then demanded arrears from the widow and children. They responded that the document was void because the witnesses themselves had not heard the testator and had merely relied on the wife and son's report made during distress. Chatam Sofer rejects that contention. The wife and son signed in their own hands and certified that they themselves heard it from the dying man; they cannot later avoid their written admission by saying they did not understand or appreciate its effect. Nonetheless, Chatam Sofer finds another basis to exempt them. The four weekly gold coins were expressly to come from the permanent fund, but in the first will the testator had already allocated that fund to its charitable beneficiaries, who had acquired their rights. The later writing could defeat those earlier beneficiaries only as a valid retraction by the dying man, and there were no qualified witnesses proving that retraction. The wife and adult son cannot by their signatures deprive others of rights already acquired. Even the additional two coins, which apparently had to come from the family's own property because the fund's annual yield was insufficient, could at most bind the adult son who signed, not the other children. The document did not clearly state from what property those sums were to be paid, and a dying man cannot simply impose an unexplained personal obligation upon his children. Because of these and other defects, Chatam Sofer recommended a compromise for the benefit of the poor brother. See there.
