A gift of the entire estate by a deathly ill person made through a kinyan is ineffective even if he dies. Even if written in a deed delivered during life, the recipient does not acquire, because perhaps the donor intended acquisition only through the kinyan, and a kinyan cannot take effect after death. If he specified that the kinyan was only to strengthen the recipient's rights, such as writing, "We made a kinyan from him in addition to this gift," the recipient acquires. Rama: Likewise, if the deed says it shall be effective in every legally valid manner, this is treated as strengthening language. Some say the same applies to one who commands because of impending death: a kinyan weakens the gift unless expressly added to strengthen it. If the donor did not request the kinyan and only the recipient requested it, that itself is treated as strengthening. If after the kinyan the donor says the gift shall remain effective, this too strengthens it. If he transfers movables together with land, the movables are not acquired merely because the land takes effect after death, since it is as though he attached them to land still belonging to the donor. If he says, "These movables to so-and-so, and to strengthen his rights I give them together with land," he acquires. If a deathly ill person appoints named administrators over his estate and a kinyan is made, the appointment is valid without strengthening language. Likewise, if the kinyan expressly transfers from now or today, the gift is valid even if enjoyment begins only after death, without further strengthening.
