Unlike those who disagree. The dissenters are Rosh and Tur, who hold that because of the doubt he does not retract. Rosh explains that we use an assessment of intent to void a gift only when the assessment is clear. A doubtful assessment is insufficient. Thus a shechiv mera who wrote away all his property and recovered does not retract where it is possible that he had other property in another country. The same applies to the unresolved questions of hekdesh and distributing property to the poor. A shechiv mera's right to retract rests upon an inferred condition based on his intention. Where that inference is uncertain, he does not retract. The explanation is that we ordinarily rule that unexpressed intentions have no legal effect. An assessment of intent works even without an explicit condition because a sufficiently clear assessment is regarded as the unexpressed intention of every person. So Mordechai writes regarding coerced vows where a person internally limits the vow to 'today.' See what I wrote in siman 12, seif katan 1. This applies only to a clear assessment that can be described as the intention of everyone. With an uncertain assessment, even if this particular donor in fact intended the condition, it remains merely his own unexpressed intention and has no effect.
And the law of hekdesh is like the law of a gift, so that he may retract. Tur writes that where a shechiv mera consecrated his property or distributed it to the poor, the Gemara leaves unresolved whether he has the same law as an ordinary donor and may retract if he recovers. It appears that the uncertainty concerns only recovery. If he expressly retracted while still a shechiv mera and then died, even if we say his retraction is ineffective because he fully intended a present consecration or charity gift, the matter would still be only a vow. One who vows and then dies does not obligate his heirs to fulfill the vow, as Rama writes in siman 252. Thus the practical issue exists only during the donor's lifetime, namely whether recovery itself cancels the disposition. This explains why Tur specifically writes, 'and he retracts if he recovers,' rather than simply, 'and he retracts,' which would include retraction followed by death. If he dies, the vow itself expires and his heirs are not obligated. The same applies to our forms of hekdesh, which operate by the law of vows rather than the rule that a statement to Heaven is equivalent to delivery. Rama's statement that hekdesh has the same law as a gift and can be retracted also refers to recovery. Although Shulchan Aruch had already said that if he recovers the gift is void, Rama teaches that even an express retraction does not help according to the dissenting authorities, excluding Mahardach, siman 30, who writes that even according to Rosh only an automatic cancellation upon recovery is ineffective, while an express retraction does work. Darkei Moshe cites him. Rama therefore rules unlike those dissenters and equates hekdesh with a gift as to the question of retraction. Sema writes that this means if he retracted while still a shechiv mera and later died. According to what I wrote, however, if he later dies, even according to Rosh the vow or consecration cannot obligate the heirs, for a vow dies with the one who made it. If the disposition no longer operates as a deathbed gift because he retracted and gave to others, the poor cannot recover through the vow. This is also true of our forms of hekdesh, which are vows. See Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 95, that oaths apply to our hekdesh. Rivash, siman 161, writes that where poor people took possession of their portion before the heir arrived, their seizure does not help against an heir whose claim is certain by kinship while the hekdesh is doubtful. Perhaps if both claims were doubtful, such as two claimants who both derive their rights from an ambiguous will rather than kinship, seizure might help the charitable side with movable property, though not with land. According to what I have written, where both claims arise from the will and the donor consecrated his property but then, before death, retracted and gave it to others, the charity side receives nothing. Once he dies the vow expires, and the later recipients acquire through the deathbed gift. Since he retracted from the hekdesh, it cannot operate as a shechiv mera gift, while the later gift can. Even according to Rosh, who treats the consecration as effective during life, the vow falls away at death, and the later donees acquire. This appears clear. There is nevertheless a difficulty. The authorities imply that if a shechiv mera consecrates or distributes property to the poor and then dies without retraction, the heirs certainly must carry out the instruction. Why? The unresolved question whether he can retract upon recovery rests on the possibility that he fully intended a present, healthy-person transfer. If so, at death perhaps we should likewise be uncertain whether he intended a present transfer. If he did, there is no ordinary deathbed gift, because Rama rules in seif 9 that a shechiv mera gift stating 'from today' is like an ordinary gift and requires a kinyan. On the other hand, if it is only a vow, the heirs do not have to fulfill it after his death. Tur and Shulchan Aruch speak only about recovery, which fits this analysis, but later responsa appear to assume simply that if he dies without retracting, the heirs must fulfill the charitable instruction. Rivash, siman 207, discusses a will stating that even if the sick person recovers the will shall remain valid. He rules this is ineffective language, because if he recovers he is no longer a shechiv mera. Once he removes the instruction from the special framework of a deathbed gift, we do not enforce it merely to prevent distress, just as a partial deathbed gift is not effective at death without a kinyan. The Sages enacted that only where the person suffers the illness from which he dies and intends to transfer because of death do his words operate as though written and delivered without ordinary modes of acquisition. So too, when the Gemara asks about a shechiv mera who consecrates all his property, renders it ownerless, or distributes it to the poor, the question is whether, although giving all one's property ordinarily indicates an intention conditioned on death, these acts may instead reflect a present disposition like that of a healthy person. Hekdesh and hefker can operate by words even for a healthy person, and charity too can be undertaken verbally through the verse that treats charity as a matter of one's spoken vow. Alternatively, the charity case may involve one of the ordinary modes of acquisition. But the Gemara cannot mean that something ineffective for a healthy person becomes effective merely by a doubtful assessment that the sick person intended a healthy-person transfer. Once he removes his gift from the deathbed framework, ordinary acquisition rules apply. Accordingly, distribution to the poor without a kinyan works during life only as a vow. Once the person dies, his heirs are not obligated to fulfill that vow. If, however, he distributed the property to the poor through a valid mode of acquisition and then died without retraction, the heirs certainly must honor it either as an ordinary gift or, at minimum, as a shechiv mera gift. Yet the language of later authorities seems broader, treating an ordinary verbal distribution to the poor as binding after death. Sema likewise writes that hekdesh is like a gift in that he may retract before death, implying that without such retraction death makes the instruction binding. According to my reasoning, even without express retraction there should be no binding will if the only basis during life was the vow. This requires study.
