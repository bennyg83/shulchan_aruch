But if he acknowledged. See Sema, seif katan 9, who explains that we say he cast the gift in the form of an acknowledgment so that he would not be able to retract. This is difficult, because even an explicit stipulation in a gift that the giver will be unable to retract is ineffective, as the Rama wrote above. Perhaps the Sema means that an acknowledgment itself is a mode of acquisition even for a healthy person. Since he transferred through acknowledgment, there is no concern that he intended acquisition only after death. The very reason he expressed the gift as an acknowledgment was so that he could not retract, and it is therefore equivalent to making a present acquisition in a manner that precludes retraction, which is effective as the Rama wrote above.
He consecrated all his property. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that the Gemara's question regarding hekdesh concerns only whether he can retract if he recovers, but there was no uncertainty regarding heirs, because charity takes effect through a vow and if one makes a vow and dies, his heirs are not required to fulfill it. See there. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Only where he says, "I will give a maneh to charity," or "give a maneh to charity," so that the obligation still requires performance, is it based upon a vow that the heirs need not fulfill. But if he says, "this item is charity," designating a specific object for charity, the object itself becomes subject to charity and his vow has already been fulfilled. This is clear from Nedarim, where even an incomplete expression can cause property to become designated for charity. See Yoreh Deah, siman 258, where even changing its use is forbidden, except that before it reaches the charity collector one may borrow it on condition of repayment. The heirs must certainly deliver such property, and the designated charity may be recovered from them. Thus the Gemara's question can concern a case where he said, "these properties are for charity." Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 290 proves that there can be a property lien for charity, following Kesef Mishneh's proof from the rule that when a person becomes mentally incompetent, support is collected from his property. Ketzot infers that the same applies where one vowed charity. In my humble opinion this does not follow. Where a person is independently obligated to support relatives even without a vow, or where a city is obligated to support its poor, Kesef Mishneh writes that the person's property is encumbered. Thus, where his body is obligated to support the poor of his city according to beit din's assessment, his property is likewise encumbered. But where he has no independent legal obligation and is bound only because of his vow, it resembles every other vow, and the heirs need not fulfill it, unless he said, "this is charity," so that the object itself became charity. In that case the heirs cannot divert it and are compelled to give what he designated.
Contrary to those who disagree. See Sema, seif katan 10, who asks that here the ruling rejects the Rosh, while below in seif 10 the ruling follows the Rosh. See Ketzot HaChoshen. According to what I wrote in the general rules of possession, the matter is simple. There, in seif 10, the uncertainty concerns the facts, and a definite acquisition is not nullified because of factual doubt. Here, the uncertainty concerns the meaning of the language, whether he intended the consecration to remain even if he recovers or only after death, like his other gifts. Whenever language is doubtful, we follow the rule that a person does not deliberately place himself into uncertainty. That means that we interpret him as intending only the definite implication of his words, not a doubtful implication, as in Kiddushin 64 concerning one who has two groups of daughters and says he betrothed the older one, and likewise with a vow "until Pesach," where according to the view that a person does not enter into uncertainty he is prohibited only according to the definite meaning of his language. A person is also believed concerning his own intent, as I wrote above in the general rules of possession in the name of the Ran; see there.
The law of hekdesh is like the law of a gift. See Sema, seif katan 11, who explains this as meaning that he could retract during his illness. Nimukei Yosef, page 228a, expressly writes that he cannot retract during his illness, and if he then dies his consecration stands. The Rama's words can be explained as Ketzot HaChoshen explains them, and this will be clarified, with Hashem's help, in siman 255. Independently, the Sema's statement that if the donor later dies the final recipient acquires is difficult. How is this different from siman 243, seif 24, concerning one who throws a purse, where because the matter is a doubt in law, the first possessor is considered the prior owner relative to the second acquirer, even though the second performed a definite acquisition? Here too, hekdesh is a legal doubt, and the donor did not retract for himself but in favor of another. Since the doubt arose between the first and second recipients, the first should seemingly be considered the prior owner against the second. According to what I wrote above, however, that where hekdesh is doubtful because of uncertain language the result is treated as a certainty that the doubtful portion is not consecrated, because a person does not enter into uncertainty, the ruling is understandable.
If the money itself still exists, if he recovers he may retract. Rashba, volume 3, siman 122, writes that retraction is effective only if he expressly retracts, but the transaction is not automatically nullified when he recovers. See there. It further appears to me that only if he recovers may he retract. If he retracts during his illness and then dies from that illness, the retraction is ineffective. In a sale, a healthy person's present acquisition is required, as the Sema writes in seif katan 12. See Nimukei Yosef, who explains that the Sages enacted the special rules of a shechiv mera only for gifts, not sales. A present kinyan is therefore required, since without present effect, acquisition of movable property cannot take effect after death. Even if we assume his intention is that the acquisition take effect at the completion of death, the sudar has already returned to its owner. We must therefore say that the sale operates as a present acquisition subject only to the condition of death. Consequently, if he ultimately dies from the illness, he cannot retract during that illness.
