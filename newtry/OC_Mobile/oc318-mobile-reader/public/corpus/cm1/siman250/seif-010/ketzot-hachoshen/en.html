Perhaps he retained other property. Rosh says that when it is uncertain whether a deathly ill donor gave all his property, we do not cancel the gift merely from doubt. Sema asked why the Shulchan Aruch here preserves the gift in doubt while elsewhere, concerning dedication to charitable use, it can cancel because of uncertainty. Rashba removes the contradiction. The presence of a kinyan itself indicates that other property probably remained. If the donor had truly disposed of everything as an ordinary deathbed gift, he would have needed no kinyan because his words alone would suffice. The kinyan therefore points to a healthy person's gift and suggests he retained other assets. Nimukei Yosef gives a similar textual inference. The Mishnah's complete gift is stated as, "all my property." In the doubtful case the donor says, "such and such field to so and so," rather than "all my property." That very formulation implies that other assets exist elsewhere. Mishneh LaMelech asks from the case of a deed that does not say whether the donor was healthy or deathly ill. Even if the deed contains a kinyan, the parties still dispute his condition. Why not infer from the kinyan that he must have been healthy? The answer is that the document there has an internal defect in the opposite direction: it also lacks the ordinary recital that he was walking in the marketplace as a healthy person. The Gemara says the omission of the healthy formula points toward illness, while omission of the illness formula points toward health. The conflicting omissions leave the matter genuinely doubtful, and the kinyan cannot resolve it. Here, by contrast, no contrary defect exists, so the kinyan itself can support the inference that he intended a healthy person's gift or retained other assets.
