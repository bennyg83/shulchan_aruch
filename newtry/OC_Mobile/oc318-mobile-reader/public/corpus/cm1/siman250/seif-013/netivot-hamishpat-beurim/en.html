A shechiv mera who wrote to one person. See Sema, seif katan 39, citing Rashba, though he abbreviated the quotation. It should read, "he shall not take, and everything shall belong to his other brothers," and so forth, because merely saying "he shall not take" cannot remove an heir from his inheritance, as explained in siman 281. Regarding Rashba's statement that Reuven's heirs acquire it, it nevertheless appears that if Shimon lived in the house, Reuven's heirs cannot demand rent from him. Since Reuven himself had no right to rent the property, it is like a courtyard that is not ordinarily rented, where one who resides there without causing loss is exempt, as explained in siman 363. Indeed, Shimon may even reside there initially, because one benefits while the other suffers no loss, since Reuven cannot live there or rent it out. Reuven also cannot protest Shimon's wish to reside there. Moreover, an occupied house is preserved, so Reuven himself benefits from Shimon's residence. A stranger who seeks to live there is different, for Reuven certainly can protest. Such a protest does not constitute challenging his father's will, because he may object not on the basis that the house is presently his, but on the basis that it is his father's estate. It likewise appears to me that Reuven may borrow money for himself and the creditor may collect from this property, as explained in Bava Kamma 109 regarding one who prohibited his assets to his son, where the son may borrow and the creditors collect from the assets. When the creditors assert the claim, it is treated as though the heirs themselves assert it. So long as Reuven himself does not challenge the condition, the condition is not nullified. This appears correct.
Whether for himself or for another. See Sema, seif katan 41. Although siman 125 states that where one transfers possession through another person, the donor cannot retract during his illness, here the Sema explains that the donor told the person making the transfer not to give it to the recipient until the donor dies. I do not know why the Sema changed the wording from what he wrote in seif katan 15, that he transferred it through another only for purposes of kinyan. Nevertheless, the two explanations appear to have the same intent.
Possession does not help. See Sema, seif katan 44, who offers another distinction: the case concerns retraction without recovery from the illness. The earlier case, where the donor wrote and transferred to the first person and then wrote and transferred to the second, and may retract even during his illness, refers to the form described above, where the first person was given control only for purposes of kinyan and was not permitted to take the property home. When the Tur writes that delivery of the gift to the recipient is effective, he means that the transfer was arranged so that the property should immediately reach the recipient and be taken home. Then it is effective to prevent retraction during the illness. In my humble opinion this answer is primary. According to Darkei Moshe's answer, that the case involves writing and delivering the deed together with a transfer of possession and that this prevents retraction, I am troubled, because if delivery of the deed can sometimes prevent retraction, Tosafot's question in Bava Batra 152 returns. Tosafot ask concerning the rule that when one "wrote and transferred" from the case in Bava Metzia 16, where the Gemara explains that a testamentary document may be returned in a case of a shechiv mera because he can retract. There would still be concern that the recipient may later bring witnesses that the donor transferred the gift to him through another by saying, "go, take possession and acquire," in which case, if the deed were not in his possession, the second gift would stand, while delivery of the deed would validate the first gift, because once the deed is in the first recipient's possession we could presume it was delivered on the date it was written and the donor could no longer retract. According to the Sema's distinction, however, that recovery allows retraction but mere continued illness does not in the same manner, Tosafot's question against Rashbam can be answered. Rashbam's term "transferred" can mean delivered, which prevents retraction only during the illness. The discussion there concerns whether one testamentary disposition nullifies another. In Bava Metzia, where the document is returned when the donor says "give it," the case may concern one who already recovered and then said "give it." In that case the deed may certainly be returned because there is no concern that it had previously been delivered, for even prior delivery became void when the donor recovered.
