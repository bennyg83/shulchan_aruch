The Sema explains: Just as we are concerned with a deathbed gift that perhaps he wrote the deed and then reconsidered, so too with this promissory note we are concerned that perhaps he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed. Therefore it is not returned to the lender named in it. The same applies to a deed of sale, for perhaps he wrote it intending to sell but never sold. From his words it appears that he has this concern even regarding a promissory note or deed of sale containing a kinyan, because he distinguishes between loan and sale documents on the one hand and a gift deed on the other. He likewise writes explicitly in siman 243, seif katan 23, that here it is explained that even with a loan document containing a kinyan we are concerned that perhaps he wrote it to borrow but never borrowed. The Bach writes similarly that the Rosh deals with a case where it is known that the kinyan was made without further specification, and therefore with a loan document we still suspect that no loan occurred, whereas with a gift the recipient acquires presumptively. In truth, however, this is incorrect. The Gemara and all the poskim explicitly show that where either a loan document or a deed of sale contains a kinyan, we do not suspect that he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed, or intending to sell but never sold, because from the moment of the kinyan he obligated himself. This is stated at the end of siman 39 and the beginning of siman 238. There is no distinction here between loan and sale documents and a gift deed, as is explicit in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and throughout the poskim. It is therefore clear that a deed of sale is returned to the recipient just like a gift deed. The Rosh, Responsa, rule 66, siman 3, mentioned a gift deed only because that was the actual case before him. When he explains the Rambam as speaking specifically about a promissory note, he means one without a kinyan. This accords with the Rosh's position that a person's witnesses acquire for him through their signatures, creating a concern that he may have written the document intending to borrow but never borrowed when there is no kinyan. According to the Rambam, who does not rule that witnesses acquire through their signatures, one may explain that the concern applies specifically to a promissory note because of possible repayment. The Rosh stated the Rambam's view briefly, but as a matter of law the conclusion is clear as I have written. Shach.
