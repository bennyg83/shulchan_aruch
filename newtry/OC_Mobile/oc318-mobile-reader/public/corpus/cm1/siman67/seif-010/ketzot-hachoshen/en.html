A term of ten years. Makkot records two versions in Shmuel's name whether a loan due only after ten years is canceled by Shemittah. One view says yes, because although the prohibition of pressing does not apply now, it will eventually apply. The other says no, because at present "he shall not press" does not apply. At first glance the entire question seems unnecessary. By setting a ten year due date, the borrower obligated himself to pay after Shemittah. This should be no worse than a stipulation that this debt not be canceled by Shemittah, which is effective because the borrower may accept a monetary obligation that the Torah did not impose. The answer is that Shmuel follows his own position in Bava Metzia concerning a stipulation against overreaching. Shmuel validates the condition because perhaps no overreaching will occur, so the person is not certainly stipulating against Torah law. Tosafot asks why the Gemara compares this to Shemittah. They answer that even with Shemittah the Torah law may never be uprooted because perhaps the borrower will repay before Shemittah. According to this, a ten year term is different. The borrower certainly conditions repayment beyond Shemittah, so if the debt would otherwise be canceled this is a direct condition against Torah law and would be void under Shmuel. Therefore the result must depend upon whether the loan is excluded from "he shall not press" before the due date. Rav, however, follows a different framework. In Bava Metzia he invalidates an unspecified condition waiving overreaching because the victim did not knowingly waive a known amount. We explain his view by distinguishing overreaching from Shemittah: with Shemittah the borrower knows exactly what right he is waiving and can obligate himself monetarily. Rambam rules this way. Knesset HaGedolah asks why Rambam permits a Shemittah condition if the Gemara compares Shemittah to overreaching. The answer is that the comparison is according to Shmuel, who distinguishes certain from uncertain uprooting. According to Rav, whom we follow, the decisive distinction is informed monetary waiver. Thus even a definite condition is effective where the borrower knows and waives the monetary benefit. Accordingly, under Rav, a ten year loan should itself be no worse than an express agreement not to cancel the debt. The borrower knowingly undertook to pay after Shemittah. This explains why Rif omitted Shmuel's statement about a ten year loan. Since Rif rules like Rav on conditions, the special uncertainty under Shmuel is no longer practical. I later found strong support in the Jerusalem Talmud. Rav says that a loan made on condition that Shemittah not cancel it is not canceled. The Jerusalem Talmud asks from the Mishnah permitting certain arrangements lasting ten years, and answers by establishing them with collateral or a prozbul. The straightforward implication is that a ten year term itself would otherwise work by the same law of condition. Thus the Jerusalem Talmud understands Rav's rule precisely as I have explained.
For ten years. Bach writes that an ordinary loan, though the default term is thirty days, is not treated like a ten year loan if Shemittah occurs within those thirty days. Shemittah cancels it.
