For now we do not call it, and so forth. Rambam writes that if the lender stipulated that he would not sue the borrower, Shemitah nevertheless cancels the debt. Kesef Mishneh connects this to the Yerushalmi. R. Yosef Korkos explains that even where the lender cannot sue, the borrower may remain morally obligated to pay, and if the lender seizes property he may be able to keep it. Therefore the debt still has enough legal force to fall within "he shall not press." His explanation sounds as though Rambam means a condition never to sue at all. But the Yerushalmi does not sound that way. The Yerushalmi compares the case to the cow divided on Rosh Hashanah, where the inability to collect exists only during the Shemitah period; afterward collection would again be possible. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 45, part 1, indeed preserves a reading in Rambam: if the lender stipulated that he would not sue the borrower during Shemitah, Shemitah still cancels the debt. That is a different case from a permanent noncollection agreement. If the condition applies only during Shemitah, the borrower will clearly have to pay afterward unless cancellation intervenes. The reason for cancellation cannot be merely a moral duty to pay. Rather, even during the noncollection period the creditor may retain some residual legal power, such as effective seizure in the presence of witnesses. That is enough to keep the debt within the category of "pressing" for Shemitah purposes. The seizure must be understood as a legally recognized possession, not merely secret seizure, because otherwise even a debt already denied before Shemitah could be framed the same way. Baal HaTerumot himself is therefore uncertain about the precise rule. If the lender stipulated that he can never sue the borrower at all, the case may be entirely different. Such an obligation may no longer have the ordinary legal character of a collectible debt and perhaps does not enter Shemitah in the same way. Thus one should distinguish a temporary noncollection condition during Shemitah from a permanent waiver of the right to sue.
