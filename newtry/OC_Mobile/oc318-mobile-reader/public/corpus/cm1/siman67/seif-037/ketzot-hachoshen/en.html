The Sema writes that the borrower must certainly reimburse the lender for the value of the paper, because the document is held as collateral for the cost of the paper. But the borrower need not reimburse the scribe's fee, because Shemittah cancels that obligation and the paper itself is worth only its material value. Taz objects that if the paper is collateral, then according to the view that collateral protects even the amount exceeding its value, the entire debt should escape Shemittah. As I wrote above in siman 66, the paper is not ordinary collateral. It came to the lender as an instrument of acquisition and evidence. Still, the Sema's law can be sustained where the lender himself paid the cost of the paper and the scribe. In that case the paper itself belongs to the lender until the borrower reimburses its cost. Even authorities who require a purchaser of a waived debt to return the physical instrument rely on the fact that ordinarily the borrower paid the scribe and the paper therefore belongs ultimately to the borrower. If the lender paid for the paper and the scribe, the paper is certainly his until the borrower repays those actual costs. Maharshal in Kiddushin explains the sugya of appraising the paper similarly: where the lender paid for the paper and the writing, he need not return it. Accordingly, the Sema can be understood as speaking in such a case. The borrower reimburses the actual paper cost, while Shemittah cancels the underlying loan and any separate claim for the scribe's labor not embodied in the lender's own property.
