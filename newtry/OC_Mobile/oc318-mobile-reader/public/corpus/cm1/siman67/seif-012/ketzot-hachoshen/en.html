One who lends on collateral. Gidulei Terumah writes that if the creditor seizes movables from the debtor, even though the debtor did not voluntarily give them as collateral, so long as the creditor holds them and need not return them until payment, the debt is not subject to "he shall not press" and Shemittah does not cancel it. Tumim objects that the Gemara does not rely merely on possession. Rava initially says collateral is excluded because the creditor holds it. Abaye asks that if so a lender who resides in the borrower's courtyard should also be exempt, since he too holds something. Rava answers that collateral is different because the creditor acquires it under Rabbi Yitzchak. Tumim therefore concludes that without voluntary delivery as collateral, Rabbi Yitzchak's rule does not apply. The Gemara, however, did not reject possession. It clarified what kind of possession is sufficient. Tosafot explains that Rava means possession "as collateral." Movable collateral is acquired under Rabbi Yitzchak, unlike occupation of land. If the creditor himself forcibly seizes the debtor's movables as collateral, that still falls within the law of a pledge. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch in siman 97 rule that even one who seizes collateral by force must return the borrower's pillow at night. That duty of return itself reflects Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition, for otherwise the Torah's special charity rule regarding collateral would not apply. Therefore Gidulei Terumah is correct. His statement that the borrower "did not give it as collateral" means only that the borrower did not voluntarily deliver it. The creditor himself nevertheless seized it with the intention of holding it as collateral. If the creditor merely possesses the debtor's property for some unrelated reason and does not hold it as a pledge, Rabbi Yitzchak's rule does not apply. See Even HaEzer siman 28. But where he seizes it specifically as collateral, even by force, he acquires the pledge and Shemittah does not cancel the debt.
