And some say that even against the entire debt it is not cancelled. The first opinion holds that Shemitah does not cancel only up to the value of the collateral. This follows those who do not rule like Shmuel's principle that if even the handle of a sickle was lost, a thousand zuz are lost with it. Shmuel himself, however, certainly holds that even a very small item of collateral can protect the entire debt from cancellation. There is therefore no difficulty. Gidulei Terumah asks: since we rule like R. Yochanan that an earnest payment acquires against the entire transaction, should we not likewise say that collateral protects the entire loan from Shemitah, as seems implied in Bava Metzia 49? There is no proof. If collateral protects the whole debt from Shemitah, then all the more so an earnest payment can acquire the whole transaction. But the converse does not follow. An earnest payment is given absolutely as part of the acquisition, whereas collateral is handed over merely as security and as a memorandum of the debt. Or Zarua in Bava Metzia makes this distinction clear.
And there is an opinion, and so forth. Mechaber states this view without qualification. Yet it is Rosh's view following Shmuel, who says that if the handle of a sickle is lost, a thousand zuz are lost with it. According to that doctrine, even a small item of collateral can protect the entire debt from Shemitah. But below in siman 72, Tosafot and Rosh are understood to hold that even according to Shmuel this applies only where the lender expressly said that although the collateral is worth less than the debt, he accepts it against the entire debt. Without such an explicit stipulation, the lender does not lose the whole debt if the collateral is lost. Tosafot Shevuot 43b likewise explain the rule that an earnest payment can acquire against the entire transaction by establishing the case as one with an express stipulation. Why then does Mechaber state the Shemitah rule here without saying the lender expressly accepted the collateral against the entire debt? There is also a Talmudic difficulty. In Gittin, the Gemara says a loan secured by collateral is not cancelled because the creditor acquires the collateral under R. Yitzchak's rule. But R. Yitzchak's acquisition clearly reaches only the value of the collateral. Why then should the excess debt not be cancelled? If the answer were simply Shmuel's doctrine that the collateral stands for the entire debt, there would be no need for the Gemara to invoke R. Yitzchak at all. One must say that Shemitah is different. Once the creditor has a proprietary acquisition in the collateral, Chazal had a basis to say that the debt as a whole is outside the category of "he shall not press." Since, had the lender expressly accepted the collateral against the entire loan, the whole debt would clearly be protected, the existence of an actual proprietary collateral right can suffice even without that express language. This resembles the rule that a pruzbul can be written even on the basis of a very small amount of land, because the land gives the debt the character of something already collectable. Thus the Shemitah rule is more lenient than the ordinary loss-liability rule of Shmuel. This is also why Tosafot Shevuot explicitly require an express condition for the earnest-payment comparison but do not say the Shemitah case itself requires such a condition. In any event, if the collateral is later lost through accident after Shemitah, the lender can still collect the corresponding debt from the borrower's other property. Even according to the opinion that the collateral itself prevented cancellation, it does not mean the debt was converted into ownership of that particular object alone. Gidulei Terumah further writes that if a creditor merely seizes movable property from the debtor, even though it was never given as collateral, the debt is not cancelled because he may keep the seized property until payment and therefore is not "pressing" the debtor. I am surprised. The Gemara rejects precisely a theory based merely on physical possession and instead invokes R. Yitzchak's proprietary acquisition of collateral. This indicates that seizure alone, without the property having been given as collateral, is insufficient. Tosafot make the point explicitly by noting that the Gemara did not choose a case where the creditor happened to hold collateral for another debt with excess value that he could retain. Mere possession is not enough. Therefore Gidulei Terumah's extension should not be accepted.
