Rambam gives two reasons: the creditor has a migo that he could say he had a pruzbul, and there is a chazakah that a person does not abandon a permitted path and choose a prohibited one. According to what I explained above, Rambam primarily relies on the permanent rule from the persecution onward, while the general presumption alone is not always enough. This explains why he combines both grounds. A migo alone is insufficient in an oral loan because it would be a migo used to extract money from the possessor, as Baal HaTerumot notes. The presumption alone may also be insufficient where the debtor expressly denies the creditor's factual claim. But when the presumption supports the migo, the combined force can overcome the ordinary possession rule. Mechaber, however, follows authorities who explain the creditor's credibility primarily through the presumption that he would not abandon the lawful method. On that view, a question arises: Why, when the creditor claims some other Shemitah exception, such as "this was store credit" or "we stipulated that Shemitah would not cancel," does he need a migo at all? The same presumption should say he would not knowingly lend money in a way that would later be cancelled without protecting himself. Sema answers regarding a stipulation that perhaps the borrower refused to agree. That is not satisfactory. If the borrower refused, no one forced the lender to lend. The presumption should tell us either that the borrower accepted the condition or that the lender would have made a pruzbul. Nor does this answer the claim that the transaction was store credit. The true distinction is evidentiary. We use the presumption to extract money primarily where the borrower does not contradict the creditor with a definite factual claim. If the creditor says, "I had a pruzbul and lost it," the borrower ordinarily does not know whether that is true. The creditor has a strong lawful-conduct presumption against the borrower's mere possession. But if the creditor says, "This was store credit," or, "You expressly agreed that Shemitah would not cancel," the borrower can answer with definite knowledge: "No, it was an ordinary loan," or, "I never agreed." A general behavioral presumption is not enough to extract money against a definite factual denial. This also explains the rule in Yoreh Deah siman 162 concerning an alleged iska. There too one might say, "A Jew would not abandon the permitted form and lend with ribbit." Yet where the borrower definitely denies the iska arrangement, Rama allows him to swear and be exempt. The presumption alone does not extract money against a bari denial. In the present cases, however, the creditor may also have a migo that he could have claimed a lost pruzbul. The borrower cannot definitely contradict that alternative. Thus the creditor has both the substantive presumption and a migo. Together they can overcome possession. This also clarifies the dispute cited by Tur. Ramah says the creditor must swear, while Rabbenu Tam says he is believed without an oath. Perhaps they are not actually disputing the same factual case. Rabbenu Tam can be speaking of a lost-pruzbul claim, where the borrower has no definite denial. There is no basis for even hesset. Ramah can be speaking of a factual claim such as store credit, where the borrower says definitely that it was an ordinary loan. In that case, even if the creditor prevails through the combined presumptions, an oath can still be required. And a migo ordinarily does not exempt a person from an oath he otherwise owes. This is the sound practical distinction.
