Rif instead cites the Mishnah in Ketubot: the first Tanna says a creditor cannot collect without producing a pruzbul, while Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says that from the time of persecution onward a creditor can collect without it. Some infer that Rif does not rule like Rav. Rambam, however, in Hilchot Shemitah 9, explicitly rules that a creditor is believed to say his pruzbul was lost, and gives as the reason the rule "from the time of persecution onward." Kesef Mishneh explains that Rambam reads Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel broadly: not merely during the actual emergency, but from that persecution onward Chazal permanently abolished the requirement to preserve and produce the pruzbul. The creditor is therefore always believed to say he had one and lost it. Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah appears to say the same, and the Yerushalmi in Ketubot also suggests a lasting enactment. If so, Rif can be read exactly like Rambam. By citing the Mishnah without further qualification, he teaches the permanent post-persecution rule. There is still a difficulty from Gittin 37. The Gemara challenges the rule that one is believed to say, "I had a pruzbul and lost it," from a Mishnah saying that if a creditor produces a debt note without a pruzbul, he may not collect. The Gemara answers that the issue is a dispute of Tannaim. Why did it not simply answer that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel already says that from the persecution onward no production is required? This is especially difficult because the challenge is directed to Rav, and Rav elsewhere interprets the Ketubot Mishnah through Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. One answer is that there are two distinct grounds for believing the creditor. One is the permanent emergency enactment: because preserving a pruzbul during persecution was dangerous, Chazal eventually stopped requiring production. The other is the substantive presumption that a person does not abandon a permitted method and act unlawfully. A lender is presumed to have made a pruzbul rather than knowingly collect a cancelled debt. These grounds can differ. Suppose we actually see that the creditor preserved another pruzbul but claims this particular one was destroyed because of danger. The emergency explanation becomes weak. If he could preserve one, why not the other? Rash in Shevi'it is uncertain about such a case. But the presumption "he does not abandon the permitted path" can still support him, because perhaps this pruzbul was simply lost accidentally. Thus when the Gemara in Gittin challenges Rav, it is testing Rav's specific reason based on the presumption of lawful conduct, not merely the historical emergency enactment. The Mishnah that says "he may not collect" contradicts that general presumption unless another Tanna disagrees. The Gemara therefore answers with the Tannaitic dispute. Rambam, however, can rule like the plain Mishnah and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel based primarily on the post-persecution enactment, without relying on the broader presumption in every case. There is another, perhaps stronger, explanation. The phrase "from the time of persecution onward" itself can mean two things: only while the danger actually lasts, or permanently from that point onward. Rav appears in Ketubot to treat it as a temporary emergency rule. He says a get collects the basic ketubah in ordinary times, which implies that once the danger passes the old documentary requirements return. R. Yochanan, by contrast, holds that one who pleads payment against an obligation created by beit din is not believed. In the system of Rif and Rambam, that requires reading Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's enactment as permanent: from the persecution onward the woman can collect the basic ketubah without producing the get, and the husband cannot defeat her even through an ordinary migo. Rambam in Hilchot Ishut follows this directly. He permits collection of the basic ketubah in a place where ketubot are not written even without producing get witnesses in the older formal manner. Thus Rav and R. Yochanan actually differ in how enduring the emergency enactment was. The Gemara in Gittin challenges Rav according to Rav's own temporary reading, so it cannot simply invoke Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel as a permanent rule. We, however, rule like R. Yochanan. Therefore Rambam correctly adopts the permanent post-persecution rule and concludes that a creditor is believed to say his pruzbul was lost. This also illuminates Rambam's law of ketubah and the principle that one who pleads payment against a beit din condition is not believed. The structure is therefore coherent.
