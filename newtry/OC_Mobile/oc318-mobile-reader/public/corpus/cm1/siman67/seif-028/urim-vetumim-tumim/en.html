Minor orphans who have a loan in the hands of others, and so forth. Sema explains that this includes both debts they inherited from their father and debts they themselves lent. According to Beit Yosef's understanding of Rashi, however, this is difficult. Rashi does not rule like R. Shimon's statement that an orphan's inherited debt is not cancelled. Beit Yosef explains that the teaching of Rav Chisda, that a pruzbul may be written for orphans on the land of their guardian, concerns orphans who themselves are the lenders. Rashi therefore understands Rav Chisda as disagreeing with R. Shimon: even debts belonging to orphans can require pruzbul. Yet another teaching says minor orphans do not need pruzbul because beit din serves as their hand. Beit Yosef must then distinguish inherited debts from loans made by the orphans themselves. I do not understand why. Why should beit din be the hand of orphans only for inherited property and not for debts they themselves acquired? On the contrary, whenever minors transact through a guardian or beit din, their interests are under judicial protection. The fourth and fifth chapters of Bava Kamma and other sugyot treat beit din broadly as acting for minor orphans. There are, however, two practical reasons why Rashi naturally explained the case as inherited debts. First, Bava Metzia 70 says that orphan funds should not ordinarily be lent unsecured. Beit din should lend them only to a person who provides reliable security, such as gold objects, or under an iska arrangement designed to preserve the principal. If the orphans themselves extended a normal unsecured loan, either it should have collateral, in which case Shemitah would not cancel it, or it should be an iska, which according to Radbaz is likewise not cancelled. Ordinary unsecured orphan lending is therefore uncommon. Second, minors themselves are not obligated in mitzvot. Why would their debt ever be cancelled by Shemitah during their minority? A minor who violates an ordinary prohibition is not personally commanded in the same manner, and Shemitah today is rabbinic. Accordingly, Rashi can mean the following: The father died and left an ordinary debt to the minor children. During their minority, beit din is treated as holding the debt for them. That judicial possession is equivalent to a pruzbul. When the children later become adults and a subsequent Shemitah arrives, they do not need a new pruzbul because the debt was already judicially transferred during their minority. Once a valid pruzbul has been created, it does not need to be renewed every day. This explains why Rashi speaks of inherited debts. It can also explain Baal HaTerumot, who follows Ramban and rules like R. Shimon that inherited debts themselves are not cancelled. He understands Rav Chisda's law about writing a pruzbul for orphans on the guardian's land as referring to the orphans on the borrower side or to a later stage when the debt needs a formal land basis. There is another possible way to read R. Shimon. Perhaps R. Shimon's statement "he cancels but heirs do not cancel" refers not to the heirs of the lender but to heirs of the borrower. The Torah says the creditor may not press "his fellow." The debtor's personal obligation is released by Shemitah. But if the borrower dies before Shemitah, his heirs have no personal bodily obligation. They are liable only through the inherited estate, which already stood as security for the father's debt. The Torah's release of the borrower's personal obligation may therefore not apply to the heirs' inherited assets. On this reading, R. Shimon says the original borrower can become "released," but his heirs are not personally the subjects of that release. The printed text of the Tosefta is uncertain enough that such a reading is possible, though the later authorities generally did not adopt it. In any event, one point remains clear: minor orphans themselves do not ordinarily need pruzbul in the same way as adults, because beit din already functions as custodian of their rights. There is also an interpretive benefit for the Yerushalmi rule that pruzbul can be written for orphans on the guardian's land. If land were needed on the lender's side as R. Yehudah Albargeloni holds, this Mishnah becomes particularly meaningful: the orphans may have no land of their own, yet their guardian's land can serve to transfer the debt rights to beit din. That reading also supports the idea that the Mishnah is speaking from the orphans' perspective as creditors. The detailed relationship among Rashi, R. Shimon, Ramban, and the guardian-land rule remains complex, but Sema's broad formulation should not be accepted without these qualifications.
