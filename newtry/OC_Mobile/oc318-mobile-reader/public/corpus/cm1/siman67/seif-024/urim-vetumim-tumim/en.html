If he had a mortgaged field, and so forth. This is an explicit Mishnah in Shevi'it, yet Tur omits it. Gidulei Terumah struggles to explain why. In my opinion the reason is simple. If the Mishnah means merely that the borrower owns land that is mortgaged to someone else, then the rule is obvious. The land still belongs to the borrower and can be sold, gifted, or used as the land basis for pruzbul. The real novelty is where the lender himself holds a field as a mortgage. The lender is then treated as having enough land under his control to transfer or credit an interest to the borrower for purposes of pruzbul. One might have thought he cannot grant the borrower an interest in land that is not fully his. The Mishnah teaches that because it is held as a mortgage, it is sufficient. But this Mishnah follows a legal framework that predates or does not rely on R. Natan's doctrine. Once we rule like R. Natan that one creditor can collect through another debtor's property rights, the rule becomes broader: even if the lender himself owns no land, where someone who owes the lender owns land, that can support a pruzbul through the chain of obligations. Tur codifies that broader rule. Once he does so, there is no need to state separately the narrower case of a field already mortgaged into the lender's possession. Thus Tur's omission is deliberate and fully understandable.
