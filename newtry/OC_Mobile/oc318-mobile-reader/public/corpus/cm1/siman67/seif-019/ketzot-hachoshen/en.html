Or the witnesses sign. The Mishnah in Sheviit says witnesses may sign a prozbul, and Gittin says it makes no difference whether they sign in the language of witnesses or judges. This is difficult because the prozbul is not written with the debtor's consent. Ordinary witness writing outside the knowledge of the obligated party would be testimony through writing. Tosafot in Bava Batra raises the same problem concerning a protest declaration and a protest against a sale, and answers that the Sages specially empowered the written testimony in order to protect the coerced party or defeat possession. A similar answer could be given here. Yet another explanation appears. We rule that a creditor is believed to say, "I had a prozbul and lost it." Thus the witnesses' signatures are not required primarily as proof. In the Mishnah's period the prozbul itself had to exist as a written instrument, because mere oral delivery of the claims to beit din was insufficient. The signatures of witnesses or judges therefore functioned to constitute the instrument, not to supply independent evidentiary proof of its contents. In that setting the prohibition of testimony through writing does not apply. It resembles witnesses signing a get or kiddushin document under Rabbi Meir, where the signatures form part of the legal conveyance. This also supports the later ruling that the creditor is believed to say he had a prozbul and lost it. Gittin challenges Rav Nachman on this point from a Mishnah implying otherwise and answers that there is a tannaitic dispute. Although Rif did not rule like Rav Nachman because of the anonymous Mishnah, the separate anonymous Mishnah allowing witnesses to sign a prozbul supports Rav Nachman's position, because otherwise those signatures would be problematic as written testimony outside the debtor's knowledge.
