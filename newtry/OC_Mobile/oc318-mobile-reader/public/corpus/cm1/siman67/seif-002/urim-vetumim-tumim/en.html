Sema infers from the wording that the first clause speaks of a place where the borrower can redeem the mortgage at any time, whereas the second says he cannot redeem it until the end of its term. He therefore understands Mechaber as requiring complete inability to redeem throughout the term; if the borrower can redeem at the end of each year, the place is still considered one where mortgages are redeemable. This is strained. The phrase "until the end of its term" simply means until whatever redemption date the parties fixed, whether a long period or even one day. Rif says that in a place where mortgages are not redeemable, the borrower cannot redeem until the agreed term is completed. Nimukei Yosef immediately cites Rashba that even if the borrower is unable to redeem for only one day, it is considered a place where mortgages are not immediately redeemable. Likewise, Rambam uses the language that the borrower cannot redeem "until the end of its term," and Maggid Mishneh cites Rashba's one-day rule without presenting it as a disagreement. Thus the language itself proves no conflict. If the parties fixed even a one-day nonredemption period, then during that period the mortgage has the legal character of a nonredeemable mortgage. The fact that the borrower can redeem after the agreed term expires does not change the classification. Taz tried to read Mechaber as creating a dispute between immediate redemption and redemption during the term, but Mechaber's language is simply Rambam's language, and Rambam presents no such dispute. The correct reading is therefore as stated.
And whenever he cannot redeem it, and so forth. Sema understands this only where the creditor was specifically assigned the field itself, which is stronger than an ordinary mortgage. In an ordinary mortgage, Sema says a one-day inability to redeem is not enough; the borrower must be unable to redeem until the full term. Taz correctly objects that Rashba states the one-day rule expressly regarding ordinary mortgages. Rashba says this in responsa and in his chiddushim to Gittin, Maggid Mishneh cites him in Hilchot Malveh, Ran says the same in Perek HaSholeach, and Shitah Mekubetzet cites it in Bava Metzia. Still, Sema likely had a real difficulty in mind. Tur in Yoreh Deah siman 172 cites two views on this question, and Beit Yosef notes that Rif disagrees with Rashba. Taz himself says Rashba stated his view as halachah but not necessarily as practical ruling. The difficulty is greater from siman 278. Tur cites Ri Migash that a firstborn receives a double portion in a mortgage only if the redemption date had not yet arrived during the father's lifetime. Rashba, by contrast, says that only a mortgage redeemable immediately is treated differently; an ordinary one-year mortgage is already sufficiently sale-like. The same underlying definition should govern both the firstborn rule and Shemitah, since the Talmudic statement is parallel. Yet Mechaber in siman 278 rules like Ri Migash and does not cite Rashba, while here he appears to follow Rashba. Sema therefore understood Mechaber as combining the opinions: Rashba's one-day rule is accepted only in the stronger case where a specific field was assigned, but not for an ordinary mortgage. That likely is Sema's intention. Even so, I do not find the substantive distinction between an assigned field and an ordinary mortgage convincing. A better explanation is that Mechaber remained uncertain between Ri Migash and Rashba. In the law of a firstborn, the ordinary brothers are in possession and the Torah itself calls the firstborn's extra share a gift. Therefore we do not remove money from the possessors on the basis of Rashba's disputed view, and Mechaber rules like Ri Migash. Shemitah today is rabbinic. There Mechaber need not be equally stringent and can rely on Rashba's lenient classification. Thus the two rulings can coexist without creating a substantive distinction between the mortgage forms. The matter still requires study.
