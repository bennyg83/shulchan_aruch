But if he stipulated, etc. See Tevuot Shor in the novellae at the end of the volume on tractate Makkot. He asks why, if such a stipulation is effective, Hillel needed to institute the prozbul. He answers that if a borrower willingly agrees at the outset to borrow on condition that shemittah will not cancel the debt, no further device is needed. But if the lender refuses to lend unless the borrower accepts that condition, while the borrower refuses, and the lender therefore withholds the loan, the lender immediately violates the Torah warning against refraining from lending because of the approaching shemittah. With a prozbul there is no such prohibition, because the matter does not depend on the borrower. The lender extends the loan without imposing any condition on him, and the prozbul is a separate legal device. This, he suggests, is why it is called prozbul, a measure for the benefit of the wealthy. See there. Responsa Chatam Sofer, beginning of siman 113, appears to hold that even where the borrower willingly accepts such a condition, the lender commits a prohibition by imposing it and violates the Torah’s warning, ‘Beware lest there be a lawless thought in your heart.’ He writes that otherwise Hillel would have had no need to institute prozbul because people were refraining from lending, since they could simply have made this condition at the time of the loan. It must therefore be that imposing such a condition itself constitutes the forbidden thought. See there. It is surprising that neither authority mentions the words of Bedek HaBayit in this siman in the name of the Ritva. See there; the matter requires study.
