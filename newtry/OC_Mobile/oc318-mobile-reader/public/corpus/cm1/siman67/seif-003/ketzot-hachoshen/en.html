Shemittah cancels the half that is loan. Hagahot Maimoniyot writes that in an iska consisting half loan and half deposit, Shemittah cancels the loan half. He argues that for all legal purposes it is a loan. Kiddushin made with a loan are ineffective, and likewise a sale is not acquired through a prior loan. He adds that Rava's explanation of why iska funds do not become ordinary movables in the hands of heirs, namely that the investor wants the capital preserved rather than consumed, does not make the loan half into a deposit for kiddushin or sale. I find difficulty in his proof. The very sugya in Kiddushin appears to show the opposite. The Gemara discusses one who betroths a woman with a deposit and distinguishes whether she accepted responsibility. Where she accepted responsibility, the Gemara calls it "like a loan" only for purposes of liability for theft or loss, yet the actual discussion suggests that if the money remains present and is not given for unrestricted expenditure, it can still function as a deposit for kiddushin. The category of loan for liability is not identical to the category of loan for acquisition. The Hagahot further argues that even if the lender once took collateral and then returned it to the borrower, Shemittah would cancel because the lender does not presently hold the collateral and still must press the borrower for payment. This follows Maharam of Rothenburg's view that collateral returned to the borrower, especially collateral taken at the time of the loan, does not create the full Rabbi Yitzchak acquisition. Mordechai cites Maharam that if such collateral is later lost in the borrower's house, the creditor loses his right because he never acquired the pledge. According to authorities who hold that even collateral taken at the time of the loan is acquired under Rabbi Yitzchak, the result should differ. The same would then apply to an iska. Since the investor may not use the principal freely and it must remain preserved, the money resembles collateral still belonging to the investor in significant respects. Rashi in Kiddushin strongly supports this. Explaining why kiddushin with a loan are ineffective, Rashi says a loan is given for expenditure and the borrower need not preserve the very money as an iska available at all times. This implies that where the funds must remain available as an iska, they resemble a deposit and can effect kiddushin. Rambam likewise says kiddushin with a loan fail because no presently existing object remains from which the woman now benefits, since the prior benefit has already passed. With iska funds, where she is not permitted to consume the capital freely, the act of kiddushin can newly free the funds for her use and thereby create a present benefit. Thus the loan half of an iska may have deposit characteristics not only for inheritance and transfer incidentally with land, but even for kiddushin and sale. See what I wrote in siman 66, seif katan 37. The matter therefore remains difficult against the Hagahot Maimoniyot's categorical ruling.
