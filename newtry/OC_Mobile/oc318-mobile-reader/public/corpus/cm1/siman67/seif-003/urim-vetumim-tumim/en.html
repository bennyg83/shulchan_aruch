The half that is a loan. This is Maharam's ruling in Mordechai: the loan half of an iska is cancelled by Shemitah. Radbaz, responsum 214, rules the opposite. Even the loan half is not cancelled. His proof is that if the recipient dies, the so-called loan half is not treated as ordinary movable property in the heirs' hands. This shows it remains legally within the owner's domain. It is called "half loan" chiefly to impose responsibility for that half on the recipient, not because ownership fully leaves the investor. If it remains the investor's property, why should Shemitah cancel it? Radbaz does not mention Maharam, and it appears Maharam's ruling escaped his attention. Ordinarily, where a later authority did not see an earlier major ruling, we give substantial weight to the earlier authority. Still, because Radbaz's view provides support for the widespread Jewish practice of doing business through iska, I will examine the matter. Maharam himself compares the case to a creditor who takes collateral and then returns the collateral to the borrower to hold for him. Maharam says Shemitah can cancel because the creditor no longer physically holds the collateral and must still collect. I do not understand the analogy as decisive. In Perek HaMekabel we say that collateral taken after the loan remains legally the creditor's property even if returned to the borrower, such that it is not treated as ordinary movable property of the borrower for heirs and Shemitah does not cancel the secured debt. The reason is that a creditor acquires collateral. And Ramban and other authorities hold that even collateral taken at the time of the loan gives the creditor a proprietary interest. Moreover, below in siman 72 the view of Ri is that if the creditor returns collateral to the borrower for safekeeping and it is lost, the borrower is merely an unpaid custodian while the creditor retains the status of paid custodian. This shows the object remains legally in the creditor's domain despite being physically with the borrower. If for custodial law we treat the collateral as the creditor's property, why should Shemitah suddenly treat it as fully returned to the borrower? Maharam therefore appears to follow Rabbenu Baruch, cited in Hagahot Mordechai, who disagrees with Ri and holds that once collateral given at the time of the loan is returned to the borrower, the creditor no longer bears responsibility and the object becomes ordinary movable property for the borrower's heirs. Within Rabbenu Baruch's system, Maharam's analogy works. But our halachah below in siman 72 follows Ri, not Rabbenu Baruch. Under that ruling, returned collateral remains legally in the creditor's domain and the corresponding debt is not cancelled. And even Rabbenu Baruch may agree regarding iska, because the Gemara expressly treats the business assets as remaining in the investor's chazakah for purposes of inheritance. Thus Maharam's own analogy can support Radbaz once we reject the collateral premise on which Maharam relied. For this reason Radbaz has a substantial basis, especially as support for the common Jewish practice of iska. Mechaber's unqualified adoption of Maharam is therefore difficult, because Mechaber elsewhere rules unlike Rabbenu Baruch on the underlying collateral law. See below, where I bring additional support for Maharam from Ramban. The issue is not entirely free of doubt, but Radbaz's position cannot be dismissed.
