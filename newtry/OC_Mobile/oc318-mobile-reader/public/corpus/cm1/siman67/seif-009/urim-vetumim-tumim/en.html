And even in Shemitah his condition is valid. Lechem Mishneh asks why this differs from a condition excluding ona'ah, where we rule the waiver can fail because the person did not know what he was relinquishing. Perhaps the borrower will pay before Shemitah. If so, when he agrees that Shemitah will not cancel the debt, he does not know whether the waiver will ever matter. Lechem Mishneh answers with difficulty that because the borrower knows the Torah contains Shemitah and knows what will happen if the debt survives until then, he is considered knowingly waiving. Gidulei Terumah and Knesset HaGedolah find that straightforward. But Lechem Mishneh was likely following Tosafot Ketubot 84, who say a husband waiving his future inheritance rights may not be considered fully knowing because perhaps his wife will not die before him and the inheritance will never arise. According to that approach, the same uncertainty exists in Shemitah. Most authorities, however, reject that Tosafot explanation. Ramban in Milchamot, Ran, Raah, and Ritva hold that knowledge of the legal right itself is enough even if one does not know whether the contingency will occur. Under that view, a Shemitah waiver is clearly knowing. There is a deeper difficulty. A monetary condition overriding Torah law works according to R. Yehudah because the protected monetary right can be waived. R. Meir says a condition against Torah law is void. Now contemporary Shemitah is rabbinic. We often say Chazal strengthened their enactments even more than Torah law. In ketubah, for example, even though the obligation is rabbinic according to many authorities, a condition reducing the basic ketubah can be void. Why then should a condition against rabbinic Shemitah work? Maggid Mishneh explains Rambam's ketubah rule carefully. A married woman cannot simply waive the basic ketubah while remaining in the marriage, because that would destroy Chazal's institution. But one might think a premarital condition should work because the husband enters the marriage only on those terms. Chazal therefore strengthened their enactment like Torah law and invalidated even that condition. This does not contradict the general rule that monetary Torah rights can be waived, because that general rule ultimately depends on the person's ability to forgive the money. Where Chazal specifically denied effective forgiveness in order to protect the institution, an advance condition cannot create a stronger waiver than direct forgiveness. Under Rambam's framework, Shemitah is different. Even Torah Shemitah permits a monetary condition. Therefore rabbinic Shemitah can permit it as well. Other authorities hold that ordinary waiver of ketubah can work but an advance condition cannot, because Chazal strengthened their enactment beyond Torah law. For them, we need another distinction. Tosafot say Chazal strengthen rabbinic law beyond Torah law mainly in common situations. A condition excluding Shemitah is uncommon, especially because the creditor can simply make a pruzbul. Chazal had no reason to invalidate a rare condition when a lawful preservation device already exists. This also answers Ritva's question: if lenders feared Shemitah, why did Hillel need pruzbul? They could all have inserted waivers. Before Hillel's enactment, if every lender had routinely conditioned away Shemitah, the condition itself would have become a common mechanism undermining the rabbinic institution, and Chazal could have strengthened Shemitah against it. Once Hillel created pruzbul as the normal lawful device, occasional contractual waiver no longer threatens the enactment. There is also another conceptual route. Chazal's strengthening of rabbinic law does not necessarily exceed what R. Meir would invalidate under Torah law. Where the condition certainly uproots the obligation from the outset, it can be void. But where the condition may never matter because the debt may be paid before Shemitah, the uprooting is not certain. Even R. Meir can distinguish uncertain future uprooting from a direct present cancellation. This helps explain the inheritance and ketubah sugyot. A reduced ketubah immediately lowers the market value of the woman's right and therefore certainly diminishes the institution now. A future Shemitah waiver may never have any practical effect. Thus the Shemitah condition can stand even in a rabbinic setting. This also explains Tur and Rosh, who say a condition that "Shemitah itself shall not cancel" is a condition against Torah law and invalid. That formulation directly uproots the Torah rule itself, unlike a personal monetary waiver by the borrower. The distinctions resolve several related difficulties. Ritva in Bava Metzia likewise notes that reducing a ketubah immediately lowers its present sale value, making the uprooting concrete. That accords with the analysis above.
