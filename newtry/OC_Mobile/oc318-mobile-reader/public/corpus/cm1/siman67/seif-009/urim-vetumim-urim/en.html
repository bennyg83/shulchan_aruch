But if he stipulated with him, etc. Since the borrower knows that shemittah cancels the debt and nevertheless agrees to the condition, this is a monetary condition and is valid. It differs from a condition waiving ona'ah, where below in siman 227 we rule that the overcharge prohibition still applies. There the buyer may not know that he is waiving anything, because he assumes the price is fair and no overcharge exists. Here he knows that shemittah would cancel the debt. See Tumim, where I discussed at length why we do not apply the rule that the Sages strengthened their enactments even more than Torah law, given that shemittah today is rabbinic. See there for two resolutions.
It emerges that he obligated himself in money, etc. This appears to be Rambam's language. According to Rambam there is no difficulty that the obligation should be an asmachta, based on the borrower's assumption that he would repay before shemittah. Had he known that he would not repay before shemittah, perhaps he never would have accepted the obligation. This resembles the classic asmachta, "If I fail to cultivate I will pay one thousand zuz." Yet according to Rambam this is not an asmachta because the condition was stated with "on condition," and anyone who says "on condition" is treated as saying "from now," while Rambam holds that an asmachta does not apply where the obligation takes effect from now. According to those who disagree below in siman 207, however, this explanation would create an asmachta. They must therefore understand the condition differently: if shemittah arrives and you have not paid me, then retroactively the money in your possession will be considered a deposit rather than a loan. A deposit is not cancelled by shemittah. It was delivered on that condition from the outset. Understand this carefully.
In the language of a deposit. Although the recipient is fully responsible for loss, a person may stipulate that an unpaid custodian assumes the liability of a borrower. The arrangement is therefore equivalent to an express stipulation that shemittah will not cancel the obligation.
We do not say that it is as though written, etc. Sema explains that we do not extract money based on uncertainty. Perhaps the parties intentionally omitted the condition because they wished to fulfill the mitzvah of shemittah. According to what I wrote in Tumim in siman 61, that a clause ordinarily and specifically written as a formal condition is not treated as though written when omitted, there is no difficulty. This is exactly the kind of condition people normally write expressly rather than relying on an implication.
