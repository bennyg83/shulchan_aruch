And a later-dated one is invalid. Ran writes in responsum that if the pruzbul was actually made in Nisan but written with an Iyar date, it is completely invalid. It does not even preserve a loan made in Nisan before the true execution date, because the instrument itself was created improperly. Tosafot Yom Tov asks why Ran ignored the Yerushalmi. The Yerushalmi cites the rule that antedated debt notes are invalid. R. Yochanan says they are completely invalid, while Reish Lakish says they are valid from the true writing date. It then cites a baraita that a pruzbul, whether antedated or later-dated, is valid but counted only from the time of writing, and asks what distinguishes pruzbul from an ordinary note. At first glance this seems to support Reish Lakish and undermine Ran. But the passage must be read more carefully. When the Mishnah says an antedated debt note is "invalid," the natural meaning is complete invalidity, even from the later true date. Otherwise there is hardly any "invalidity" at all. Obviously a note written in Iyar but falsely dated Nisan cannot collect from purchasers who bought in Nisan before the debt or instrument existed. No one would imagine allowing that. The Bavli in Bava Metzia likewise discusses whether such a note may at least collect from the true later date, and explains that according to R. Meir Chazal penalized the creditor completely, just as with an interest-bearing document. Thus R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish in the Yerushalmi are not disputing the plain meaning of the Mishnah. They are disputing the practical halachah according to the Sages. R. Yochanan holds that even according to the Sages, an antedated loan note is completely invalid because Chazal penalized the scheme lest creditors intentionally antedate documents and lose nothing if caught. Reish Lakish holds that the Mishnah's total invalidity follows R. Meir, whereas according to the Sages the note can still collect from the true date. The Yerushalmi then challenges Reish Lakish from the baraita of pruzbul. The baraita specifically says an antedated pruzbul remains valid from its true date. Why state that leniency only for pruzbul? If ordinary antedated debt notes were also valid from the true date according to the Sages, the same formulation should have appeared there. The answer is that ordinary loan notes are more dangerous. If a creditor intentionally antedates, and knows that at worst he will retain collection from the true date, he has every incentive to try the fraud. Chazal therefore invalidated the document completely. Pruzbul is different. The creditor can easily create a valid pruzbul on any day and has many lawful ways to preserve his debt. There is little incentive for deliberate fraudulent dating, so Chazal did not impose the same penalty. Accordingly, the Yerushalmi and Bavli fit together. We rule like R. Yochanan against Reish Lakish, and like the Sages against R. Meir regarding ordinary interest documents. Yet even the Sages can agree that antedated debt notes are completely invalid because of the special dating concern. Ran therefore correctly follows the plain Mishnah that a later-dated pruzbul made with an improper date is completely invalid. There remains, however, a difficulty according to Rambam's distinction that an antedated loan note is invalid only against encumbered property but can still operate against free assets. Likewise, Rabbenu Yonah says the borrower cannot deny the underlying transaction entirely. If the factual debt remains valid, why should an improperly dated pruzbul lose all effect? According to Rambam, pruzbul itself must be written. The writing is the legal mechanism that prevents Shemitah. Once the writing was made in a disqualifying form, it is worthless for that purpose even though the underlying loan still exists. But according to Rosh and Rama, who allow the creditor merely to say before beit din, "I transfer my debts to you," without a formal written pruzbul, the issue becomes harder. If the creditor actually made the proper oral declaration and only the memorial document contains the wrong date, why should the valid oral transfer be nullified? The Mishnah proves only the older formal system where written pruzbul was itself required. One can answer that Chazal penalized the fraudulent pruzbul so completely that even the otherwise valid declaration is disregarded. Otherwise the person who deliberately misdated the document would lose nothing, and the deterrent would vanish. This is conceptually plausible. Still, after R. Nachman and later authorities recognized a valid oral declaration, the source for extending the old document penalty to the independent oral act is not explicit. The matter therefore retains some difficulty, though Ran's ruling regarding the written pruzbul itself is well founded.
