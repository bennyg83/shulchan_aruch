In every place. Cancellation of debts does not differ between Eretz Yisrael and the Diaspora, as stated in Kiddushin 38b. The same applies today, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of Sheviit, halachah 3. Numerous incidents in the Gemara also occurred outside Eretz Yisrael, including Gittin 37b concerning Abba bar Marta and Rav, and the practice of Rav Ashi.
(Supplement) The Yerushalmi records that Rav Huna asked Rabbi Yaakov bar Acha how Hillel could institute a measure affecting Torah law according to the view that tithes remain biblical. Rabbi Yose answered that exile did not exempt Israel from land-dependent commandments. The Yerushalmi first suggested that cancellation of debts applies by Torah law both in Eretz Yisrael and abroad. Rabbi Yose then retracted and expounded the verse, This is the matter of the release: when agricultural shemittah applies by Torah law, debt cancellation applies everywhere by Torah law; when agricultural shemittah applies rabbinically, debt cancellation applies everywhere rabbinically. The scholars there add that even the authority who regards tithes as biblical concedes that shemittah is now rabbinic. Rabbi teaches that the two releases, shemittah and Jubilee, are linked: when Jubilee applies, shemittah applies by Torah law; once Jubilees ceased, shemittah remained rabbinic. (End of supplement.) In the words of Maharik. Maharik writes that one may rely upon Rabbi Yochanan's statement in Gittin 37a that a document containing a property lien is not cancelled. Although the Gemara attributes this only to Beit Shammai, Mordechai at the end of the chapter HaBa Al Yevimto writes that the halachah follows Beit Shammai on this issue. In Terumat HaDeshen. It relies upon one of Ri's answers concerning tithes, that they apply only in regions near Eretz Yisrael, such as Babylonia and Egypt. Although challah applies everywhere because it resembles a personal obligation of the dough's owner rather than a duty tied to land, and debt cancellation is even more clearly a personal obligation, the Torah nevertheless links it to Jubilee. Although all the early authorities write that shemittah applies throughout the Diaspora and acted accordingly, Terumat HaDeshen relies regarding shemittah upon this isolated answer and labors greatly to defend it.

And the principal ruling. The correct ruling follows Rambam in the name of the Geonim, in Hilchot Shemittah chapter 10, based on Arakhin 13a, where Rav Ashi says that the six years in question are counted in the stated manner, and following the Sages as Rashi explains. Rambam understands that the Temple was destroyed in the year 420, as indicated on 12b, and that the Gemara's statement that it was destroyed after a sabbatical year refers to the Tishrei following the destruction. Since most of that year was spent while the Temple stood, it is not counted among the years of desolation. For this reason old manuscripts of Avodah Zarah 9b do not read, add one year. Rambam explains that the calculation there, subtract two from each hundred, also fits the Sages because after the destruction they no longer counted Jubilees separately to sanctify shemittah years. Arakhin 31b, which says they counted Jubilees, applies only while the Temple stood. He further writes that all Talmudic calculations begin from the second year of creation, which is the first year counted in our era. According to our reckoning the Temple was destroyed in the year 3829, which was a sabbatical year. Thereafter only cycles of seven were counted. Thus the year 5320 was 1,491 years after the destruction, and all totals are divided into cycles of seven. Rashi in Arakhin reaches the same practical reckoning, although he says the Temple was destroyed in 421, the year after shemittah, and interprets Avodah Zarah according to Rabbi Yehudah. The Rosh and Tur discuss these calculations. On this basis the year 5274 was a shemittah year according to one reckoning, while Rashi's other chronology yields 5289. The principal ruling follows the reckoning shared by Rambam and Rashi in Arakhin. See Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, including the discussion attributed to Mahari L. Chaviv. Rambam also innovates that all Talmudic chronological calculations begin with the second year of creation.
(Supplement) And the principal ruling. Rashi in Arakhin holds that the Temple was destroyed in 421, as the sugya indicates under either explanation, while Avodah Zarah must then follow Rabbi Yehudah. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah and Rashbam state similarly. Rashi in Avodah Zarah, however, writes that the Temple was destroyed in 420, following Rav Ashi in Arakhin and Rabbi Meir. Each explanation has difficulties. Rambam's calculation accords with Rashi in Arakhin, allows the sugyot to fit together, and follows the Sages. The Rosh in Avodah Zarah interprets Rav Ashi according to Rabbi Yehudah and begins the cycles from 420, one year earlier than Rambam and Rashi. Rabbeinu Tam and Rabbeinu Chananel follow Rambam and Rashi's reckoning but read, subtract two years, referring to the Greek document era. This accords with the first answer in Arakhin and with Rashi there. According to Rabbeinu Tam, one must say the Temple was destroyed in 421, because the passage concerns the document era rather than the destruction itself. Arakhin can then be understood as Rambam does, referring to the year after the destruction. (End of supplement.)
(Supplement) The Tur's statement that according to Rashi the relevant year is 5289 refers to Rashi in Arakhin, who says the entire discussion assumes the Temple was destroyed during shemittah and answers that it was destroyed immediately afterward, in 421. Tosafot in Avodah Zarah cite Rashbam similarly. The Tur's statement that according to Ri the year is 5288 reflects Rashi in Avodah Zarah, who says the Temple was destroyed in 420, immediately after shemittah, as does the Rosh there, who resolves Tosafot's difficulty.
