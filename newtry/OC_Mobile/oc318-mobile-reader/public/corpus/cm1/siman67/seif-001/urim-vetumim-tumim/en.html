And it appears that they relied on these opinions, and so forth. Even if the view of Baal HaMaor and those who agree with him were correct, that Shemitah does not apply at all in our time, it would nevertheless be proper and fitting for us to act stringently and preserve the practice of cancellation of debts. The prophet warns us in exile to "set up markers for yourself," to preserve reminders of Hashem's mitzvot that were obligatory when we dwelled in Eretz Yisrael and the light of Hashem shone upon us. How could we fail to preserve even a remembrance of Shemitah and place this mitzvah behind the door as though it had no place among us? This mitzvah was among the first causes of the exile of the land, as the verse says, "Then the land will appease its Sabbaths all the days of its desolation." Because of our sins, from the time we ceased to observe it properly we were driven from one exile to another and did not again merit the complete restoration of the land. Even during the Second Temple period, according to Rambam and many authorities, Yovel did not apply by Torah law because all of the Jewish people were not dwelling on the land. According to those who rule like Rebbi that Shemitah depends on Yovel, even agricultural Shemitah was then not Torah law. Yet this mitzvah was the setting for an extraordinary public miracle. In Eretz Yisrael, in the sixth year before Shemitah, the produce was blessed enough to sustain three years, and when the cycle approached Yovel, four years. The blessing repeatedly appeared at its precise appointed time, not early and not late, so that everyone could recognize it was not chance or the influence of celestial systems. Chance does not recur with such precision, and the stars do not arrange themselves year after year to bless one particular calendrical year. The nation could therefore know that Hashem dwells among Israel, blesses His people, and sanctifies them through His mitzvot. The inner purpose of the mitzvah is equally profound. A Jew learns that his days on earth are like a shadow, that we are sojourners like our forefathers, and that "the earth and all it contains belong to Hashem." Human perfection does not consist in endlessly accumulating possessions and heaping wealth without knowing for whom it is gathered. When the land rests as a Sabbath to Hashem, rich and poor become equal in access to its produce. The mitzvah points toward the world that is wholly good, where the weary rest from their toil, rich and poor meet, small and great are there, and the servant is free from his master. Shemitah is a year of favor to Hashem, when a person casts away his idols of silver and recognizes that his own handiwork, property, and accumulated wealth are not his god. Money is then revealed as powerless. For this reason both the land and debts rest in that year, and the yoke of the creditor ceases. The mitzvah also restrained Israel from becoming completely absorbed in commerce, multiplying business ventures like merchant ships and spending day and night in pursuit of profit like the nations. "Not everyone who increases business becomes wise." Extensive commerce requires constant lending and credit between people. But when Shemitah arrives and cancels debts, a society cannot build its entire way of life on endless leveraging and indebtedness. A Jew is therefore pushed toward proper sufficiency, toward the measured life appropriate for a servant of Hashem, rather than directing all his thought, strength, and first energy toward imagined wealth and schemes for accumulation outside the spirit of Torah. Instead, he should be among those of wholehearted way who walk in Hashem's Torah, trust in the God of Yaakov, Who gives food to all flesh, and meditate on His Torah day and night. Wealth and honor are with Him. Hashem gives and Hashem takes, and human schemes are vain. Our forefathers themselves lived this way, tending flocks, warming themselves from the wool and drinking from the milk of the flock. "The blessing of Hashem makes rich." If this is the character-forming power of the mitzvah, how could we not cling to it with joy? If one of the philosophers renowned for ethics had taught such a discipline as a path toward perfect character, people would praise the wisdom and follow it. All the more so when it comes from Hashem, the Source of wisdom and understanding, and when the Sages of the Oral Torah fixed its laws for us. Every person who fears and trembles at the word of Hashem should therefore preserve at least a remembrance of this mitzvah that applied when we dwelled on our land. Jerusalem remembered in the days of her affliction all her former precious things, while enemies mocked her cessation. And in truth, this is not merely a pious remembrance. Baal HaMaor's view is opposed by the majority of the great authorities. The straightforward sense of the Gemara, accepted by most poskim, is that Shemitah continues to apply in our time, at least rabbinically. Ramban, whose words are quoted by Baal HaTerumot in Shaar 45, part 1, goes even further and holds that cancellation of debts by Shemitah applies by Torah law even outside Eretz Yisrael today, because we do not rule like Rebbi that Shemitah depends on Yovel. Rather, it depends on agricultural Shemitah so long as that mitzvah applies in Eretz Yisrael. According to Rambam and Tosafot, agricultural Shemitah in Eretz Yisrael can itself retain Torah force because Ezra's sanctification was not nullified, and debt cancellation therefore applies everywhere by Torah law. For all these reasons, every person should certainly be careful at least to preserve this mitzvah through a pruzbul as explained below, so that the memory of Shemitah not disappear from Israel.
And they have already written, and so forth. Many authorities struggled to explain why Shemitah has not been observed in these lands, but the proposed explanations do not truly cure the difficulty. Terumat HaDeshen suggested that distant lands such as Germany and its regions were never included in Chazal's decree of debt cancellation, just as Ri in Tosafot limits certain rabbinic agricultural obligations outside Eretz Yisrael to countries near the land. But that comparison helps only us who live in distant lands. It does nothing for Jews living in Egypt, Babylonia, Aram Tzova, Assyria, and other lands near Eretz Yisrael. Bach already notes that Chazal nowhere indicate such a geographic distinction regarding Shemitah. The analogy is also fundamentally weak. Terumot and maasrot are obligations of the land. Their Torah obligation is centered in Eretz Yisrael, and Chazal extended them to nearby lands so that those border regions would not be confused with Eretz Yisrael. It is therefore natural that distant lands were excluded. Cancellation of debts is a personal obligation. There is no inherent distinction between Eretz Yisrael and outside the land. Either the mitzvah applies to Jews everywhere or it does not apply at all. If Chazal preserved it rabbinically, there is no obvious reason to distinguish nearby from distant countries. Mahari Weil's collected rulings give another explanation: many contracts contain a clause granting the creditor four cubits of land, and perhaps this turns the debt into something like a loan secured by land or collateral, which is not cancelled by Shemitah. That reasoning is very weak. A mortgage protects the debt because the creditor relies on a specific asset and is therefore not simply "pressing" the borrower. A vague statement that the borrower grants four cubits somewhere, without identifying where, creates no comparable practical reliance. Even according to those who say every Jew is presumed to own some land, the poskim still require a real basis before writing pruzbul. We do not simply assume an unidentified parcel creates sufficient reliance. Mahari Weil also suggests that contracts commonly say collection may be pursued both under Jewish law and under the law of the nations, and that this functions as a condition that Shemitah should not cancel the debt. But Tur cites Rosh in this very siman that language authorizing collection by both religious and secular means does not itself override Shemitah. A stipulation against Torah law must be an actual explicit monetary condition. Perhaps Mahari Weil followed Rabbenu Tam, who holds ketubah is of Torah origin. Then the special rule that a woman cannot waive the basic ketubah by ordinary condition need not prove that Chazal always strengthen rabbinic law like Torah law. He may have understood rabbinic Shemitah as more open to contractual waiver. Even so, the ordinary clause "collect according to Jewish or secular law" does not expressly say that the borrower waives Shemitah. It can simply mean that if payment is otherwise due, enforcement may use those systems. We should not interpret routine language as an intentional waiver of Chazal's enactment. Moreover, most authorities hold ketubah is rabbinic, yet a condition reducing it is still ineffective. This shows Chazal can strengthen their enactments even beyond ordinary Torah monetary waiver rules, especially where the rabbinic law has a Torah root. Maharik, shoresh 92, offered another justification: perhaps we follow Beit Shammai that a document standing ready for collection is treated as already collected. If so, an outstanding note would not be within Shemitah. Bach and Shiltei HaGibborim correctly object that we do not rule like Beit Shammai. They try to distinguish ketubah from other notes, arguing that a ketubah has special force because it is a condition of beit din. But Mordechai repeatedly rules that if a widow dies before taking the oath required for collection from heirs, her ketubah can be lost. If we treated ketubah as already collected like Beit Shammai, that result would be impossible. The Gemara itself says that according to Beit Shammai there is no rule that a person cannot transmit an oath-bound claim to heirs. A better explanation of Mordechai is possible. When the husband is alive and the woman is divorced, collection of the ketubah may require no prior beit din process unless the husband raises a specific oath claim. The debt can therefore be treated as substantially ready for collection. When she is a widow collecting from heirs, however, she must take the heir-facing oath before collection. Judicial involvement is indispensable. Tosafot in Sotah explain that where actual judicial collection is still required, we do not necessarily say "standing to be collected is as collected." Thus Mordechai can distinguish a divorced woman collecting from her husband from a widow collecting from heirs. But even this does not justify ignoring Shemitah. Gittin 37 explicitly attributes the "already collected" theory in Shemitah to Beit Shammai. According to Beit Hillel, the possibility that the borrower may resist means the creditor is still "pressing" him. If every Torah lien made the debt already collected, Shemitah would almost never apply, since according to the view that liens are of Torah force every debt attaches to property. The strongest defense of the custom, in my opinion, lies elsewhere and perhaps this is what Maharik intended. Ramban, in Baal HaTerumot, proves that R. Yochanan held Shemitah today is of Torah force from the following episode. R. Yochanan initially thought a document containing property warranty was not cancelled by Shemitah. When a real case came before him, he refused to rely on his own comparison and said, "Although we compare in study, shall we act on that comparison?" Ramban explains: if Shemitah were only rabbinic, R. Yochanan could have relied on his own legal reasoning. His hesitation shows he regarded the matter as Torah law and would not act leniently based on an unproven analogy. That reasoning itself supports us. We do not rule like R. Yochanan that Shemitah today is Torah law. Later Amoraim, including Abaye, appear to follow Rebbi that it is rabbinic, and many later enactments regarding pruzbul presuppose that status. Once we rule that contemporary Shemitah is rabbinic, R. Yochanan's original substantive reasoning can reemerge. No later Amora necessarily rejected the underlying comparison that a strongly secured written obligation may stand outside ordinary cancellation; R. Yochanan himself merely refused to act on it in what he believed was a Torah case. Thus in a rabbinic Shemitah setting, there is room to rely on R. Yochanan's original logic concerning formal documents with explicit property warranty. This provides a plausible basis for the widespread custom regarding ordinary written obligations containing such warranty. And as I wrote above, where contemporary instruments are structured as iska, there is an additional basis for leniency. Nevertheless, because the majority of authorities maintain some form of Shemitah today, one should preserve the mitzvah through proper pruzbul rather than rely casually on custom alone.
And regarding the calculation of the Shemitah year. There is a well-known dispute, and because Beit Yosef and the later authorities did not fully trace its Talmudic source, I will summarize the root of the matter and also explain Rashi in a way that answers the principal objections. Chazal say in Arakhin that, because of our sins, both the First and Second Temples were destroyed in the year following Shemitah. The Second Temple stood 420 years. According to Avodah Zarah, its destruction occurred in year 3828 from the creation of Adam, leaving 172 years until the end of the fourth millennium. Rashi, as explained by Raah, holds that year 419 of the Second Temple was Shemitah, corresponding to 3827, and year 420, in which the Temple was destroyed, was the first year of the next Shemitah cycle. How does Rashi obtain that calculation? The Gemara asks according to the Sages, who hold that the fiftieth Yovel year does not also count as the first year of the next cycle. Four hundred years would divide evenly into Yovel and Shemitah cycles, leaving twenty years. That would make the destruction fall in the sixth year of a Shemitah cycle, not the year after Shemitah. The Gemara answers according to R. Yehudah, who holds the fiftieth year counts in both directions. Over four hundred years this removes eight separate Yovel years from the count. Add those eight to the remaining twenty and one gets twenty-eight, exactly four Shemitah cycles. Thus year 420 itself would appear to be Shemitah. R. Elazar gives another answer: the first six years after construction of the Second Temple, before Ezra's ascent, were not counted; Ezra then began the Shemitah calculation. Rashi in Arakhin explains this answer in the framework of the Sages, and some texts explicitly read "also according to the Sages." According to that reading, year 420 again comes out as Shemitah. But in Avodah Zarah Rashi effectively combines the two answers and applies R. Elazar's six-year deduction within R. Yehudah's system. Remove six from 420, leaving 414. Add the eight overlapping Yovel years under R. Yehudah and one gets 422. Twenty-one years before the destruction are then exactly three Shemitah cycles, making year 419 the Shemitah year and year 420 the year after Shemitah. Tosafot agree with Rashi that the destruction cannot itself have occurred in a Shemitah year because the baraita says it occurred after Shemitah. But they do not accept combining the two Talmudic answers. They therefore say year 420 was Shemitah, while the Temple was actually destroyed in year 421, corresponding to 3829. Rambam, in Hilchot Shemitah VeYovel, follows the Geonim and also treats year 420 as the Shemitah year, but he does not need Tosafot's historical claim that the Temple stood 421 complete years. Rather, the new year beginning with Tishrei shortly after the destruction can still be called the year of the destruction under the Temple-year reckoning. For our present calendar, the practical difference is only one year: Rashi counts one year earlier than Tosafot, Rambam, and the Geonim. As for whether we rule like R. Yehudah that the fiftieth year also counts as the first year of the next cycle, the Geonim and Rambam explain that in exile, where we do not actually count or sanctify Yovel years, the separate fiftieth year is irrelevant. We count continuous seven-year Shemitah cycles. Rambam therefore mentions that during the Babylonian exile they counted Shemitah cycles but not Yovel, precisely to teach that our later exile follows the same structure. Hagahot Mordechai records that according to Rabbenu Tam and Rambam, year 39 of the sixth millennium was Shemitah, while according to Rashi year 38 was Shemitah. This is exactly the one-year difference explained above. Hagahot Mordechai also cites the Savoraim that year 4564 was Shemitah and seems to treat that as a third calculation. Likewise Rama says year 320 of the sixth millennium was Shemitah, which at first glance appears inconsistent. The resolution is the standard distinction in counting creation years. When the Gemara and early calculations say the Temple was destroyed in year 3828, they count from the creation of Adam. But our conventional creation-era calendar includes the preceding notional "year of tohu," because the few days before Rosh Hashanah of Adam's creation are counted calendrically as a year for calculating tekufot and moladot. Thus Adam was created in the second calendrical year of the creation count. When we call a current year, for example, 5509 from creation, it is only 5508 years from Adam. That one-year offset resolves the Savoraic and Rama calculations. Their dates align with Tosafot and Rambam once one compares the same counting system. This also means calendar printers err if they calculate the years since the destruction merely by subtracting from the conventional creation-era year without adjusting for the Adam-count. The destruction date was stated in the Adam-based system, while our calendars use the tohu-based system. Now to a further explanation of Rashi. The Talmudic statement that the destruction was "after Shemitah" was first said regarding the First Temple and then "likewise" regarding the Second. According to R. Yehudah, the Gemara must explain how the First Temple also fell after Shemitah. After the first fourteen years of conquest and division, the nation counted Shemitah and Yovel. On the ordinary arithmetic, the First Temple destruction would not align correctly. The Gemara answers that when the ten tribes were exiled the Yovel count ceased, and King Yoshiyahu later restarted Shemitah and Yovel counting in the eighteenth year of his reign. Thirty-six years later the Temple was destroyed, after five Shemitah cycles, in the post-Shemitah year. According to the Sages, however, there is another calendrical subtlety. The verse says construction of the First Temple began in the 480th year after the Exodus. Subtract forty years in the wilderness and one gets 440 years in the land before construction. But the royal and Temple years were counted from Nisan. The year in which construction began counts in both the pre-Temple and Temple totals. The actual number of full residence years therefore differs by one from a simplistic total. The Temple itself was destroyed on Tisha B'Av, while Shemitah cycles begin in Tishrei. The Temple-year count did not end until the following Nisan. Thus the Tishrei immediately after the destruction was still within the same "year of destruction" in the Temple's Nisan-based chronological language, but it was already the first year after Shemitah. This is, in my opinion, Rambam's precise intent when he says that the year of the final destruction, beginning from the Tishrei two months after the destruction, was the year after Shemitah. At first glance the wording is strange: why call the following Tishrei part of the year in which the Temple was destroyed? Because Temple years were counted from Nisan, while Shemitah years begin in Tishrei. The same resolves the statement that fourteen years after the destruction a Yovel began in Tishrei. Counting from the Nisan boundary of the Temple-year framework and from Tishrei for Yovel produces the stated result. Accordingly, for the Sages the First Temple destruction can be described within a Shemitah-aligned year, while according to R. Yehudah the renewed count from Yoshiyahu places it in the post-Shemitah year. The phrase "likewise in the Second Temple" can therefore be understood according to each opinion's own calendrical framework. This removes a major objection to Rashi. His calculation is internally exact once the distinct Nisan and Tishrei year-counts, the Yovel theory, and the one-year creation-calendar offset are kept separate.
