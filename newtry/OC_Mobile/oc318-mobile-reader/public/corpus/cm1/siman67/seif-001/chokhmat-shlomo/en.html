Seif 7. See Sema, seif katan 45, who writes that the same applies if he rented it to him, and that this is even more obvious in a loan because a borrowed item is more fully in the borrower’s domain, even regarding accidents. His wording is difficult because the present law concerns land, to which the ordinary laws of custodians do not apply. Perhaps his intent is only conceptual: since elsewhere a borrower bears greater responsibility than a renter, borrowing demonstrates a stronger form of possession, and therefore certainly satisfies the requirement here.
There, seif 7: if beit din wrote him a judgment, it is considered collected and is not canceled by shemittah. My sharp young son, Avraham Binyamin, asked why, when a debt document is sold, we do not similarly distinguish that once beit din has issued a judgment ordering the debtor to pay the purchaser, the original creditor can no longer forgive the debt because it should be regarded as already collected. I answered that the rule here is itself part of the rabbinic mechanism of prozbul. The Sages treated a judgment as though collection had already occurred for purposes of shemittah. Biblically, however, a debt is certainly not actually collected merely because a judgment has been entered. Therefore the original creditor can still forgive it. This is clear.
