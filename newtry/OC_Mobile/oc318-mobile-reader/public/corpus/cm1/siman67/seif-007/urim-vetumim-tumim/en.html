And he denied it and swore to him. Rambam, Hilchot Shemitah 9, says generally that if the debtor denied the claim and after Shemitah admitted it, he must pay. He does not say the debtor had already sworn before Shemitah. Raavad objects from the Mishnah that Shemitah cancels an oath. If the debtor is still denying the money when Shemitah arrives, then if he later admits he should once again owe the money and seemingly the oath as well. It is strained to say Shemitah cancels only the oath while leaving the underlying contingent money obligation intact. Raavad therefore explains the Yerushalmi's statement that "a loan became a denial" as a case where the debtor had already sworn before Shemitah. Tur and Mechaber follow this. Beit Yosef defends Rambam by saying that when the debtor later admits part of the claim, Shemitah has already cancelled that admitted amount. He is therefore no longer a partial admitter and no Torah oath remains. Ran says something similar. Darkei Moshe calls this answer weak. At first glance that criticism seems surprising because Ran says it expressly. But the difficulty remains against Rambam's broad formulation. Even if the partial-admission Torah oath disappears, what about an oath against one witness, which exists even in a full denial? And today a person who denies everything can owe hesset. Why should Rambam say broadly that Shemitah cancels the oath? The better explanation is conceptual. So long as the debtor owes an oath, the creditor can still press him in beit din: swear or pay. That itself is a form of "he shall not press." Rosh repeatedly treats an oath as having monetary consequence because refusal to swear results in payment. Therefore Shemitah cancels the creditor's power to compel the oath. But where the denial itself carries no oath at all, there is no remaining act of collection or pressing. The debtor has denied the money and no judicial compulsion remains. Shemitah has nothing to cancel in practice. If, after Shemitah, the debtor then admits the debt, the money obligation can revive because it was never judicially collectible during Shemitah and therefore was not within the operative "pressing" mechanism. This is the case the Yerushalmi means by a loan that became a denial and is not cancelled. Rambam can likewise be speaking of a denial that carries no oath, which is why he states simply "he denied" without making the prior oath central. Thus Rambam's formulation is coherent and practically sound.
