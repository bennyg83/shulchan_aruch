And if she impaired it, and so forth. At the beginning of Perek Af Al Pi the Gemara says that the additional ketubah has the same law as the basic ketubah for several matters, including Shemitah. Rashi explains that neither is cancelled because both are obligations arising from a condition of beit din. Tosafot and Rosh object that even without that reason a ketubah should not be cancelled, because it is not a loan, just as store credit is not cancelled. They therefore explain the novelty differently: if she partially collected from the basic ketubah, that impairment affects the additional ketubah as well for Shemitah purposes. Tur in Even HaEzer siman 101 says a ketubah is not cancelled because it is a condition of beit din. This sounds exactly like Rashi and appears contrary to Rosh. The answer is that Tur wanted to include nichsei tzon barzel as well. The basic ketubah and its addition are amounts the husband obligates himself to pay from his own assets. They are not originally created by the wife handing him money as a loan. Nichsei tzon barzel, however, can involve property or money that the wife actually transfers to the husband, with the husband becoming obligated for their assessed value. Once divorce occurs before the end of Shemitah, that obligation can resemble an ordinary monetary loan. Tur therefore gives the broader reason that all these obligations are founded on the enactment of beit din. The rabbinic institution of ketubah protects them from ordinary Shemitah cancellation. Thus Tur does not reject Rosh's narrower explanation regarding the basic ketubah and its addition. He adds a reason sufficient to include the full marital-property framework.
One who comes by virtue of a gentile, and so forth. Sema asks: Mechaber generally holds that we do not say a Jew who comes through a gentile has the gentile's legal advantages when that would worsen another Jew's position. How then can this rule operate here? The source is Rashba, who himself holds that the principle "he stands like the gentile" is generally applied only to the detriment of the person who voluntarily entered the gentile transaction. There is no contradiction. In the first rule, where a Jew purchases from a gentile a debt note the gentile held against another Jew, Rashba does not rely on the doctrine that the purchaser "stands in the gentile's place." His reason is different: a debt that was never subject to Shemitah at its inception does not become subject to Shemitah merely because it later passes to a Jew. This resembles store credit, which remains outside Shemitah until transformed into a loan. The second rule is the one Rama states about a Jew who became guarantor to a gentile for another Jew and, after paying the gentile, received the gentile's note by proper transfer. There Rashba cannot use the same "inception" argument, because the guarantor may already have held a written reimbursement right from the Jewish debtor from the outset. One might therefore think the later reimbursement claim was always a Jewish debt subject to Shemitah. Rashba instead says the guarantor comes by virtue of the gentile. And here the general limitation does not apply. Usually we say: why did the Jew voluntarily enter a gentile-controlled arrangement and thereby seek to disadvantage another Jew? His hand is therefore on the lower side. Here, however, the debtor himself asked him to become guarantor to the gentile. Through the debtor's own request, the guarantor was compelled to step into the gentile's legal position. The debtor cannot then object that the guarantor should lose the advantages of that position. Therefore even according to the restrictive view, the guarantor can invoke the gentile source of the right in this case.
