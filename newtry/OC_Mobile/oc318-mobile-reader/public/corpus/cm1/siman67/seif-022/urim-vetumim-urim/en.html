On land, etc. Rashi explains that the Sages did not institute a prozbul in an uncommon case, because who ordinarily lends to a person who owns no land? Rosh gives another reason: if the borrower owns land, the debt is treated as though already collected, and therefore the Sages saw fit not to have shemittah cancel it. Even the smallest amount of land suffices, because there is no law of ona'ah regarding land. If the borrower owned land when the prozbul was written but no longer owned it when shemittah arrived because he had sold it, the law depends on these two explanations. See Tumim, seif katan 24, where I discussed this at length and concluded that Rosh's reasoning appears primary.
And another person owes him. Since that debt is subject to R' Nasan's lien, as below in siman 86, it is treated as though the borrower himself owns property, and a prozbul may be written on that basis.
Even without his knowledge. Because one may confer a benefit on a person in his absence. Although this particular benefit also creates a disadvantage for the borrower, because the prozbul will prevent his loan from being cancelled, the Sages were lenient regarding rabbinic shemittah, Rosh and Sema.
