Except on the basis of land. Sema cites Rashi's reason: lenders ordinarily do not lend to a person who has no land, and Chazal did not institute pruzbul for an uncommon situation. According to Rashi, one can ask: What if the borrower owned land when the loan was made but no longer owned land when the pruzbul was written? Since the loan was originally an ordinary common loan, perhaps pruzbul should still work. One might answer that this scenario itself is uncommon. Who would buy land already encumbered to the creditor? In an ordinary documented loan, the creditor's lien tends to keep the land attached to the debt. But that answer works poorly for an oral loan, which ordinarily cannot be collected from purchasers. The borrower may easily have owned land at the time of the loan and sold it before pruzbul. Perhaps, then, according to Rashi pruzbul was primarily enacted for documented loans. This appears in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 45, part 1, citing R. Yehudah Albargeloni, who requires land on the lender's side as a means of transferring the debt documents to beit din. Such a theory naturally fits written claims better than oral loans. It is forced to say that Chazal simply did not distinguish and extended pruzbul to oral debts as well. Yet Rama below, seif 19, expressly teaches that pruzbul works for an oral loan, and Sema says this is obvious. According to the present analysis it is actually a significant novelty. Rash gives a different reason for requiring land. When the borrower owns land, the debt is treated as though already collected, and even a tiny parcel suffices because land is not subject to ona'ah. Ran says the practical difference between Rashi and Rash is where the borrower owned land when the pruzbul was written but sold it before the end of Shemitah. According to Rashi, the pruzbul remains valid because the enactment depended on the ordinary lending situation at the time it was made. According to Rash, perhaps it should fail because the debt is no longer viewed as presently secured by land at the moment Shemitah arrives. Ran leaves the issue unresolved. There is, however, a proof favoring Rash. The Mishnah says an antedated pruzbul is valid even though antedating generally weakens the lender's position. According to Rashi, antedating can sometimes improperly strengthen the pruzbul. Suppose the borrower owned land in Nisan and sold it in Iyar. The creditor actually writes the pruzbul in Iyar, when the borrower has no land, so it should be invalid. If the document is antedated to Nisan, it will falsely appear valid and allow collection after Shemitah. That would be a serious concern. According to Rash, by contrast, a valid pruzbul depends on the legal relationship between the debt and land over the relevant period in a way not easily manipulated merely by changing the written date. One could answer for Rashi that beit din writes a pruzbul only after verifying that the borrower presently owns land. But this is difficult in practice. The creditor can transfer all of his claims to beit din in a general formula, even where the actual notes are far away. How will the judges know all of his borrowers and each borrower's land status? This makes Rash's explanation more attractive. It also helps explain Tosafot Gittin 36. Tosafot first discuss Rabbenu Tam's practice of writing pruzbul before ordinary contemporary batei din, not only before a court comparable to Rav Ami and Rav Assi. They then immediately cite the Mishnah that an antedated pruzbul is valid. The connection is not obvious. According to the present analysis, it is elegant. If pruzbul works merely through the exceptional confiscatory power of a supreme court, one might require a particularly powerful beit din. But according to Rash, pruzbul has an internal legal rationale: where the borrower owns land, the debt is treated as effectively collected, and the court document simply formalizes that status. Once the rule has its own legal basis rather than relying solely on extraordinary hefker authority, any competent beit din of the generation can administer it, though not an ignorant or unserious court. The Mishnah's treatment of antedating supports the Rash-style structural rationale, and therefore supports Rabbenu Tam's broader practice. Thus the balance of the evidence leans toward Rash's explanation.

This is because Chazal were lenient with rabbinic Shemitah, even though creating the pruzbul is detrimental to the borrower and ordinarily one may not impose a detriment on a person outside his presence. I am uncertain, however, whether the judges may be relatives of the borrower. Since the pruzbul causes a direct monetary detriment to him, perhaps relatives cannot participate in creating it. On the other hand, perhaps the same rabbinic leniency applies here as well, especially because the creditor transfers all of his debts to beit din in a general declaration rather than litigating this particular borrower's case. There is also a related difficulty from the rule that an antedated pruzbul is valid. Suppose the creditor transferred all of his debts in Nisan, when the judges were unrelated to one particular borrower. In Iyar one of the judges became the borrower's son-in-law. If the pruzbul were actually made only in Iyar, perhaps it would not work against that borrower. Yet if the document is antedated to Nisan, it would appear valid because at that time the relationship did not yet exist. This suggests that either relationship does not disqualify the judges in this nonadversarial pruzbul process, or else there must be some other limitation on antedating. The issue requires study in practical halachah.
