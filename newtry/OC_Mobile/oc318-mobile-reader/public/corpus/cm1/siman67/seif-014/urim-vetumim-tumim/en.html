Store credit, and so forth. Sema, citing Beit Yosef, distinguishes between ordinary running store credit and a one-time sale. Where a shopkeeper regularly extends credit and does not demand payment until the end of a year or two, allowing many small purchases to accumulate into one account, Shemitah does not cancel the debt. But if a person sells food or produce on credit in a single transaction, Sema says this is not "store credit" and Shemitah can cancel it. Beit Yosef did not codify this distinction in Shulchan Aruch, and in my opinion that omission was deliberate. Beit Yosef introduced the distinction only to reconcile Rambam with the Mishnah about one who slaughtered a cow and divided it on Rosh Hashanah, where an intercalated month can make the debt subject to cancellation. As a general rule, however, the distinction has no clear source. The Yerushalmi itself asks why that cow case differs from store credit and answers that the Mishnah follows R. Yehudah, who holds even store credit is cancelled. The Yerushalmi clearly did not accept Beit Yosef's distinction. Mordechai gives another answer: the cow case concerns a debt that was formally converted into a loan. That answer does not dispute the general rule of the Yerushalmi, since everyone agrees that once store credit is formally converted into a loan it is cancelled. One can suggest a different factual distinction. Where a merchant ordinarily extends credit during the week, the debt is not immediately pressed and therefore retains the character of store credit. But if the merchant normally never gives credit and sells only for cash, yet on Yom Tov must allow deferred payment because money cannot be handled then, he expects payment immediately once payment becomes permissible. Such temporary deferment may not have the status of ordinary store credit. This seems close to Rambam's understanding. Still, the Yerushalmi attributes the cow case to R. Yehudah, so this distinction cannot be the Yerushalmi's own primary answer. There is, however, a broader framework. One reason store credit is not cancelled is that the merchant ordinarily does not expect payment for a substantial period. It resembles a loan not yet due until after Shemitah. That reasoning works according to the halachah that a ten-year loan is not cancelled. But according to the other Talmudic version that even a ten-year loan is cancelled, one must instead explain store credit differently: it is not originally created as a money loan at all. The obligation arises from merchandise, not from a direct loan of money. The Yerushalmi itself appears to follow the stricter view that a long-term loan is still cancelled. It therefore cannot use the "not yet due" explanation and has to attribute the cow case to R. Yehudah. According to our halachah, which follows the view that a loan not yet due is not cancelled, Beit Yosef's distinction can explain Rambam conceptually. Nevertheless, the mainstream authorities, including Tosafot, Ran, Rash, Raavad, and Mordechai, do not formulate a general one-time-sale exception. That is likely why Mechaber omitted it. For practical halachah, ordinary commercial store credit should therefore remain outside Shemitah until it is formally converted into a loan, even if the purchases were not spread over a long period.
From the time he fixed a due date, and so forth. Knesset HaGedolah asks that Rashi in Perek Eizehu Neshekh, and likewise Nimukei Yosef and Ran, explain "converted into a loan" as meaning that he wrote a formal document acknowledging the debt. Yet Rosh, regarding Shemitah, explains it as fixing a time for payment. There is no necessary contradiction. The expression used in the Shemitah context need not have exactly the same technical meaning as elsewhere. Indeed, the ketubah itself is said to become subject to Shemitah once it is "converted into a loan," yet there is no need for new writing because the ketubah was already written from the beginning. Moreover, the Mishnah regarding store credit does not literally say "he converted it into a loan." It says that ordinary store credit is not cancelled, but if he "made it a loan," it is cancelled. In the cow case, if the transaction occurred on Yom Tov, formal writing may not have been possible. It is therefore reasonable to say the parties merely fixed a payment time or otherwise transformed the commercial credit into a defined debt. The Yerushalmi can also be understood this way. It asks how there can be collection on Rosh Hashanah if collection itself is restricted on Yom Tov, and answers through the fact that the transaction can still create a presently recognizable monetary obligation, especially where the day later turns out to have been an ordinary weekday. Thus "conversion into a loan" can mean establishing a definite payment obligation, not necessarily producing a new instrument. This reading may also help explain Rambam. There is a difficulty in the Bavli, Shabbat Perek HaShoel, which asks according to the view that a Yom Tov loan cannot be judicially collected: why does the cow-debt get cancelled if Elul was intercalated? If it was not judicially collectible, why is it within "he shall not press"? One cannot answer simply that seizure would still be effective, because Rambam elsewhere says even a debt the creditor agreed not to sue during Shemitah can still be cancelled. The better answer is that the cow case involves a debt whose due time was fixed for immediate payment if the day turned out to be a weekday. Once the month was declared intercalated, it became clear retroactively that ordinary payment was legally possible. The fixed obligation therefore became a true collectible loan. If, by contrast, one had expressly agreed that no demand could be made during the entire Shemitah period, there is a separate legal question whether the debt is within "he shall not press." This analysis also bears on Bach's ruling concerning a loan made near the end of Shemitah. Bach says that even though an ordinary unspecified loan has a thirty-day term, if one lends shortly before the end of Shemitah, the debt is nevertheless cancelled because the parties did not expressly fix a later due date. At first I thought the cow case might contradict him. But if the cow case itself involves a specifically fixed immediate due date, it does not. Still, the Yerushalmi supports Bach. The Yerushalmi derives from "the seventh year, the year of release" that one might have thought a loan made within thirty days of Shemitah would escape cancellation because the lender cannot sue during the thirty-day period. It then rejects that inference and compares it to one who agreed not to sue, whose debt can still be cancelled. The natural reading is that an ordinary thirty-day term arising by law does not remove the debt from Shemitah. Only an expressly fixed contractual maturity does. This is a sound distinction. If the parties set a definite future date, even seizure before that date may be recoverable because the debt is not yet due. But in an ordinary loan whose thirty-day term exists by default, the underlying obligation is already a present loan and can still fall within Shemitah. Thus Bach's practical ruling is correct.
Some say that this is called conversion into a loan, and so forth. Rama appears to say that when the merchant totals the account into a money sum, this itself constitutes conversion into a loan. He derives this from Mordechai in Perek Kol HaNishba'in. I do not think Mordechai means that. Mordechai asks why the cow case is cancelled when ordinary store credit is not. He answers that there the merchant "writes money," meaning he records the value of the produce as a monetary amount, whereas in ordinary store credit he merely records quantities of goods. If Mordechai meant that this bookkeeping step itself is the formal conversion into a loan, why would he need to distinguish between money entries and produce entries at all? The Mishnah already says explicitly that if store credit is converted into a loan, it is cancelled. Nor would Rama's explanation require saying that ordinary store credit records only measures. One could write "oil, one dinar; milk, one dinar" separately without aggregating them. If aggregation were the key, the commodity distinction would be irrelevant. The correct source is the Tosefta in Shevi'it. R. Yehudah and the Sages dispute store credit, and Rebbi says the words of R. Yehudah appear correct when the merchant records money, while the Sages appear correct when he records produce. Mordechai understands the cow Mishnah according to Rebbi's compromise. Thus even without a formal "conversion into a loan," there is a difference in the original credit arrangement. If the merchant records only quantities, such as measures and weights of merchandise, the account retains the character of commercial store credit and is not cancelled. If he reduces the transaction to a definite sum of money owed, it takes on the character of a monetary debt and is cancelled. This is not necessarily the same as formal zekifah. Formal conversion into a loan can also occur by fixing a due date, as explained above, and then even produce-based store credit can become a loan. Rama may simply mean to codify the practical distinction of Mordechai, not to define all zekifah by account aggregation. The wording is therefore somewhat imprecise. One further point follows from the cow case. Mordechai's rule cannot depend specifically on physical writing, since writing ordinary accounts on Yom Tov is prohibited. The operative fact can be an oral calculation or agreed monetary total. His use of "writes" follows the language of the Tosefta. Thus what matters is that the debt is stated and fixed as a monetary sum, whether orally or in writing.
