Store credit, etc. Sema writes in the name of Beit Yosef that this applies specifically to a shopkeeper who extends credit repeatedly and does not demand payment until a long period has passed. A person who made a single sale on credit would not be included. See Tumim, where I wrote that Tosafos, Ran, and other authorities do not appear to agree. They hold that even a single sale on credit is included in store credit and is not cancelled. Bach writes that if a person made a loan within thirty days of the end of shemittah, although we rule that an unspecified loan is not collectible for thirty days and the lender therefore could not yet demand it, shemittah nevertheless cancels the loan. See Tumim, where I brought proof for his ruling. The reason must be that if the lender had seized the borrower's property during those thirty days, we would not remove it from him, unlike a loan with an expressly fixed due date. See there.
And it is called converting it into a formal loan, etc. Even if nothing was written and the parties merely agreed orally, that is considered conversion into a formal debt. See Tumim for my discussion reconciling Rosh.
Who wrote the entire account in his ledger. Meaning as one combined total. See Tumim, where I wrote that Rama's wording requires study. According to Mordechai, the source of this opinion, if the seller merely says, "So many measures of oil and so much wine," without mentioning a monetary total, shemittah does not cancel it. But if he says, "So many measures," or, "So many pounds of meat, totaling such and such in money," then because a monetary amount was stated, shemittah cancels it. Writing is not essential. The same applies if he stated it orally. Likewise, even if he mentions only the quantity of measures or pounds and no monetary sum, if he converts the account into a loan by fixing a date for payment, shemittah cancels it. See Tumim.
