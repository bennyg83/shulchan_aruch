If he has no property, he is not believed. Baal HaTerumot asks why the seller should not be believed through a migo that he could have forgiven the debt. He answers that the statement means only that the seller's assertion is not independently credible, and the case is one in which the debt was transferred through ma'amad sheloshtan, so that, in his view, it could not be forgiven. One cannot answer that the seller is unwilling to forgive and lose his debt, as Tosafot and the Rosh say regarding a creditor who calls his own note a trust document, because that distinction applies only while the creditor still holds the note. Once he has sold it and it is held by another, the reasoning does not apply. Rabbenu Yerucham and the Rosh say this explicitly. According to what I wrote above, however, every assertion that invalidates the note is itself equivalent to forgiveness. Moreover, I concluded above that even a debt transferred through ma'amad sheloshtan may be forgiven. Accordingly, the seller is believed in every case, but he must compensate the purchaser under the laws of indirect damage for the full value the note would have possessed had there been a prozbul. See also what I write below in siman 86, where I explain that a creditor who says his note is a trust document cannot in fact forgive it, making the Rosh's distinction unnecessary.
If he has property, etc. Although Baal HaTerumot and the Tur rule that when the seller has property he is believed to prejudice himself and must repay the purchaser because he sold a note whose lien had already lapsed, this is not persuasive. Once the note has been sold, the seller is no longer believed, because he lacks a migo to forgive it. In particular, their implication that he need return only the purchase price as for a mistaken transaction is incorrect. Since his admission would harm the purchaser, it is not accepted at all. This differs from a creditor who declares a note still in his own possession to be a trust document and is believed when he has other property. There, because the borrower would otherwise suffer two losses, the debt is treated like encumbered property that is not collected while free property remains, and the creditor is speaking about his own note. Here the note has been sold and left the seller's possession, so he has no power to prejudice the purchaser by admission. The Rashba's words in Gittin support this, as does the general rule that a seller cannot defeat a purchaser's acquired right by later admitting that the property was stolen. Rabbenu Yerucham and Mahari Ibn Lev state this explicitly. This is the correct ruling.
