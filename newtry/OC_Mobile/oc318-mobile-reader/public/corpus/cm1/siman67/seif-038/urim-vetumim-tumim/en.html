And the seller must pay the purchaser. I am uncertain how much he must pay: the full value of the note or only the price he received. Mechaber's unqualified wording could imply the full value. Otherwise Shach's objection becomes very strong: how can the original creditor be believed by his own admission to say the debt was cancelled by Shemitah when that admission harms the purchaser? If the purchaser receives back only the price he paid, he can lose the large remaining value of the note through the seller's self-serving statement. Ordinarily, a litigant is not believed to make an admission that harms another person's established right. One could therefore say the seller is not believed merely to destroy the purchaser's claim. Rather, his admission binds only himself. Since he sold as valid a note that he now says was already cancelled, he has caused the purchaser the entire loss. To avoid pointless circuity, he immediately pays the purchaser the note's full value. That would explain Mechaber well. However, Tur and Baal HaTerumot appear to say the seller returns only the purchase money because he misled the purchaser. The resolution may be that this case differs from an ordinary admission of prior payment. Here the note already carries an objective defect: Shemitah has passed. The purchaser's ability to collect depends on an affirmative presumption that the lender certainly made a pruzbul or otherwise protected the debt. The lender now says explicitly, "I did not." He is not creating a new defect in an otherwise presumptively perfect note. He is negating the very factual presumption that was needed to overcome the visible Shemitah problem. In that setting, beit din cannot simply extract the debt from the borrower on the basis of a presumption contradicted by the person who would know. Still, we should not extend this further than necessary. Where the seller's admission would leave the purchaser entirely uncompensated, perhaps we would refuse to accept it against the purchaser. Where the seller returns the purchaser's actual consideration, however, the purchaser knowingly bought a note that had passed through Shemitah without seeing a pruzbul. He assumed some risk. As Rav Hai Gaon says in the context of forgiveness, one who buys a debt note buys a right inherently exposed to certain powers of the original creditor. Therefore repayment of the purchase price may suffice. The issue remains difficult, but Tur and Baal HaTerumot's language favors limiting ordinary liability to the money received unless a separate rule of damages applies.
