And it was lost through unavoidable circumstances in Levi's possession. According to the authorities who treat a creditor holding a pledge as a paid custodian, the same applies to theft or loss only where the event was one for which such a custodian is exempt. The Beit Yosef explains that “lost” here means a complete unavoidable accident, such as a ship sinking at sea, for otherwise even Shimon would be liable. The Giddulei Terumah objects that if the loss was wholly unavoidable, the creditor should collect his entire debt from everyone, and no dispute remains. The answer is that the section addresses the allocation of liability among Reuven, Shimon, and Levi, not merely the creditor's ultimate right to collect. Whether Shimon is discharged depends upon the standard of custody applicable to him and upon the authority with which he transferred the pledge to Levi. The word “unavoidable” must therefore be read relative to the relevant custodian's level of responsibility.

He cannot swear and therefore pays. The Sema says that Shimon pays the entire claim, namely two dinars. In my view the inability to swear concerns only the one dinar directly contradicted by the witness. As to Shimon's assertion that the pledge was worth only three dinars, he may take a Torah oath and be exempt. The text deliberately says that Shimon partially admits and must swear, teaching that the rule “one who must swear but cannot, pays” does not automatically extend from one component of the claim to the other. If he makes a definite claim as to the remaining value, he swears. If he says only that he does not know whether it was worth more than three, then he is obligated to swear and cannot, and must pay that amount as well.

If it was lost through unavoidable circumstances, Shimon is exempt. The Sema explains that Reuven cannot say, “Had it remained with you, the accident would not have happened,” because with rare accidents we say that the event could have occurred here or there, whereas theft and loss are common and may result from the initial deviation. I do not understand this distinction. Even an unpaid custodian is exempt when he proves that the article was stolen from the second custodian under circumstances that would exempt the first according to his own standard. The Rambam expressly rules that if the second custodian brings proof sufficient to exempt the first custodian under the first custodian's law, the first is exempt. The issue is therefore not whether the event is common, but whether the transfer itself was unauthorized and whether the proven event would have imposed liability upon the original custodian.

Shimon is exempt. The Bach writes that Reuven is nevertheless not required to repay the loan merely on Levi's oath or on witnesses, because Shimon himself does not assert the matter with certainty. In the presence of witnesses, this does not appear correct. Since witnesses establish the unavoidable loss, Reuven should pay the debt. The Bach's comparison to one who says, “I lent you, but I do not know whether you repaid me,” is inapposite where witnesses establish the relevant fact. Even where the matter rests on Levi's oath, it requires study whether Reuven must pay. The statement that Shimon is exempt may address only Shimon's custodial liability and not Reuven's underlying debt, as the Sema explains in a comparable context above.
If Levi was negligent, Shimon must pay. The Sema writes that even the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, who hold that when a custodian entrusts property to his wife or children and they are negligent, they rather than he are liable, would agree here because Levi is an unrelated third party. This is unpersuasive. At first glance the reverse is more likely: even Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh, who sometimes impose liability upon the first custodian for family members, may exempt him here. Maharshal rules that where the owner has already litigated with the second custodian and the second cannot pay, the first is exempt. The special rule for one's wife and children exists so that every custodian should not escape liability by handing the deposit to household members who consume or lose it. That policy does not necessarily apply to an independent third party whom the owner commonly trusts. The result therefore depends on whether Shimon was authorized to entrust the article to Levi and whether Levi is financially responsible. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch cannot be read as imposing automatic liability upon Shimon in every case of Levi's negligence.
