And pays. According to the Sema, he pays the entire claim, namely two dinarim. Although he knows the item's value and could swear regarding the admitted portion, and it would be unreasonable to make him pay more than its value merely because he cannot swear that it is not in his possession and that he was not negligent, nevertheless the three oaths arise together. Since he cannot swear all of them, we treat him as though he cannot swear even the oath on the admitted portion. Even according to the view that where an oath is extended to another matter and he cannot swear one part we do not require him to pay both, as stated in siman 75, seif 15, here all are treated as one. In the Perishah the Sema preferred to explain that the lender also refuses to pay the third dinar because he is certain Levi was not negligent. He is therefore properly a person who cannot swear and must pay. Even if, after hearing the judgment, he offers the dinar and wishes to swear regarding the remainder, since initially he refused to pay and swear, he is judged according to the rule of one who cannot swear and must pay. The Shach calls this astonishing. If the person stands before us making a definite claim and is able to swear, how can we treat him as unable to swear? Moreover, if two witnesses had testified that the loss occurred through negligence, he would still swear that the item was worth only three dinarim. All the more so when he merely does not know whether the loss was unavoidable. The Shach therefore explains that the rule of payment applies only to the one dinar concerning which he cannot swear. As to his claim that the item was worth only three dinarim, he takes a Torah oath and is exempt. Only if he himself is uncertain whether it was worth more than three dinarim is he one who cannot swear and must pay. See there. The Taz likewise challenges the Sema and writes that the meaning of this ruling was obscure to him; see there at length.

From him. The Sema writes that Reuven cannot say, 'Had it remained with you, it would not have been lost through unavoidable circumstances.' With any unusual unavoidable event we say, 'What difference is there between here and there?' Only theft and loss are common events and generally arise through some initial negligence or change, and there we may say that had the custodian not altered the arrangement, the loss would not have occurred. The Shach writes that he does not understand this. Certainly an unpaid custodian who brings witnesses that the item was stolen from the second custodian is also exempt, as shown in siman 291, seifim 21 and 26. The reason the case here speaks of unavoidable loss is simply that a lender holding a pledge is a paid custodian, as explained above in seif 2. The Taz asks why the borrower does not still have to pay the debt, as stated above in seif 2, that when the pledge is lost through unavoidable circumstances the borrower remains liable. One can answer that the case does not involve a complete unavoidable loss. If it truly was a complete unavoidable loss, the entire debt must certainly be paid, contrary to the Bach. See there. The Shach also challenges the Bach and writes that where witnesses establish unavoidable loss, the borrower certainly must pay the debt. Even where Levi establishes it through his own oath, the matter requires study, for perhaps the debt must still be paid. The statement that Shimon is exempt addresses only Shimon's liability, not Reuven's underlying debt. The Sema made a similar point above in seif 27. See what I wrote in seif katan 71.
Below. The Sema writes that even according to the Rambam, whom the Tur and Shulchan Aruch cite there in seif 24 as holding that negligent sons or wives are themselves liable rather than the husband who entrusted the item to his wife, here he agrees that the original custodian is liable. Here the pledge was completely handed over to another man. Although Reuven commonly deposits property with him, he still should not have done so, and this counts as negligence for this purpose. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch therefore state the rule here without qualification. The Shach disagrees. At first glance the opposite is more reasonable: even according to Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh there, the custodian here should be exempt, and Maharshal so writes, though his proof is not decisive. The Mordechai and Agudah explicitly show that according to Rabbeinu Tam he is liable here as well, and the same follows from Baal HaTerumot and the Tur. Nevertheless, their words also show that according to the Rambam and the Mechaber he is exempt here too. So write the Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, Bach, and She'erit Yosef. The Sema's proof from the Tur's silence is no proof, since the Tur follows the Rosh, who rules there that even entrusting to one's wife and children leaves the original custodian liable. Nor is there proof from the Mechaber, who did not state this law here at all. Perhaps the Sema contains a scribal error and should read 'the Rama' instead of 'the Mechaber.' But even the Rama proves nothing, because he relied on siman 291, where this law is treated principally. Indeed, in Darkei Moshe he writes, 'See below siman 291, where some disagree.' This confirms the Shach's explanation. The Taz agrees with the Sema.
