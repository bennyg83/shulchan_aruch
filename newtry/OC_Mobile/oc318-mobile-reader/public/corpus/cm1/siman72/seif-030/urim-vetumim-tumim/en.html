Sema asks: even if the first custodian cannot swear that the loss was unavoidable because he transferred the object to another custodian, why can he not at least swear that the collateral was worth only one dinar? Sema answers that all the required oaths come together, and inability to swear one of them causes payment on the entire dispute. That is very difficult. Separate oath issues do not normally collapse into one merely because they arise in the same case. Shach instead says the first custodian can still swear the valuation oath and therefore pays only the one dinar he admits. His inability to swear about the accident affects only the claim of complete exemption. That reading also has problems. The language of the poskim groups the matters together and sounds as though the custodian pays the full amount claimed. It also omits the supposedly central Torah oath on the valuation. And the stated cherem against one who exaggerates the value makes little sense if only the admitted dinar is ever being paid. The text therefore indicates that the custodian pays the full four dinars claimed. Why? Gidulei Terumah suggests that the oath that the object is not in the custodian's possession is a Torah oath. Since the first custodian cannot personally take it after transferring the object to someone else, he is one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, and therefore pays. Perishah objects that this oath is only rabbinic and that the unable-to-swear rule applies only to a Torah oath. But whether the nonpossession oath is Torah or rabbinic is itself disputed. Ritva records both views. More importantly, Rambam treats the original custodian oath as retaining its essential force even after the custodian says, "I will pay." The obligation does not vanish merely because he offers money. Rambam repeatedly calls it a custodian oath and allows other matters to be included within it. Maggid Mishneh's discussion also implies that this oath can have Torah-level consequences where the underlying pleading is definite. Accordingly, where the first custodian cannot swear because the object was in the second custodian's hands and the claimant does not trust the second custodian's oath, the first custodian can fall within the unable-to-swear rule and must pay according to the claimant's definite monetary demand. Otherwise every custodian could evade a Torah oath simply by handing the object to another person, even to his wife or minor children, and then saying he personally does not know. The Gemara's collateral-oath structure also supports this. It does not resolve the cases by placing the collateral with a second custodian and moving the nonpossession oath to that person, because doing so would leave the first custodian unable to swear on the monetary dispute and could trigger payment rather than exemption. The same applies where the object merely diminished while in the second custodian's hands. The first custodian's lack of personal knowledge is itself the problem, and the second custodian's statement does not automatically substitute where the claimant does not accept him. It is better to accept some compression in the wording of the source than to create an artificial rule that the early authorities never stated. The practical principle is that a first custodian who cannot take the required oath because he transferred the object can be liable for the full definite claim.
