(Seif 39) “Since they are not in his possession.” The Tur concludes in the name of the Rosh that Levi has no migo, because a migo concerns only a claim within the claimant’s own power; perhaps his companion would refuse to lie. I previously noted that the Rashba may disagree. Yet the Rosh’s principle is explicit in Bava Batra: one who says, “I bought the land from the person who bought it from you,” cannot rely on the migo that he could have claimed direct purchase and three years’ possession, because his present claim itself does not establish title and depends on the seller. He must return the land and recover his purchase price from the seller. This is exactly the Rosh’s rule. Maharshal rejects the responsum because Tosafot and Terumat HaDeshen recognize a joint migo where partners can both escape through the same account, even though one might contradict the other. There is no conflict. In those cases each partner has a legally sufficient present claim, and the only question is whether the migo is undermined by fear of contradiction; partners trust one another to maintain the same account. Here, however, the possessor’s present claim is legally insufficient even according to himself and depends entirely on Levi’s independent rights. A migo cannot supply the missing legal claim. The Rosh’s responsum is therefore settled halakha.
