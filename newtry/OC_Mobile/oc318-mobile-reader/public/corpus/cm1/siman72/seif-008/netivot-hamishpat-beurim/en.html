An ordinary sword belonging to a non-Jew. This rule appears limited to a case where the borrower was not informed that the sword belonged to a non-Jew. If he was informed, he is liable even for an arbitrary claim imposed by the non-Jew. Siman 131, seif 7 rules that where a Jew becomes guarantor to a non-Jew on behalf of another Jew, the Jewish borrower may be liable even for an additional claim wrongfully imposed by the non-Jew upon the guarantor. All the more so here. If Reuven had borrowed the sword directly from the non-Jew and Shimon had served as guarantor, Reuven would bear the consequence of the non-Jew's claim against Shimon. Here Shimon borrowed the sword and the non-Jew recognizes only Shimon, while Reuven then received it from Shimon. Shimon is therefore effectively Reuven's guarantor to the non-Jew in the strongest sense. Reuven is accordingly liable for the non-Jew's additional claim, provided there is clear evidence that the claim arose solely from this matter, as required in siman 131. I later found that Urim briefly cites Maharam Gershon that if the borrower was informed, he is liable for such claims. I was pleased to find the conclusion, though the reason and proof were not stated. In my view the analogy to siman 131 is exact. The general custodial rules that Shach discusses regarding an expensive sword will, God willing, be explained in Hilchot Pikadon.
