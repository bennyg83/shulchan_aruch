An ordinary one. The Shach writes that the Mechaber's words initially imply that the non-Jew is making an excessive claim against the Jew and demanding more than the sword's value, while its actual value is unknown. Therefore the Jew need pay only the value of an ordinary sword. The Sema likewise appears to understand it this way, comparing it to the ruling at the end of siman 378 from a responsum of the Rosh concerning one who damaged another person's property while it was being held for a non-Jew. The Rosh concludes that if the non-Jew acts oppressively because of this, the person who caused the damage is not liable, since he did not intend that result. In truth, however, one who studies the Hagahot Mordechai sees that its intent is that he need not pay even the full value of this sword. The case is that the sword was unusually valuable and the borrower did not know that it was worth so much. Thus, even if the non-Jew makes no excessive claim and merely demands the value of such an expensive sword, the borrower pays only the value of an ordinary sword. The source itself concludes that this ruling requires study, and Maharshal likewise writes that it requires great study. It appears to me that this is a monetary doubt. Nevertheless, one who directly damages the sword with his hands is plainly liable for its full value, on the principle, 'What was your property doing in my domain that I damaged it?' If the damage resulted from negligence but not from a direct act, its law for this purpose is like loss. If the non-Jew makes an excessive claim and takes more than the item's actual value, the damager is plainly exempt, and Maharshal so rules. Even one who damaged it directly is exempt from the excess caused by the non-Jew's oppression. See below siman 378. These are the words of the Shach.
