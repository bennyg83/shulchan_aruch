Seif 41. A non-Jew’s collateral is in Reuven’s possession, etc. There is a doubt whether Shimon acquires it. See the Shach, who wonders how acquisition is possible, since even if the collateral were in the creditor’s own unguarded courtyard he would not acquire it, and all the more so when it is physically in someone else’s possession. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Pesachim that when a custodian lends the depositor space in his house for the deposit, the location can be called the depositor’s house. A courtyard guarded through one’s agent is considered guarded for the owner, because an agent stands in the principal’s place. With ordinary collateral, the second lender holds the item for his own benefit. The place where the collateral rests is therefore not granted to the first lender, and it cannot be regarded as the first lender’s courtyard. With a true deposit, however, the space in which the custodian places the item is effectively assigned to the depositor for that very item. The custodian has undertaken to guard it for him, and is therefore functioning as his agent. The location is thus analogous to a courtyard guarded with the depositor’s knowledge and can effect acquisition. Although we rule elsewhere that a rented courtyard can remain legally attributed to the lessor for certain acquisitions, that concerns something new that happens to enter the courtyard and was not the subject for which the space was rented. Here the very question concerns the item for which the custodian specifically made the place available. For that purpose, the space was assigned to the depositor. It can therefore be considered the depositor’s guarded courtyard. This, in my view, is precisely the Mordechai’s doubt, and it is a sound and correct distinction contrary to the Shach.
