Reuven borrowed, etc. The Mechaber's words appear at first to concern a non-Jew who falsely demands more than the sword's true value, so that Reuven pays only the ordinary value of a sword. The Ir Shushan and Sema understand the case this way and compare it to one who causes another loss through a non-Jew's wrongful demand. But the Hagahot Mordechai means that even the actual value of this unusually expensive sword need not be paid where the borrower did not know its exceptional value. It compares the case to one entrusted with a gold coin after being told it was silver: he accepted responsibility for ordinary silver, not exceptional gold. Thus, even if the non-Jew demands only the sword's true high value, the borrower pays merely the ordinary value of a common sword. Maharshal understood this but strongly objected. In the coin case the depositor misrepresented the object, and in the case of grain concealing valuables the owner himself acted unusually. When a person visibly entrusts another with a precious stone, the custodian accepts its full actual value, whether great or small; he cannot claim that he assumed it was an inexpensive stone. Maharshal therefore rules that the borrower must pay the sword's full value. In my view the analogy to the gold coin is defensible. The depositor's statement that it was silver is not necessarily negligence; he may say so precisely to induce the custodian to accept it. The custodian could have suspected this, yet he is still liable only for the level of custody he accepted. Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri, and Nimukei Yosef distinguish between valuable contents that people sometimes conceal in an ordinary container, which a wrongdoer may have reason to anticipate, and pearls in a place where no one ordinarily puts them. A precious stone is ordinarily valuable, so one who accepts it must contemplate substantial value. An ordinary sword, however, is not commonly worth an extraordinary amount. One who borrows it need not assume responsibility beyond the usual market value unless informed. Shiltei Giborim therefore rules that if the owner disclosed its special value, the borrower must pay that value; otherwise he pays only the ordinary value of such an object. Since the Hagahot Mordechai itself ends with "requires study," the law remains doubtful. This limitation concerns custodial loss. Shiltei Giborim extends it even to one who directly damages the object, reasoning that he could not have anticipated the unusual value. I disagree. One who directly destroys another's property is ordinarily liable for its actual value under the principle, "What was it doing in your possession that you damaged it?" The exemptions for a purse hidden in a stack or pearls in an unusual container rest on the owner's own negligence in placing valuables where no one would expect them. Here the owner did nothing negligent by handing over the sword itself. Negligent damage that is not direct may follow the rule of loss, but direct damage requires full payment. This is even clearer according to my conclusion elsewhere that where witnesses establish that pearls were actually in the container, direct damage is fully compensable. If the non-Jew merely extorts more than the sword was worth, however, the borrower is plainly exempt from the extortionate excess, even if he damaged the sword directly.
