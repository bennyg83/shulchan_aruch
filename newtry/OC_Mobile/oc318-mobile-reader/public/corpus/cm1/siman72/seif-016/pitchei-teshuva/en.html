See Baer Heitev, through his citation of the Shach that another case arises where the lender transfers the oath to the borrower, and that the Bach likewise writes this, from which a proof is drawn. The wording there is not precise, because according to the Shach's case this proof does not follow; it follows only according to the Sema's case. See the Shach himself and Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 87, seif katan 9. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9, concerning a lender holding collateral where the parties disputed the value of the pledge. The oath initially fell upon the lender, who was the defendant regarding the excess value, and he transferred the oath to the borrower. The question was whether the lender must also swear that the pledge is not in his possession even where its type is commonly available in the market. Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg first writes that the matter appears explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 72, seif 15, and Shach, seif katan 61. He then questions why the Sema formulated a remote case and made the Beit Yosef appear to retract what he had criticized in siman 28, namely the view that even one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear is exempted by a supporting witness. The explanation that the oath was transferred is much simpler. The same difficulty applies even more strongly to Gidulei Terumah cited by the Shach. He therefore suggests that the precise wording of Baal HaTerumos, the source of this law, forced the Sema and Gidulei Terumah to distinguish. Baal HaTerumos writes that this applies specifically where 'the oath comes upon the lender,' but where 'the oath comes upon the borrower,' the lender never escapes the oath that the pledge is not in his possession. This language implies that the primary oath originally rests upon one party or the other, not that it arrived there through transfer. He discusses the rationale at length and concludes that in the case before him the party in possession may certainly say, 'I rely on the authorities who require the lender to swear.' Ordinarily, one cannot say 'I rely on' where one authority explicitly rules one way and the other authority does not demonstrably disagree. Here, however, although the Shach explicitly requires the oath while the Sema merely gives another answer, the very fact that the Sema chose a remote construction indicates that he did not accept the Shach's rule in practice. Even a judge who rules this way initially should not be criticized, because Baal HaTerumos's wording is precise and the reasoning is sound. This is the substance of his words. See there. Regarding Baer Heitev's statement that this proves that one obligated to swear who cannot swear is not exempted even by one supporting witness, see the Shach, who concludes that this is unlike what appears at first glance from the Beit Yosef above in siman 28, seif 2. Dagul Merevavah writes that there is no contradiction. In a case of fifty known and fifty unknown, if there is a supporting witness that only fifty is owed, then there is no oath at all and the defendant was never one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Likewise, a person disqualified from swearing who partially admits a claim and has a supporting witness has no oath at all. So too in our case, if the lender had claimed with certainty that the pledge was worth five dinarim and he did not know the remainder, while a witness supported that it was worth only five, he would be exempt. Here, however, the lender did not admit even one dinar, apart from a perutah; the one dinar he must pay arises through the witness. Therefore, he must pay even the remainder. The rule is: one witness can exempt from an oath, but cannot exempt from an already established monetary liability. Once the defendant is already in the category of one obligated to swear, the witness no longer exempts him from money. Although the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, in the passage beginning 'there are those who ask,' does not appear to say this, his words can also be reconciled accordingly. This is what appears to me, though the matter still requires study. End of quote. See Ketzos HaChoshen, siman 87, seif katan 9.
