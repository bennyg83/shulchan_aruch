And he is exempt even from negligence. Shach explains that the phrase "responsibility" includes even responsibility for negligence, and Maharam in Mordechai says the same. One might object from Mishnayot that use the phrase "liable for their responsibility" to distinguish unavoidable accident from theft or loss, while ordinary negligence remains liable in every custodial category. But there the phrase follows the word "lost," and ordinary "loss" does not mean negligent destruction. It refers to theft, loss, or accident according to context. Here the agreement itself excludes "responsibility," which is broader. Still, there is a further question. Maharam derives his interpretation from the vow, "I undertake an offering on condition that I bring it at the house of Chonyo." If he brings it there, the Gemara treats him as though he had said, "I undertake an offering on condition that I am not responsible for it." In that case he may actually slaughter it outside the proper place, which is a direct destructive act, and nevertheless the original vow obligation does not remain. Does this prove that "not responsible" exempts even direct damage? The cases differ. In the offering case, the obligation was created conditionally from the outset. If he performs the excluded act, the original undertaking retroactively never created the ordinary responsibility. He has not damaged another person's property; the sacrificial obligation itself was limited. Here, even if a custodian says, "I accept no responsibility," that can remove custodial liability for negligence. But direct destruction of another person's property is an independent law of damages. One cannot normally say "tear my garment" and be exempt unless the owner expressly adds "on condition that you be exempt." Therefore a general exclusion of custodial responsibility does not necessarily authorize active destruction. There is, however, a practical consequence in a true gift. If one gives property on the explicit condition that the recipient bears no responsibility for it, even if the recipient later destroys it, one can argue that the gift itself was conditioned so that the property became fully his and the donor retained no claim. The exact wording controls. Another difficulty arises according to Rambam and those who treat negligent damage to documents and other excluded custodial objects as direct damage. Rabbenu Baruch of Regensburg says an unpaid custodian cannot exempt himself from negligence merely by an ordinary custodial condition, because negligence is equivalent to damaging with one's hands. He infers this from the Mishnah's language that an unpaid custodian may stipulate to be exempt from an oath, not necessarily from all payment. Authorities who reject Rambam can disagree because they regard negligence as custodial liability rather than direct tort. Therefore Mechaber's broad exemption from negligence is not conclusive for Rambam's system. According to Rambam and those who classify negligence as direct damage, an ordinary "no responsibility" clause may still not exempt negligence unless the language clearly waives even direct-damage liability. The matter requires study.
