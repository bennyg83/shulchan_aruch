He is exempt even from negligence. Mordechai derives that language excluding "responsibility" includes even negligence. Maharam proves this from one who vows an offering on condition that he bring it in the temple of Onias. The Gemara treats it as if he said, "I undertake an offering on condition that I bear no responsibility for it," showing that general language of no responsibility includes negligence. Tumim infers that if the recipient actively destroys the object he may also be exempt, because the original gift can retroactively fail and he then damaged his own property. I do not accept this. Active damage is not a form of custodial responsibility. The rule in Bava Kamma already distinguishes, "to guard and not to destroy." Where an object was received for safeguarding and the owner later says, "Tear it," special language of exemption may be needed, but where the arrangement from the outset says no responsibility, that governs custodial liabilities, not an independent tort committed by hand. The offering case is different. One who says "It is upon me" normally accepts every form of replacement responsibility. His condition removes all that responsibility, including negligence. If he then actively slaughters outside the proper place, liability as an ordinary damager of consecrated property may independently be absent, because Torah tort law exempts damage to consecrated property. Therefore the Gemara's result does not prove that active damage falls within the word "responsibility." Accordingly, one who receives a gift on condition that he bear no responsibility remains liable if he intentionally destroys it by hand. The condition exempts custodial responsibility, not ordinary tort liability.
