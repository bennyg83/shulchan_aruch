And he is exempt even from negligence. Tumim asks that Maharam derived this rule from Menachot 109, where one says, "I undertake an olah on condition that I offer it in the house of Chonyo." The Gemara asks, "Has he not killed it?" and answers that the statement is treated as though he said, "on condition that I not be liable for its responsibility." Tumim infers that even active destruction can be exempted by such a condition and therefore develops a novel rule: if one gives a gift "on condition that I not be liable for responsibility," he would be exempt even if he actively damages it. This cannot be correct. In Menachot the animal remains subject to the neder, as shown by liability for slaughtering an offering outside the Temple. Yet the comparison does not prove that a recipient of a gift may destroy another's property. In the korban case, if one says, "This animal is an olah," and designates that specific animal, even if he himself kills it there is no owner to whom he owes compensation because he is himself the owner who consecrated it. Only when he says, "I undertake an olah," does he assume a separate obligation of responsibility to replace it. A condition that he will not be responsible means that his words are treated like designation of "this" animal rather than a personal undertaking, and he is therefore exempt even if it is killed. A gift is different. The item belongs to the recipient, and exemption from custodial responsibility does not imply permission to damage the recipient's property. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise reaches this conclusion.
