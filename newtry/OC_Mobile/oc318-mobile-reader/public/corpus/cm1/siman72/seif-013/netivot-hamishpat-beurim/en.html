There were witnesses that it was stolen and that it was worth more. See Shach, seif katan 53, who writes that if there are no witnesses that the collateral was worth more than the debt, the creditor is exempt from swearing that he was not negligent because he has a migo that he could have said it was not worth more, and this is not considered an audacious migo, as explained in simanim 75 and 93. Shach adds that he is believed through a migo that he could have said, "I do not know." These words are extremely difficult, as Tumim observes. An audacious migo is not used to exempt from an oath according to any opinion, for otherwise every custodian would possess such a migo and the Torah's custodial oaths would disappear. The second migo, "I do not know," is also difficult. In seif 12, where one says the collateral was worth two and the other says five, a d'oraisa oath is imposed, although the defendant could likewise say he does not know. We should not attribute an obvious error to Shach. His meaning can be understood as follows. Where witnesses establish that the object was stolen, and the custodian says, "I never became a custodian. I merely told you to leave it before me," while the depositor says, "You accepted custody and must therefore take the custodial oath that you were not negligent," it appears that the custodian is exempt even from heset. The dispute concerns an obligation to take an oath rather than a definite monetary debt, because everyone, including beit din, entertains the possibility that the loss was unavoidable. Indeed, we make a claim of unavoidable loss for orphan custodians. Only where the living custodian says he does not know whether it was unavoidable do we apply "one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay." Thus denial of the custodial relationship in this situation is comparable to denying a partnership in siman 96, a denial of an oath obligation. Siman 296, where one who says "I never became a custodian" takes heset, concerns a case without witnesses of theft. There the depositor says, "I deposited it with you and you must return it," and the defendant's denial is effectively a denial of the deposit. His additional statement that it was stolen supplies a second defense. If he admitted it remained in his possession he would owe money, so the denial concerns money and there is reason to suspect the item remains with him, as Sema writes in siman 294. But where witnesses establish the theft, the dispute over custodial status is a dispute over whether an oath is owed. Accordingly, if a person admits that he became custodian of one cow that died through negligence, while as to a second cow which witnesses saw die he says he never became its custodian and was not negligent, there is no d'oraisa partial admission oath. Even if he admitted the entire asserted custodial relationship concerning the second cow, the immediate consequence would still be an oath about negligence rather than a certain monetary debt. The Torah imposes an oath for partial admission only where admission of what is denied would itself establish definite monetary liability. This explains Shach's first migo. He combines the claim that the collateral was not worth more with the claim that he was not negligent. In that setting even an audacious migo can exempt from an oath because he is not brazenly denying money. He is already exempt from money by his claim of no negligence and is only denying the basis for an additional oath, as Shach explains in siman 93, seif katan 4. His second answer is also intelligible. A person normally ascertains at the time of a loan that the collateral is worth more than the debt so that his money is secure. Therefore in seif 12 he has no good migo to say he does not know whether it was worth more at all, because that would be audacious. If he says, "I know it was worth more, but I do not know how much," then he is one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, and must pay. Here, however, because he also asserts that he was not negligent, he has a migo to say, "It was worth more but I do not know how much," together with the claim of no negligence. Since, as explained, there is no underlying d'oraisa oath concerning money, the rule of inability to swear does not apply in the same way. This appears to be Shach's intent. Nevertheless, the principal halachah appears to follow Sema that even in this situation a d'oraisa oath is required, and therefore there is no effective migo. Shach seif katan 58 is also printed at the wrong location. It should be attached to the Mechaber's words in seif 13, "he swears that it is not in his possession," and Shach there refers back to seif katan 55, explaining that he swears like an ordinary unpaid custodian.
