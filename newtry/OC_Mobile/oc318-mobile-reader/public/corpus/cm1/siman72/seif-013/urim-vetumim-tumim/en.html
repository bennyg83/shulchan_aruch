Sema explains that even if witnesses do not know the collateral's value and only the lender himself admits it was worth more, he still must take the formal custodian's oath that he was not negligent. He cannot escape through a migo that he could have denied the excess value altogether, because that would be a brazen migo. Shach objects that we do sometimes use a brazen migo and therefore limits the formal oath to a case where witnesses establish the value. Without witnesses, he says, the lender takes only hesset on negligence through his migo that the collateral was not worth more. This is very difficult. The Gemara itself asks how the Torah's custodian oath can ever operate. The custodian always has a migo that he could deny receiving the deposit. The Gemara gives special explanations. Ri Migash says a migo does not exempt from an oath. Tosafot and Rosh say the alternative denial is brazen, and even where such a migo may help for money, it does not remove the Torah oath. Whichever explanation is adopted, the entire institution of the custodian oath proves that an ordinary denial migo does not eliminate it. Shach's approach would substantially erase that rule. He further says the lender could claim, "I do not know whether the collateral was worth more." Ordinarily a person is reluctant to make an "I do not know" claim when he should know, but perhaps collateral value is something he genuinely may not know. That too is difficult. The Mishnah in Shevuot says the borrower claims, "You lent me a sela and the collateral was worth two," while the lender says it was worth only five dinars, and the lender swears. Why does the lender not have a migo that he could say, "I do not know its exact value"? If Shach is right that a lender is not expected to know collateral value, the ordinary Tosafot answer that people avoid an "I do not know" claim should not apply. This is actually evidence for Shach's separate proposition that a lender is expected to pay attention to the collateral's value. Once he is expected to know, the "I do not know" alternative is itself a weak or brazen claim and does not create a usable migo. Rashi's responsum discussed above can be read differently because Rashi often holds generally that a migo does not exempt from an oath, so he need not decide whether the lender was expected to know the value. For practical halachah, however, the main point is clear: a normal migo does not remove the formal custodian's oath. Sema's ruling is therefore preferable.
