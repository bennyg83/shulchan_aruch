The collateral was burned. Tumim says this follows Rambam and the ruling in siman 92 that a suspected person who claims a deposit was lost through unavoidable accident is exempt. According to other authorities who would make such a person pay because he cannot swear that he was not negligent, a gentile creditor here might theoretically be liable under Torah law, even if local gentile law prevents collection from him. Then Shimon, who borrowed from the gentile, would remain indebted to him and that debt could be subject to Rabbi Natan's lien in favor of Reuven. This is not correct. Since the collateral was lost, under our unresolved law following Rabbah's view the lender is treated as having agreed that loss of the collateral means loss of the money. Therefore Shimon is legally discharged from the gentile creditor. Why should Shimon become liable to Reuven merely because Shimon happens to profit from the result? Reuven has no claim to Shimon's gain. There is also a more basic reason. Just as a Jew who accepts custody from a gentile is exempt from the Torah custodial laws because Scripture says "his fellow," so too the reverse relationship should not create Torah custodial oaths and liabilities. Although damages involving gentiles can differ because of separate enactments, we do not find such a special rule in the laws of custodians. Therefore even actual negligence by the gentile custodian need not create Torah custodial liability to the Jew. This also resolves Beit Yosef's question in siman 28: just as Jews do not swear Torah custodial oaths on gentile claims, because the verse says "his fellow," gentiles likewise do not incur those Torah oaths on Jewish claims.
