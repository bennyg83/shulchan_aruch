And the collateral was burned. Tumim writes that since the non-Jew is required to swear, and being disqualified from an oath must pay, the second Jew has a lien upon that liability through Rabbi Natan's rule. Ketzot HaChoshen challenges him, arguing that the second Jew is exempt because it is as though he expressly stipulated, "If it is lost, I lose my money," etc. This is not correct. There remains a concern that perhaps the collateral is still in the non-Jew's possession, in which case the second Jew is not exempt from his debt at all. Shimon remains liable to the non-Jew even if Reuven waives his collateral claim against the non-Jew, because Shimon's stipulation related only to a case where the non-Jew would fail to return Reuven's collateral. Moreover, if the non-Jew was in fact negligent he is liable to pay under our law, and because of his debt his testimony or oath against the Jew is disqualified. Were it not for his own debt, the non-Jew would have been obligated under our law to pay. If, because of the money he lent to the second Jew, the non-Jew is now freed from that liability under our law, he is effectively paying his own debt with another person's property, because he discharges himself from liability to the non-Jew by means of the non-Jew's wrongful retention of Reuven's collateral. Ketzot's further statement that the verse "He saw and released" applies only to damages is certainly incorrect. Wherever a Jew would be liable toward another Jew, a non-Jew is likewise liable by virtue of that verse, and in disputes between a Jew and a non-Jew we say that this is our law. Moreover, it remains possible that the collateral is still in the non-Jew's possession and he simply intends to appropriate it, as I wrote above.
