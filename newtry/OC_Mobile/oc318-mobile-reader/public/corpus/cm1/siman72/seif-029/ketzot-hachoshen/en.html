And the borrower makes a certain claim. The Tur says the borrower swears hesset and is exempt through a migo. The Sema and Shach discuss whether he is believed if no migo exists. In my view this depends upon the dispute between Rabbah and Rav Yosef. According to Rav Yosef, the lender is a true paid custodian and the two obligations remain separate: the lender collects the loan and the borrower separately collects compensation for the lost pledge. A counterclaim is not a defense without migo. If no migo exists, the lender swears and collects. According to Rabbah, the lender's liability to the amount of the debt rests on the implied condition that if the pledge is stolen or lost, the debt itself is lost. The borrower therefore says directly that the condition canceling the debt was fulfilled. This is a substantive repayment defense and does not need a migo. It differs from a claim of waiver, which is unusual and normally needs a migo because debts stand for repayment, not waiver. Here everyone knows the parties entered a pledge arrangement containing the relevant loss condition. Since our practical law remains doubtful between Rabbah and Rav Yosef, if the borrower makes a certain claim that the pledge was stolen or lost, we cannot extract from him where Rabbah would believe him even without migo. If the borrower is uncertain and the lender says certainly that the pledge was lost through unavoidable accident, the borrower is effectively saying, "I do not know whether I repaid you," and remains liable. Even authorities who sometimes exempt where a person had no reason to know do not necessarily help here, because the implied pledge condition was not reasonably intended to make the creditor lose his debt whenever the borrower himself was uncertain. Otherwise the creditor would lose even in genuine accidents simply because the borrower can never know what occurred. Therefore, if the borrower dies and the lender claims an unavoidable accident while the heirs say only that perhaps the pledge was stolen or lost, the heirs are not believed merely because heirs usually receive advanced claims. Where the loan is established by witnesses or a document and the borrower himself would have lacked a migo, the heirs likewise cannot defeat the lender on a mere uncertainty. Under Rav Yosef the two claims are separate and require an actual defense; under Rama's implied condition theory, the creditor did not accept the risk of the borrower's mere uncertainty, only a known loss or a certain claim that the condition occurred.
