I know this definitely. If the borrower did not make a definite claim, the lender would swear that the pledge was lost through an unavoidable accident and would collect his debt, as explained above in seif 14. See what I wrote there.
The borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. He is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "I owe you nothing," or, "I repaid you." These are the Tur's words. It follows that where the borrower has no such migo, for example where the loan is evidenced by a document, or where the lender advanced the money before witnesses and stipulated that the borrower could repay only before witnesses, or in a similar case, the lender swears and collects his debt. This is contrary to the Sema above in seif 2, seif katan 13, who explains that if the borrower has no migo he must take a stringent oath. That cannot be correct. If he has no migo, why should he be exempt from payment? Moreover, if he were exempt from payment, why would he take a stringent oath? We rule that a stringent oath is imposed only upon those whom the Torah requires to swear and who, by swearing, avoid payment, such as one who admits part of a claim, one confronted by a single witness, or one taking a custodian's oath, and similar cases, or upon those who swear and collect. Here, however, the borrower is not collecting and is not among those upon whom the Torah imposes an oath. What place is there for a stringent oath? Rather, the law is certainly as I have explained: the purpose of the ruling is to teach that where the borrower has no migo, the lender swears and collects. The Bach likewise appears to agree with this. It is also explicit in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, end of part 2, which is the source of this law. He writes in the name of Rabbi Yehudah: if a lender says that the pledge was lost from him through an unavoidable accident and the borrower says, "No, you sold it and received its proceeds," one opinion says that the lender swears and collects, while another says that the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt, because he is believed by a migo that he could have said, "I owe you nothing," or, "I repaid you." Since he could have made one of those claims, he is believed when he says that the pledge was not lost, although he must take a hesset oath. This plainly means that it is specifically because of the migo that he is exempt. Where he has no migo, even the latter opinion agrees that the lender swears and collects his debt.
