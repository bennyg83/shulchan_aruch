But when the borrower swears how much it was worth, etc. Gidulei Terumah, folio 274 at the end of column 3, asks that if an oath is needed in order to determine how much the object was worth, then the object cannot be one whose value is fixed and which is commonly available in the marketplace. If it is an object with a known market value, the dispute should disappear, for one can simply go and see its market price. This is no difficulty. It is possible that whether the lender's description is correct or the borrower's description is correct, such objects are available in the market. For example, the borrower says that the pledge was worth two sela'im, while the lender says it was worth only five dinars. One can buy an object like the lender describes for five dinars, while a finer object of the same type can be bought for two sela'im. Even so, we are concerned that if the lender later produces the pledge, the borrower will be forced to admit that it is indeed his own object and that it was worth only five dinars, for example where everyone recognizes it as belonging to the borrower, or the borrower's name is written on it, or the like. Therefore the lender must swear that it is not in his possession. Other examples can likewise be constructed. This is straightforward.
How much it was worth, in order to collect, etc. The Sema writes that one can find a case where the borrower swears and collects as follows. The borrower says, "You lent me one sela upon it, and it was worth two sela'im." The lender says, "It was worth more than the debt, but I do not know how much more," and there is one witness who says that it was worth only five dinars. Without the single witness the borrower would collect without an oath, as explained above in seif 12. Because of the witness, the borrower swears against the witness and collects one sela from the lender. The Sema adds that the Mechaber gives another case of one who swears and collects in siman 73 at the end of seif 15. It also appears that such a case exists where the lender reverses the oath onto the borrower. The Bach writes this as well, contrary to Gidulei Terumah, folio 274, column 4, who leaves unresolved how one could find a case in which the borrower swears in order to collect. This latter explanation is more precise, because according to the Sema's explanation the wording of Baal HaTerumot and of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch, "even if he wishes to pay," is difficult, since in the Sema's case the lender is compelled to pay whether or not he wishes. Nevertheless, as a matter of law the Sema's case is also correct, and it is explicit in Responsa Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in his twenty fourth additional note. This proves that even where a person is obligated to take an oath and cannot swear, the existence of a single witness who supports him does not exempt him from that oath, and he must still pay. This also appears from the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia in the discussion beginning "and some ask, but," and Terumat HaDeshen, end of siman 334, writes likewise. This is contrary to what appears at first glance from the Beit Yosef above in siman 28, seif 2. Here, however, the borrower must swear against the witness because he makes a definite claim. As for the Sema's reference to another case in siman 73, end of seif 15, that follows the Sema's own interpretation there. According to what I write there in seif katan 43, there is no separate case at all; it is the same case as here. See there.
And even if the borrower believes the lender that it was lost and is not in his possession. The Bach disagrees with this based upon Tosafot. In my humble view, however, Tosafot themselves agree to this according to the final position of Rav Ashi. Tosafot say only that Rav Ashi and the other Amoraim did not wish to explain the opening clause as speaking where the borrower believes the lender, according to Shmuel's approach of removing the lender's oath and placing it upon the borrower, because ordinarily the borrower will not wish to trust the lender when doing so will leave him unable to swear. But according to the conclusion, where Rav Ashi does not accept Shmuel's approach and explains the Mishnah as "this one swears and this one swears," it follows that even if the borrower believes the lender he must still swear that the pledge is not in his possession. Rav Ashi does not accept the transfer of the lender's oath to the borrower. The opening clause can therefore also speak where the borrower believes him, just like the latter clause. This is clear in my humble view, contrary to the Bach. See carefully.
And the Divine Name will thereby be profaned. Alternatively, the borrower's oath would turn out to have been taken in vain, as Rabbeinu Chananel explains, rather than Rashi's explanation that the borrower would become disqualified from testimony and from taking an oath. See what I write below in siman 87, seif 33. According to Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation, the lender can say, "I will not swear that it is not in my possession, since such an object is commonly available in the market; instead, you should accept an unspecified cherem concerning how much it was worth." This would not follow according to Rashi, because one who violates a cherem is also disqualified from testimony and from taking an oath, as explained above in siman 34 and below in siman 92. The reason stated at the end of siman 75 likewise supports Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation. See there. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 24, writes that Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation is more reasonable: if the lender later produces the pledge, the borrower's oath will be shown to have been in vain, and in this way the Divine Name is profaned. Accordingly, the Rif and the Mechaber's words that "the Divine Name is profaned" may likewise be understood this way, contrary to the Bach. See there.
