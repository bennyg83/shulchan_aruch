In order to collect. See Baer Heitev through its citation of the Shach that another case arises where the creditor reverses the oath onto the debtor, as the Bach also writes. Baer Heitev says that this proves the stated rule, but the wording is imprecise: according to the Shach's illustration that proof does not follow, while according to the Sema's illustration it does. See the Shach inside, and Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 87, seif katan 9. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9, concerning a loan on collateral where creditor and debtor disputed the value of the pledge. The oath as to value would ordinarily fall upon the creditor because he is defendant regarding the excess, but he reversed the oath onto the debtor. Must the creditor nevertheless swear that the pledge is not in his possession, even when items of that kind are readily available in the market? At first glance the matter appears explicit in siman 72, seif 15, and Shach, seif katan 61. Yet it is difficult why the Sema chose a remote factual illustration and thereby made the Beit Yosef appear to retract what he had written in siman 28, that even one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so is exempted by a single supporting witness. The simple explanation would have been the reversal of the oath. It is even more difficult that Gidulei Terumah, cited by the Shach, did not say so. He therefore explains that the wording of Baal HaTerumot, the source of the rule, troubled both the Sema and Gidulei Terumah. Baal HaTerumot writes that this applies specifically "where the oath comes upon the creditor," but when the oath comes upon the debtor, the creditor can never escape the oath that the pledge is not in his possession. That phrasing indicates where the essential oath lies from the outset, on creditor or debtor, not where it was transferred only by reversal. He develops the distinction at length and concludes that in the case before him the possessor may at least plead kim li. Ordinarily one cannot plead kim li where one authority expressly rules one way and another does not clearly disagree. Here too, the Shach's answer expressly requires the creditor to swear that the pledge is not in his possession, while the Sema merely gives another answer and does not expressly state the opposite. Nevertheless, because the Sema deliberately chose a remote illustration, it appears that he did not accept the Shach's rule in practice. A decisor who rules this way initially should not be criticized, especially because Baal HaTerumot's wording supports it and the reasoning is sound. See there. Baer Heitev further says that one who is obligated to swear but cannot, and has a single supporting witness, is nevertheless not exempted. See the Shach inside, who concludes that this is contrary to what appears from the Beit Yosef above, siman 28, seif 2. Dagul MeRevavah responds that there is no contradiction. In a case of "fifty I know and fifty I do not know," if a supporting witness testifies that only fifty are owed, there is no oath at all and the party was never in the category of one obligated to swear but unable to do so. The same applies to one disqualified from swearing who admits part of the claim and has a supporting witness. Here as well, had the creditor admitted with certainty that the pledge was worth five dinarim and said that he did not know about the rest, while a supporting witness testified that it was worth only five, he would be exempt. In the present case, however, the creditor did not admit even one dinar, only a perutah, while the witness necessarily obligates him in the additional dinar. Therefore he must pay even the balance. The governing rule is that one witness can exempt from an oath, but not from money. Once monetary liability has arisen through inability to swear, the witness cannot remove it. Although the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia may initially appear otherwise, his words can be reconciled with this rule. Dagul MeRevavah concludes that the matter still requires study.
