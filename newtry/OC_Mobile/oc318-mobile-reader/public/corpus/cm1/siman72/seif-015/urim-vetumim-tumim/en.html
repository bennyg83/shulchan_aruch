Where every item of its kind has the same value, and so forth. See Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah in Shevuot for the basis of this distinction. Tiferet Shmuel asks from Bava Metzia 34. Rav Huna says a custodian who claims the deposit is no longer in his possession must swear that it is not presently in his possession, because perhaps he coveted the object. The Gemara challenges him from the collateral Mishnah, where Chazal transferred the valuation oath from the borrower to the lender lest the borrower swear and the lender later produce the collateral. Why is a separate oath that the collateral is not in the lender's possession necessary? The Gemara initially answers that witnesses saw the object burned or stolen. Why not simply establish the Mishnah with a type of collateral readily available in the market? Rambam says that in such a case there is no special suspicion that the lender coveted this particular object. Ritva already asks this and gives two answers. First, the Mishnah does not sound limited to such a special standardized commodity. Second, if every item of the type is readily available at a fixed price, how can the parties dispute its value at all? Both answers need explanation. The first is not fully satisfying because the Gemara's own answer, that witnesses saw the object burned or stolen, is at least as special and is nowhere stated in the Mishnah. Perhaps Ritva means that the Mishnah may indeed include standardized collateral, but then the Gemara would have had to divide the rule: where the kind is readily available, the lender need not swear nonpossession; where it is not, he does. Since the Mishnah states one general oath allocation, the Gemara seeks an explanation covering the full rule. Witnesses of destruction can occur even with nonstandard goods, so the Mishnah need not be drafted around a market-category distinction. This may itself be one source for Rambam's rule that standardized readily purchasable goods do not generate the "perhaps he coveted it" oath. There is another reason the Amoraim may have preferred the witness answer. According to Rabbah, who elsewhere allows a migo even to extract money, if the lender says the collateral was worth only a shekel while the borrower says it equaled the full sela debt, the lender might seem to have a migo that it was lost by unavoidable accident, which would let him collect the entire loan and avoid collateral liability. To prevent such a migo, one may need witnesses establishing the manner of the loss. Thus Rabbah already requires an evidentiary setting of witnessed loss independently of the nonpossession oath. Even R. Yosef can have parallel reasons in the later clauses because R. Yochanan also accepts the transferred-oath structure and other migo questions arise. Ritva's second answer, that known market goods leave no room for valuation dispute, also needs refinement. Shach points out that there can be two common sizes or grades, both readily obtainable. The parties disagree whether the particular collateral was the large or small version, while each version has a known market price. That seems to answer Ritva. But the point of "readily available" must be understood correctly. If the borrower says the lender is hiding a large item worth two sela and the lender says it was a smaller item worth one, the borrower still has a legitimate concern that the lender specifically coveted the valuable large item. The fact that large items exist in the market does not mean one can buy a two-sela item for one sela. The suspicion of coveting is reduced only when an equivalent substitute can actually be obtained at the same value the lender is paying. If the parties disagree about which market category the object belonged to, the very dispute recreates the financial incentive to hide it. Therefore the standardized-market exception does not necessarily resolve the Mishnah's valuation dispute. This explains why the Gemara could not simply answer with "its kind was readily available." There is another important feature. Why did Chazal institute the nonpossession oath at all when the lender is already paying full market value? Even if a person can become attached to a specific object despite readily available substitutes, Chazal generally do not legislate an oath merely for unusual personal obsession. Rambam's distinction is therefore best understood from the borrower's side as well. When the market contains an equivalent object at a known price and the lender pays that price, the borrower suffers no meaningful economic loss from substitution. Chazal did not create a special oath merely to protect idiosyncratic preference for the exact physical item. Where no equivalent is readily available, the borrower has a legitimate nonmonetary and practical interest in his particular property. Then the fear that the lender is withholding it justifies the oath. Now consider the valuation dispute. If Chazal transfer the oath from borrower to lender because of concern that the lender may later produce the collateral and embarrass the borrower's oath, the borrower has an especially serious interest. Without the transferred oath he might have been believed and retained money; because of the concern about the lender's hidden object, the law now makes him pay. That is a far greater practical injury than simply receiving full market replacement value. Therefore even with a generally available item, where the valuation dispute itself affects who must pay money, the borrower has a legitimate reason to insist on the nonpossession structure. This is similar to Tosafot's observation that the transferred oath protects against a concrete adverse consequence, not merely sentimental attachment. Accordingly, Rambam's standardized-market exception and the Gemara's collateral-oath sugya are consistent. The exception applies where equivalent replacement at the acknowledged value truly leaves the owner economically whole and there is no independent monetary consequence from possible concealment. Where the parties dispute the category, size, quality, or value in a manner that affects the judgment, the concern can return and the ordinary oath structure remains.
