The possessor. The Sema explains that anyone who lends upon a pledge worth more than the debt presumably intended from the outset that if it were not redeemed, he would retain it for the amount of the loan because the object was precious to him. This is the case here. Just as possession enables him to claim against the item up to its value, he is believed to say that this was his original intent. The Shach writes that this reasoning is not correct. According to it, if he does not claim that he originally intended this, and all the more if he only now seizes the item for an unrelated debt and did not originally lend upon it, the owner of the utensil should prevail. That is not the law. Baal HaTerumot proves the contrary from the case of goats that ate peeled barley, where the possessor can say, 'I will take the goats for one hundred.' Rather, the reason is that since he could have claimed that he purchased the utensil and retained it, he is also believed to say that the owner owes him more than its value, and he need not return it until paid the amount he claims. See below siman 115, end of seif 1. It nevertheless appears that the possessor must swear while holding a sacred object. These are the words of the Shach. The Taz writes that this concerns an item requiring expert appraisal, as indicated by the phrase 'appraisal of experts.' But with an item whose value is known and readily available to everyone, one cannot claim more than its value. See there.
The burden of proof. The Shach writes that this applies specifically where the lender has a migo; then the law is with him. When the Rama says that the burden of proof rests on the claimant, he means that because there is no decisive resolution between the two views, the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to extract. A practical consequence is that if the borrower later seizes the utensil back, even in the presence of witnesses, then although he must pay its value because he seized it with witnesses, he may nevertheless retain the utensil. The Torat Chaim sought to prove from the Talmudic discussion of a creditor who is not owed the full amount of the land and its improvement, where the buyer is paid money and removed, that even when the lender is not in possession of the pledge the borrower cannot remove him for less than the amount he claimed upon it. The Shach rejects the proof. There, the debtor lacks money and the land is worth the entire debt, as explained in siman 115, seif 6. Moreover, here the alleged debt itself is uncertain. Consider this carefully. These are his words.
