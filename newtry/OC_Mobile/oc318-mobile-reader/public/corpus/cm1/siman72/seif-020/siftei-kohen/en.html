"If he does not live in the tongue, but the half is." It must be and is in the husband of the donations, and according to the fact that we want to say no half and no less is:
Some say that the possessor prevails. The Giddulei Terumah writes that where an ordinary borrower admits the full amount of the loan, but the lender refuses to return the article in his possession until the entire debt is paid, the Tur would not permit the lender to say, “To me it is worth the whole debt.” Rather, the borrower may redeem it at its market value and the balance remains an ordinary debt. This is not correct. All the more so, the Tur maintains that the creditor may say, “It is worth my entire debt to me, and I will retain it until you pay me.” Indeed, it appears that even the Baal HaTerumot agrees, as his language indicates. See also what I wrote below in siman 115 at the end of seif 1.
The possessor prevails. The Sema explains that one who lends more than the pledge's value presumably intends from the outset that, if it is not redeemed, he may retain it for the full loan because it is especially valuable to him. He therefore says that this was his original intention. In my view this reason is incorrect. According to it, if the possessor does not expressly make that claim, and certainly if he seized the article only later for an existing debt, the owner should prevail. Yet the Baal HaTerumot, citing the Raavad, indicates otherwise. The true basis is the rule governing a person who possesses another's article and has a credible claim supported by a migo. Since he could have asserted a claim to the article itself, he may retain it until the amount he claims is paid, even though its objective value is lower. His power does not arise from a presumed special affection for the article, but from the strength of his possession and claim.
The possessor prevails. Nevertheless, it appears that the possessor must take an oath while holding a sacred object. This is clear according to the Rambam and those who agree with him, who maintain that a migo does not exempt a person from an oath. It is also true according to the Rosh, who explains that one claiming against a pledge must swear because he is not claiming the body of the pledge as his own but seeks to collect money from another's property. Here too he is not claiming the body of the article, for if the debtor pays the amount claimed he must return it. A claim to the article itself exists only where he says it was purchased, given as a gift, or the like.
It appears to me that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. The author of Agudat Ezov understood the Rama to mean that even without a migo the possessor prevails by virtue of the rule that the claimant bears the burden of proof, and that he need take only a rabbinic oath. He therefore found the Rama's words obscure. This is mistaken. The Rama plainly speaks only where the creditor has a migo. He then prevails and must swear while holding a sacred object, as explained above. The Rama's statement that the claimant bears the burden of proof means that, because the authorities disagree and neither view is decisive, property remains with its present possessor. A practical consequence is that if the borrower later retakes the article, even in the presence of witnesses and without any migo, he may keep it upon paying its actual value, while the creditor must prove entitlement to the excess. This accords with the general rule that, in an unresolved monetary dispute, possession determines the result.
