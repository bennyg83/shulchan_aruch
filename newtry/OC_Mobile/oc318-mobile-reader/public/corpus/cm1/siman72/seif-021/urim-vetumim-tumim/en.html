Shach says that if there are no sighting witnesses, even with livestock or other property lacking ordinary chazakah the possessor is believed through a migo of return, just as with items ordinarily lent or rented. Tosafot in Shevuot may sound as though roaming livestock and lent items follow one rule, but the broader sugyot point the other way. In Ketubot 85, a creditor says he seized cattle during the debtor's lifetime. Rav Nachman says that if witnesses establish he seized them only after death, his claim fails. Ran asks why actual post-death seizure witnesses are needed. If witnesses simply saw the cattle in his possession, he should already lose his unsupported debt claim. Ran answers that the case lacks ordinary sighting witnesses, so the possessor would otherwise have a migo of return. But the Gemara itself then invokes Reish Lakish's rule that roaming livestock has no chazakah. If Shach were correct that this rule disappears whenever there are no sighting witnesses, the Gemara's question would be misplaced. The explanation is that property of this class remains presumptively in the original owner's ownership. Tosafot in Bava Metzia say the same. A migo cannot ordinarily extract property from an established original ownership chazakah. The goat case in Bava Kamma likewise supports this. The Gemara asks from the rule that roaming animals have no chazakah and does not answer simply that there were no sighting witnesses. Instead it gives the special answer that goats are normally entrusted to a shepherd. Thus the absence of sighting witnesses does not automatically restore a purchase claim for property whose very nature defeats possessory chazakah. Shach's broad analogy is therefore not correct.
