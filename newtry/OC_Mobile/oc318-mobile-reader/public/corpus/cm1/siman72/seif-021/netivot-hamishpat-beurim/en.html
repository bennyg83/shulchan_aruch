It goes out and grazes on its own. See Shach, seif katan 105, who writes that where there are no witnesses who saw the item in his possession, he is believed by virtue of a migo. Tumim challenges the Shach from Ketubot 85, where the Gemara says: "If you have witnesses that they seized it after death, etc." Ran asks there that once witnesses come and testify that they seized it, the possessor is no longer believed to make a claim against the borrower. Ran answers that if there are witnesses who saw it, that is indeed so; the case there is where there are no such witnesses, and if there are no witnesses that she seized it after death she has a migo that she returned it. Tumim asks that if so, why does the Gemara there challenge from Reish Lakish's rule that roaming livestock have no chazakah, since where there are no witnesses who saw it even Reish Lakish agrees? From this he proves that with roaming livestock, even where there are no witnesses who saw it, one is not believed, because such livestock remain in the chazakah of the original owner and the migo would be a migo used to extract property. In my humble opinion this is incorrect, and there is no proof at all from Ran. Ran's answer is that for this reason one would have to bring proof specifically that they seized it after death, because if one brought proof merely that they seized it, one would also need witnesses who saw it. The Gemara there asks regarding the statement: "And if not, since he could have said, 'I purchased it,' he is believed to say, 'I seized it during his lifetime.'" It cannot be that the case is both without witnesses who saw it and with witnesses to the seizure, because then even a claim of purchase would be believed only through a migo that he returned it, and the Gemara should simply have said, "Since he could have said, 'I returned it.'" Rather, it said a migo of purchase because the questioner did not ask whether there were witnesses who saw it. The answer was therefore: if you have witnesses that he seized it after death, it does not help even if there are no witnesses who saw it; and if not, meaning that you have no witnesses to the seizure at all, then even if there are witnesses who saw it he is nevertheless believed through a migo of purchase, since a claim of purchase is believed even without a migo. The Gemara then properly asks that roaming livestock have no chazakah and one is not believed to claim purchase where he lacks a migo that he returned it. Likewise, Tumim's proof from Bava Batra, where the Gemara challenges the ruling concerning goats that ate peeled barley, that the possessor is believed by a migo that he purchased them, from the rule that roaming livestock have no chazakah, and does not answer that the case is where there are no witnesses who saw them, is also not difficult. The Gemara infers from the fact that it says a migo of purchase rather than a migo of return that it must be discussing a case where there is no migo of return; nevertheless, it says he is believed by a migo of purchase. The challenge is therefore entirely proper.
