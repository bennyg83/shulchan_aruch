The orphans claim that their father instructed them. The Sema infers from the Baal HaTerumot that this is not a definite claim, especially because the case speaks of minor children, who are generally not recipients of a formal instruction. I do not see the proof. The relatives may lack certainty, but the orphans may maintain that their father expressly told them, and when they mature and persist in that definite claim they may swear and collect. This follows from the concluding clause: if they do not establish the instruction, the debtor pays only the pledge's value and takes a rabbinic oath regarding the balance. That distinction presupposes that a definite claim by the heirs can support their recovery.
Until the orphans mature and swear that their father did not instruct them otherwise. Although one who was a minor when his father died ordinarily need not swear that his father gave him no contrary instruction, because a minor is not normally capable of receiving such an instruction, the case here concerns perceptive minors who were capable of understanding it. So writes the Bach. See what I wrote below in siman 108.
They take the heirs' oath. Although a person who merely retains property already in his possession generally need not swear, here the heirs seek payment from the pledge and are therefore regarded as taking, not merely retaining. This is consistent with the rule explained above. The responsum of the Rosh stating that heirs may retain a deposit without an oath concerns a deposit consisting of money, where the possession itself supports the claim. That case is distinguishable.
"We did not command a father. “And it is not for my father’s words that this is not the case of my father, that I shall swear to him, and that he shall not be cursed, and that he shall not swear to me, and that he shall be blessed with his father’s hand, and that he shall not be given to me.”
They collect the fifty. The Baal HaTerumot records that the borrower demanded that the relatives swear that the deceased had instructed them that one hundred was due. Rabbi Yitzchak ruled that neither an oath nor a general ban is imposed upon them, because an administrator for minors must advance every possible claim for their benefit. This does not contradict the later rules. Here the relatives may say that even if the deceased did not instruct them personally, the debt may still be genuine, and they are not asserting a fact within their own knowledge. A general ban is also inappropriate where no one is charged with concealing known information. The orphans themselves, when they later seek affirmative collection based on a definite inherited claim, remain subject to the heirs' oath.
