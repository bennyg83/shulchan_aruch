And the orphans claim that their father instructed them. See Shach, seif katan 143, who writes that if they do not make a definite claim, the law favors the borrower, etc., and he leaves the matter requiring study. Tumim wonders that if so, whenever one dies leaving collateral, the borrowers would need to pay only one perutah. In my humble opinion, according to what I wrote above in seif katan 30, the lender acquires the collateral fully for the limited purpose that he need not return it until his debt is paid. Therefore, when there is a denial regarding that right he need take only a shevuat heset, as the Shach writes in seif katan 71. It appears that the same distinction applies here. If the orphans wish to collect their debt from the collateral, so that they must first assert a claim against the borrower personally, this is certainly an attempt to extract property, and beit din does not advance such a claim on their behalf. But if they seek only to retain the collateral, this is considered maintaining possession, and beit din does advance the claim for them. This seems clear. There is a decisive proof, because otherwise the Shach would contradict what he himself wrote in siman 65, seif katan 13: "If they are written in a manner that permits deduction, even if we know that they did not belong to their father, we claim on their behalf that he acquired them as collateral." See there. This implies even up to their full value. The distinction must therefore be as I have written. In siman 65 the Mechaber discusses a note that the holder is not seizing in order to collect it, as explained in siman 64. The Shach therefore properly writes that if the instrument were payable to whoever presents it and their father held it only as collateral, in circumstances where the borrower would be the claimant against the lender, we advance the claim for the orphans because they are maintaining possession. Here in siman 72, however, the lender is the claimant against the borrower and seeks to collect from the collateral. The Shach therefore properly writes that we do not advance the claim for the orphans, because they are attempting to extract property.
Our father did not instruct us. See Shach, seif katan 146, through his statement that they need not take this oath unless the borrower says, "Swear to me," etc. This must refer to a case where the borrower claims, "I repaid." If instead he claims that he never borrowed more than the stated amount, then the rule that one who says, "I never borrowed," is regarded as saying, "I never repaid," applies, and no oath at all is required. This also resolves Tumim's question that with collateral an oath is required even if the borrower did not say, "Swear to me." According to what I wrote above there is no difficulty. The requirement to swear concerning collateral applies only when the parties contradict each other regarding the substance of the loan. But when the borrower claims repayment, Baal HaTerumot, Gate 49, explains that the case is exactly like one who claims repayment against a promissory note. See there. The reason must be that the lender can say, "Why then is your collateral in my possession?" Thus its law is exactly like that of a promissory note. See Shach, seif katan 147, where there is a textual error. It should read "we do not administer an oath to him," and the word printed there as "we do not heed him" is erroneous.
