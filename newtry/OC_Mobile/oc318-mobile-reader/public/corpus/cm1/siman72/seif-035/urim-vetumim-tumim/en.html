And we plead for the orphans, and so forth. Shach says this applies only if the orphans make a definite claim. If they are uncertain, then although we generally plead for heirs whatever their father could have pleaded, we do not plead that the object was collateral for its full value unless they say their father expressly told them so. According to Shach, a lender who dies leaving collateral without instructions would leave the borrower able to recover the object merely by admitting a minimal debt. I do not know the source for this limitation. The general rule is that we plead for heirs every defense their father could have made. This is especially difficult according to Shach himself, who earlier follows Rashba that a creditor acquires collateral strongly enough to be believed up to its value even without an ordinary migo. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, part 3, also indicates like Sema that the heirs need not assert a personal definite instruction. He says disputed money should be held until the orphans mature and take the heir's oath that their father did not instruct them otherwise. If Shach were right, they would instead need to swear that the father actually told them the exact amount owed on the collateral. Baal HaTerumot further says that when relatives claim the father instructed them about the debt, the guardian need not swear because his duty is to plead every defense available for the orphans. That makes sense only if the guardian may raise the legal defense without inventing a factual instruction. If the law required a definite statement, "The father told us exactly this," the guardian obviously could not fabricate it. It would be absurd to call lying on behalf of the orphans his duty. Therefore Sema is correct. Where the heirs are uncertain, beit din can plead the collateral claim their father could have made, subject to the ordinary heir's oath and burden rules. They need not falsely assert that the father explicitly instructed them.
And they shall swear the oath of heirs. Shach refers to siman 108 and says the heirs need swear only if the debtor demands, "Swear to me." That comparison is not exact. In siman 108 the creditor held a formal note. Had the creditor himself been alive and the debtor pleaded payment, the creditor would generally not swear unless the debtor specifically demanded an oath. Therefore the heirs likewise swear the inherited oath only upon demand. Here the claim rests on collateral rather than a formal note. If the original creditor were alive and the parties disputed the amount secured by the collateral, the creditor would collect only with the formal oath required by the Geonim, even without a special demand, because he is a claimant collecting through possession rather than merely enforcing an unquestioned note. The heirs who stand in his place should therefore take the heir's oath even if the debtor does not expressly demand it. The two situations are not parallel.
But if it is an object ordinarily lent, and so forth. Sema says this can apply even without ordinary sighting witnesses. The claimant gives identifying marks for an object he says was lent to the deceased. Beit din requires the heirs to show the object. If the marks correspond and the item is of a type commonly lent or rented, the object can be returned even though the father, had he been alive, might have claimed ownership through a migo. Shach rejects this and follows Maharshal: whatever the father could have pleaded, we plead for the heirs, so ordinary sighting evidence should still be required. Neither formulation is fully precise. The correct rule, as I explain below in siman 297 from Tosafot and Rosh, is that even highly specific identifying marks do not by themselves prove ownership strongly enough to return the object. Rather, the marks create substantial reason to think this is the claimant's object. Beit din can then require the heirs to present it to witnesses. If those witnesses recognize it by direct visual identification as the claimant's property, the ownership proof is complete. Thus Sema is wrong if he means identifying marks alone suffice. But Shach is also too strict if he requires preexisting sighting witnesses. The claimant can supply identifying marks, beit din can order exhibition of the object, and ordinary recognition witnesses can then identify it. This is the practical meaning of the rule. If, however, the heirs themselves make a definite claim that their father told them he purchased or otherwise acquired the object, identifying marks alone do not defeat that claim. Rama below in siman 297 makes this clear. Sema's interpretation fits best where the heirs themselves have no definite instruction. Taz tries to distinguish ordinary lent items from other objects by saying a later sale of a commonly lent item is more plausible. That misses the point. The reason identifying marks matter is not that a later sale is unlikely. It is that it is unlikely another object will coincidentally carry the same distinctive marks. The marks justify requiring exhibition; the witnesses' visual recognition supplies the actual ownership proof.
