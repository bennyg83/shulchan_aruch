And we plead for the orphans that their father instructed, etc. Baal HaTerumot writes this, and the Tur copies it. Baal HaTerumot further writes that the borrower wanted the relatives to swear that the deceased had instructed that one hundred remained in his possession. Ri ruled that no oath and no excommunication is imposed upon them, because an administrator for orphans is obligated to advance every argument and every claim that can be made on their behalf. This is as the Gemara says regarding an administrator: one who appoints an administrator appoints a person who knows how to turn every argument to the orphans' advantage. It is evident from this that the assertion that their father instructed that the debt was still due was not a definite claim, as I wrote regarding the preceding law. This is also clear internally, because the case states that the lender left minor sons, and minors are not capable of receiving a formal deathbed instruction in a manner that makes their assertion a definite claim. This is clear.
But if it is among objects that are normally lent out, etc. In the Derishah I wrote that this applies even if witnesses did not see the object in their father's possession before his death, and even if they did not presently see it in the heirs' possession before the litigation. Nevertheless, the defendant is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have said, 'It was borrowed by their father.' Although their father himself would have been believed to say, 'I returned it to you,' if witnesses had not presently seen it in his possession, as the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 18, the father is different because he makes a definite claim. The heirs do not make a definite claim. Beit din merely advances on their behalf any claim their father could have made, and a claim of this sort is not advanced for them. If this defendant came before beit din and said, 'I lent their father an object bearing such and such identifying marks, and it is still in the heirs' possession,' beit din would compel the heirs to produce it for inspection. If they then saw that it is an object ordinarily lent or rented out and bears the identifying marks stated by the defendant, they would remove it from the heirs and give it to the defendant, even though they could not have compelled their father himself to produce it. The Tur and Mechaber rule similarly below in siman 296, where Reuven comes to beit din and says, 'I have witnesses that I deposited with the father of these orphans an object bearing such and such signs,' etc. See there. Objects ordinarily lent or rented out, even without witnesses to the original loan, have the same rule in the hands of heirs as objects not ordinarily lent out where witnesses establish that they were deposited with their father. This appears correct. Examine carefully.
