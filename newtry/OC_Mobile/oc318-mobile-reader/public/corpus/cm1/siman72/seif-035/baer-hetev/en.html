The Sema cites Baal HaTerumot's case in which the borrower made a particular claim, and infers that when the heirs say their father instructed them concerning the matter, this is not necessarily a definite claim. He further infers this from the fact that the lender left minor children, who are not capable of receiving a deathbed instruction as a definite factual claim. The Shach says he does not know where this is proven. Even if the relatives do not make a definite claim, the orphans themselves, though minors, may possess definite knowledge. When they mature and continue to assert it definitely, they swear and collect. Proof is that the conclusion says that on the remainder the defendant takes a heset oath. That can only be when the heirs make a definite claim, since otherwise no heset would be required. The opening case must therefore also involve a definite claim. If the heirs do not assert it definitely, it appears that the borrower should prevail, because one who lends on a pledge has only a lien upon the borrower, and one cannot extract from another on a doubtful claim, as explained above in seifim 14 and 24. The matter requires study. These are the words of the Shach. The Taz likewise challenges the Sema and writes that this is why the source says the father instructed them, so that their claim is definite, and the defendant takes a heset oath.

To lend. The Sema writes that the case applies even if witnesses did not see the item in the father's possession before his death and do not now see it in the heirs' possession before litigation. Even so, the defendant is believed through a migo that he could have said it was merely lent to the father. Although the father himself would have been believed to say, 'I returned it,' when there were no witnesses who had seen it, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in seif 18, the father is different because he would be making a definite claim. For heirs, beit din advances only claims that their father could have made, and does not advance this type of uncertain claim for them. If this defendant came to beit din and said, 'I lent their father a utensil bearing these identifying signs and it is still in the heirs' possession,' beit din would compel them to produce it. If it is an object ordinarily lent and bears the signs he described, it is given to him, even though he could not have compelled their father to produce it in this manner, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in a similar law in siman 297. The Shach, however, cites Maharshal that the case must specifically be one where witnesses saw the item in the father's possession, and this is the primary ruling. Where it was not seen, we advance for the heirs a claim of purchase or gift even if the heirs do not assert it definitely, even for objects ordinarily lent or rented, or where witnesses testify that the object originally came to their father as a deposit, as the Shach explains at length in siman 297. The Sema himself writes in siman 134 that where there are no witnesses of current possession, property is not removed from a purchaser, evidently because we advance for him any claim the seller could have made, and all the more so for heirs. Accordingly, we could also advance a claim that the item was pledged for such and such an amount. Nevertheless, it appears that where the heirs admit the item is a pledge, we do not advance for them the possibility that it was pledged for a large sum, even if it is not an object ordinarily lent, because a pledge gives only a lien and therefore requires a definite claim. We advance a claim of purchase, however, because it may mean the object itself belongs to them. See responsum R. Aharon Sasson, siman 222, and responsum Maharit, siman 9. The Taz likewise agrees with the Shach that witnesses of current possession are required, and challenges the Sema. He adds that a claim that the item was merely lent can recover it even from heirs, as stated at the end of the chapter HaMekabel, where Rabbah removed property from heirs. See there.
