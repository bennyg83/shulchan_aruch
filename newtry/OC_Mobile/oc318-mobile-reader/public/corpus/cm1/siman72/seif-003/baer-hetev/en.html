Liable. According to the first opinion cited above, which regards the lender as a paid custodian, he is obligated to pay the entire value of the pledge. According to the opinion that regards him as an unpaid custodian, the meaning here is that the lender loses from his debt an amount equal to the value of the pledge. The author of Ir Shoshan was not precise on this point. Sema.
Shimon. Thus states the Mordechai: the woman cannot seize these items for payment of her ketubah, for they are still in the lender's possession. Likewise, they have not become movable property in the hands of the borrower's heirs, and the lender may collect from them. See the Derishah for the opinion of those who disagree with this. These are the words of the Sema. See the Shach, who distinguishes between garments the husband made for her, which he may sell or pledge even against her will, and garments that she brought into the marriage, which he may not sell or pledge without her consent. See Even HaEzer, end of siman 73, in the Beit Yosef. There is room to question the Mordechai's further statement that if the garments still remain in the woman's possession she is believed, with an oath, to say that her husband pledged them without her consent. After all, she would nevertheless be required to return the money because of the market regulation protecting good faith purchasers. I found an answer based on the Shach at the beginning of siman 356, citing a responsum of R. Moshe Alshich, that if the stolen object has come back into the owner's hands he need not observe that market regulation. See there. This resolves our case as well, and it is clear.
Custodian. He loses only to the extent of the money he advanced, but he does not become a paid custodian, even according to Rav Yosef, because no mitzvah is involved. One cannot say that he should nevertheless be a paid custodian because he must air out and shake the article even if he did not lend money upon it. Here, since it was given to him to pledge with a non-Jew, he cannot air it out, because the owner assumes it is in the non-Jew's possession. This resembles the case where the owner is with him in the same city, at the end of siman 292. According to this, if the owner told him that he gave a different pledge to the non-Jew and that his own pledge remains with him, the law requires study. These are the words of the Shach.
Lent to him. The Ran writes in responsum 19 regarding Reuven, who lent a sefer to Shimon, and Shimon left another sefer with Reuven as security, after which both books were taken from them. Later Reuven's book, which had been in Shimon's hands, was returned to Reuven. The Ran ruled that Reuven must return Shimon's pledged book, because Shimon is not liable for the unavoidable loss of Reuven's book that had been in his possession. This is cited by the Sema in Darkei Moshe. In the responsum itself the Ran explains that the lender of the book considered himself to be performing a mitzvah. Therefore all the benefit was not exclusively the borrower's, whereas a borrower is liable for unavoidable loss because all the benefit is his. Here the lender also receives the benefit of Rav Yosef's perutah. See there. The Ran follows his own view that the halachah accords with Rav Yosef. According to the authorities who rule like Rabbah, however, Shimon is a full borrower and is liable even for unavoidable loss. For us, therefore, this is a monetary doubt, and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. Reuven, who is in possession, need not return the pledged book to Shimon. The Sema should not have cited the Ran's ruling as undisputed. These are the words of the Shach.
