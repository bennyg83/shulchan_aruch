The creditor again deposited it. I was asked about a lender who lent his collateral back to the borrower for Yom Tov, and the borrower promised him a gift for this favor. May the lender accept the gift, or is it interest? It appears to be full interest. Tosafot asks how Rabbi Yitzchak can derive that a creditor acquires a pledge from the verse saying that returning it to the poor debtor is "righteousness." If the creditor did not acquire the pledge, Tosafot says, then even receiving a blessing from the poor debtor for returning it would be verbal interest. All the more so would receiving money or a gift for returning it constitute interest. Collateral given at the time of the loan is subject to a Torah level doubt whether Rabbi Yitzchak's ownership applies, and Rama rules stringently concerning kiddushin with such a pledge. Since interest is a Torah prohibition, the lender should not accept the gift. If, however, the collateral was taken after the loan in a manner where Rabbi Yitzchak certainly applies, whether through beit din, with the borrower's consent, or even by force under the broader ruling explained above, then the creditor is returning property in which he has an actual acquired interest. He may receive compensation for temporarily lending his own interest back, because the payment is for use of the pledge rather than for the loan.
Since it was given as a loan. For the same reason the collateral does not become ordinary movable estate in the hands of the borrower's heirs. Hagahot Mordechai cites Maharam that if the creditor redeposited the pledge with the borrower and the borrower died, the object becomes ordinary movables in the heirs' hands, unless the original pledge had been collected through beit din. I find this difficult. In Ketubot, property specifically designated to a wife's ketubah can remain bound even while physically in the husband's house, because she relied upon those objects. Similarly, a pledge that had already been acquired for the creditor should not lose its lien merely because the creditor lent it back temporarily. The mere act of lending it to the borrower should not transform it into free movable estate for the heirs. Therefore, where the pledge was one in which the creditor had a valid acquired interest, it remains subject to that interest even after temporary redeposit with the borrower.
