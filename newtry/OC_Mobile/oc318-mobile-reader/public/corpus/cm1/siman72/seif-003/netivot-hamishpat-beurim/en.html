And then deposited it back with him. Ketzot HaChoshen questions whether a creditor may charge the borrower rent for permitting him to use the borrower's own collateral. He seeks to prove from Tosafot in Kiddushin 8 that even the borrower's blessing of the creditor constitutes verbal ribbit where the creditor does not acquire the collateral, and therefore payment of money would constitute monetary ribbit. He infers that collateral taken after the loan, which the creditor acquires, may be returned for rent. In my view, even there it is forbidden. Yoreh Deah siman 164 states that even a mortgage held with a fixed deduction may not be rented back to the borrower, and this is explicit in the Gemara. All the more so where the creditor holds the collateral without paying rental value. Tosafot's proof is different. Even if one were to say that a verbal ribbit stipulation made at the time of the loan is d'oraisa, a benefit conferred later without stipulation is certainly not d'oraisa. How then can Tosafot derive a d'oraisa rule from the verse that the borrower blesses the creditor when his garment is returned? Their meaning must be that the debt has already become due, and the Torah nevertheless permits the creditor to return the collateral and receive the borrower's blessing. This implies that even if at the time of return he expressly says, "I am returning it so that you will bless me," the arrangement is permitted, as the verse says, "He shall sleep in his garment and bless you." Since the creditor could otherwise collect immediately and instead continues the debt while taking collateral that he must periodically return in exchange for the blessing, the arrangement resembles an express benefit stipulated when extending additional time, which would ordinarily be d'oraisa ribbit. From the Torah's permission the Gemara proves that collateral taken after the loan is acquired by the creditor. Moreover, siman 97, seif 22 and Baal HaTerumot, gate 49, indicate that where the collateral must be returned the creditor cannot permanently sell it and cannot proceed against the debtor in the ordinary way while withholding that required return. Therefore the blessing is not viewed as compensation for extending a collectible debt in the usual manner. But this d'oraisa permission does not establish d'rabbanan permission to rent the collateral back to the borrower. For d'rabbanan purposes, even an acquired mortgage is treated stringently enough that renting it back is forbidden. One cannot infer d'rabbanan permission from the Torah's limited allowance of a blessing.
He becomes its custodian as though he had lent him the money. See Shach, seif katan 27. His words are difficult to understand as written. It appears that his intent is as follows. Maharam Padua holds that Rabbi Yosef's perutah applies when one lends money, but not when one merely lends an object, as stated in his responsum. Ran likewise applies the mitzvah rationale specifically to lending a sefer. Therefore, if Reuven did not pay Shimon any profit for arranging a loan from a non-Jew, we would ordinarily say that since Shimon supplied his own collateral and the non-Jew recognizes only Shimon, Shimon became the borrower from the non-Jew and then lent his own money to Reuven. This is especially so where the non-Jew refused to lend on Reuven's collateral. It is as though Shimon borrowed for himself and then lent to Reuven, so Rabbi Yosef's perutah applies. If, however, Reuven pays Shimon the interest charged by the non-Jew, we cannot say that Shimon borrowed for himself and then lent to Reuven, because in that structure Shimon would be taking interest from Reuven, and we do not presume a Jew to act wickedly. Necessarily Shimon served as Reuven's agent to borrow from the non-Jew and transferred to him, through another person, an interest in Shimon's collateral in a manner permitting the payment of interest. In that agency structure Rabbi Yosef's perutah does not apply, particularly in Maharam Padua's case where Shimon himself also wished to borrow from the non-Jew on his own collateral. Shach therefore speaks of a case where the non-Jew would not lend even Reuven's portion on Reuven's collateral. Nevertheless Rabbi Yosef's perutah still does not apply because Shimon's contribution is more like lending or transferring an object than lending money, all the more so where he acted partly for his own benefit. If, by contrast, the non-Jew was willing to lend Reuven's portion on Reuven's own collateral, then even if Reuven pays no interest, Shimon performs no mitzvah by substituting his own collateral because Reuven did not need that favor. At the end Shach says that if Shimon told Reuven he had given a different pledge to the non-Jew, the meaning appears to be this: if he did not inform Reuven, we see that Reuven never relied upon Shimon to air and care for Reuven's collateral. If he did inform him, Reuven may rely upon Shimon, the Jew, to care for it. Nevertheless Shimon is not thereby a paid custodian because from the outset Reuven did not insist upon such care. In Shach seif katan 28 there is a textual error. The text should read that even if Shimon told him that he gave another pledge to the non-Jew and Reuven's own pledge remains with Shimon, Shimon need not air it, and so forth.
As though he had lent him. See Sema, seif katan 21, citing Ran that one who borrows a sefer and gives another sefer as collateral is not liable for unavoidable loss because the lender of the sefer performs a mitzvah and benefits from Rabbi Yosef's perutah. Tumim objects that siman 342 implies that even one who borrows a sefer is liable as a borrower except where it tears through normal use, and no authority states generally that a borrower of a sefer is exempt. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise objects from Kiddushin, chapter HaIsh Mekadesh, where the Gemara says that even according to the opinion that loaned money remains in the lender's domain for purposes of return, the borrower is nevertheless liable for accidents because it is no less than borrowing, even though making a loan is a mitzvah. These objections initially appear strong. The correct explanation, however, is that an ordinary borrower of a sefer is indeed liable for unavoidable accidents. The act of borrowing is completed when the object is drawn into the borrower's possession. The mitzvah has already been performed and any Rabbi Yosef benefit belongs to the lender himself, not as compensation supplied by the borrower. Ran speaks specifically where the borrower gives collateral. Through the loan the lender receives collateral, and by airing and caring for that collateral he may be exempted from giving a perutah to a poor person. The borrower has therefore supplied him with an item that produces monetary benefit, which is comparable to giving compensation for the loan and can alter the custodial relationship. With an ordinary loan of an object against collateral, Rabbi Yosef's perutah does not arise from airing the collateral because lending an ordinary object is not itself a mitzvah. Lending a sefer is different, as Ran writes. As explained above, Rabbi Yosef's perutah applies only when the obligation of care originated through a mitzvah imposed by the Torah. This is Ran's intent. Nevertheless, one who borrows an object to rescue persons or property from a fire in circumstances treated as life saving is exempt, for Shabbat may be violated for life saving. Similarly one who borrows a utensil for another act of saving life should be exempt, as indicated in Sanhedrin, chapter Zeh Borer, concerning one who pursues a pursuer and breaks property while saving the victim. Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 34, seeks to exempt a borrower of a sefer because mitzvot were not given for physical benefit, comparing it to borrowing merely to look at an item, which siman 346 exempts because borrower liability requires a monetary benefit from the object. He overlooked Taz in Yoreh Deah siman 221, seif katan 43, who writes that Torah study does provide benefit in this context. Moreover, the borrower can teach from the sefer to another person for payment. Even Ketzot would presumably agree that borrowing a non Torah book is monetary benefit because the borrower uses the substance of the object. Torah study for compensation likewise constitutes concrete benefit. Merely looking at an object is not treated as use of the object's substance, which is why even if the viewer later derives some incidental benefit it does not create the same borrower status. A borrower of a sefer may also, within the period of borrowing, permit another person to use it in circumstances where the rule against a borrower lending further does not apply, such as study in the borrower's home where there is no concern that the book will be taken away, or by a member of his household, as explained there.
