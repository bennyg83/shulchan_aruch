Bach says Rabbenu Baruch in Hagahot Mordechai disagrees with the rule that the original lender remains responsible after returning the collateral to the borrower for safekeeping. Shach says that dispute belongs only to the view that rules like Rabbah and Shmuel. If we rule like R. Yosef, the creditor remains a paid custodian and the borrower, holding it as a deposit, is only an unpaid custodian. I discussed above why Maharam may indeed follow Rabbenu Baruch. There is, however, another conceptual difficulty. Suppose the lender has no proprietary acquisition in the collateral and is merely its paid custodian. He returns the object to the borrower, who owns it. The borrower can now use his own object. This use can itself be a benefit resulting from the lender's return, much like the rule that one who receives unrestricted money for safekeeping becomes a paid custodian because he may use it. Why should the borrower not at least become a paid custodian toward the lender's security interest through the benefit of regained use? It is not enough to say, "He is using his own property." The benefit is precisely that the lender released physical control and enabled that use. Had the collateral remained with the lender, the borrower could not enjoy it. Rosh's discussion also points this way. One could otherwise answer Rosh's question regarding collateral seized after the loan by saying R. Yitzchak is needed specifically for the period when the collateral is returned to the borrower. If the creditor's status came only from "holding it against his money," that rationale would disappear during return. Rosh does not frame it that way, suggesting another mechanism remains. Perhaps the correct case is one where the collateral equals only the debt and its use physically depreciates it. Then even though the borrower owns the object, he cannot freely use it because doing so diminishes the creditor's security. If the lender returns it merely as a deposit, unauthorized depreciating use would impair the lender's collateral rights. The borrower therefore does not automatically gain a use benefit sufficient to change custodial status. But if the collateral is worth well above the debt or can be used without any depreciation, the borrower can use his own object without harming the creditor. In such a case, the benefit of restored use is real, and his custodial classification may indeed become stronger. The exact allocation of responsibility therefore depends on the nature and value of the collateral and requires further study.
And also those garments. Sema explains that if the wife herself held the garments and said her husband had pledged them without her authority, she could be believed through a migo that she had returned them. But now that the garments are in the creditor's possession, we presume the husband pledged them with her knowledge and consent. Shach limits this to garments the wife brought into the marriage. Regarding garments the husband purchased for her, Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 73 indicates that the husband can sell or pledge them. But one of Rashba's reasons is takkanat hashuk, the protection afforded to ordinary commercial transactions. If that is the reason, I do not understand why garments bought by the husband differ from garments she brought from her father's house. In either case, if the creditor received ordinary women's garments in a normal transaction, the concern for commercial stability is the same. And if we say a lender should recognize that women's garments may belong to the wife and should therefore be cautious, that applies equally to clothing the husband purchased for her. Who can tell whether a particular garment came from the husband or from her family's property? Accordingly, Rashba's responsum should not be read as an absolute determination. His language is one of uncertainty: perhaps the husband has sufficient authority, perhaps commercial protection applies. Therefore, where the creditor possesses the garments, the wife cannot extract them from him. But where the wife possesses them, the creditor likewise cannot extract them from her. The burden of proof rests on the claimant. I later found that Rashba in his chiddushim to Bava Kamma gives two explanations of the rule that one who consecrates all his property is treated as though he had already transferred his wife's and children's clothing to them. According to one explanation, once the husband purchases clothing for his wife and children, his intent is to confer ownership upon them. On that view he can no longer sell or pledge those garments. According to Rashba's second explanation, which he calls preferable, there is no actual prior transfer of ownership. Rather, a person who consecrates his estate simply does not intend to include items already designated for the ordinary clothing needs of his household. Under that explanation, the husband can still sell or pledge them. This explains why Rashba's responsum remains cautious. Although he preferred the second interpretation, he recorded the first as a substantial possibility, and money cannot be extracted from a possessor against that doubt. Hagahot Rosh in Bava Kamma appears to adopt the first explanation outright, and Beit Yosef below in siman 97 cites Baal HaTerumot in the name of Ramban that the husband immediately grants such clothing to his wife and children and therefore cannot sell it. There is also a dispute among the poskim between ordinary weekday clothing and special Shabbat and Yom Tov garments, as codified below in siman 97, seif 26. The same distinctions apply here.
He becomes a custodian over it. In Responsa Maharam Padua, the source of this ruling, the explanation is that the perutah of R. Yosef does not make Shimon a paid custodian, because no mitzvah of lending is being performed by Shimon. Whether he gives his own collateral to the gentile or passes along Reuven's collateral, he receives his agreed share of the profit or interest arrangement. His custodial liability instead arises from Shmuel's principle that loss of the collateral can affect the corresponding debt, at least up to the amount of the debt. Shach asks: why does Maharam emphasize that Shimon pays interest to the gentile? Even without interest, what mitzvah-benefit would arise merely because Shimon substituted his own collateral for Reuven's? Shach answers that the gentile refused Reuven's collateral and would lend only against Shimon's. Therefore Shimon's provision of his own property enabled Reuven to obtain the money. But if so, the perutah of R. Yosef should apply strongly. What difference is there between directly lending Reuven money and lending him the use of one's own collateral so that a gentile will advance him the money? In both cases Shimon performs a real mitzvah by obtaining credit for Reuven. Maharam's reference to interest has a different purpose. The gentile had no direct dealings with Reuven. Shimon borrowed from the gentile and delivered the money to Reuven. One might therefore say Shimon himself is the true borrower from the gentile and then becomes Reuven's Jewish lender, exactly like an ordinary Jew lending money from his own pocket. On that reading the ordinary mitzvah of lending would indeed apply. Maharam therefore stresses that Shimon pays the gentile interest. If Shimon were Reuven's direct lender, how could Reuven's transaction produce prohibited interest to his Jewish lender? The permitted structure necessarily treats Shimon as Reuven's agent and the gentile as the actual lender. Reuven is the true debtor to the gentile and Shimon merely facilitates the transaction. Once that is clear, substituting collateral does not itself create the ordinary mitzvah status of a Jewish lender. Shach further asks that even if no perutah of R. Yosef existed at the initial loan, once Reuven's collateral remains with Shimon, Shimon must spread and air it. At those times he performs a mitzvah and receives the perutah benefit. Shach answers that Reuven contemplated that the collateral would be placed with a gentile, where it might not be spread and aired, and nevertheless accepted that risk. Therefore Shimon too has no such duty. I do not understand that comparison. Where a depositor remains in the same city and can care for his own object, failure to do so may be treated as self-inflicted neglect, and the custodian need not assume the recurring care. Here Reuven needed the loan and had no practical alternative to placing the collateral with the gentile. His willingness to take that risk does not mean that if a Jew actually holds the object and can preserve it, the mitzvah of returning or preserving another's property disappears. A better answer is that the gentile, because he receives interest for the loan and holds the collateral under his own commercial law, is himself expected to preserve it from deterioration. If Reuven's collateral had been delivered directly to the gentile, the ordinary care would have been part of the gentile lender's responsibility. Shimon's later care therefore does not add a distinct mitzvah benefit that Reuven otherwise lacked. There is an even broader answer from Ritva, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. Ritva asks why every ordinary deposit should not make the custodian a paid custodian through the mitzvah of spreading and airing the object. He answers that where the custodianship did not begin as a mitzvah, later maintenance does not transform it into paid custodianship through the perutah of R. Yosef. We treat the original arrangement as implicitly excluding that added status. Only an aveidah custodian or a lender on collateral whose relationship begins through a mitzvah carries the perutah rationale through the later care. Here Shimon's role did not begin as the mitzvah of lending. Therefore even later spreading and airing does not turn him into a paid custodian through R. Yosef. This gives a satisfactory foundation for Maharam Padua's ruling.
As though he had lent him, and so forth. Sema and Shach cite Ran's responsum, siman 19. Reuven lent Shimon a sefer, and Shimon gave Reuven another sefer as collateral. If Reuven also needs Shimon's sefer for his own use, then Shimon plainly is not a borrower with all the benefit, because the arrangement resembles "lend me yours and I will lend you mine." Ran goes further. Even if Reuven does not need Shimon's sefer and accepts it only as collateral, Shimon still does not become a full borrower liable for unavoidable accidents. Why? Reuven performs a mitzvah by lending a sefer. During that mitzvah he enjoys the perutah of R. Yosef, namely the possible exemption from giving charity while occupied with the mitzvah. Reuven therefore also receives a legal benefit from the transaction. Since not all benefit belongs to Shimon, Shimon is only like a renter or paid custodian, not a full borrower. Shach says that according to authorities who do not rule like R. Yosef, if Reuven is already in possession he may say kim li that Shimon was a borrower and is liable for unavoidable accidents. That is reasonable within their system. Still, Ran's reasoning raises a difficulty. Whenever one person borrows an object and gives the owner collateral, the owner must spread and air the collateral. According to the perutah of R. Yosef, the owner receives a mitzvah-benefit from that care. Then no borrower who gives collateral would ever receive "all the benefit," and borrower liability for unavoidable accidents would disappear. Why, then, does the Mishnah need the special reciprocal-loan case "lend me and I will lend you" to reduce borrower liability? It could have taught the stronger novelty: "lend me and I will give you collateral." One possible answer follows Ritva's principle stated above. If the transaction did not begin as a mitzvah, later spreading and airing does not create paid-custodian benefit through R. Yosef. An ordinary loan of a mundane object is not itself the mitzvah of lending money or sacred study material. But that answer still seems incomplete. Even if the later care does not create formal paid-custodian status, the owner does receive some actual benefit from the mitzvah activity, which weakens the claim that all benefit belongs to the borrower. It is therefore more likely that Ran follows Rashba's theory that the operative perutah of R. Yosef is the benefit existing at the moment the mitzvah transaction begins. When Reuven lends a sefer, the very act of lending sacred study material is a mitzvah. If a poor person appears then, Reuven may be exempt because he is occupied in that mitzvah. That immediate benefit belongs to Reuven and prevents Shimon from being the sole beneficiary. In an ordinary borrowing of a nonmitzvah object, no such benefit exists at inception. Later collateral care does not change the original classification. But this creates another difficulty. If lending a sefer itself gives the lender a perutah benefit at the moment of the loan, then even where Shimon gives no collateral at all, he should not be a full borrower because Reuven benefited from the mitzvah of lending the sefer. Yet below in siman 342 the ordinary rule appears to be that one who borrows a sefer is liable as a borrower for unavoidable accidents, except for damage arising through normal permitted use. This is significant evidence against Rashba's theory that the one-time perutah at the moment of lending governs the entire custodial status. It supports instead the view developed above that the principal perutah of R. Yosef in collateral cases comes from the lender's later recurring duty to preserve the pledged object. Ran's particular case can still be sustained because lending the sefer itself and receiving collateral were structured together in a way that gave Reuven a real reciprocal benefit, but his reasoning should not be generalized too broadly. One final point is clear. Ran's actual case must have involved Reuven first lending the sefer and only afterward receiving Shimon's collateral. If both acts occurred simultaneously as a reciprocal exchange, it could become shemirah beba'alim, where the borrower might be exempt even from ordinary custodial liability. Ran would not need the elaborate discussion. Thus the acts were not simultaneous, and that procedural detail is necessary to understand the responsum.
