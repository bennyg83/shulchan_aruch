Since it was given as a deposit. Bach writes that some disagree, namely Rabbeinu Baruch in Hagahot Mordechai to Bava Metzia. Shach explains that their dispute exists only for those who do not follow Rabbi Yosef and instead make the lender a paid custodian only because of Shmuel. Under Mechaber, who rules that he is a paid custodian because of the mitzvah benefit described by Rabbi Yosef, everyone agrees that if he deposited the pledge back with the owner and it was stolen or lost, the lender remains liable as a paid custodian. That is why Mechaber states the rule without qualification and Rama does not object. As to any value above the debt, the lender is merely an unpaid custodian because we do not extract money from doubt; up to the amount of the debt, the borrower can say he is certain like Rabbi Yosef. In Tumim I wrote that the case must therefore involve a pledge not worth more than the debt and one that depreciates through use. If it is worth more, or does not depreciate through use, the lender is a paid custodian according to all opinions.
The lender remains responsible. One need not object that if so he should be liable even for unavoidable accident, because a paid custodian who hands property to an unpaid custodian has reduced the level of custody and is treated as negligent, and a negligent custodian is often liable even for unavoidable accident. Below in siman 296 it is clear that where witnesses establish the theft, that does not count as negligence. Here the pledge was stolen from the borrower himself, and there can be no clearer witnesses to the event. See what I write in siman 296.
And afterward he returned them to her. Sema says that if the garments had remained in the wife's hands and she claimed, 'My husband pledged them without my permission,' she would be believed on oath that she never consented. Shach limits this to garments she brought into the marriage. Garments her husband made for her can, in his view, be sold even without her consent. In Tumim I wrote that if the garments are presently in her possession, she may claim certainty like the opinion that he cannot sell them, even concerning Shabbat and Yom Tov garments, and they cannot be removed from her. The marketplace enactment does not apply because these are recognizable women's garments, and the purchaser should not have taken them without asking her.

This custodial status exists even according to authorities who follow Rabbi Yosef only as to the mitzvah-based paid-custodian rule, and the result therefore does not extend beyond the value of the pledge. If Shimon seized property of Reuven, he can say he is certain like those who reject Shmuel and follow Rabbi Yosef, which would leave him only an unpaid custodian here. I concluded in Tumim that this assumes the gentile was also willing to lend against Reuven's pledge. But if the gentile would lend only against Shimon's pledge, then the mitzvah benefit of Rabbi Yosef applies to Shimon just as in an ordinary money loan. I further left unresolved a case where the gentile lends without interest. Perhaps Shimon borrowed for himself from the gentile and then became the actual lender to Reuven, especially where Reuven does not know the gentile at all. Where interest is charged, Shimon must necessarily be Reuven's agent. I also discussed whether Shimon is a mitzvah custodian because the pledge must be aired and maintained. If the gentile would have been obligated to do that anyway, clearly there is no special mitzvah benefit. If the gentile would not have been obligated, I left the matter unresolved.
As though he had lent him. If Reuven borrows Shimon's book and Shimon gives him another book as collateral, Ran holds that Shimon is not considered a borrower liable for unavoidable accident with respect to Reuven's book, even if Reuven does not need Shimon's book at all. Shimon does not have all the benefit, because Reuven also benefits from the mitzvah advantage described by Rabbi Yosef. See Tumim for my observations. At minimum, if Reuven seized property he can say he is certain like those who do not follow Rabbi Yosef, making Shimon a full borrower. All this assumes the loan of the book and delivery of the collateral did not occur simultaneously. If they did, it would already be borrowing while the owner is employed with him.
