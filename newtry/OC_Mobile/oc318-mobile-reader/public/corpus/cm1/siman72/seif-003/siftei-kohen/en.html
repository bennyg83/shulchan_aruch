One who lends upon collateral and then, etc. Some authorities disagree with this law, as appears in the Hagahot Mordechai at the end of Bava Metzia. I am surprised that the Mechaber rules it without qualification, since the very source records a dispute. The question is whether the transfer of the collateral to another person ends the first lender's special status and creates only ordinary custody, or whether the first lender's rights and responsibility continue through the substitute holder. Since the early authorities disagree, the matter should be treated as a monetary doubt, and payment should not be extracted from the party in possession without proof.
The lender then returned, etc. This law applies according to all views. Even according to the opinion that a lender upon collateral is only an unpaid custodian, there remains a practical consequence regarding the amount of the debt and the lender's implied acceptance that loss of the collateral will cancel that amount. Furthermore, once the lender delivers the collateral to another for a purpose connected to the debt, the recipient's custody may be attributed to him. The exact liability depends on whether the recipient acts as the lender's agent, whether the debtor consented, and whether the transfer increased the risk. Where the lender had no right to transfer the collateral, he may remain liable for resulting loss even if the immediate holder would otherwise be exempt. Where the transfer was authorized, the recipient assumes the status appropriate to the benefit he receives and the purpose for which he holds it.

It implies that according to Rav Yosef, Reuven must pay Shimon the amount Shimon lent, because Maharam Padua does not accept that the benefit involved is too remote to create paid-custodian status. Even where no direct payment is received, the benefit of holding security for money can be sufficient. At minimum, the issue remains disputed, and possession should not be disturbed without proof.
An unpaid custodian, and he loses the amount of his money. He is not a paid custodian even according to Rav Yosef, because there is no mitzva benefit here. Reuven pays his own share of the profit or interest owed to the non-Jew, and the holder is not lending his own money as an act of kindness. Nevertheless, he loses the amount secured by the collateral because that amount was entrusted to the collateral itself. Liability for value beyond the debt follows the ordinary rules of an unpaid custodian unless an express undertaking or independent benefit creates greater responsibility.
As though he had lent him, etc. The Ran was asked about Reuven who lent a book to Shimon, while Shimon left another book with him as security, and robbers later took the pledged book. The legal relationship is treated like a loan secured by collateral rather than a simple exchange of deposits, because the second book stands as security for return of the first. Consequently, the rules governing a lender upon collateral apply, including the dispute over paid-custodian status and the amount offset by loss. The precise result depends on whether the first book was returned, whether the collateral remained for security after repayment, and whether the loss was theft, negligence, or unavoidable accident.
