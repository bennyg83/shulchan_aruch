The law is with Reuven. Shach strongly supports Rashba's responsum that even where there is no migo at all, if witnesses prove the object was delivered as collateral, the creditor is believed as to the amount secured by it. He supports this from Rambam. Rambam explains that a lender on collateral must take a formal oath even though he sometimes has a purchase migo, because if the pledge were established by witnesses he would still have to swear. This shows that a migo does not exempt from the already established collateral oath. At first glance this should have led Mechaber to cite Rashba expressly. His omission suggests the matter is not so simple. Rambam's point is narrower. Where Chazal or the Torah established an oath in a class of cases that sometimes lacks a migo, that oath remains even in individual instances where a migo happens to exist. This does not prove that the collateral holder wins the underlying monetary dispute without a migo whenever the pledge itself is witnessed. There is also an important difference between a strong and a weak migo. If witnesses establish that the item was given to him as collateral or to an artisan for repair, and there are no sighting witnesses, the claim "I returned it" is a relatively weak migo. The visible facts and witnesses support the holder's current story that the object came for a compensated transaction, while a return claim requires him to suppress the object indefinitely to avoid later sighting. A weak migo may preserve money but need not remove a formal oath. If, by contrast, no witnesses at all establish how the object came to him, the migo "I bought it" is strong. In that setting a migo may have greater force even with respect to oath. This harmonizes Rambam with the artisan rules without saying that the creditor always wins absent migo. Rashba distinguishes an artisan from a collateral holder because, under Ramban's view, a creditor acquires collateral when it comes into his hand as a pledge, whereas an artisan who merely retains an object for wages has no comparable proprietary acquisition. But Rosh rejects that distinction. He argues in Kiddushin that if ordinary loan collateral at the time of the loan carried such acquisition, an artisan holding an object for wages should be analogous. Rama in Even HaEzer 28 likewise rules that where the artisan still holds the utensil, kiddushin through the wages can work as with a loan secured by collateral. Ran too treats an artisan retaining an object for wages as analogous to a collateral holder. And Ramban himself, according to Ran's discussion of the artisan oath, can give the artisan an even stronger position than an ordinary collateral creditor while still recognizing that, where there are sighting witnesses and no migo, the artisan is not simply believed. Thus Rashba's sharp distinction is not universally accepted. Maharik correctly treats the issue as disputed, and the responsum printed among the responsa attributed to Ramban likely reflects a Ramban position rather than Rashba's. Therefore, where the creditor lacks any migo, he should not automatically be believed merely because the original pledge is established. Mechaber's more cautious ruling is preferable.
