The law favors Reuven, since there is one witness, etc. The Rashba disagrees and holds that the lender is believed upon an oath. The Rama cited that opinion above in seif 18. In his responsa, siman 86, folio 168, column 1, the Rama writes that although Responsa Ramban appears otherwise, money should not be extracted contrary to the Rashba. See there. In my view, moreover, there is actually no dispute here. It appears that the responsum printed as Responsa Ramban, siman 84, is in truth a responsum of the Rashba. It is well known that the collection printed as Responsa Ramban consists of responsa of the Rashba that printers mistakenly attributed to the Ramban, as the Beit Yosef also writes in the introduction to his work. Anyone who reads responsum 84 carefully will see from its language that it is the Rashba's, not the Ramban's. If you examine Responsa Rashba, siman 1040, you will see that the Rashba first ruled that the lender must return the pledge because his case resembles that of an artisan, as below in siman 134 and siman 89, seif 5, but afterward retracted and held that the cases are not comparable. Here the object came into the lender's possession as a pledge, and since a creditor acquires a pledge, the lender is regarded as the possessor. The responsum printed under the Ramban's name therefore preserved only the Rashba's initial view, that the case resembles an artisan. Since the Rashba himself later retracted and there is no authority who expressly maintains that the lender must return the pledge, we should rule that the lender swears and collects. See there carefully. This also contradicts Responsa Maharik, siman 94. There is further proof from the Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, and the Maggid Mishneh. The Rambam writes in chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh: "One who lends upon a pledge, since he collects from what is in his possession, and if he wished he could say, 'I purchased it and it is mine,' swears while holding a sacred object and collects, like all those who swear and collect. Why does he not merely take a hesset oath? Because he does not swear regarding ownership of the pledge itself, but regarding money that he is collecting. Had he said concerning the object itself, 'You sold it to me' or 'You gave it to me,' he would take a hesset oath and be exempt. But if there are witnesses that this object is in his possession as a pledge and they do not know the amount of the loan, he can collect only with an oath. Since, when there are no witnesses, he could have said that it is his own, he is believed to say, 'I have such and such against it,' with the same oath he would have had to take if witnesses established that it was a pledge, because we do not use a migo to exempt a person from an oath." Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, beginning of part 2, writes similarly. The Maggid Mishneh likewise explains that if witnesses established that the object came into his possession as a pledge, as he claims, but did not know the amount, the lender would require an oath resembling a Torah oath. Since witnesses establish that it entered his possession as a pledge, there is no migo that it was purchased. Therefore, even when there are no witnesses establishing that it is a pledge, although he would have been believed with a hesset oath had he claimed outright ownership, we do not use that migo to exempt him from the stronger oath required for collection. The Beit Yosef cites this in seif 11. Thus these authorities state explicitly that even when there are witnesses that it came into his possession as a pledge, but they do not know the amount, the lender is at least believed with an oath. All the more so where there is only one witness in such a case, he is not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear and therefore must pay. One cannot say that the Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, and Maggid Mishneh speak where there are no witnesses who presently saw the pledge in his possession, so that he has a migo that he returned it. If so, what advantage does the case with witnesses have over the case without witnesses for purposes of proving that, with witnesses, he needs an oath while holding a sacred object? In the witnessed case itself, if he retained a migo that he returned it, perhaps he should be believed with only a hesset oath by that migo. Rather, they must be speaking where there are witnesses who also presently saw it in his possession. We find many places in the Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, and other poskim where they write simply "witnesses," while meaning a case that also includes witnesses who saw the object presently in his possession. This appears clear to me. Although above in seif katan 9 I concluded that the rule that a creditor acquires a pledge applies only where the pledge was taken other than at the time of the loan, nevertheless, because this object was originally delivered to him as a pledge, he is treated as its possessor for purposes of being believed up to its value even without a migo. Below in seif 24 he cannot claim from uncertainty because he has only a lien against the pledge, whereas here he makes a definite claim and is believed because he lawfully possesses it. And he shall collect and swear, etc. Meaning that if Shimon produces no proof, Reuven collects and swears, etc. The reason he takes only a hesset oath is that the pledge itself is regarded as "here it is." According to this, the case is one where he has not yet taken the pledge, and nevertheless he needs only a hesset oath because once Shimon produces no proof the pledge is Reuven's, so Shimon's monetary claim is like an ordinary oral claim, which requires only hesset. Alternatively, the case is one where Reuven immediately pays the ten that he admits, which likewise constitutes "here it is."
