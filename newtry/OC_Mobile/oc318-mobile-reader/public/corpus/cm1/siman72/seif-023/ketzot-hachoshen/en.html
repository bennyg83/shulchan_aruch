The law follows Reuven. Rashba disagrees and holds that because a creditor acquires the pledge, the lender is regarded as the possessor. The Shach rules like Rashba. The apparent difficulty is that Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition reaches only the value of the debt. If witnesses establish that a loan occurred but do not know whether it was a small or large amount, beit din itself is uncertain whether the creditor acquired a small or large share of the pledge. Ordinarily we should leave property under the original owner's presumption. The answer follows Tosafot concerning a dispute over the price of a slave. Where witnesses establish the underlying transaction and only the amount is unknown, there is a direct monetary uncertainty independent of the parties' claims. In such a case seizure can be effective. Here too, witnesses establish that a loan and pledge existed. The only uncertainty is the amount, and because a creditor acquires the pledge to the extent of the debt, beit din itself faces a genuine monetary uncertainty as to the size of the creditor's acquired share. The creditor's possession therefore helps when he makes a certain claim. If he himself says he is uncertain, possession does not help and we return to the original owner's presumption. This also explains why an artisan ordinarily needs a migo. Where there are no witnesses to the transaction, the uncertainty arises only from the litigants' competing stories. There is no independent monetary doubt before beit din. Mere seizure does not help. When witnesses establish the transaction but do not know the amount, there is a direct doubt and possession can then support the claimant. Even one witness can create the relevant structure where the party admits the witness's account, because his admission plus the witness makes the existence of the transaction certain. Therefore Rama records the dispute specifically even in the case of one witness, and Rashba's rule has genuine novelty there.
