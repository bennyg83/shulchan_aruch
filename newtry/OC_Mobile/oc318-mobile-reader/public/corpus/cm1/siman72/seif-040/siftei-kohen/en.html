The non Jew told him, etc. The Sema writes that the same rule applies if the first Jew pledged the item on his own, without the non Jew’s instruction. Although while the non Jew is alive the first Jew may redeem it again, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 169, seif 18, that does not apply here after the non Jew has died. The Bach likewise rules so. The Sema adds that if the non Jew has heirs who know of the collateral and intend to redeem it, the first Jew may redeem it after the non Jew’s death before the heirs do so, paying the second Jew principal and accrued interest, and the balance belongs to the first Jew. The Bach appears to dispute this distinction and to hold that the first Jew can never redeem it while the heirs themselves have not come to redeem it. Raavan’s case, in which the non Jew instructed the first Jew to pledge it to another Jew, merely records the facts that occurred and does not create a legal distinction. The matter requires further study in practice.
Shimon acquires the entire collateral. The Sema and Bach explain that when Reuven pledged it to Shimon he completely withdrew his own right from it, for otherwise Shimon’s taking interest would amount to a Jew lending to another Jew on interest. I do not know why they make the result depend on the prohibition of interest. In my view, even if Reuven pledged it to Shimon without interest, Shimon acquires the collateral. It is no worse than collateral of a convert or non Jew located in the lender’s courtyard, where another person who takes possession acquires the excess value. All the more so here, where it was already in Shimon’s possession. Raavan, cited by the Mordechai, states explicitly that the entire collateral belongs to Shimon, because one acquires against the amount of his money and the other acquires the remainder; since Shimon already advanced his money, the whole collateral is his. No interest is mentioned there. The Mordechai likewise indicates that the question arose only in a case of deposit, implying that with collateral Shimon acquires it in every case. I also question the law of a deposit below in seif katan 163; see there.
And the non Jew said to give the collateral to another Jew to redeem it, etc. Meaning that the non Jew gave it as a gift to another Jew and then died. The first Jew nevertheless acquires the collateral even if its value exceeds the loan. Even if the non Jew originally deposited it with the first Jew and later gave it as a gift to a second Jew, the first Jew acquires it upon the non Jew’s death, as Rabbeinu Yeruham writes in the name of the Rashba and as the Rama rules below in siman 194, seif 2. It appears from Rabbeinu Yeruham, the Rama, and the Rashba that this can apply even if the non Jew did not die.
And if the non Jew owed money to a Jew, etc. See below at the beginning of siman 86. The Rama rules there that when Reuven holds collateral from a non Jew and the non Jew owes Shimon money, some say Shimon may redeem the collateral from Reuven by paying Reuven principal and accrued interest, then collect his own debt from the excess. The Rama accepts this because where the non Jew has no other means of payment, Shimon receives the non Jew’s rights by the rule of Rabbi Natan. The Beit Yosef disagrees with Maharil and holds that the holder acquired the collateral for all further interest. The Darkei Moshe defends Maharil, reasoning that because the non Jew can pay the second Jew only from the excess value of this collateral, the second Jew has all of the non Jew’s rights in it under Rabbi Natan’s rule and may redeem it just as the non Jew could. The Bach agrees. Their proof from the episode of Mari bar Rachel is not decisive, because there Rava acquired the right by a complete sale. Nor does the Rashba cited in siman 104 necessarily disagree with Maharil. The Rashba’s responsum shows that a first lender whose collateral was pledged until a fixed date has priority even for interest not yet accrued, because the lien for the agreed interest attached from the original undertaking, just as an obligation to support a wife’s daughter binds property from the time of acceptance although maintenance accrues daily. Thus, when the non Jew pledged the item until a fixed date and died during that period, the first lender collects the interest through the stated maturity date. It appears that the Bach and Darkei Moshe would agree to this, and it is quite possible that the Beit Yosef likewise disputes only how to formulate the holder’s acquisition, not the practical result. In any event, the principal ruling appears as I have stated.
