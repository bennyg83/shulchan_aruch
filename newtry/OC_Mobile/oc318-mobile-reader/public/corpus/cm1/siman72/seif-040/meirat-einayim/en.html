Shimon acquired the entire collateral. The reason is that when Reuven pledged it to Shimon, he completely removed himself from it; otherwise Shimon would be prohibited from taking interest from him, for it would be an Israelite lending to an Israelite, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 169. For this reason it also appears that if an Israelite pledges to another Israelite collateral belonging to a non-Jew that is in his hands for interest, even without the non-Jew's instruction, the second Israelite acquires the entire collateral. Although the first Israelite retains the right to redeem it while the non-Jew is alive, because the custom is to redeem it and it is considered as though he stipulated that he could do so, once the non-Jew dies the second Israelite acquires the collateral. Since the non-Jew's right has ended, the right of one deriving through him also ends.
If, however, the non-Jew has heirs who know of the matter and intend to redeem it, then the first Israelite may redeem it after the non-Jew's death before the heirs come, paying the second Israelite the principal and accrued interest, with the balance belonging to the first Israelite. This differs from the present case, where the Israelite demanded his money from the non-Jew and the non-Jew instructed him to pledge it with the second Israelite; in that case he cannot redeem it after the non-Jew's death. This appears to be why Rashba states the law where the non-Jew instructed him.To give the collateral to the Israelite. Meaning, to permit him to redeem it, or the case is where the non-Jew had already paid the Israelite the amount of the loan.This one acquired it, etc., while it is in his possession. There the reason is explicit: once the money reached the non-Jew, he withdrew from the collateral, while the recipient did not acquire it until performing meshichah. This language is cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe.
It requires study where the collateral is worth more than the loan, for the non-Jew certainly did not withdraw from the excess and the second Israelite gave the non-Jew no money, since this case concerns a gift. In the responsum of Rashba cited there this is clear. See also the end of siman 194, seif 2, where Rama repeats this law and explains that once the non-Jew gave it to the other person by deed he withdrew from it. One must therefore say that the wording here about the money reaching his hand borrows the language of the Gemara concerning an Israelite who purchases land from a non-Jew and pays him, after which another Israelite takes possession of the field. The second acquires it because the non-Jew withdrew upon receiving payment.
From there we learn that the same applies to a gift: the non-Jew withdraws from the moment he gives it.
