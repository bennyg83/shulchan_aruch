The Sema writes that if the non Jewish owner independently repledged the item to another Jew, without direction to do so, and then died, the second Jew need not return it to the first creditor unless the non Jew left heirs who know of the matter and wish to redeem the pledge. If the non Jew expressly directed that it be repledged to another Jew, the second Jew need not return it at all. The Shach cites the Bach as disagreeing and saying that until the heirs actually come to redeem, the second Jew need never return it, and therefore leaves the matter unresolved. I do not understand the doubt. We rule that a non Jew inherits his father by Torah law. If during the father's lifetime the first Jewish creditor was required to permit redemption, as the Rama rules in Yoreh Deah, siman 169, why should the law change when the father dies and his son stands in his place? The son's legal power is the same as the father's. The Sema's rule is therefore correct whenever a son inherits. The case may differ where the deceased left no descendants and only remote relatives. Rambam writes that a non Jew inherits his father by Torah law, but other successions are left to their custom, and Maggid Mishneh explains that the Torah's order of inheritance was stated for Israel. There may therefore be no Torah succession in the remote relatives at all. In that circumstance one can understand why the second Jew might not be obligated to allow redemption merely because such relatives claim the pledge.
