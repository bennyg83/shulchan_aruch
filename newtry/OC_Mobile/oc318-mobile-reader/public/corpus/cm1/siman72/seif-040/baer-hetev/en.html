Acquired. The Sema writes that if the non-Jew has heirs who intend to redeem the pledge, the first Jew may himself redeem it after the non-Jew's death before the heirs do so, by paying the second Jew the principal plus accrued interest until redemption, and the excess value belongs to the first Jew. This is where the first Jew pledged it on his own initiative without the non-Jew instructing him to do so. If the non-Jew told him to pledge it to the second Jew, the first Jew cannot redeem it after the non-Jew's death. The Shach notes that the Bach appears to reject this distinction and leaves the law requiring study.
The pledge. The Sema and Bach explain that when Reuven pledged it to Shimon, he completely removed his own interest, for otherwise Shimon could not take interest because it would be interest paid by one Jew to another. The Shach says the reason should not depend on ribbit. Even if the pledge was re-pledged without interest, Shimon acquires the excess, no less than the pledge of a convert or non-Jew found in a lender's courtyard after the owner's death, where another person who takes possession acquires the excess. Here the pledge was already in Shimon's own possession. Raavan and Mordechai likewise do not mention interest. The Taz rules plainly that there is no distinction: even without interest, even if the first Jew pledged it without the non-Jew's instruction, and even if the non-Jew has heirs, the second Jew acquires the pledge. The Taz also discusses a case involving wine bought on credit with collateral worth more than the debt that was later stolen, distinguishing when the holder is an unpaid or paid custodian.
To give. The Sema explains this as giving the item to the Jew for redemption, or as a case where the non-Jew already gave the Jew the money to redeem it. The Shach explains instead that the non-Jew gave the pledge as a gift to another Jew and then died. The first Jew then acquires the pledge even if worth more than his loan. The same applies where the non-Jew first deposited the pledge with the first Jew and afterward gave it as a gift to another Jew and died. The first Jew acquires it as the Rama states in siman 194, seif 2, and according to the Rashba's responsum this can apply even if the non-Jew did not die.
See below. See also siman 194, seif 2, concerning a non-Jew who has a pledge or deposit in a Jew's possession and sells it to another Jew. Seif 3 there implies that the second Jew acquires it with money, because a Jew acquires movable property from a non-Jew by money alone.
