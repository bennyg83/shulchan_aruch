And that certain person has, etc. Meaning, this too is written inside the document produced by the heir.
And the heir claims that the collateral was lost through unavoidable accident, etc. This does not mean that the heir asserts this with certainty. If so, why does the Mechaber conclude that the defendant takes a hesset oath that the collateral was not returned to him, when the heir himself claims definitely that it was lost by unavoidable accident from his father's possession? Rather, the heir says: perhaps it was lost through unavoidable accident, and therefore you certainly owe my father such-and-such money, since the promissory note remained in his possession. One might have thought that this too is included in the general principle that beit din advances for an heir whatever claim his father could have made. The reason the heir does not instead claim, 'Perhaps my father returned the collateral to you,' is that this is a weak claim. Lenders do not ordinarily return collateral to borrowers without receiving payment; if we suppose it was returned, we should equally suppose that the debt was paid. Likewise, in the following law where the Tur and Mechaber say that the orphans claim their father instructed them that one hundred was owed on the collateral, the meaning is not that they definitely say he gave such an instruction. This is explicit in the responsum, cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, seif 29, and I summarized it in Sema on the following law.
The defendant takes a hesset oath, etc. The Tur writes that we do not say the heir should take the heirs' oath, as with an ordinary promissory note in an heir's possession. In an ordinary document, the debt is certain and repayment doubtful. Here, however, the decedent had received collateral against the loan, and it is uncertain whether the collateral was lost through unavoidable accident or through negligence. Therefore the document's force is impaired, and the defendant takes a hesset oath, etc. See Perishah. Eir Shushan writes that what the heir claims, that the collateral was lost from his father through unavoidable accident, is not accepted, and that a person cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children because the heir cannot know how to swear. This shows he understood the heir as asserting a definite claim, which is incorrect. His additional invocation of the principle that a person cannot transmit an oath to his children is likewise wrong, for an oath of this kind can be inherited. Only where the borrower died during the lender's lifetime and the lender's heirs seek to collect from the borrower's heirs do we say the lender cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children, as the Tur and Mechaber explain below in siman 108. Eir Shushan himself writes so there. See Perishah.
