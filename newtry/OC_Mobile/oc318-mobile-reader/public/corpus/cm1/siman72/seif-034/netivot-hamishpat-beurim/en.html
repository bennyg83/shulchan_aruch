And the heir claims that the collateral was lost through unavoidable circumstances. See Shach, seif katan 142, through his statement that if the heir definitely claims that it was returned, the defendant must take a shevuat heset. Tumim asks that if the heir definitely claims that it was returned, he should be believed by a migo that he could claim it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. There is no difficulty. Even if the heir claimed that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances and the borrower definitely claimed, "You received its value," the Sema in seif katan 13 holds that even where the borrower has no migo, such as a loan evidenced by a note, the borrower is exempt. See Shach, seif katan 126. Even according to the Shach there, who holds that whenever the borrower lacks a migo the lender swears and collects, I already explained above that this is because a claim that the lender sold the collateral or was negligent with it is equivalent to saying, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim." Since we do not presume people to be thieves or negligent parties, that is a weak claim and is not believed without a migo. See siman 294, seif 1, seif katan 3. But where the borrower claims, "It is still in your possession and you did not return it to me," the borrower is certainly believed. On the contrary, the lender's claim that he returned collateral without receiving payment is an unusual and weak claim. Even against a claim of unavoidable loss, the concern that perhaps the collateral is still in the lender's possession is treated as a substantial basis for suspicion, as the Sema writes there. Moreover, because the collateral is recorded in the note, the lender should not have returned it without writing a receipt. Since he cannot sue the borrower until he presents the note to beit din, and the collateral is also recorded in that note, it is as though the note stating that the collateral was given remains in the lender's hands. A claim of "I returned it" against an express statement in the note is weak and is not believed without a migo. Here, however, such a migo would be a migo used to extract property. Tumim's point that with a note we do accept a migo to extract is no difficulty. The main reason a migo to extract is accepted with a note is that the chazakah that a valid note would not remain in the holder's possession without cause supports the migo. Here that chazakah does not exist, because the collateral was already in his possession from the outset. This is clear and straightforward.
