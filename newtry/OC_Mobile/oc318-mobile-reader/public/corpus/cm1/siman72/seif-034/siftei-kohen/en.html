And that certain person has property. The Tur's wording could imply that this fact is written in the document. The Shulchan Aruch changed the conjunction, apparently to teach that it need not be written there. Even if the heir merely admits that the named person has property, the ruling follows, because the heir is not making a present definite claim that could be believed through a migo. The Tur's wording is therefore not exact. The Sema says that this fact too is contained in the document, but that is unnecessary and, in my view, contrary to the Mechaber's deliberate revision.
The heir claims that the pledge was lost through unavoidable circumstances. This means that the heir says it may have been lost, not that he knows with certainty that it was. Otherwise, why would the debtor take a rabbinic oath that it was not returned, when the heir himself says with certainty that it was lost? Moreover, if the heir had definite knowledge of the accident, he could swear to it and collect the debt just as the creditor could. The Sema, Bach, and responsa of Mahari ibn Sasson understand it this way. Yet the ruling still requires study. Why should the debtor be compelled immediately to swear that the pledge was not returned merely because the heir raises a doubtful claim? Ordinarily we do not exact an oath upon a doubtful allegation unless a specific enactment applies. It may be that the heir's documentary proof and possession create enough basis to require the debtor's rabbinic oath, but the scope of that requirement must be limited to the facts the debtor can know with certainty.
