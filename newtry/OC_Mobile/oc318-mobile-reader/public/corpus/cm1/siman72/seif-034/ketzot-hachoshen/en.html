And the heir claims. The Tur writes that we do not say the heir should take the ordinary heirs' oath. A document produced by an heir ordinarily represents a certain debt and only doubtful payment. Here, however, the deceased creditor received a pledge equal to the loan, and the uncertainty is whether it was afterward lost through accident or through negligence. The document therefore loses its force. At first glance this follows the Tur's own view that the pledge and debt are netted against one another as though expressly stipulated. Since it is known that the creditor received a pledge on a condition under which loss of the pledge would extinguish the debt, and it is uncertain whether the condition occurred, the creditor loses the debt out of doubt. According to Shulchan Aruch, however, who follows Rabbi Yosef that the creditor is a paid bailee, the liabilities are independent: the creditor collects his debt and the borrower separately collects bailee damages, as explained in seif katan 7. If the debt is certain and we merely do not know whether the borrower has a counterclaim, why should the debt be lost? Perhaps the answer is that beit din does not advance for heirs the claim that the pledge was lost through an unavoidable accident, as explained in siman 108. The deceased's bailee liability is therefore treated as established, and the heirs would even owe any excess over the loan were there no countervailing authorities. Since some poskim do hold that we advance the claim of unavoidable loss for heirs, as noted in Shulchan Aruch siman 108, seif 7, we cannot definitively require the heirs to pay any excess. But the amount of the debt is certainly extinguished, because absent a definite claim of unavoidable loss the heirs possess only an uncertain defense to the bailee liability. This assumes, as Sema and Shach write, that the heirs themselves say they do not know. If an heir makes a definite claim, he can collect the document, because the debt is certain. Even according to Rama, who treats the arrangement as though the parties expressly netted the pledge against the debt, where the borrower says he does not know whether the event occurred, the creditor should prevail because it is a case of 'I do not know whether I repaid you.'
