The Shach writes that even if it is not written in the document and only the heir admits it, the law is the same. We cannot say that the heir is believed through a migo, because his present claim is not a definite claim to which a migo can lend credibility. These are his words.
The defendant. The Tur states that we do not say the heir should take the heirs' oath on the theory that any document produced by an heir is certain and repayment is doubtful. Here, because a pledge equal to the loan came into the decedent's possession and it is uncertain whether it was lost unavoidably or through negligence, the document is void, and the defendant takes a heset oath. So cites the Sema. The Shach asks why the defendant should take a heset oath at all, since one does not swear on a doubtful claim, and why he should have to swear that the pledge equaled the debt. Gidulei Terumah raises the same difficulty and leaves it unresolved. One might suggest that beit din advances claims on behalf of the heir, as the Bach writes. On reflection, however, we find that such claims are advanced for heirs only to prevent extraction from them, not to impose liability upon the opposing party based on a doubtful claim. This is especially clear from the Mechaber's ruling in siman 133, seif 5, concerning objects normally lent or rented: they are removed from an heir, and the claimant is not made to swear even heset, although the deceased holder could have imposed such an oath. We do not advance a doubtful claim for the heir merely to require an oath. All the more so here, no heset is required. Even authorities who disagree there do so because there the claimant comes to remove property from the heirs, so imposing an oath can be viewed as maintaining their possession. Here all agree that no claim is advanced for the heir. The Shach therefore interprets the Tur as presenting alternative cases. If the heir merely claims that the pledge may have been lost through unavoidable circumstances, the document is void. If, however, the heir definitely claims that the pledge was returned, the defendant must take a heset oath like any other oral claim. We do not infer from the fact that the document was not returned that the pledge must certainly have been lost unavoidably or returned. The Mechaber's language is difficult to interpret this way and requires study, but as a matter of law this is the Shach's conclusion.
