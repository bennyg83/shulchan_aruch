And that person has a pledge. The wording implies that the pledge is not mentioned in the promissory note, as the Shach writes. One must ask: if the heir does not know whether the pledge was lost, and the borrower likewise does not know, why should the debt not be collected? There is a valid document. Above and below, where neither creditor nor debtor knows whether a documented debt was paid, the document remains in its chazakah and is collected. One answer is that there the borrower's uncertainty is inferior because a person ordinarily pays carefully and should know whether he paid. Here, the borrower has no reason to know whether an accidental loss occurred in the creditor's possession. Alternatively, in the ordinary case the note begins with an unimpaired presumption that it was never paid. Here it is known that collateral stood against the note. That circumstance already weakens the document, and the only question is what later happened to the collateral. Once the note left its original unimpaired status, doubt is insufficient to collect with it.
The defendant takes a heset oath, etc. The Shach asks how an heir who has only an uncertain claim can impose a heset oath. He answers that the rule is stated in alternatives. If the heir himself does not definitely claim that the pledge was returned, no oath is imposed. If he makes a definite claim, the defendant takes heset. One must then ask why the heir should not be believed by a migo. If he definitely says, "the pledge was returned to you," he could instead have said definitely that it was lost through unavoidable accident. According to the Shach himself, that claim would be believed with an oath and the borrower would have to pay. Thus the return claim should also be believed by migo. The answer is that the heir's definite assertion need not mean that he personally saw the return. It may mean only that his father expressly instructed him that he had returned it. Such an instruction is enough, together with the promissory note supporting the heir, to require the defendant's heset. But there is no migo, because the father himself could have recovered on a claim of accidental loss only by swearing, and he died without taking that oath. The heir cannot extract money through a claim whose force depended upon an oath his father never took. This also allows the language of the Shulchan Aruch to be read literally when it says the heir claims definitely that the pledge was lost, meaning that his father left definite instructions. The heir need not have personally witnessed the accident. Likewise, the borrower's oath that it was not returned is not necessarily tied to the first factual alternative. The seif presents the possibilities separately. There is another way to avoid the migo entirely. As I explained in the general rules of migo, a migo used with a document is not inherently a migo used to extract merely because the claimant has a document. Its force often derives from the chazakah that if payment had occurred the debtor should have retrieved the note. Here the debtor does not claim payment. He says instead that the pledge was damaged, misused, or lost through the creditor's responsibility, a matter the heir cannot know. The ordinary evidentiary force of "why is your note still in my hand" does not answer that claim. Therefore the migo would truly be one used to extract and is ineffective. In either explanation, the Shulchan Aruch can be understood.
