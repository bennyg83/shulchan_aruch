If the collateral was not lost and the parties dispute the amount of the original loan, the lender saying that he lent a sela and the borrower saying that he lent only a shekel, then if the circumstances are such that the lender could have retained the collateral and claimed, "It is purchased and belongs to me," or "No such transaction ever occurred," or "I returned it to you," the lender is believed to say that he lent against it up to its value, provided he takes an oath while holding a sacred object. Rama: This applies when he holds an object, but if he seized money he takes only a heset oath. Even if the borrower died first and the lender later died, the lender's heirs his orphans collect from what is in their possession. Therefore, if the collateral is worth a sela, the lender swears and collects. If it is worth only one dinar, the lender takes one dinar from the collateral, the borrower pays another dinar that he admits, and swears regarding the two dinars he denies. If the borrower denies everything and says, "This is not collateral but a deposit in his possession, and I owe him nothing," the lender collects from the value of the collateral. If it is worth less than the debt, he takes its value and the borrower takes a heset oath regarding the remainder and is exempt. Rama: Even if the item he holds is an implement used in food preparation or a widow's garment, we do not say that because he is required to return it he has no migo. Since there are no witnesses and he could have denied possession, he may assert a claim against it up to its value. The same applies where an enactment forbids a person from retaining another's property that came into his possession as a loan or deposit, or where a teacher holds books from which he teaches the children. Even though he is required to return them, he does not lose his migo and may assert a monetary claim against them up to their value wherever he otherwise has a migo. Even under such an enactment, a teacher may retain a book as security for his teaching wages. Some say that wherever a person could assert a claim against property in his possession, he may swear generally that he owes the claimant nothing, provided he knows with certainty that the claimant owes him, such as where he has a loan or deposit belonging to the claimant and knows clearly that it was not lost through accident and that the claimant must return his deposit or its value. Initially, however, we instruct him to clarify his claim, as explained below in seif 25 and at the beginning of siman 75. His ability to assert a claim against property in his possession applies where he says the opposing party owes him money. He may not retain the property merely until the other party provides releases or satisfies unrelated demands, because those matters are not connected. Whenever a person may assert a claim against property in his possession, his legal right in that property begins only when the parties stand in judgment, not from the time of seizure. Therefore, if its value increased before they stood in judgment, the increase belongs to the original owner.
