With an oath. Shach writes that the lender must take the formal oath even where the claimant himself is uncertain, and he argues this at length. I have serious doubt. Shach's principal proof is the case of goats that ate another person's produce, where the injured party may recover the amount of damage through a formal oath even though the animal owner does not know how much was eaten. There the oath is appropriate because the defendant was not expected to know exactly how much his goats consumed. Where one party naturally knows and the other naturally does not, the party with knowledge may establish the amount by oath. Our collateral case is different. The borrower should know how much he borrowed. If he himself says, "I do not know the amount," his claim is materially weakened. We should not impose a formal oath on the lender merely to answer the borrower's avoidable uncertainty. The Geonic rule requiring a formal oath in these collateral valuation cases was already questioned by early authorities. We should not extend it further to a claimant who pleads uncertainty about a matter he was expected to know. Indeed, in ordinary monetary law Rav Huna holds a definite claim can defeat an uncertain defense even to extract money, while R. Nachman disagrees because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Here the lender is already in possession of the collateral. Even R. Nachman's possession rationale therefore favors the lender. If the borrower does not know how much he borrowed, why should the lender lose his possessory advantage without any definite opposing claim? Moreover, the entire analysis assumes the collateral counts as presently held property and can function as "here it is" for oath purposes. Otherwise, once the borrower admits at least a perutah of loan but cannot state the amount, he himself could fall into the category of one obligated to swear who cannot swear, and the lender would not need the present oath at all. Thus the legal structure itself treats the lender as possessor through the collateral. For these reasons, where the borrower makes only a doubtful claim about an amount he should have known, the lender should not be required to take the full formal oath merely on that basis.
But if he is holding money, and so forth. The plain wording sounds as though even money held merely as a deposit is included. That is not correct. The source is Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, part 5, law 6, where the case is money owed as a loan, which need not be returned in the identical coins and is therefore treated only as an accounting obligation. If, however, he holds deposited money that must be returned in its original form and remains legally in the depositor's domain, there is no reason to distinguish it from any other deposited object. This is especially so according to Mechaber, who holds that a migo does not exempt from an oath. If so, the earlier answer of Rosh, that the claim concerns the value rather than the body of the collateral, cannot itself remove the oath, and there is no principled distinction between cash and an object. The correct case is therefore unrestricted deposited money, where the custodian is permitted to use it and, according to our law, has essentially the status of a moneychanger. Such money is fully in the custodian's monetary responsibility and resembles a loan. But sealed or tied money, which he may not use and which remains the depositor's property in every respect, is like any other deposited object. The claimant who seeks to collect against it must take the formal oath required of one who collects by oath.
And even if the object was, and so forth. This is explicit in the Gemara at the end of Perek HaMekabel, concerning the man who seized a knife that was a utensil used in preparing food. Rava nevertheless believed him up to its value through a migo. At first glance this seems to support the Geonim, who require a formal oath even where the holder has a migo. Ordinarily, someone suspected of monetary wrongdoing is not automatically suspected of a false oath because he may think he is retaining money against an old debt and can later restore it. But with a utensil used for food preparation, seizure as collateral may itself violate the Torah prohibition against taking such an item. If the holder claims, "It is mine," that violation can no longer be repaired by later returning the object according to his own claim. Why then is the alternative claim a valid migo? Perhaps he avoids it precisely because of the prohibition. If, however, even under his present collateral claim he must take a formal oath, then choosing the allegedly easier false claim would expose him to the even more severe problem of a false oath. The migo is therefore restored. One can also answer without relying on the Geonim. The prohibition against taking a food-preparation utensil as collateral applies where a real debt exists and the creditor seizes the object as security. If in truth there is no debt and he simply takes the object by force, the primary prohibition is theft. That wrongdoing is capable of restitution. We should not invent extensions of Torah prohibitions merely from a fortiori reasoning. Thus the Gemara does not decisively prove the Geonic oath requirement, though it is consistent with it.
