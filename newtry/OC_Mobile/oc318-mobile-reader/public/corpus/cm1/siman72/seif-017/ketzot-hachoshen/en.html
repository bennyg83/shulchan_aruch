If it is in such a manner. At the end of chapter HaMekabel, concerning a slaughtering knife, the Gemara says that the possessor is believed through a migo that he could have claimed purchase. Likewise, concerning goats that ate barley, the Gemara says he is believed through a migo of purchase. This requires explanation, because both the Gemara and poskim imply that one is believed to say an object is held as collateral only through a migo that he could have claimed purchase. Why should he not be believed on the collateral claim itself, when the object is of a type not ordinarily lent or rented, just as he is believed to claim purchase? After all, a creditor acquires a pledge. In Ramban's Sefer HaZechut I saw the great authorities struggle with this question. Raavad, in chapter HaKotev concerning a bundle of documents, writes that some argued that even without a migo she should be believed to say, 'They are pledged to me.' Raavad rejects that argument: a pledge is not like a purchase. Since she admits that the underlying object belongs to the other person, it remains in his established ownership. When she says, 'It is pledged to me,' she must bring proof. If she has a migo, we believe her; otherwise not. Even where orphans' cattle keeper seized an ox and could say, 'I purchased it,' the collateral claim was believed only because of the purchase migo. Raavad's reading is plainly supported by the Gemara, but the reason requires explanation. Ramban himself holds that where it is known that the object came to the creditor as a pledge and only the amount of the loan is unknown, the creditor is believed to state the amount even without a migo because, under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule, the creditor acquires the pledge and is therefore considered in possession. Shulchan Aruch rules this in seif 23. If he has a proprietary right in the pledge, why should he not be believed without a migo just as one who claims outright purchase? This is exactly the objection cited in Sefer HaZechut: what superiority does purchase have over pledge, since both involve acquisition and possession? It appears that the matter can be explained from the Ran in chapter HaKotev. The Ran asks why one holding a promissory note is not believed to say, 'I purchased it.' The Gemara in chapter Get Pashut says that according to the view that letters are acquired by delivery, Rava holds that the possessor need not bring proof of delivery. Even according to our ruling that letters are not acquired by simple delivery, why is the holder not believed to say that the note was transferred to him together with land or by another valid form of assignment, or that he had a written bill of sale that was lost? Ramban answers that movable property is acquired through delivery, lifting, or another act of possession. The very act by which the possessor holds it is itself capable of acquiring it. He is therefore believed to say, 'I purchased it,' because we do not remove property from one who is physically and legally in possession. A document is different. Possession of the paper itself is not the acquisition of the underlying debt. If he says, 'The debt was assigned to me through land or by a written transfer,' he must prove it, because the underlying obligation remains presumptively with the original creditor whose name appears in the document. Only according to the view that letters are acquired by delivery does Rava treat the holder like an ordinary possessor, because then the very act of holding and delivery is itself the acquisition. According to those who require a written transfer or another distinct act, possession of the document alone is not enough. The same reasoning applies to a pledge. If one gives another a maneh and merely places an object with him as a pledge without first creating a personal debt, the pledge is ineffective: there is 'no maneh here and no pledge here,' as Tosafot and Rosh explain in Kiddushin 8a. Rosh explains that so long as the debtor's person has not first become obligated, his property cannot become subject to the creditor, because a person's property is only his guarantor. Thus possession of the object itself does not create the pledge relationship. The claimant must assert an additional fact beyond his possession: that the owner first became personally indebted through money lent or a kinyan and then gave this object as security. Since the possession itself does not constitute the acquisition, he is like one who holds a promissory note and claims that the debt was assigned together with land or by some other mode. He is not believed without proof. This is why a person claiming an object as collateral is not believed merely from possession unless he has a migo of purchase. The objection of the authority cited by Ramban is nevertheless correct in a case where the debt itself is independently known. In the case of the bundle of documents, if the debt is known by witnesses and the only uncertainty is whether she took the documents as security during the debtor's lifetime, she should be believed, because once the debt is established the possession itself can function as the acquisition of the pledge. There the difference between purchase and pledge disappears. This is consistent with the Rosh's position in chapter HaKotev that she is believed to say the bundle was pledged to her even without a purchase migo. See what I wrote in siman 64, seif katan 5. The distinction is therefore clear: where the debt itself is unknown, a collateral claim requires proof because possession cannot create the underlying personal obligation; where the debt is already established, possession may itself support the pledge claim.
With an oath while holding a sacred object. Maggid Mishneh explains the requirement of an oath by saying that we do not use a migo to exempt from an oath. This still requires explanation. The principle that a migo does not exempt from an oath ordinarily applies where, even without the migo, the litigant would be believed as to the money upon taking an oath, and he seeks to use the migo only to remove the oath. But with a pledge, without the migo he would not be believed as to the money at all. The migo therefore supports his very monetary right, and the exemption from an oath would merely follow incidentally. Knesset HaGedolah cites this distinction in the laws of migo: where the migo changes nothing about the right to the money and is invoked only to eliminate an oath, it is ineffective; but where the migo establishes the monetary claim itself, we use it even though exemption from an oath follows. The Sema therefore explains that here too the migo is relevant specifically to the oath. If there were witnesses that the object came to him as a pledge, the creditor would be believed as to the amount through the migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' but would still have to swear. The Shach's explanation of Maggid Mishneh is clearer: if witnesses establish that the object came to him as a pledge but do not know the amount of the loan, the creditor is believed upon an oath, as stated in seif 23 and in Rambam, chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh. This resolves Ramban and Ran's difficulty from the baraita of one who gives a garment to an artisan. The artisan says, 'You fixed my wage at two,' while the owner says, 'I fixed it at one.' So long as the garment remains with the artisan, the owner must bring proof. If the artisan returned it within the time, he swears and collects; after the time, the burden of proof is on him. This implies that while the garment is in his possession he collects without an oath even though his claim rests only on a migo. According to what we have explained, the reason is that an artisan is different from a secured creditor. In chapter Chazakat HaBatim, if there are witnesses that the item came to him as an artisan and he has no migo, the artisan is not believed concerning the amount. Shulchan Aruch so rules in simanim 89 and 134. Rashba therefore distinguishes between artisan and pledge: a creditor acquires a pledge under Rabbi Yitzchak, while an artisan does not acquire the object as a pledge. Since the artisan is never believed without a migo, the migo is needed for the very monetary claim and therefore removes the oath as well. One might say the baraita follows Rabbi Yehudah, who requires the artisan to swear regarding the amount fixed. But Rabbi Yehudah requires a partial admission, and so long as the garment remains in the artisan's hand the admitted part would be 'here it is.' This does not resolve Rambam, however. Rambam in chapter 11 of Hilchot Sechirut rules that when the artisan still holds the garment he must swear while holding a sacred object, and Shulchan Aruch rules likewise in siman 89. There the Maggid Mishneh's reason does not fit, because if there were witnesses eliminating the migo, an artisan who does not acquire a pledge would not collect even with an oath. This requires study. See what I wrote in seifim ketanim 23 and 38.
He seized money. Tumim writes that this applies only where he seized loan money or unsealed deposited money that he may use and that is therefore like a loan. Sealed deposited money, he says, resembles other objects. This forces the language away from its plain meaning. The ruling clearly implies that whenever he seized money he needs only a hesset oath. The reason is that money can be applied toward one's own claim without a beit din appraisal. This differs from other objects, which require beit din valuation. With an object the creditor is therefore regarded as coming to collect and must take an oath, whether because Rambam holds that migo does not eliminate the oath or because Rosh regards him as one who comes to collect. Money requires no appraisal. If he says, 'He owes me,' he may act for himself and collect the money. According to his claim, the very coins are now fully his. He is therefore like one claiming, 'I purchased this object,' regarding the body of the item, and only a hesset oath applies. It also appears to me that the same applies where a seller sold merchandise on credit, the very merchandise remains in his possession, and he admits, 'I sold this item, but its purchase price is still owed to me.' He may retain the item toward its price without an oath. Merchandise sold on credit that remains intact is comparable to an existing iska, from which one takes without an oath. The Bach rules that where goods were sold on credit and remain extant, the seller takes priority over all other creditors, comparing them to an intact iska. We have compelling proof that a seller who admits the sale but claims the price remains unpaid may retain the goods without an oath. See below, siman 91, where this is discussed.
