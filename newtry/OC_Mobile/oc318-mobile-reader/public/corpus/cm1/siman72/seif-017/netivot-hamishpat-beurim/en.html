For the lender could have retained the collateral and claimed that he purchased it. Sema and Shach appear to say that he is believed because of a migo. Ramban in Sefer HaZechut challenges Raavad: even where there is no migo that the object was purchased, such as with a document, why should one not be believed to say that he holds it as collateral just as one is believed to say that movable property was purchased? This is a strong question, since where it is known that an object came into his possession as collateral and the parties dispute the amount of the loan, the lender is believed even without a migo. Ramban answers that collateral differs from a sale. When the holder admits that the body of the object belongs to the other party, it remains in that owner's chazakah to the extent of the admission. A claim that it is merely collateral requires proof, unless supported by a migo. Even in the case of orphans' cattle, one who seized an ox is believed because he could have claimed purchase, implying that without that migo a collateral claim would fail. See also what I wrote in siman 64. Ketzot HaChoshen explains Ramban to mean that where physical possession itself is not an act of acquisition, as with a promissory note that requires writing and delivery, possession does not create the status of muchzak, and therefore one cannot claim purchase even if one alleges an agav acquisition. Ketzot extends this to a claim of collateral in movable property when the lender would need to allege an independent underlying loan, reasoning that possession alone acquires nothing unless the other alleged transaction is established. In my view this cannot be Ramban's intent. If it were, according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that money effects acquisition of movable property and no other sale acquisition works without money, a possessor would never be believed on a Torah level to claim purchase of movable property, since he would always have to allege the additional act of payment. Likewise, according to the many poskim who hold that meshichah does not acquire from a non Jew, possession could never support a purchase claim without a migo. Rather, Ramban's distinction is that with a document, physical possession can never itself serve as the relevant acquisition, and therefore it does not make the holder a muchzak. With movable property, however, meshichah can itself be an effective acquisition in some circumstances, such as a gift or a sale for an antecedent debt where no money remains to be paid. Because that mode of possession can function as acquisition, it also creates the legal status of muchzak even when the possessor must allege another act as part of his particular transaction. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 8 similarly indicate that if riding alone were an act of acquisition, the rider would be treated as a muchzak even in a case where riding in the presence of another person leading would not itself complete acquisition and an additional act would have to be alleged. Only where the mode of possession can never constitute acquisition does it fail to make one a muchzak. Thus, according to Rabbi Yochanan, although the possessor must allege payment of money, because meshichah itself is an effective acquisition where no money is lacking, his possession still supports his claim. So too here, after a loan has been made, drawing the collateral into one's possession acquires a lien in it. That physical possession therefore makes the lender a muchzak for his assertion that he already advanced the money, and the poskim needed other reasons for any contrary result. Siman 39, seif 17, further states that one who has taken possession of collateral on condition that he make a loan becomes obligated to lend, and the loan can be compelled in beit din. This resembles one who says, "You sold me the object, I acquired it by meshichah, I became obligated for the price, and I paid you." Here too he can say, "You delivered the collateral to me so that I would lend; I acquired the collateral, thereby became obligated to lend, and did lend." Delivery of the collateral itself created the security right and triggered the obligation to lend. It must therefore be understood as though the borrower said, "Acquire the body of the pledge for your lien." This is unlike one who says, "I give you one hundred as a gift," and separately gives collateral, where no debt ever arises and there is no valid pledge. Ketzot's comparison between the two cases follows his own view in siman 39, but the cases are not alike. Every beit din rules both that collateral delivered for the purpose of obtaining a loan is effective and that collateral given merely to secure a promised gift is not.
With an oath. See Shach, seif katan 67, who says this applies even if the borrower claims uncertainty. Although the borrower admits at least one perutah and is uncertain about the remainder, this is not treated as a Torah case of partial admission in which one who cannot swear must pay. Even though Shach in seif katan 118 rules that collateral is not considered immediate tender, Shach in seif katan 3 explains regarding the oath generated by one witness that because the borrower is seeking to recover his collateral, the required oath is only the rabbinic oath of the Mishnah. The same is true here for a partial admission: because he is seeking to recover the pledged object, the oath is rabbinic, and the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay does not apply to a rabbinic oath. By contrast, seif katan 118 concerns a creditor who claims an amount exceeding the value of the collateral. There he swears a full Torah oath and is not collecting by virtue of the collateral itself. He seized money. Tumim writes that seizure helps only with loose money that the holder is permitted to use, while tied money that may not be used is like seizure of an ordinary object. This is incorrect. If he seizes money against the owner's will, he certainly has no permission to use it and it is equivalent to tied money, yet seizure is nevertheless effective. See Shach, seif katan 74, where there is a textual error and the correct reading is, "if the borrower does not admit."
