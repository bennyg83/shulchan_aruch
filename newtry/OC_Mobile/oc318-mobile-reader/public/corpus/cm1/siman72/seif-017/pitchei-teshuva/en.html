Because there is an enactment not to withhold, etc. In the glosses of Rabbi Akiva Eiger, of blessed memory, he notes that Be'er HaGolah in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 334 states that Rabbeinu Tam placed a ban concerning this matter. See also Terumat HaDeshen, siman 305, regarding this enactment.
He may swear generally that he owes him nothing. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 194. Reuven sued Levi on a document stating that Levi owed him one hundred Reichsthaler. Under ordinary law the undertaking was an asmachta and would not bind, but the document stated that it had been made with a formal handshake undertaking and an actual oath. Beit din therefore ruled that Levi must pay, as stated in siman 207, seif 19, that an oath is effective even where the underlying obligation is an asmachta. Levi protested that he had never sworn and that the document merely said so, but since it said that the oath was actually taken, his protest did not help, as the Sema writes in siman 73, seif katan 18. Many years later property of Reuven came into Levi's possession, and Levi refused to return it. Reuven sued him again. Levi answered, "I owe you nothing." When asked how that could be, he repeated only the general statement that he owed Reuven nothing. Beit din ruled that Levi must swear. The question was whether, since everyone knew that the first claim was merely an asmachta and Levi knew in his own heart that he had in fact never sworn or given the formal handshake, he could now swear in general terms that he owed Reuven nothing. Chavot Yair answers that he certainly may swear generally that he owes him nothing, or that Reuven owes him an offsetting amount, as stated in the Rama, siman 72, seif 17. There, however, the rule that he should not clarify his words and should swear only generally is difficult, because in that case he could explain the truth openly. Here he cannot explain without contradicting a prior binding record. This permission applies only where the earlier document was phrased as a recital of a past act, such as "it was done with a formal handshake and an oath," and witnesses signed it, or even where Levi himself wrote or signed it with language such as "I accepted upon myself by handshake and oath." Such wording is an admission that an act occurred, like the common formula beginning "I acknowledge," and all subsequent clauses follow that character. If his conscience knows that he never actually gave the handshake or swore, he may in the second suit swear generally that he owes nothing. But if he wrote, "I hereby swear to pay," or "I hereby accept upon myself under curse, oath, and formal handshake," that itself appears to constitute the oath, and Levi was rightly obligated to pay the first claim. See there. See also what I wrote concerning an oath in writing in Pitchei Teshuva, Yoreh Deah, siman 236, seif katan 1; Even HaEzer, siman 108, seif katan 4 and siman 125, seif katan 10; and below, siman 73, seif 6, seif katan 9.
Or a deposit. See Baer Heitev through its distinction concerning a deposit, where the issue is who is entitled to assess the value. Responsa Shevut Yaakov III, siman 144, discusses Reuven, who seized several hundred from Shimon and claimed that the entire amount was due him for work performed on Shimon's behalf in secular litigation over one or two years. Shimon had promised to pay for all his labor and effort. Shimon answered that Reuven should itemize his work and have beit din assess proper compensation. Reuven replied that after one or two years he could no longer remember and detail every service, but he knew with certainty that the total value of his labor equaled the amount seized and was willing to swear to that effect. Shevut Yaakov writes that similar cases had arisen before him several times and that it was difficult to apply strict Torah law because the claimant himself could not clarify how much he was owed, no fee had been fixed in advance, and even a possessor cannot simply assess his own services at whatever amount he chooses. Their practice was therefore to do everything possible to reach a mutually accepted compromise. If agreement could not be achieved, he proposed an approach based on cases in the Gemara and poskim where one pays the reduced value of barley, namely one third less than full value. The possessor should swear in substance: "I swear that, in proportion to the labor and effort I expended for so-and-so, if beit din knew the full extent of that work they would assess my compensation at no less than such-and-such; and since beit din has reduced the amount by one third, I am taking only two thirds, which is certainly due to me." His beit din agreed with this method and acted on it repeatedly. He suggests that it may be comparable to the rule in siman 12, seif 5, that a judge sometimes has power to render a judgment resembling compromise where circumstances require it. See there.
Until the other party gives him releases. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov II, siman 170. Reuven held in escrow a promissory note against Shimon. Shimon demanded return of the note. Reuven answered that he was withholding it because long ago he had paid Shimon a debt documented by another note, but Shimon had not returned that note and had claimed that it was lost. Reuven therefore demanded a release and receipt covering the earlier note. Shimon claimed that he had in fact returned it at the time. At first glance the rule in the Rama, siman 72, seif 17, appears explicit that one may not seize property merely in order to obtain releases, based on Mahari Weil, because a migo from a monetary claim to a nonmonetary demand is not recognized. Shevut Yaakov writes, however, that on closer examination the rule is not so clear when money or a document is already in the possessor's hands and he has a migo. One may argue that if a migo works from one monetary claim to another, it should certainly work from money to a demand for protective documentation. In the case before him there is an additional reason: the principal item seized is itself a third-party escrow document, so the migo is from one document to another, which is effective like a migo from one monetary right to another, as the Shach concludes in siman 82 in the laws of migo. Furthermore, Shimon admits that Reuven owes him nothing and claims only that the old note was returned. What harm, then, is there in giving Reuven a release? Shevut Yaakov therefore rules that Reuven may retain the escrowed note until he receives the releases, and records that he so ruled in practice. In volume III, siman 143, Shevut Yaakov similarly discusses Reuven's oral claim of one hundred gold coins against Shimon. Shimon answers that the debt is true, but he has rights and documents in Reuven's hands and is withholding Reuven's money until they are returned. Shevut Yaakov rules that Shimon may withhold payment and assert that Reuven possesses a document in which he has rights, because he has a migo that he could have denied Reuven's monetary claim. He notes that he proved this in volume II, siman 170, and at the beginning of his treatise on migo. See there.
