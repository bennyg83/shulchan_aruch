With an oath. Shach holds that even if the borrower claims only uncertainty, the lender must still swear while holding a sacred object. See Tumim. This requires study because the borrower ordinarily should know how much he owes against the pledge. Yet with an heir who does not know how much his father owed, everyone agrees the lender must take such an oath.
While holding a sacred object. Even though if he had claimed, 'I bought it from you,' or, 'I returned it,' he would have been believed with only a hesset oath, and in the case of an heir who claims death there might have been no oath at all, Mechaber and his supporters do not use a migo to exempt him from this oath. According to authorities who do apply such a migo, one needs Rosh's other reason: he is not claiming the body of the object but only money against it, so he is considered one who is collecting and must swear. See my rules of migo at length for the remaining difficulty that perhaps he has a migo to claim the object itself.
Specifically where he holds an object. Sema cites Ran that this means where the lender demands that the borrower redeem his pledge. Then the lender must swear while holding a sacred object. But if the lender is silent, does not ask the borrower to redeem, does not seek to sell the pledge, and merely wishes to retain it until the borrower comes voluntarily and pays, while the borrower is the one demanding return of the pledge, only a hesset oath is needed. Shach writes that we do not follow this in practical law. Ran gave it only as one answer to a difficulty against the Geonim from the Mishnah of the craftsman holding the cloak. Below in siman 89 we rule differently, that a sacred-object oath is still required. Ran himself gave another stronger resolution, as I wrote there at length. Rama and Mechaber therefore omitted this answer.

He has not lost the migo. The same applies where he seized in the presence of witnesses and the object was seen, but the authorities dispute the law. He remains the possessor and can say, 'I am certain like the view that supports me,' even though he may be obligated to return the object. If we said that because it must be returned he is not considered the possessor, then there would be no migo either, because it would be a migo used to extract. Terumat HaDeshen and Shach.

And it is clear that it was not lost through unavoidable accident. If the custodian claims unavoidable accident, the lender must know with certainty that he is lying and that no such accident occurred. But if the custodian claims, 'It never happened,' or, 'I returned it,' he himself admits there was no unavoidable accident. The lender, who knows he did not receive it back, can therefore swear.
