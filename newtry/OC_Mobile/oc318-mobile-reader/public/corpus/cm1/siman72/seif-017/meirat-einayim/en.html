With an oath while holding a sacred object. This is the view of the Geonim cited by the Tur. In the Derishah I explained their reasoning in the name of the Maggid Mishneh. They hold that the lender is not believed with merely a hesset oath by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed, 'I purchased it,' or, 'I returned it to you,' because we do not use migo to exempt a person from an oath otherwise imposed upon him. Since he admits that the object is not his but is held as collateral, his admission is equivalent to a hundred witnesses. If witnesses had testified that it came into his possession as collateral but did not know for what amount, then although the borrower would not be believed to swear and collect, because the borrower was granted such credibility only where the lender could not have claimed that he returned it or that the transaction never occurred, nevertheless the lender too would not be believed to state the amount of the loan without taking a stringent oath while holding a sacred object, as explained below in seif 23, because he lacks a migo that he purchased it. Even if the witnesses did not presently see the item in his possession and he could have claimed that he returned it, he is not believed with a hesset oath to say, 'I lent him such and such amount on it,' by virtue of a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' because that is not a valid migo. A person prefers to lie by stating a particular amount loaned rather than to lie by claiming that he returned the item. Therefore, even now, when there are no witnesses and he himself admits the collateral relationship, we do not use migo to exempt him from the oath that would have been required had there been witnesses. See the Perishah for further discussion.
But if he seized money, etc. Only an object that is supposed to be returned in kind to its owner causes the lender to be viewed as one seeking to extract property from the borrower, and therefore he must take a stringent oath like one who swears and collects. Money is different, because according to the lender's claim the money is to remain in his possession in payment of the loan. This is clear. Even with an object, the Ran writes that we require the lender to take a stringent oath only when he comes to demand that the borrower redeem his collateral. But if the lender remains silent and the borrower comes to extract the item from the lender, then the lender is believed up to its value with a hesset oath. We should not extend the stringency beyond its source. See there, and see below in siman 89 for further discussion.
