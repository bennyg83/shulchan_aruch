He is believed to say that he lent him, etc., upon taking an oath. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that whenever a possessor may claim up to the value of the item in his hand, he must take an oath even if the claimant asserts only a doubtful claim. The Darkei Moshe and Beit Yosef cite this, and it follows from the general language of the Rambam and the later authorities. The principle is that where one party knows and the other does not, the knowledgeable party swears and collects. The ruling below concerning land, where collection is made without an oath against heirs who assert only a doubtful claim, is different. The Geonic oath does not apply to land, and a hesset oath was not enacted against a doubtful claim. Here, however, movable property is involved, and the Geonic oath applies even against an uncertain opposing claim when the possessor seeks to collect.
With an oath while holding a sacred object. The Sema explains that this is the view of the Geonim. A lender is not exempted with a mere hesset oath through a migo that he could have claimed ownership or return of the object, because a migo does not remove an oath. Once he admits that the object is not his but is held as a pledge, his admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. Had witnesses testified that it came to him as a pledge and presently saw it in his possession, without knowing the amount lent, he could collect only after taking a stringent oath. Therefore, even where he has a migo of ownership or return, the basic law of one collecting from a pledge remains a stringent oath. The Sema's suggestion that it is easier for him to claim the amount of the loan than to claim return is unconvincing; both are ordinarily valid migo claims. The correct explanation is simply that a migo does not exempt from the oath that applies to one collecting from a pledge.
While holding a sacred object. The Bach argues that even according to the Rosh, who generally allows a migo to exempt from an oath, this is only where the migo would remove the oath entirely. If some oath remains, the migo cannot reduce its severity. He overlooked the Rosh's explicit discussion. The Rosh rejects that distinction and explains that an oath has monetary consequences because a person may prefer to pay rather than swear. The Geonim nevertheless required a stringent oath from one collecting against a pledge for a different reason: he is not claiming ownership of the pledge itself, but seeks to extract the borrower's money through it. That, not the Bach's distinction, is the basis of the rule.
Specifically, etc. The Tur cites the Raavad as requiring only a hesset oath, like one who claims ownership. The Bach questions the source, but the Baal HaTerumot explicitly records the Raavad's objection to the Geonim. Even so, the Raavad did not finally dare rule against them, for the words of the Geonim are a received tradition. His other writings also treat the Geonic rule as controlling. The settled halachah is therefore that one who collects from a pledge takes a stringent oath while holding a sacred object. I have also shown elsewhere that this special rule applies specifically to a pledge, because the borrower could not extract it from the lender even by taking an oath. In other cases of persons who ordinarily swear and collect, if they have already seized property and possess a valid migo, they need take only a hesset oath. The Bach further limits the Geonic rule to disputes over the principal loan and not a dispute over depreciation of the pledge. I have shown below that this is incorrect; in every such case the lender must take the stringent oath.
But if he seized money, etc. A specific object that must ultimately be returned in kind is treated as though the lender is extracting value from the borrower. Money is different, because according to the lender's claim it remains with him in payment of the debt. The Ran writes that the stringent oath is required when the lender seeks to realize the pledge. If he merely remains silent and retains the pledge until the borrower pays, he need take only a hesset oath. The Sema misunderstood this as depending on which party first brings the claim. That cannot be correct, because the Geonic reason is that the lender is not claiming the body of the pledge as his own but seeks payment from it. It makes no difference who first speaks. Rather, the Ran distinguishes between realizing the pledge, whether by demanding redemption or by selling it, and merely retaining it as security without seeking present collection. In the latter case he asserts a continuing lien in the body of the pledge and therefore takes only hesset. Many authorities who require a stringent oath even where the lender is silent may be discussing a case where he nevertheless seeks to consume or realize the collateral. The Ran's precise distinction remains difficult in light of the ruling concerning an artisan, where the Rambam and Tur require a stringent oath, but it may be that they too concern an artisan who seeks payment from the garment.
Even if the borrower died first, etc. We do not apply the rule that a person cannot transmit an oath to his heirs, because the pledge was already in their father's possession as a source of payment. Nevertheless, the heirs must take the heirs' oath that their father did not instruct them that the debt was paid or otherwise impaired. This is so even according to the Rosh and Tur, who generally require the heirs' oath only when collecting from the borrower's heirs. Here, the father himself could collect from the pledge only by taking an oath in every case. His heirs therefore must likewise swear in every case, even if the borrower himself demands the pledge.

Or the garment of a widow, etc. The Baal HaTerumot understood the Rambam to hold that one who possesses a widow's garment has no migo, and left the matter difficult. It is clear that the Rambam agrees that a migo may exist. He refers to a case where the widow delivered the garment as collateral in the presence of witnesses and it was now seen in the lender's hand, so that no migo of ownership or return remains. The Rivash and Kesef Mishneh explain the Rambam this way.

Nevertheless, he has not lost his migo, etc. Terumat HaDeshen writes that he likewise has not lost his status as possessor. This matters where there is a doubt in the document, a dispute among the authorities, or another unresolved legal doubt, because possession may be effective even without a migo, as explained above in siman 42.
Some say that wherever one can assert a claim concerning what is under his control, etc. The Rama's wording is imprecise. This applies plainly to money owed on a loan: the holder may say generally that he owes the other nothing. But where an object was deposited with him, the bailee may not simply retain it and swear generally that he holds nothing for the depositor. He must state the matter accurately before beit din: “I hold his deposit, but he owes me such-and-such against it.” This is the ruling of the students of the Rashba, the Ritva, Rav Hai Gaon, the Baal HaTerumot, and the Nimukei Yosef. One may not retain a deposit or pledge without specifying the counterclaim, because who has appraised it for him? The Rivash adds that even where the other owes him wheat from a loan, he may not seize property silently, since the wheat requires appraisal.
