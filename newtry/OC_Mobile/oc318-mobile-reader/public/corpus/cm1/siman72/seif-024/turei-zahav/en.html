(I was asked) by the distinguished and pious Rabbi Yonah, dayan of Krakow, about Reuven, who purchased wine from Shimon on credit and gave him a pledge worth more than the price. The pledge was stolen. He proposed that everyone agrees Shimon is a paid custodian, like an artisan who is paid through the benefit of not having to pursue his money and who holds property corresponding to his claim. I agreed as practical halakha. Yoreh Deah 227 rules that one forbidden by vow to benefit from all Jews may not buy from or sell to them at the average market price, because such a sale benefits both buyer and seller. The same benefit makes the seller a paid custodian of a pledge taken for the wine price. One cannot answer that vows are uniquely strict. A person forbidden benefit from all Jews may borrow from them but may lend to them, because he says that he receives no benefit. Yet if every lender on a pledge is a paid custodian because of the small benefit described by Rav Yosef, why may he lend? The answer is that vow law disregards Rav Yosef’s uncommon benefit, as the Gemara says concerning returning lost property: the chance that a poor person will arrive precisely while he is occupied is too remote to constitute prohibited benefit, though it is sufficient to create paid-custodian liability. Therefore, if an ordinary sale is prohibited by vow because it benefits the seller, it all the more surely creates paid-custodian liability. Wine is always regarded as benefiting the seller, since the Sages advised selling wine rather than risk its souring. For other merchandise, beit din must examine the market. If it is an average sale or slow-moving merchandise, the seller benefits and is a paid custodian of the pledge. If it is readily marketable merchandise with many buyers, the sale does not particularly benefit the seller and he is only an unpaid custodian.
