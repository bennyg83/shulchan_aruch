And not to the communal trustee. Sema's presentation of Rashba is awkward. Shach's formulation is clearer: why should the trustee be a paid custodian at all? What wage does he receive? One might say that he gains a reputation for trustworthiness, just as an administrator appointed by beit din is treated as a paid custodian because of the honor of the appointment. But communal trustees are often appointed against their will by the community itself, and that rationale is therefore weak. They are not paid custodians by default. Shach asks why the mitzvah benefit of Rabbi Yosef does not apply. See Tumim for my answer.
Unless there is a custom. Shach explains that the custom must be that communal leaders advance the tax from their own money and later collect from individuals, taking pledges from those who fail to pay. They are then actual lenders. A mere communal trustee does not become a paid custodian simply because of a custom, because custom cannot uproot the law in that manner. In Tumim I argued the reverse: regarding communal leaders who first paid from their own pockets, there is substantial doubt whether they are truly paid custodians, while regarding an ordinary trustee, a binding local custom can indeed establish paid-custodian responsibility.
