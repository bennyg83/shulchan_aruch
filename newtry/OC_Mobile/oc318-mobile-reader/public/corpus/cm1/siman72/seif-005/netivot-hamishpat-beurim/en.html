The community and the treasurer have no liability. It appears that if money collected from certain taxpayers is lost while held by the communal treasurer, and the treasurer had no permission to use the money, those particular taxpayers bear the loss and must pay again, while the other taxpayers do not share that loss. This assumes the community expressly prohibited the treasurer from using the funds. If the tax collectors are paid, they are paid custodians. With respect to a tzedakah collector who takes collateral, it appears that according to the opinion based on Shmuel he certainly is not a paid custodian, because one cannot say that by taking collateral he implicitly agrees that its loss cancels the donor's neder or communal obligation. Even according to the opinion based on Rabbi Yosef's perutah, that rationale does not apply to a tzedakah collector. Rabbi Yosef's perutah applies where a person is occupied in a mitzvat aseh personally incumbent upon him and for which he may not charge, such as lending money or returning a lost object. The obligation of tzedakah requires every Jew to give charity, but there is no mitzvat aseh upon every individual to serve as a communal charity collector. If there were, all Jews would have to become collectors. Rather, beit din has a duty to compel the community to fulfill tzedakah obligations and to appoint officers for that purpose, and those officers may receive compensation. Rabbi Yosef's perutah therefore does not apply. A proof is Bava Metzia 58 concerning one hired to guard a cow or produce. Without payment for Shabbat he is only an unpaid custodian and is not converted into a paid custodian by Rabbi Yosef's perutah, because there is no mitzvat aseh upon him to serve as a custodian and he may charge for doing so. Rashba, cited by Shach, writes that an apotropos can be a paid custodian because of Rabbi Yosef's perutah. That is understandable where orphans cannot manage their affairs and their property would be lost without intervention. Every person has an obligation of hashavat aveidah to save their property from loss, so the apotropos functions as one returning a lost object. The rule permitting an apotropos to take compensation concerns one who actively conducts business for the orphans and generates profit for them. Even concerning an apotropos, the Mechaber discusses limitations in siman 290.
Nor the treasurer. See Shach, seif katan 34. According to what I wrote above, Rabbi Yosef's perutah applies only where the obligation to air and care for the item arose because of a mitzvah and Torah decree. Accordingly, Shach's entire discussion here does not apply. This is certainly true of a third party stakeholder, concerning whom Shach rules categorically that he is a paid custodian. In my view he certainly is not. He is no better than an artisan who says, "I finished it," who is not a paid custodian even though he holds the item for his fee and refuses to release it, nor than a lender who needs the collateral, where according to the opinion rejecting the mitzvah rationale he is not a paid custodian. The rule is therefore as stated above. A treasurer who receives no salary is not automatically a paid custodian, but where he is paid he is a paid custodian according to all opinions.
