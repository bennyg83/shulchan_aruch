And the trustee does not have the status of a paid custodian. Shach asks why. According to Mechaber, the perutah of R. Yosef ordinarily makes a person occupied with a mitzvah into a paid custodian. The usual reason for exempting a guardian because people would refuse the office does not fit here, since people seek these communal appointments, as Rashba himself notes. But Mechaber below in siman 290 rules that an guardian, whether appointed by the father of the orphans or by beit din, is an unpaid custodian. Evidently the perutah of R. Yosef does not apply merely because one manages another's property. The orphans can pay someone to handle their affairs, so the guardian is not classified as one engaged in a mitzvah in the relevant custodial sense. Shach disagrees there and treats a guardian as paid through R. Yosef, which is why the present rule troubles him. In any event, the communal trustee here is even easier. He takes collateral from householders for taxes. He is not voluntarily lending them money as a mitzvah. To the contrary, if they have the funds they must pay the tax; only when they cannot pay is property seized. At the moment the collateral is taken there is therefore no lending mitzvah that would exempt him from giving charity and create the perutah of R. Yosef. Later he may have to spread and air the collateral. But according to Rashba's approach discussed above, the decisive perutah must arise at the inception of the loan, which is absent here. And even according to the recurring-care approach, Ritva says that later spreading and airing creates paid-custodian status only where the custodianship itself began as a mitzvah. Where the relationship did not begin through a mitzvah, later care does not transform it. Therefore the communal trustee is not a paid custodian through the perutah of R. Yosef. Rashba's discussion of that perutah in the responsum concerns the guardian case, not necessarily this trustee. However one reads his wording, the practical rule stated here is correct.
Unless there is an established custom, and so forth. Shach asks how custom can overturn the halachah if it is not a proper ancient custom. He therefore explains that the custom refers not to the trustee but to the community itself. If the community customarily pays the entire tax from communal funds and afterward collects each person's share, the individuals effectively become debtors to the community and the community may be treated as a paid custodian over collateral. I do not see the perutah of R. Yosef there. The community is not lending as a mitzvah. It is compelled by the tax system to pay the assessment and then recover each member's share. Nor does the community itself spread and air the collateral, which is held by the appointed trustee. Perhaps paid-custodian status could instead arise from the benefit of holding security for money the community was forced to advance. The Tosafot objection, that without the loan there would have been no need for collateral, is weaker because the community had to advance the tax money in any event under the established arrangement. Still, the simpler reading is that the custom refers directly to the trustee. If local custom is that a person accepting this trusteeship bears paid-custodian responsibility for all collateral, then anyone who accepts the office accepts it on those terms. One who enters an established commercial or communal role enters according to its known custom. This is not custom abolishing halachah. It is an implied contractual condition. An unpaid custodian may expressly undertake the liability of a paid custodian. Established custom can supply that undertaking. Therefore Rama's wording can be read literally and presents no difficulty.
