If the circumstances are such that the holder cannot claim, "It is purchased and belongs to me," or "No such transaction ever occurred," or "I returned it to you," the borrower swears that the loan was only a shekel, pays that amount, and takes back his collateral. How is this determined? With objects not ordinarily lent or rented, if there are no witnesses showing how they entered his possession, then even if witnesses now see them in his hand he is believed to say, "I purchased them." Likewise, even with objects ordinarily lent or rented, or objects not ordinarily lent or rented where witnesses saw that they originally came into his hand as a loan, if there are no witnesses who see them now in his possession, he can claim, "I returned them," or, where there are no witnesses that they were delivered to him, "No such transaction ever occurred." But with objects ordinarily lent or rented where witnesses now see them in his possession, he cannot claim, "I purchased them," or "I returned them." Rama: Some say that the witnesses need not have seen the property at the time the case was brought to beit din. If they saw it earlier in his possession and he then claimed it was sold to him or made another claim under which he would not have been required to return it, he no longer has the migo that he could say, "I returned it," because we have effective testimony that he did not return it. Likewise, if it is publicly known to be in his possession, even without witnesses who actually saw it, he cannot say, "I purchased it," because he has no credible migo that he returned it, as he would be afraid to contradict what is publicly known. Even if, when the witnesses saw it, he said that the other person owed him the same amount he now claims in beit din, he is not believed. This applies where witnesses saw it before the claim was raised in beit din. If they saw it only after the parties' claims and dispute had already arisen before beit din, he did not lose his migo, and he is believed in the claim that it was purchased or that he returned it, because we look to the time the claims were made in beit din. If there is one witness concerning the matter, some say that the lender is not believed, because he is obligated to swear in order to contradict the witness but cannot do so, since he admits the witness's factual statement and merely advances another claim to exempt himself. He is therefore one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and must pay. Others say the lender is believed with an oath. The Mechaber's conclusion follows the first view, as will be explained below in this siman, seif 23. See also siman 75, seif 13. See above, siman 58, regarding whether a person may assert a claim against property in his possession because of a debt owed by someone other than the person who gave him the property. The rule that a person may say, "It is purchased and belongs to me," or "I lent money against it," applies only while the object is in his possession. If Reuven deposited an item with Shimon and Levi brings witnesses that it belongs to him, or Shimon admits that it belongs to Levi, and Shimon claims that Reuven owes him money secured by it, Reuven is not believed to say that he purchased it from Levi. If Shimon says that he knows Reuven purchased it from Levi, however, Shimon is believed through the migo that he could have said that he himself purchased it from Levi, which would have been believed where there are no witnesses showing how it came into his hand or witnesses who saw it in his possession. If Shimon does not claim that it was purchased, but says Reuven gave Levi's property to him as collateral, see siman 356, where he must redeem it because of takkanat hashuk.
