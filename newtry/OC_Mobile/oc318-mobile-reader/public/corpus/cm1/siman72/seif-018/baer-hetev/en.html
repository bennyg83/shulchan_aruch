And collects. The order of the words is reversed. The borrower first takes back his pledge and only afterward takes a shevuat heset. The Rambam states this explicitly. Shach.
How. Even if witnesses saw the item come directly from the borrower into the lender's hands, if they do not know whether it was a deposit, pledge, sale, or gift, the lender is believed to say, "You sold it to me" or "You gave it to me," as stated in siman 133. Consequently he is also believed to claim against it up to its value. Shach.
Now. The Rambam writes that regarding any item presumed not to be ordinarily lent or rented, even if witnesses testify that this owner once lent or rented it, that does not destroy the possessor's chazakah. The Bach finds this astounding because testimony should override a presumption. He suggests that the Rambam means that even though witnesses establish a past loan or rental, we presume it was later returned and the possessor may claim that he subsequently bought it. The Bach concludes that other authorities disagree. The Shach says this is mistaken. The Rambam means that the witnesses testify that the owner lent or rented the item to someone else, not to the present possessor, as Maggid Mishneh makes explicit. The law is therefore correct and undisputed.
That he delivered them. The Sema explains that the case also assumes that witnesses did not see the items presently in the lender's hands. Thus, even if he later admits that he has them and claims an amount against them, he is believed through a migo that he could have denied possession or claimed return. But if witnesses currently saw and identified the items, neither denial nor return is available. The Shach cites Baal HaTerumot: with items ordinarily lent or rented, if there are witnesses that the owner delivered them, or with other items if there are witnesses that he lent them and there is also present sighting, the possessor is not believed to claim purchase or pledge. The owner takes his items and swears a shevuat heset. Gidulei Terumah asks why an oath is required when witnesses support the owner. The Shach answers that the presumption or witnesses merely prevent the possessor from retaining the item, but it remains possible that the owner subsequently borrowed money against it. The owner's position is therefore at least like an oral monetary claim and requires a shevuat heset.
Who saw it. The Sema understands that the witnesses must recognize the actual objects and know with certainty that they belonged to the borrower. If they merely saw a similar object in the lender's possession, beit din does not compel him to produce it so that it can be identified by the claimant's signs, as stated in siman 297. The Shach cites Hagahot Ashri that where present sighting is established, an heir may recover an inherited item from a possessor based on its being of a type ordinarily lent or rented even if the heir does not know whether the possessor borrowed it or bought it from his father. All the more so, if witnesses establish that the father lent it, then even for an item not normally lent or rented, if it is now seen in the possessor's hands, the heir recovers it despite not knowing whether the possessor later bought it. Baal HaTerumot and other authorities similarly indicate that where there are witnesses and present sighting, seized property is removed and returned to the heirs.
Sold. The Shach explains that this passage concerns items ordinarily lent or rented, so that when the possessor originally claimed they were sold to him he would not have been believed. With items not ordinarily lent or rented, even if witnesses had seen them earlier, when at that time he claimed purchase and now claims a debt against them, he is believed up to their value through a migo that he could have remained with his purchase claim. Even with items ordinarily lent or rented, if he continues to maintain his original claim that he bought them, he is believed in the sense that otherwise no one could safely buy such an item from another, since the former owner could arrange a present sighting and thereby recover it. See the cited responsum of Mahari bei Rav.
Witnesses. Even if he expressly claims that he returned the item, he is not believed where the circumstances are publicly known so that such a claim is unavailable. Ri questions whether publicity can truly prevent a claim of return and therefore allows purchase or other claims through a migo of return. The Shach questions why the Rama rules simply against Ri. He suggests that the Rama's earlier wording, "some say that present sighting is not required," may allude to the fact that not all agree when the matter is publicly known. See Responsa Maharit 9 and 105.
Before. The same rule applies if beit din itself saw the item before the litigants began their dispute, as Rivash 345 states. Maharam Alshakar 121 cites Ri HaLevi and the Rambam that a migo is recognized only where the possessor links his statements without interruption. If he takes out a garment and immediately says, "This belongs to so-and-so, but he owes me such-and-such upon it," that is a migo. If he first says only that the garment belongs to so-and-so, remains silent, and after being told to return it adds that the owner owes him money, there is no migo. He must return the garment and collects nothing. See siman 80.
One. At first glance the Rama appears to mean that instead of two witnesses presently seeing the item there is only one witness. The Shach says this would be astonishing, because according to all opinions inability to swear against that witness would require payment. The Rashba's responsum instead concerns one witness who testifies that the item came into the lender's hands as a pledge but does not know how much was lent. Since the witness establishes that the lender received it lawfully, the rule of inability to swear does not apply. The Rama himself makes this distinction in siman 75, seif 13. Thus this passage too must mean a witness who testifies that it came as a pledge. Even so, the Rama's wording is imprecise, because the same dispute exists with two witnesses concerning the amount: according to the latter view the lender swears and collects, whereas according to the first he must return the pledge. The Shach and Taz criticize the formulation. See also siman 277, seif 7.
The first. The Shach concludes that the latter opinion is the principal law and that no one truly disputes it.
His. Even if the witnesses do not know that it still belongs to him. See siman 99. Sema.
In his possession. The Shach objects to the Sema's reading and explanation. The correct text is "in my possession." The Rama means that the holder does not claim, "I bought it and wish to keep the object itself," but only says, "Reuven gave me Levi's item as a pledge without my knowing it had been deposited with him," or, "Shimon sold it to me," while he does not insist on retaining the actual article and is willing to return it upon reimbursement. In such a case Levi must redeem it or reimburse the money because of takanat ha-shuk. This applies whether the holder describes it as a purchase or pledge, as stated in siman 356. The Shach adds that the Rama's wording is too terse and explains the ruling from the Rashba's responsum, its source.
