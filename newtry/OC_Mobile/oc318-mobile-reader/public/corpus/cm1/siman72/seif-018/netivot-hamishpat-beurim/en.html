Since he possessed a legal defense, we apply the presumption that he would not have returned the property without first presenting his claim before beit din, because, as Bava Batra says, who says that the rabbis would rule against him? This applies where his original claim was that he had purchased the item. If he claimed that it was collateral, however, he may say, "You repaid me and I returned your collateral," because that later repayment does not contradict the nature of his earlier claim. One might object from siman 79, seif 8. There, if outside beit din a person says before witnesses, "I never borrowed," and he did not say, "You are my witnesses," then even if witnesses later prove that he borrowed, he can still claim that he repaid before the denial. If he did say, "You are my witnesses," he cannot claim earlier repayment, though he may still claim repayment afterward. It might therefore appear that if he was confronted before witnesses in Nisan and said, "You are my witnesses, I never borrowed," and is sued again in Tishrei, he may say he paid between Nisan and Tishrei. If so, why should a prior claim of purchase prevent him from saying that he later returned the item? The Tur there gives a similar rule for a deposit. The answer is that siman 79 does not mean that one may claim repayment in the interval after the denial but before litigation in beit din. In that circumstance he is not believed. Once he denied the debt before witnesses and revealed an intention to deny liability, we presume he did not voluntarily repay afterward. Rather, the rule permitting a claim of later repayment means this: he denied the loan outside beit din, afterward the claimant produced witnesses in beit din proving the loan, and once those witnesses appeared the defendant remained silent and did not repeat his denial. Beit din then entered judgment against him. If he later says, "After the judgment I paid," he is believed. This distinguishes one established as a denier only before witnesses from one established as a false denier in beit din. A litigant established as a false denier in beit din is no longer believed concerning that money even to say he paid after the judgment, whereas one whose false denial occurred only before witnesses can say he paid after judgment. Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, rule 10, states this expressly: "But if he said after they concluded the litigation, 'I paid,' he is believed." Thus, before beit din has obligated him, no one is believed to say that after his deliberate denial he voluntarily paid, just as here. This is so even if he never said, "You are my witnesses." The requirement of that phrase in siman 79 concerns only a claim that he had paid before the denial. His words "I never borrowed" operate as an admission that he had not repaid. Since an admission outside beit din generally requires "You are my witnesses," without that phrase he can explain, "I meant only to jest with you. Just as you jested with me by demanding money I did not owe, I jested by saying I never borrowed, although in truth I borrowed and repaid." But a claim of payment after the denial is different. By saying he paid later, he admits that the debt was still due when he denied it. There is therefore no plausible explanation of joking, because he admits the claimant was then asserting a true debt. His denial necessarily expressed an intention to withhold the other person's money. Once he intended to deny the debt, we presume he would not pay without compulsion by beit din. Consequently there is no distinction based on whether he said, "You are my witnesses." These principles governing when a possessor is believed to claim that an item is collateral, and why a migo of unavoidable loss does not always help, will, God willing, be explained later in Hilchot Chazakat Metaltelin.

The case concerns witnesses who saw the item near the time of litigation, or where the claimant warned him, "Do not return it to me," as explained in siman 133. Otherwise the possessor has a migo that he could say, "He repaid me and I returned his collateral." See Shach, seif katan 91. Shach writes that if the possessor originally claimed purchase and now in beit din again claims purchase, he is believed. But if he originally claimed collateral and now in beit din again claims collateral, he is not believed, because a collateral claim ultimately depends upon the oath that beit din will impose. To understand Shach, one must say that even according to Raavad, cited by Shach in seif katan 70, who does not require such an oath, there is still a distinction. A creditor claiming collateral cannot finally appropriate the value without collection through beit din, whereas one claiming purchase says that the object already belongs to him outright. Yet this remains difficult. If one who claimed purchase before witnesses and then changes to a collateral claim is not believed, while Shach says that if he continues to claim purchase he is believed, why not believe the collateral claim through a migo that he could have continued to claim purchase? Tumim raises this point. The answer depends upon the rule in siman 80 that a litigant may sometimes change from one exempting claim to another. This is not fundamentally based on migo. The authorities sometimes explain it by saying he could have remained with his first defense, but that language is not literal. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 80 notes that Knesset HaGedolah, in the rules of migo in siman 82, rule 133, citing Rivash siman 193, states that one who contradicts his earlier words is not believed even with a migo. Rivash proves this from Bava Batra, where one first says, "He is my servant," and later says, "He is my son," and is not believed, although he could have given him the property as a gift. Thus a migo does not ordinarily cure an express contradiction. Tumim explains that changing from one exempting defense to another is permitted because the first statement is treated as imprecise, so he may clarify or replace it. A proof is siman 80, which says that once claims are formally written down a litigant may no longer change even from one exempting defense to another, because written claims are presumed precise. If the rule were truly based on migo, why should precision matter? He could admit that his first claim was false and say the second is true while still possessing the migo that he could have stood by the first. Necessarily, as Rivash explains, a migo does not help against one's own contradiction. The reason a shift between exempting defenses can work is that the first imprecise statement is treated as though it had not been made, or as though the second explanation had been intended from the outset. Shach therefore holds that such a change is possible only where, had the litigant said nothing initially, he would have been entitled to assert the second defense when he later spoke. With items not ordinarily lent or rented, even if witnesses merely saw the item in his possession and he said nothing, he could later claim purchase. Thus if he first claimed purchase and later says it was collateral, the first imprecise defense can be disregarded and the second considered. With items ordinarily lent or rented, however, mere observation creates the law of re'ah against the possessor. If he originally claimed purchase and later changes to collateral, even if we disregard the first statement as imprecise he is left in the position of one whose possession was seen without a valid contemporaneous defense. Indeed, even an immediate collateral claim before witnesses may not suffice where he cannot collect on that claim without an oath. All the more so where he now admits that the first purchase claim was false. He then effectively had re'ah without a valid claim. It follows that even in beit din he may be unable to change from purchase to collateral in such a case. Shach's statement that an original purchase claim is believed through a migo that he need not have shown the item applies, in my view, only where he intentionally displayed the item to the witnesses for the purpose of asserting purchase. Then "I need not have shown it" is a genuine contemporaneous migo. If he merely wore or used the item and witnesses happened to see it, then said it was purchased, he has no such migo because the witnesses saw it independently. All the more so if he asserted purchase only after more than the time of toch kedei dibbur from their observation. A retroactive migo is not recognized. Shach in siman 133, seif katan 1 similarly states that if the witnesses first saw the object and only afterward he claimed purchase, he is not believed. Everything depends upon the dayanim's assessment whether a genuine migo existed at the moment of disclosure. This resolves Tumim's challenge from Bava Batra 45, where an owner sees his garment in the artisan's possession and the artisan is not believed to say, "You sold it to me," even if he says so when it is seen. There the artisan did not intentionally reveal the item in order to make his claim; the witnesses happened to see it, so there is no migo that he could have concealed it. Shach adds that otherwise no one could ever safely purchase an object from another person, because the seller could later create re'ah against him. If the buyer truly purchased it, he would immediately claim purchase. Shach's meaning is that if a purchase claim made outside beit din were ineffective even when supported by a contemporaneous migo, then absent witnesses to every sale no buyer could protect himself. Under Shach's rule, once the buyer deliberately shows the item before witnesses and claims purchase while he still could have concealed it, that claim is supported by migo. Even if witnesses later see the item at a time when no migo exists, he remains believed by virtue of the earlier supported claim. This also explains Shach's concluding statement that if a second purchaser truthfully says he bought the item from the first possessor, he may nevertheless have to return it. The first purchaser, when directly confronted by the original owner, can be believed through his own migo that he could have concealed the item. A second purchaser who merely says, "I bought it from the first purchaser," and does not know whether the first purchaser actually acquired it from the original owner, cannot use his own migo to establish the first purchaser's title against the original owner. Once the original owner creates valid re'ah, the second purchaser may have to return the item despite truthfully proving his purchase from the first.
