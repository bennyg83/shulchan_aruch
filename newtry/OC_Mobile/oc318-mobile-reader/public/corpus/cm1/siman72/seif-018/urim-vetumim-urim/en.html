And he takes his collateral. The Shach writes that the order is reversed: first he takes the collateral, and afterward he swears concerning what remains in his possession.
Items not ordinarily lent, etc. The Shach cites the Rambam that even if witnesses testify that this owner lent this particular utensil to someone, but they do not know that he lent it to this possessor, the utensil does not thereby acquire the legal status of an item ordinarily lent. The Shach agrees. See Tumim, where I left his words requiring study, because according to the authorities below in seif 19 who disagree with the Rambam, this ruling likewise cannot stand. See there.
How it came into his possession. Even if witnesses saw the object delivered directly from Reuven to Shimon, but they do not know the legal basis of delivery, Shimon can say, "It was delivered to me as a purchase." Sema and Shach.
He can say, "I returned it." This excludes the view attributed by many authorities to the Rif that where there are witnesses to the original loan of the object, no witnesses of present possession are required. The accepted law in Israel is that witnesses must also have seen it presently in his possession. Shach, and this is correct.
He cannot say that it is purchased. The same applies to an item not ordinarily lent where there are witnesses to the original loan and witnesses of current possession. He is not believed to claim ownership. Nevertheless he must take a hesset oath that the owner did not subsequently sell it to him, because although witnesses testify that it was originally lent, perhaps it was sold afterward. Shach, correctly. If, however, he claims that it was sold at the very moment of delivery and the witnesses testify that it was delivered as a loan, there is obviously no hesset oath, because his claim is directly contradicted by witnesses. So writes Gidulei Terumah.
It is purchased in his possession. Likewise, where heirs are uncertain and find in another person's possession an object that belonged to their father, if it is an item ordinarily lent and there are witnesses of current possession, they may recover it even though they do not know whether their father lent it or sold it. This is certainly so where there are witnesses to the original loan. Shach in the name of many authorities, and this is clear. See below, siman 133, where this subject properly belongs.
For now he has no migo, etc. This applies specifically to items ordinarily lent, because witnesses of current possession deprive him of his migo. But for items not ordinarily lent, even where there are witnesses of current possession, he would have been believed had he claimed, "It was sold to me." Therefore if instead he claims, "It is collateral in my possession," he is likewise believed. So writes the Shach, correctly.
For we are witnesses, etc. The Shach writes that if immediately when witnesses saw the item he stated, "It is purchased in my possession," and consistently maintains that claim, he is believed even though he did not make the statement in beit din but only before witnesses. See Tumim, seif katan 35, where I proved at length that this is incorrect. Even if at the moment they see the item he explicitly tells witnesses that it is his by purchase, a declaration outside beit din is not a legally effective pleading. Once witnesses saw it in his possession, the owner may recover it. See there.
Likewise, if it is publicly known, etc. Public reputation that the item is in his possession defeats a migo, because we do not accept a migo against an established public report, as explained below in siman 150, seif katan 5. The Rama therefore states the rule without qualification, although Ri disagrees in a gloss to the Rosh and asks how such possession could become publicly known. There is no difficulty in a matter of public reputation. See Shach.
And if there is one witness. Meaning that one witness saw the item delivered to him specifically as collateral, but does not know for what amount it was pledged. The ruling holds that not only where there are two witnesses, in which case witnesses to present possession together with witnesses to the pledge plainly prevent the lender from denying receipt or claiming return and the borrower is believed regarding the amount, but even where there is only one witness, although the lender has a migo to deny the event and contradict the witness, as long as he does not actually contradict that witness, the witness is treated like two because this is a case in which one who cannot take the required oath must pay.
And some say that the lender is believed with an oath. This opinion also agrees that one uncontradicted witness has the force of two for this purpose. It holds, however, that even where there are two witnesses who testify that the object came into the lender's possession as collateral, together with witnesses of current possession, the lender is nevertheless believed concerning the amount, as stated below in seif 23. See what I wrote there in Tumim. Certainly this view applies where there is only one witness.
And Levi brought witnesses that it is his. Meaning witnesses who identify the object as Levi's and testify that Levi deposited it with Reuven. This constitutes both witnesses to Levi's title and witnesses explaining Reuven's possession.
Or Shimon admits it to him. Meaning that Shimon knows the object had been deposited by Levi with Reuven. Shimon's admission is therefore treated as equivalent to witnesses establishing Levi's title and the nature of Reuven's possession. See Tumim, where I concluded that this ruling requires study. As a matter of law, what does Shimon's admission accomplish? His legal position is derived from the seller or pledgor, and just as that person could have asserted ownership, we may raise that defense for the purchaser as well. See there.
Or they did not see it in his possession. Meaning that even if there are witnesses to the original deposit, so long as there are no witnesses that it is presently in Shimon's possession, Shimon has a migo that he could say, "I returned it," and is therefore believed to say that he purchased it.
And if he does not claim that it is purchased in his possession, etc. The Sema explains that he does not claim, "I purchased it from you in your presence," and therefore emends the text to read "in his possession," meaning in Reuven's possession when Reuven pledged it to me. The Shach questions this, though I do not understand his difficulty. The Shach retains the reading "in my possession" and explains that if there is no dispute over ownership of the physical object, and Shimon admits that he will return the object itself but claims only that it is pledged to him for money, then the entire preceding discussion is practically irrelevant. Even if Levi's ownership is established, Levi must repay the money because of the market protection enactment. Rashba likewise writes, as cited by the Sema, seif katan 62, concerning Reuven who deposited books with Shimon and Shimon transferred them to Levi, claiming that he had guaranteed a debt of Reuven to Levi. Although books are items ordinarily lent and there are witnesses of current possession, if they are in Levi's hands Reuven must pay because of the market enactment. However, in the case where Shimon admits that the books belong to Levi and had merely been deposited by Levi with Reuven, the market enactment should not apply, because Shimon knowingly dealt with deposited property and thereby caused his own loss. It further appears that although Levi may be obligated to reimburse Shimon, it requires study whether money can be extracted from Reuven. Perhaps witnesses to a deposit and current possession suffice only to recover the object itself, while Reuven can still say that he later returned and repurchased it, and therefore those witnesses cannot be used to extract money from him. This requires study. The cited Rashba responsum concerning books, quoted in Beit Yosef, chiddushim 18, likewise appears to hold that Levi has no monetary claim against Shimon merely because Shimon transferred the books to him. Perhaps there is a distinction between items ordinarily lent and a case with specific witnesses to a deposit. This requires study.
Because of the market enactment. The claimant must swear, as stated in the Mishnah. See Tumim, seif katan 37, whether the market enactment applies where the underlying loan was structured as an iska.
