How it came into his possession. Meaning, even if witnesses saw the borrower deliver it to him, if they do not know in what capacity it came into his possession, whether as a deposit, pledge, sale, or gift. The rule is stated explicitly below in siman 133.
Or, 'it never happened,' if there are no witnesses that he delivered it to him. Meaning, and they also did not presently see the object in his possession. In that case, even if he later admits in beit din that he has utensils ordinarily lent or rented and claims a lien against them up to their value, he is believed through a migo: he could have remained silent and answered, 'It never happened.' But if witnesses saw the object in his possession and recognized it, he cannot claim, 'I returned it,' or, 'It never happened.' See what I write in siman 64 concerning why we apply a migo of 'it never happened' or 'I returned it' even though had he made such a claim he could not have displayed the object, and see siman 133.
And there are witnesses who saw it presently in his possession. In Perishah I wrote that this appears to mean the witnesses recognize the utensils and know certainly that this particular object belonged to the borrower. If they do not know this with certainty, then even if they saw a similar object in his possession matching the claimant's description, we do not compel the lender to produce it before beit din merely so that it can be identified by the signs the borrower gave, as the Mechaber writes below in siman 296.
Some say we do not require that the witnesses saw, etc. See Hagahot Darkei Moshe there, where I wrote that it appears the Tur agrees.
If there is one witness concerning this, etc. Nearby in seif 23 the Mechaber states this law explicitly. I explain it there; from that explanation you can understand its meaning here.
See above siman 58, where I wrote, etc. There in seif 8 Rama states distinctions. If he did not deposit the object with him, but rather seized an object from another and said, 'This object is not yours but belongs to So-and-so, and he owes me,' he may assert a lien upon it. Rosh, klal 106, writes that if an agent deposited Reuven's property with Shimon and Shimon withheld it because of a claim against Reuven, Reuven can sue the agent and the agent can sue Shimon. So cites Darkei Moshe.
Because the object is under his control. Rashba writes in a responsum, cited by Beit Yosef, concerning Reuven who claimed against Shimon, 'You have my books.' Shimon answered, 'Yes, but I guaranteed your debt, and when the lender came to collect I gave him the books as collateral so he could be paid from them.' Rashba ruled that even if the books are considered items ordinarily lent, they are no longer in Shimon's possession. From the person who presently holds them, Reuven cannot recover them until he pays what was lent against them. See Darkei Moshe there.
And Levi brought witnesses that it is his. Even if those witnesses do not know that it still belongs to him presently. See below siman 99.
Reuven is not believed to say, etc. Responsa Rosh, klal 3, also proves this. The Tur cites it at the end of this siman.
If he does not claim, 'It was purchased and is in my possession.' It appears the text should read, 'in his possession.' This refers to what immediately precedes it. Meaning, Shimon does not claim that he knows the object was purchased into Reuven's possession; rather, he only says it was given to him as collateral.
