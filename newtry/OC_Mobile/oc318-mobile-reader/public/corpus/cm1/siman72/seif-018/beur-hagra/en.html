(Collected note) Some say prior viewing is not required... specifically... See there 46, "he spoke well..." Tosafos asks how the prior claim of complete denial fits the rule of viewing. It appears that the initial exchange did not occur in beit din, because in beit din the court could have compelled him to produce the item. Rather, outside beit din he denied possession, while in beit din he later claimed a debt against it or the like and produced it. The court then recognized the owner's item. Since he produced it only after the pleadings, he remained believed, whereas the owner wished to establish prior viewing before the court session. This explains the Gemara's phrase, "he spoke well..." The earlier denial was not fraudulent, because a person need not reveal his claims except in beit din. Rava's novelty, "viewing was taught," therefore means viewing before coming to beit din. This also explains, "If he is clever..." If the statement had been made in beit din he had already denied possession, and once he admits the debt in beit din he must pay. Outside beit din, however, such statements can be casual or evasive. Thus the entire discussion concerns events outside beit din. If he establishes prior viewing, he can no longer later claim return. (End of note.)

Some say that the lender. That opinion writes that even where two witnesses saw the pledge, the lender is believed because a creditor acquires a pledge under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule. The borrower is therefore the one seeking to extract and bears the burden of proof. This differs from an artisan, who does not acquire the object. Bava Metzia 48a supports acquisition by meshichah. (Collected note) Some say... Even with two witnesses that it came as a pledge, Rabbi Yitzchak's rule gives the creditor a proprietary interest. Ramban applies the rule even to a pledge taken at the time of the loan, because the sugyos in Pesachim, Kiddushin, and Gittin indicate that the limiting statement was only a rejected answer. Rambam, chapter 13, halachah 3, expressly indicates that the creditor can still assert his claim even where witnesses exist. The Rama's restriction to one witness, here and in siman 75, seif 13, therefore requires study, as the Taz and Shach note. It appears the Rama relied not on this rationale but on another responsum distinguishing the case from the ingot of Rabbi Abba because there the object was snatched unlawfully, creating the status of a robber. That also resolves Tosafos' question regarding Avimi. Yet the responsum is abbreviated, and its case may have been where one witness testified against the lender and the lender claimed an additional fifty, which creates only a rabbinic oath, not the Torah rule of inability to swear. The primary law remains that even with two witnesses the lender can assert the claim, as the Shach rules. The Taz's objection from simanim 89 and 90 is mistaken because here the object itself is returned without an oath, and only afterward does the lender take a shevuas hesses on his monetary claim, as the Shach explains. (End of note.)

(Collected note) And the fact that a person... Bava Batra 46b. If the artisan himself says that he purchased it, he is not believed. The artisan lacks chazakah only while the item is in his own possession. Once it is in another person's possession, the other person's independent possession controls and there is no distinction between an artisan and anyone else. The Rama's words derive from a responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur in seif 33, which concludes that since the claimant knows only through his friend, he is not believed; this implies that if he knew the facts himself, he would be believed. This explains the Rama's later phrase, "but if Shimon..." and not as the Shach understood it from a responsum of Rashba. (End of note.)
But if Shimon. The Gemara asks, "What is the difference between the first clause and the latter clause?" It implies that only because the artisan is not believed regarding himself is he also not believed to establish another's right. An independent third party, however, is believed. This is the meaning of the Gemara's comparison.
