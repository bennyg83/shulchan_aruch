The borrower swears, etc., and takes his pledge, etc. The wording is reversed. The law is that the borrower first takes his pledge and only afterward takes a hesset oath. The Rambam writes this explicitly in chapter 9 of Hilchot To'en, halachah 3, and Baal HaTerumot likewise writes so, as I cite below in seif katan 84. This also appears from seif 23 below and is straightforward.
Which are not normally lent or rented, etc. The Rambam writes in chapter 8 of Hilchot To'en that any object presumed not to be normally lent or rented retains that presumption even if witnesses come and testify that this person has lent or rented such an object. The Beit Yosef cites this. The Bach, seif 13, writes that the Rambam's words are astonishing, since surely a presumption does not prevail against witnesses. He suggests that the Rambam means that even though witnesses testify that the owner once lent or rented this very object, we presume that it was later returned to its owner, since it is not an object normally lent or rented, and the present possessor can therefore claim that after its return he purchased it from the owner. Thus the witnesses' testimony is true, while the possessor's claim that he later purchased it is also accepted by virtue of the presumption. The Bach adds that all the poskim disagree with the Rambam and rule like the Rif and Tur. This is imprecise. When the Rambam says, "even if witnesses came and testified that he lent or rented this," he means that witnesses testify that the owner lent or rented an object of this kind to someone else, not that he lent it to the present possessor. This is explicit from the Maggid Mishneh and is straightforward. The ruling is correct and no authority disputes it. This is clear.
How they came into his possession. Even if witnesses saw the objects pass directly from the borrower's hand into the lender's hand, if they do not know in what legal capacity they came into his possession, whether as a deposit, pledge, sale, or gift, the lender is believed to claim, "You sold them to me," or, "You gave them to me," as the Rosh and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write below in siman 133 at the end of seif 2. Consequently he is likewise believed to make a claim against the object up to its value.
He is believed to say, etc. Therefore, even if he now brings the object before beit din and does not claim that it was purchased, returned, or that the alleged transaction never occurred, but instead says only, "He owes me such and such against this object," he is believed up to its value by virtue of a migo that he could have made one of those other claims. See Responsa Mahara ibn Chaim, siman 105.
And likewise even objects normally lent. The same applies to objects normally rented, or to objects not normally lent or rented where witnesses establish that they came into his possession as a loan but there are no witnesses who presently saw them in his possession. He may say, "I returned them to you," or, "I purchased them," or, "I hold them as a pledge for such and such an amount," by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "I returned them." Know that the Mordechai in Chezkas HaBatim, in the sugya of an artisan, writes that according to the Rif and the Rambam, chapter 6 of Hilchot She'eilah, where there are witnesses that the owner lent him the object, even if the witnesses did not presently see it in his possession, the possessor is not believed by a migo that he returned it. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 335, likewise understands the Rif and Rambam this way. Other poskim, however, imply that the Rif and Rambam agree that he is believed, and the Beit Yosef in this siman appears to understand them this way. The Maggid Mishneh at the end of chapter 6 of Hilchot She'eilah also expressly explains the Rambam accordingly. Although the Rif and Rambam do not expressly state anywhere that witnesses must also have presently seen the object in his possession, this nevertheless appears to be their primary view, for throughout the law a migo that one returned the object is a strong migo. The Mechaber himself writes in Bedek HaBayit below in siman 133 that this is the view of the Rif and Rambam. Many poskim in many places likewise rule that one is believed by a migo that he returned the object, and that witnesses must specifically have presently seen it in his possession, as the Tur and Mechaber hold. This is also the primary conclusion of the Gemara in many places and is the accepted law in Israel.
Or that the matter never occurred, if there are no witnesses that he delivered them to him. This also assumes that witnesses did not presently see them in his possession. But with objects normally lent or rented, even if they were not originally delivered before witnesses, or with objects not normally lent or rented where there are witnesses that the owner lent them to him and witnesses presently saw them in his possession, the possessor is not believed to say, "I purchased them," or, "They are a pledge." Rather, the owner takes his objects and takes a hesset oath against the lender's claim. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, beginning of part 2, writes this. Gidulei Terumah asks there that if witnesses testify that the owner lent the objects to him, why should the owner have to take a hesset oath that they were not later made a pledge? He already has witnesses. He discusses this somewhat and leaves it difficult. In my humble view there is no difficulty. The same question could be asked regarding objects normally lent or rented: since their established character is sufficient to overcome the possessor's chazakah and extract the object from him, why should that presumption not also exempt the owner from an oath? Clearly the answer is that the presumption that the object is normally lent or rented, and likewise the witnesses that it originally came to the possessor as a loan, establish only that the lender is not to be treated as the owner merely because he possesses it. They do not exclude the possibility that afterward the owner borrowed money upon it. At most, the lender's claim is an ordinary oral monetary claim. Even if the pledge had always remained physically with the borrower, if the lender now claimed, "I lent you money," would the borrower not have to swear? This is straightforward.
Objects normally lent, etc. Where there are witnesses, Hagahot Ashri at the end of HaMekabel writes in the name of Or Zarua that if the object is of a type normally lent or rented and witnesses presently saw it, Reuven can recover his inheritance from Shimon even though Reuven himself does not know whether Shimon holds it on loan or purchased it from Reuven's father. The definite ownership claim defeats the possessor's chazakah. This comes from a responsum of Ri Baal HaTosafot in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 32, where Ri brings proof for the rule. It appears that further proof can be brought from the beginning of HaIshah Shenitarmelah, where Rabbi Yehoshua agrees that if a possessor says, "This field belonged to your father, but I purchased it from him," he is believed because the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted, but if there are witnesses that it belonged to the father he is not believed. This is explained below in siman 115, seif 20. There is no reason to distinguish here between land and movable property. Movable property that is normally lent or rented, when witnesses presently saw it, has the same status as land for this purpose. See below in seif katan 93. It further appears that all the more so where Reuven has witnesses that his father lent the object to Shimon, even if Reuven does not know whether Shimon later bought it, if witnesses presently see it in Shimon's possession Reuven recovers his inheritance, even if the object is not one normally lent or rented. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 14, part 1, likewise writes that where there are witnesses and present sight, the possessor gains nothing from his possession and we remove what he seized and return it to the heirs. The Beit Yosef cites this below at the beginning of siman 110, seif 5, and the other poskim there likewise imply the same. See there.
And there are witnesses who presently saw it in his possession. This applies even if the possessor said nothing before the witnesses concerning how he holds it. Once they saw it in his possession without qualification, he can no longer say, "This never occurred." He likewise cannot say, "I returned it," when they saw it continuously until he came before beit din, or in any similar situation that leaves him unable to claim that he purchased it. It is also necessary that the witnesses know that the object originally belonged to the borrower. However, even if they do not identify it with absolute certainty but only by distinguishing signs, the possessor has no migo that the matter never occurred, because he is afraid to deny having it after witnesses saw in his possession an object matching those signs. The complete laws relevant here are explained in simanim 133, 134, and 296. See there.
Rather, they saw it earlier, and at that time he claimed that they had been sold, etc. In my humble view, this speaks of objects normally lent or rented. When, at the time witnesses saw them, he claimed that they had been sold to him, his claim had no legal force, and we treat it as established that he did not return them, since he asserted a claim under which he was entitled to retain them. But with objects not normally lent or rented, although witnesses saw them earlier, at that very time he claimed that they were sold to him. Therefore, when he later claims, "I have such and such against them," he is believed up to their value by virtue of a migo that he could have remained with his earlier claim of purchase. This appears correct to me. See carefully. See also Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 2, in the responsum of Rabbi Yosef ibn Lev.
For from now on he no longer has a migo, etc. Nevertheless, it appears that if afterward, before beit din, he continues to claim that the object was purchased, as he had already said before the witnesses, he is believed. The moment he displayed the object before the witnesses he said that it was purchased, and at that time he was believed by virtue of a migo that he could have declined to display it. He now simply remains with that same claim. This differs from the ruling stated immediately afterward, that even if, when the witnesses saw the object, he said, "He owes me such and such against it," he is not believed. There, by admitting before the witnesses that the object belongs to the adverse claimant, there are now both witnesses and present sight. His further statement that the owner owes him such and such has no force, because everything depends upon the oath that beit din ultimately imposes upon him, as explained in Responsa Rosh and in the Tur below in siman 133. Here, by contrast, he never admitted that the object belonged to the claimant. Alternatively, in that later case the possessor is obligated by law to take an oath based upon the Mishnah's rule concerning those who swear and collect, and therefore everything depends upon that oath. Here, however, he is obligated only in hesset by the rabbinic enactment of Rav Nachman. Moreover, if this were not so, no person could safely buy from another an object normally lent or rented, because the seller could later arrange for witnesses to see it and say, "I lent it to you." Rather, when witnesses see it, the buyer must be able immediately to tell the truth and say that he purchased it. The Maggid Mishneh similarly writes in chapter 8 of Hilchot To'en, in proving that the market protection enactment applies even to objects normally lent or rented. Otherwise, no one would buy such objects from another. The Maggid Mishneh must be speaking where there are witnesses who presently saw the object. If so, why is his proof necessary? Perhaps every buyer can simply be given the claim that the seller from whom he bought it could have said, "I purchased it," by virtue of a migo that he returned it. The answer is that where there are witnesses of present possession, the original possessor from whom the buyer derives his right could no longer make such a claim. The Maggid Mishneh's proof is therefore that without the market protection enactment no one could buy an object normally lent or rented, because its original owner could later create present sight and, when the buyer truthfully said that he purchased it from the intermediate seller, the object would have to be returned because that intermediate seller himself could not have claimed purchase by a migo that he returned it. See carefully.
For we, etc. The same law applies if the matter is publicly known. Even if the possessor expressly claims, "I returned it to you," he is not believed. However, Teshuvot Maimoniyot and Hagahot Ashri cite Ri as questioning this: how can a matter be so publicly known that the possessor cannot claim that he returned it? If he can claim that he returned it, he should likewise be believed to claim purchase or other claims by virtue of that migo. Ri's objection appears correct to me, and I am surprised that the Rama rules simply like Rabbeinu Tam rather than Ri. Perhaps when the Rama begins the gloss with "some say that we do not require, etc.," he means to include this dispute concerning a publicly known matter, so that it is only one view and not universally accepted. Regarding the earlier statement at the beginning of the gloss, by contrast, I have found no authority who disputes it. See carefully. See Responsa Maharit, siman 69 and siman 105, folio 128.
Specifically where witnesses saw it before he sued in beit din, etc. The same applies if the judges themselves saw it before the parties' claims and dispute arose. So rules the Rivash, siman 345. Responsa Maharam Alashkar, siman 121, similarly writes in the name of Rabbi Yosef Halevi and the Rambam: "We rule every day that a migo exists only when the person's words follow one another immediately without interruption. If he pauses, there is no migo. Thus, if one produces a garment from his possession and says, 'This belongs to so and so, but I have such and such against it,' that is certainly a migo. But if he produces the garment and says only, 'It belongs to so and so,' remains silent, and they tell him, 'Since you admitted that it belongs to so and so, return his property,' and only then he says, 'I have such and such against it,' there is no migo. He returns the garment and collects nothing." See there and see below in siman 80.
And if there is one witness concerning this, some say, etc. The Rama's wording is not precise if, as it first appears, he refers to the case currently under discussion, where witnesses presently saw the object in his possession, and means that if a single witness presently saw it there are two opinions. In such a case, according to all opinions he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and therefore must pay. The Rashba says otherwise in Responsa, siman 998, only where the witness testifies that the object came into his possession as a pledge but does not know how much was lent upon it. There the lender is not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear, because the witness himself testifies that the object lawfully came into the lender's possession on account of his money and was acquired to him as security for that money. By contrast, where the single witness merely establishes present possession in the face of the owner's claim, Responsa Rashba there and siman 1040 clearly imply that the lender is obligated to swear and cannot swear and therefore must pay, comparable to the law below in siman 75, seif 13. The Rama himself makes this distinction in his gloss below in siman 75, seif 13. We must therefore read his words here as follows: "If there is one witness concerning this," meaning one witness that the object came into his possession as a pledge but who does not know the amount lent upon it, then there are two opinions. Even so, the Rama's phrase "if there is one witness" remains imprecise, because the same dispute exists where two witnesses testify that the object came into his possession as a pledge and presently see it there. According to the latter view, the lender swears and collects, as expressly implied by Responsa Rashba, siman 998, and stated explicitly in Responsa Rashba, siman 1040, even with two witnesses, because once a creditor acquires a pledge he is treated as the possessor. See what I write above in siman 278, seif 7. According to the first view, even with two witnesses the lender must return the pledge, as explicitly stated in Responsa Ramban, siman 84. If the Rama intended to state a novel point that even with only one witness the lender is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and therefore must pay, that novelty does not belong here. Both the responsum attributed to the Ramban and the Rashba's responsa there assume that if the law with two witnesses were that the lender must return the pledge, then with one witness the creditor would be one obligated to swear who cannot swear. That issue belongs in siman 75, where the principal rules of one obligated to swear who cannot swear are discussed, and the Rama already writes it there in seif 13. The correct wording here should therefore have been: if there are witnesses that the object came into his possession as a pledge and they do not know how much was lent upon it, some say that the borrower takes the pledge and swears, while others say that the lender swears and collects. See carefully.
And the Mechaber's conclusion follows the first view. See what I write below in seif 23, seif katan 109, where I prove that the Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, and Baal HaTerumot follow the latter view, that this appears to be the primary law, and that no one truly disputes it, because the responsum printed as Responsa Ramban is in fact the Rashba's own responsum, reflecting his initial ruling before he later retracted. See there.
And see above, siman 58, etc. See what I wrote there at length.
And regarding the rule that a person can claim that it was purchased, etc. Until the Rama's words, "since there are no witnesses how it came into his possession, or they did not see it in his possession, etc." The Rama's language is obscure and awkward. The result of the Responsa Rashba from which these laws are taken, cited by the Beit Yosef in his twenty seventh additional note, is as follows. If Levi brings witnesses that he deposited the object with Reuven and witnesses presently see it in Shimon's possession, Shimon must return it to Levi, and Reuven is not believed to say that after receiving it as a deposit he later bought it from Levi. This is also proved below in siman 134, seif 4. Nevertheless, the fact that Reuven is not believed to say that he later bought it means only that Levi may recover the physical object. Levi must still reimburse Shimon for the money Shimon paid or lent against it because of the market protection enactment, as explained below in simanim 133 and 357. Even with objects normally lent or rented that are presently seen in the buyer's possession, the buyer must be reimbursed under that enactment. Witnesses that the object had been deposited with Reuven are no stronger than the presumption applying to objects normally lent or rented. See also what I write below in siman 356 concerning whether the market protection enactment applies to one who bought from an artisan. Similarly, if Shimon admits that the object is Levi's in the sense that Levi deposited it with Reuven, Shimon must return it to Levi without receiving anything immediately, even if he lent Reuven money upon it, because Shimon's own knowledge places him in the position of witnesses that the object was deposited and is presently seen. See Responsa Maharit, siman 39. If Shimon does not make that admission, however, then even if there are witnesses and present sight, if Shimon claims that he knows Reuven purchased it from Levi, he is believed by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "You sold it to me," which would be believed because there are no witnesses how the object came into Shimon's own possession. This is also proved in siman 134, seif 4. And if there are no witnesses and Shimon makes no admission, then even if it is known that the object once belonged to Levi and Reuven claims that Levi deposited it with him, Reuven's statement cannot prejudice Shimon. All this is explicit in the Rashba. The Rama's words must be forced into conformity with this structure. The Sema, seif katan 64, on the Rama's statement that Reuven is not believed to say that he purchased it, writes that this is also proved from Responsa Rosh as cited by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of this siman, seif 39. In my humble view the cases are not comparable, because there, although there are no witnesses, the object was not deposited with him. See there carefully. However, the Rashba appears to hold further that if Shimon admits only that the object once belonged to Levi and that Levi deposited it with Reuven, but Shimon does not know whether Reuven later purchased it from Levi, we do not plead for Shimon that perhaps Reuven did later purchase it. Shimon's own admission is treated like witnesses of deposit and present sight, even though there are no independent witnesses who presently saw the object and Reuven himself could have claimed purchase by virtue of a migo that the transaction never occurred or that he returned it. One cannot explain the Rashba as speaking where Shimon admits that the object still belongs to Levi, for then there would have been no need for the reason that Shimon stands in the place of witnesses of deposit and present sight. It would be obvious that he must return Levi's property if he knows that it still belongs to Levi and that Reuven improperly pledged it. The proofs the Rashba brings from Chezkas HaBatim and Eizehu Neshech likewise clearly show that he speaks where Shimon does not know with certainty that Levi still owns the object. Yet this aspect of the Rashba's ruling does not appear primary to me. Why should Shimon's admission stand in place of witnesses of present sight? Since Shimon is believed to say that he purchased the object from Reuven, or that Reuven gave it to him as a pledge, by virtue of a migo that the entire transaction alleged by Levi never occurred or that it was returned, we should likewise plead on Shimon's behalf that Reuven first purchased it from Levi. Reuven himself could have claimed purchase by virtue of the same migo that the alleged deposit never occurred or that he returned it. Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 32, in the responsum of Ri Baal HaTosafot, expressly says so, as does Hagahot Ashri at the end of HaMekabel in the name of Or Zarua. The Sema below in siman 134, seif katan 12, likewise writes regarding one who bought from an artisan that where there are no witnesses of present sight we do not extract the object from the buyer. This necessarily rests on the principle that we plead for the buyer whatever claim the artisan himself could have made, rather than saying that the buyer stands in the place of witnesses of present sight. Tosafot in Chezkas HaBatim, Bava Batra 30 at the end of column 1, likewise prove the same principle. There the Rashba Baal HaTosafot writes that the land should remain with the buyer even without a personal migo, because we plead for a buyer based on the seller's ability to say, "I bought it from you," by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." Although this buyer himself knows that the land had belonged to the claimant and therefore resembles a single witness, the seller's migo remains effective, as appears from the case of Rabbi Abba's ingot: absent the rule that one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay, the defendant would have been believed to say, "I seized what was mine," by virtue of a migo that he could have denied seizing it and thereby contradicted the witness. In the land case there is no oath because one does not swear concerning land. Ri in Tosafot there appears not to dispute the law itself. He says only that the proof from HaMocher Et HaBayit is not a proof, as Hagahot Maimoniyot, chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en, explains, and as I cite below in siman 146, seif 14. I have also already written above that Ri in Teshuvot Maimoniyot expressly disagrees with the responsum of Rabbi Shlomo ben Aderet. Nor can one answer that if Reuven had claimed that he returned the object, Shimon would contradict him and thereby function as a single witness. Shimon is not a valid witness on this point because he has a financial interest in the outcome. He could have claimed, "Reuven returned it to Levi, and Levi later sold it to you," and therefore Shimon is compelled to say that Reuven did not return it. If Shimon himself said that Reuven had returned it to Levi, he would have to swear against Levi, who knows definitely that it was not returned. Any witness who requires an oath is not a witness, as proved in HaIsh Mekadesh and explained below in siman 121, seif katan 49. Only with respect to the claim, "It was never yours," did Tosafot say that the buyer would be like a single witness, were it not for the rule that no oath is taken regarding land. A practical difference arises with movable property, such as an object normally lent or rented, where witnesses presently see it in the buyer's possession and the buyer knows that the object once belonged to the claimant, even though the claimant has no witnesses that it was his. The buyer must return the physical object, because in such a case the seller could not have claimed purchase by virtue of a migo that the object was never the claimant's, since the buyer would contradict him and has no financial interest in testifying merely that the object originally belonged to the claimant. The seller would therefore have to swear against that witness, and there is no migo. It appears that Rabbi Shlomo ben Aderet follows his own view here, for he does not accept this rule of the Rashba Baal HaTosafot, as the Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en in his name and as I cite below in siman 146, seif 14. According to the conclusion I reach there following the Rashba Baal HaTosafot, the same rule should apply here. This appears correct to me. It is possible to interpret the Rama's words in a manner consistent with everything I have written. See carefully.
And if he does not claim, "It was purchased and is in my possession." The Sema writes that the text should read "in his possession" and that it refers back to the immediately preceding clause, meaning that Shimon does not claim that he knows Reuven purchased it, but only that Reuven gave it to him as a pledge. This explanation is not satisfactory, because the Rama writes that Levi must redeem it from Shimon because of the market protection enactment, and for purposes of that enactment there is no distinction between Shimon claiming that he purchased it and claiming that it was given to him as a pledge, as explained below in siman 356. Rather, in my humble view the meaning is this: "If he does not claim, 'It is purchased and is in my possession,'" meaning that Shimon does not claim outright ownership in order to retain the physical object itself. Instead he merely says that Reuven gave him Levi's object as a pledge, that he did not know Levi had deposited it with Reuven, and that he lent money upon it. In that case Levi must redeem it because of the market protection enactment, even if there are witnesses that Levi deposited it with Reuven and witnesses presently see it in Shimon's possession, as I explained above. The same applies if Shimon says that Reuven sold it to him but Shimon does not seek to retain the object itself and asks only to be repaid the purchase price. Levi must reimburse that purchase price, as explained below in siman 356. The Rama's phrase means only that Shimon does not claim, "It is purchased and is in my possession," for the purpose of keeping the object itself. According to this reading, the word "in my possession" is correctly retained, as it appears in all the printed editions and in the Levush. See carefully.
