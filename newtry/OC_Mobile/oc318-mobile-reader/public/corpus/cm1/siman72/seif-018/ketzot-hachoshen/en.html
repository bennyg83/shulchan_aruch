Rashba rules that where one witness establishes the amount of the loan or supports the creditor's claim, the creditor can be believed with an oath because a creditor acquires the pledge and is regarded as the possessor. The artisan is different because, according to Rashba, an artisan does not acquire the item as collateral when it did not enter his hands in that form. Rama in Even HaEzer, however, appears to hold that an artisan can acquire pledge rights in the object when he later holds it for his wages. This revives the question: why is an artisan not believed without migo in the same way as a creditor on collateral? The answer lies in the timing of the debt. As long as the artisan has not returned the completed object, the wage does not yet have the status of a matured loan. Although we say that wages accrue from beginning to end, the monetary loan arises only when the object is returned. Until then there is no personal loan debt upon which Rabbi Yitzchak's pledge acquisition can rest. "There is no maneh and therefore there is no collateral." Once he returns the object, the debt becomes a loan retroactively, but at that moment he no longer has possession. Therefore he cannot rely on present pledge possession to establish the amount. This also explains the kiddushin case. Rabbeinu Yerucham says that if an artisan already completed jewelry for a woman and afterward betroths her with the wages while still holding the jewelry, she can be betrothed because the item then functions as collateral for his accrued wage. The correct reading is that the work was completed as an ordinary paid job, so he was holding it for his wages, and when he returns it as part of the kiddushin arrangement the monetary wage becomes fixed and the pledge relation can support the transfer. But if from the outset he was making the jewelry as the very act of kiddushin, he never held it for a separate wage and there is no pledge. Ran and Ritva cite the opinion that an artisan does not acquire a pledge and attempt to prove it from the law of delayed wages: if an artisan truly acquired the item as collateral, the employer should never violate the prohibition against delaying wages after the item is returned, because it would be as though he had already paid through the pledge and then received it back as a loan. The proof can be understood this way. An artisan who acquires the improvement in the object has a real property interest against his wages, and therefore no delayed wage prohibition applies even after return. If ordinary artisan possession had equivalent pledge status, the same should follow. Since the law indicates otherwise, they infer that ordinary artisans do not acquire a pledge. Rama can still distinguish. The baraita saying that while the garment remains with the artisan there is no delayed wage prohibition can be explained because the time for payment has not yet arrived. The end of an artisan's hiring is the return of the object. Only then does the first payment period begin. Yet if the artisan truly had acquired pledge rights before return, then even after return the prohibition should remain absent because the debt had already been secured like payment. This is the force of Ran's proof. Accordingly, if a householder actually gives the artisan a separate pledge for the wages, then even if the artisan later returns that pledge, there should be no prohibition of delayed wages, because the artisan had already acquired a pledge against the wage and the later return resembles repayment followed by a new loan. This is a practical consequence of the analysis.
