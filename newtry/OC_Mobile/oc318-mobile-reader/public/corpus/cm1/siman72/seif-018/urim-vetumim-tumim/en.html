How so, objects not ordinarily lent, etc. The Shach cites Rambam that even if witnesses testify that this particular utensil was once lent to someone else, but do not know that it was lent to the present possessor, that alone does not classify the utensil as one ordinarily lent or rented. The Shach treats this as undisputed. I am very doubtful. Rambam follows his own definition that an object is "ordinarily lent or rented" only when it was made or designated from the outset for lending or rental. Under that view, proof that the owner lent it occasionally does not change its category. But most authorities disagree with Rambam and make the matter depend on the owner's ordinary conduct: whether he is accustomed to lend or is particular about his objects. According to them, witnesses who show that this very utensil was lent to others demonstrate that the owner is not particular. If the present possessor is also a person close to the owner, as described later, it is entirely reasonable to assume that it was lent to him as well. The Shach's broad conclusion therefore requires study.
That according to that claim it should not be returned. Responsa Panim Meirot asks that this contradicts the Rosh cited below, where one who says in front of witnesses that an object is held as collateral must warn the other party not to return it except before witnesses, lest he later claim that it was returned. There is no contradiction. There the claimant says that the object is collateral. His own account therefore contemplates eventual repayment of the debt and return of the collateral. He must therefore protect himself by requiring witnessed return. Here he claims that the object was purchased outright. According to his own account there is no reason for him to return it at all. The cases are plainly different.
And even if, when the witnesses saw it, he said, etc. The Shach limits the rule. He says that if the possessor initially said in front of the witnesses, "I bought it," and afterward in beit din says, "It is collateral in my hand," he is not believed, because the collateral claim requires an oath. But if he maintains the purchase claim from beginning to end, he should be believed, because at the moment the witnesses saw the object he already explained his possession. This does not appear correct. In the immediately preceding seif katan the Shach himself rules that with an object not ordinarily lent, if witnesses saw it and the possessor then changes from a purchase claim to a collateral claim, he is believed by a migo that he could have maintained the purchase claim. If so, the same reasoning should apply here. If his purchase claim would have remained credible, the collateral claim should be credible by migo. The fact that collateral requires an oath does not resolve the inconsistency, because even with an object not ordinarily lent the collateral claimant must swear, yet the Shach still relies upon the migo. More fundamentally, the Rosh's rule that even a person who announces his claim in front of witnesses when they see the object is not believed is drawn from Bava Batra 46. The Gemara advises a clever person to create a "seeing" by saying, "Why are you holding it, is it not because I have money with you?" This indicates that even if the possessor explains his claim while showing the item, he loses the special credibility of possession. If the Shach's distinction were correct, this passage would be a clear proof for the Geonim that every collateral claim requires their special oath. No authority uses it that way, and Ramban, who limits the Geonic oath, would be difficult in the case of the Pumbedita deceivers. The correct explanation is that a statement made casually before witnesses, outside beit din, does not preserve the evidentiary force of the possession. The rule is not limited to claims requiring an oath. The baraita likewise says that if one sees his garment in an artisan's hand and asks what it is doing there, and the artisan says, "you sold it to me," his statement has no effect. The natural reading is that this exchange occurs at the very time of seeing. Yet the artisan is not believed. An artisan and an object ordinarily lent have the same evidentiary status. The Hagahot HaRosh at the end of Perek HaMekabel also says that once witnesses saw the item, the possessor may no longer rely on claims whose force comes from private possession. If there was no "seeing," he can rely on the migo of denial or return. This clearly contradicts the Shach's distinction. Responsa Maimoniyot likewise supports the Rosh. The matter is therefore clear against the Shach. His proof from Maggid Mishneh concerning market protection for objects ordinarily lent actually points the other way. If a purchaser could always say, "the seller told me it was purchased," and we made for him every claim available to his seller, market protection would often be unnecessary according to the Shach. Yet Maggid Mishneh says market protection is required precisely because otherwise no one could safely buy such an object. This indicates that the seller's earlier assertion does not preserve his credibility once the object is seen in another's hand.
Reuven is not believed. In the first case, where witnesses establish that the property belonged to Levi and also saw it in Reuven's possession, there is no difficulty: Reuven is not believed. The second case is more difficult. There Shimon knows that the property came to Reuven as a deposit, and Rashba treats Shimon's knowledge like witnesses of deposit and seeing. Most authorities appear to disagree. Rashba's foundation is the passage in Bava Batra concerning one who admits that the land originally belonged to the other party, and the passage in Bava Metzia that orphans' money should not be lent against a distinctively identifiable object because the depositor may later identify it and take it. He treats the guardian's knowledge as equivalent to witnesses who saw. But the Rama below rules that where the father of the orphans explicitly instructed that an object was his, identifying marks alone do not require its return. This can be reconciled because there the guardian does not recognize the object by sight, only through signs. Signs do not establish ownership absolutely. Where the father gave instructions, we may say the signs happened to coincide. But if the guardian personally recognizes the item by sight, even that instruction would not help. Still, Rashba's proof from the land case depends upon his position that even if the seller occupied the land for one day, we do not make for the purchaser every claim the seller could have made. The Mechaber and Rama below rule otherwise: if the seller occupied it for one day, we make for the purchaser the claims available to the seller. Ramban explicitly compares this to movable property deposited with a person who dies. Even if the heirs admit that the item came from the depositor, if there were no witnesses who both saw and established the deposit, we make for the heirs the claims their father could have made, such as return or later purchase. The same should apply to a purchaser. The Bava Metzia case of orphans' funds can also be explained differently. It concerns minors whose property is under the direct supervision of beit din. Once beit din itself sees the identifiable object, beit din functions as the effective witness who has seen it. The child's possession does not preserve the father's private migo against the beit din that now controls the property. With adult heirs or an ordinary purchaser, however, their possession is an extension of the predecessor's legal possession, and they receive the claims he could have made. Accordingly, where the seller told the purchaser that the item was purchased and the purchaser now holds it, we make for him the seller's available claims. Since the purchaser is in possession, he can at least plead that the halachah follows these authorities. Rashba's broader rule therefore requires study.
He must redeem it because of the market enactment. Asifat Zekenim, Nimukei Yosef, and Ritva ask why, when an identifiable pledged object is later claimed by its original owner, the orphans should lose. The owner who recovers his object should reimburse them under the market enactment. They cite Raavad and others that in an iska arrangement market protection does not apply because the funds remain substantively under the owner's control and the pledge is only security. I discussed the difficulty at length elsewhere. There is another answer according to those who hold that for heirs, and certainly purchasers, we make the claim that the deposited half of an iska was lost through unavoidable accident. The loan half may indeed have to be reimbursed under market protection. But with respect to the deposit half, the claimant says: "The object itself is mine. You seek to extract money from me only through market protection. I can answer that perhaps the deposited funds were lost through unavoidable accident, so nothing is collectible from me." We do not allow the heirs' failure to articulate that uncertain claim to worsen their legal position. The same is especially clear where the borrower has died. If the identifiable object itself returns to its owner, the lender's heirs can no longer keep it merely because it served as security, while the lender's underlying claim concerning the deposit half can be met with the usual claim made for heirs. This explains the possible loss to the orphans.
