Which they recognize in his possession. See Baer Heitev, who writes that even with articles normally lent or rented, if the possessor maintains his original claim that he purchased the item, he is believed. The Shach explains that immediately when he showed the item to witnesses he said that it was purchased and was then believed by a migo that he could have refused to show it. He now merely maintains that earlier claim. This differs from the later case where, even when witnesses saw the item, he said that the owner owed him a specified sum. There, once he admitted before witnesses that the item belonged to the claimant, there are witnesses plus observation, and his further statement that the claimant owes him money is ineffective because everything depends on the claim he makes when taking the oath. Tumim, seif katan 35, disagrees. In his view, even if the possessor explicitly told witnesses when they saw it that he had purchased it, a statement made outside beit din has no legal standing in his favor. His admission outside beit din that it once belonged to the claimant binds him adversely, while his self serving claim that he purchased it does not. Consequently, the case is deemed one where the item was seen. According to Tumim, the Rosh's reference to the need for an oath is not exact. See there. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 135, writes that the correct distinction is as follows. With items that the witnesses do not independently know belong to the claimant, and their only basis is the possessor's own statement before them that the item belonged to so and so, the possessor is likewise believed when he adds that he purchased it, even outside beit din, because his entire adverse admission arises from his own mouth, and we apply the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permits. An exception is where he claims that money is owed to him, because that claim requires an oath, and outside beit din such an assertion is insufficient. Where the witnesses themselves know from observation that the item belonged to the claimant, however, and the migo consists only of the fact that the possessor could have hidden the item from them, then we disregard his claim outside beit din and the case is like one where they suddenly saw the item in his possession. This is why the Rosh precisely gives the reason that an oath is required: it explains why even where the witnesses' knowledge depends upon his own statement, that statement can still create the relevant observation. This also resolves the sugya that 'if he is wise, he will make it a case of observation.' Tumim had argued that if the sole reason were the need for an oath, the Gemara would itself prove the Geonim's disputed ruling that a pledge claimant requires an oath. According to Rabbi Akiva Eiger, however, that sugya concerns a case where the witnesses independently recognize the item and need no statement from the possessor at all.
