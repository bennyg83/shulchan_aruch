One who lends upon collateral, etc. This means that after the loan was made, the borrower granted the lender permission to use the collateral; the loan was not originally conditioned upon that use. The Rashba's responsum concerns that case, and immediately afterward explains the consequence of the later permission. Where use was part of the original loan terms, the issue may also involve interest and the lender's status as a hirer. The ruling here addresses only permission subsequently granted.
It is possible that mere permission makes him a borrower and liable for unavoidable accidents. The Rashba writes that the permission may be equivalent to the lost article's value, making the lender a borrower. This follows his general view that a custodian of a lost object is a paid custodian because of Rav Yosef's benefit. Yet the matter depends on the nature of the permission. If the owner merely allows occasional use while retaining the principal benefit, the lender may not receive all benefit and may remain a paid custodian rather than a borrower. If the permission grants unrestricted personal use, he resembles a borrower and is liable even for accidents. The same distinction applies where the permission was given after the debt was paid. One must also distinguish between permission that is legally effective and use that is prohibited as interest. Where the use is forbidden, the prohibition does not necessarily reduce civil liability after the lender actually benefits. Because the Rashba presents the matter as a possibility rather than a definitive rule, one should treat extraction of money beyond ordinary custodial liability as doubtful unless the facts show that all benefit of use belonged to the lender.
