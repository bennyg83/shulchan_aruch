(Seif 8) “Reuven borrowed a sword from Shimon.” The Hagahot Mordechai explains that Reuven accepted the ordinary care required for an ordinary sword, not the care required for an unusually valuable sword. It compares the rule to the Gemara’s case of one who gave a gold dinar to a woman and said, “Guard it, for it is silver.” If it was stolen, she pays only the value of silver; if she damaged it directly, she pays the value of gold. The same is therefore true of the sword: if he damaged it directly he must pay its full exceptional value. The Mechaber accordingly speaks of the sword being lost, meaning without negligence. At first glance the case might involve a gentile extorting an inflated payment for the sword, as the Sema implies by comparing it to the case in siman 378 of one who damaged another’s horse and the gentile owner then made an excessive claim. But if so, even direct damage should not create liability for the extorted amount, just as the horse-damager is exempt from the gentile’s excess demand. Moreover, the Mordechai says “not the guarding of an expensive sword,” indicating that the sword was genuinely valuable. The correct reading is therefore that the sword truly had unusual value. If damaged directly, the borrower pays its entire true value, like the gold dinar. Maharshal questions the Mordechai: the depositor of the dinar himself caused the misunderstanding by calling it silver, whereas one who deposits an object without qualification should be protected for its full value. Otherwise a depositary of a gem worth one hundred gold pieces could say he thought it worth only one. Maharshal agrees that if a gentile merely extorts more than the object’s real value, the custodian is exempt. The Mordechai can be defended. The value of a gem depends on expert assessment, and a custodian accepts responsibility for its potentially great value. A sword, by contrast, has a generally known ordinary value, and an extraordinarily valuable sword is uncommon. A custodian does not contemplate such an unusual case, much as one does not accept responsibility for an uncommon accident. His liability is therefore limited by the ordinary appraisal of an ordinary sword.
