Seif 12. He must include in his oath that he does not know that the collateral was worth more than the amount of the debt. See the Shach, who asks why we do not apply here the special enactment made for one whose property was taken or lost while in another’s custody, allowing the borrower to swear and collect. There is no difficulty. In an ordinary deposit the depositor usually does not carefully know the precise value of the article. If the custodian can simply say, “I do not know,” the depositor may suffer a loss even when telling the truth. The Sages therefore enacted that he may swear and collect so that an honest claimant is not left uncompensated. A lender who takes collateral is different. A lender ordinarily evaluates the collateral and knows approximately what it is worth, and he can therefore make a definite claim just as the borrower can. When both make definite claims there is no basis to favor the borrower by creating a special oath-and-collection remedy. The ordinary rule should apply, placing the oath upon the defendant. Only in the unusual case where the lender truly does not know the value would one have reason to consider allowing the borrower to swear and collect, and the Sages did not institute a special enactment for an uncommon situation. This is clear and correct.
There, in the same seif, concerning one who is obligated to swear but cannot and therefore must pay. See Shach, seif katan 51. Regarding the dispute whether this rule applies when the defendant had no reason to know the facts, I initially thought to prove from Rashi that where he could not have known he does not have to pay. Rashi in Bekhorot 9 explains that where the lamb belongs to the owner and is not given to the kohen, the kohen is the one seeking to extract property and must bring witnesses. Why, I asked, does Rashi say “witnesses,” implying two? One witness should suffice to create an oath, and once the owner admits uncertainty he would be one obligated to swear but unable to do so. That would suggest that because he had no way to know what the donkey gave birth to, the rule does not apply. On reflection this proof is mistaken because if he denied the claim he would not have been obligated to swear at all, since this is money with no single claimant and he could say, “I will give it to another kohen.” There is, however, a strong proof for the Rambam’s view that even where the defendant had no reason to know, inability to swear can create liability. Bava Kamma 17b derives from “by the testimony of witnesses” an exclusion for one who admitted a fine and witnesses subsequently came. The Gemara asks what that verse teaches according to the view that later witnesses do create liability. Why not answer that the verse requires two witnesses, whereas with only one witness the injured party would remain exempt if he says he does not know, because he could not have known? The Gemara’s failure to give that answer indicates that even where he had no reason to know, a single witness can impose the oath and inability to swear results in payment. If he affirmatively contradicts the witness, of course one witness cannot extract payment. This appears to me a significant proof.
