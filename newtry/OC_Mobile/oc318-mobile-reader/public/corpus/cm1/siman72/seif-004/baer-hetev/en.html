Permission. This means that only afterward did the lender give him permission to use the pledge, but he had not lent him the money on that condition. This is the case addressed in this responsum of the Rashba. The Beit Yosef then cites another responsum holding that even if the lender already used the pledge, he does not become a borrower, because all the benefit is not his, as the borrower permits him to use it in consideration of the loan. See there. That second responsum explicitly concerns a loan made on this condition, contrary to the Sema, who questions why the Mechaber and Rama rule simply like the first responsum when the second says the opposite. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. The Taz makes the same distinction; see there. Still, there is a difficulty with the Rama, because the Rashba follows his own view that one who guards a lost object is a paid custodian. Accordingly, where one uses the money of a lost object he should be a borrower. The Rama should therefore have stated that according to the view that a lender on a pledge is only an unpaid custodian, permission to use the pledge does not make him a borrower. Perhaps one can distinguish that here the owner expressly gave permission, which is worse, whereas with money from a lost object he gave no express permission. Nevertheless, as a matter of law it appears that money cannot be extracted from the lender if the pledge was lost through unavoidable circumstances. If it was stolen or lost, however, I would rule that the lender is liable, because express permission makes him a paid custodian according to all opinions. It also appears to me that one who deposited a utensil with another, whether the custodian was unpaid or paid, and afterward gave him permission to use it, plainly becomes a borrower according to all opinions. These are the words of the Shach; see there.
