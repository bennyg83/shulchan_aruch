It is possible that even by merely granting permission, and so forth. These words come from Rashba's responsum. He is uncertain whether a lender who merely receives permission to use collateral thereby becomes a borrower liable even for unavoidable accidents, and he compares the question to money belonging to a finder of lost property which may be used and for which the finder can become liable. Shach asks why there is any doubt. A borrower becomes liable from the moment he receives an object for borrowing, even before actual use. If the owner permits use, why is this not an ordinary loan? Shach therefore suggests that collateral is different because permission was given only in connection with money already owed to the lender. In an ordinary deposit where the owner later permits use, Shach thinks everyone would agree the custodian becomes a borrower. This is strained. Borrower status requires more than the owner's unilateral permission. The recipient must accept the object as something he wishes to borrow. If the owner merely says, "You may use it if you want," while the custodian gives no indication that he wants to enter a borrower relationship, a person cannot be made a borrower against his will. This is exactly the principle Ran gives regarding unrestricted deposited money. Even though the custodian may use it, R. Nachman holds he is not automatically a borrower liable for unavoidable accidents, because perhaps he has no desire to borrow the money. That is the force of Rashba's phrase "mere permission." And even after actual use there is room to say he does not become a full borrower. The permission to use was itself the benefit for which he already bears paid-custodian responsibility. If the benefit of use is treated as compensation for guarding the object, not all benefit belongs to him in the manner of a borrower. Money differs because once it is actually spent it becomes an ordinary loan, and a borrower of fungible money must repay regardless of accident. That liability does not depend on the usual rule that all benefit belongs to a borrower of a specific object. Rashba nevertheless compares collateral to found money because found money presents a stronger model. Money held for a lost owner may be used under a beit din arrangement, and the finder can become liable on a heightened basis even without first expressly asking to borrow it. The permission arises for the owner's benefit as part of the judicial structure of returning lost property. There are two ways to understand this. First, fungible money is different. From the moment use is legally permitted, the custodian need not return those exact coins. The relationship is already close to a loan, whereas a pledged utensil remains the owner's specific object until actual use. Second, and more fundamentally, Rashba can follow R. Ephraim's approach. A finder of lost property is already a paid custodian through the perutah of R. Yosef. Beit din permits use of the money so that the lost owner receives an additional benefit: the finder now bears even broader responsibility. The permission is not gratuitous enrichment of the finder but a judicial device securing the owner's property. That explains why found-money permission can elevate liability more than ordinary permission by a private owner. It also answers Tosafot's objection: why should doubling a custodian's benefit automatically convert a paid custodian into a borrower? The answer is that it does not happen automatically. In the lost-property case Chazal specifically conditioned the use privilege on enhanced responsibility for the owner's benefit. This framework clarifies several difficult passages concerning me'ilah. Rambam rules that where unrestricted money of hekdesh was deposited with a moneychanger and the moneychanger spent it, neither party necessarily commits me'ilah in the ordinary private-deposit manner. The owner never directly instructed him to spend it, and the use permission can be understood as a custodial arrangement rather than a completed transfer. The ordinary Mishnah that imposes me'ilah can be read in the earlier Torah framework where hekdesh custodians did not bear ordinary shemirah liability. There, granting use to the custodian produces a clear benefit to him without obtaining the compensating custodial protection familiar from private property. After Chazal strengthened responsibility for guarding hekdesh so that people would not treat sacred property lightly, permission to use can again be viewed as tied to increased custodial exposure, reducing the independent benefit that would create me'ilah. This also helps explain why Rambam distinguishes a Jewish craftsman from a gentile in certain me'ilah cases. With a Jew, rabbinic acquisition and custodial rules affect when the benefit becomes legally complete; with a gentile, the monetary acquisition can occur differently.
And after clarifying all this, Rashba's doubt is substantial. A private owner who merely permits a lender to use collateral has not imposed a beit din condition comparable to found money. The lender was already a paid custodian under the accepted collateral rule. Why should an additional permitted benefit automatically make him a borrower liable for unavoidable accidents? Merely increasing a paid custodian's compensation does not turn him into a borrower. For this reason there is strong ground not to extract money for unavoidable accidents merely from permission to use. Rama's stronger ruling can nevertheless be defended through Raavad's distinction concerning found money. Ordinary deposited money does not make the custodian a borrower because he fears the depositor may arrive suddenly and demand the exact value; the use benefit is therefore limited. With found money, the owner is unlikely to appear soon, so the permission is much more valuable and can produce borrower-like liability. Collateral resembles the latter more than an ordinary deposit. During the loan term the borrower is not expected to redeem it immediately, and when the collateral is a specific object the lender can use it while keeping the object intact and instantly return it if repayment occurs. The practical fear of being unable to return it is much smaller than with spent money. On that reasoning, permission to use collateral can indeed make the lender a borrower. Thus Rashba's doubt is real, while Rama has a substantial basis for the stricter side.
