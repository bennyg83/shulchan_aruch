And he gave him permission to use it. This means that permission was given after the loan and after delivery of the collateral. If permission was part of the loan transaction, not all benefit would belong to the lender, since the borrower also receives the benefit of the loan money in exchange for granting use. Here, however, after the loan was already made, the borrower separately grants permission to use the collateral, unrelated to the loan itself. It is therefore reasonable to classify the lender as a borrower of the item. Shach, and this is correct.
It is possible, etc. The Shach understands the doubt to arise because the relationship began as a loan. Even though permission to use the collateral was later given, perhaps not all benefit is considered the lender's. But if a person deposited an item with another and afterward gave him permission to use it, the Shach holds that all agree he becomes a borrower. I wrote in Tumim that according to the Mechaber the logic is the reverse. The case here is where the lender or custodian did not ask for permission to use the item, for if he asked he is plainly a borrower. Rather, the owner volunteered permission. In an ordinary deposit this resembles unrestricted money deposited with a custodian, which he is permitted to use, yet until he actually uses it he is only a paid custodian. So too here. But in the special case of a lender holding collateral, where according to Rashba and the Mechaber he is already a paid custodian because of Rabbi Yosef's perutah, the additional permission may elevate him to a borrower. See Tumim, where I discussed at length the distinction between this case and money from a lost object that one is permitted to use and for which he is treated as a borrower. It is for this reason that Rashba and the Shulchan Aruch remain uncertain and use the expression "it is possible."
He is a borrower, etc. The Shach asks that according to the Rama, who allows the view of Rabbah that a lender holding collateral is only an unpaid custodian, this should resemble unrestricted deposited money which the custodian may use yet for which he is only a paid custodian. See Tumim. I distinguished between money and a tangible object. With an object, even the Rama may hold that permission to use it renders the holder a borrower according to all opinions, and therefore the Rama saw no need to express doubt. According to this, even an ordinary custodian who is given permission to use a deposited object becomes a borrower. This is also reasonable. Still, see Tumim, where I concluded that the matter does not escape doubt, and the burden of proof rests on the claimant.
