He may swear generally that he owes him nothing. See Responsa Chavos Yair, siman 194, concerning Reuven, who sued Levi on a document for one hundred Reichsthaler. The obligation was not enforceable under ordinary law because it was an asmachta, but the document stated that it had been made with a formal handshake and actual oath. Beit din therefore ruled that Levi must pay, as stated in siman 207, seif 19, that an oath validates an asmachta. Although Levi protested that he had never actually sworn and that the scribe merely wrote the formula, his protest was rejected because the document expressly said that the oath had been performed in actual effect, as the Sema writes in siman 73, seif katan 18. Many days later property belonging to Reuven came into Levi's possession, and Levi refused to return it. Reuven sued again. Levi answered generally, 'I owe you nothing.' Beit din ruled that Levi must swear. Chavos Yair was asked whether, since everyone knows that the first obligation was only an asmachta and Levi himself knows that he never swore or gave a formal handshake, though this is known only before Heaven, he may swear generally that he owes Reuven nothing. Chavos Yair answers that he may certainly swear in general terms that he owes nothing, or that he has a counterclaim equal to that amount, as stated in the Rama to siman 72, seif 17. He notes that the case there requires analysis, because there the person could seemingly explain the facts directly rather than swear in general terms, whereas in the present case he cannot reveal the underlying basis. This permission applies, however, only where the document records the past fact that the undertaking 'was made with a formal handshake and oath,' and witnesses signed, or even where the obligor himself wrote or signed it using language such as, 'I accepted upon myself with a formal handshake and oath,' meaning an admission of what had already been done. Most handwritten obligations begin, 'I admit,' and what follows is governed by that introductory admission. In such a case, if he knows in his heart that no formal handshake or oath actually occurred, he may later swear generally that he owes nothing. If, however, he wrote, 'I hereby swear to pay,' or, 'I hereby accept upon myself by oath, curse, and formal handshake,' Chavos Yair holds that the writing itself has the status of an actual oath, and Levi was properly obligated to pay Reuven's first claim.
