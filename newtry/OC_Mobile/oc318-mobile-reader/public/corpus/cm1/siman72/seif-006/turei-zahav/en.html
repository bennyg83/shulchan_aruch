(Ibid.: If there is one witness to this.) This means one witness saw the pledge in the creditor’s possession when it first came to him and also sees it in his possession now at the litigation. The Rama’s view requires explanation. The established rule is that “one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay” applies only where, had there been two witnesses giving the same testimony, their evidence would itself have imposed monetary liability. The Rashba explains that if one witness saw the pledge enter the creditor’s hands and now sees it there, but does not know the loan amount, the creditor is one who must swear and cannot swear, because two such witnesses would remove his migo and make the borrower’s claim prevail. The Rashba then presents the opposite possibility: even two witnesses would not make the creditor liable, because by drawing the pledge into his possession he acquired a proprietary interest in it. On that view, one witness does not create the status of one unable to swear. The entire reason for denying that status is thus the proposition that even without a migo the creditor prevails against two witnesses, which contradicts the Tur’s ruling in seif 13 that the borrower prevails. Here the Rama implies that two witnesses do enable recovery from the creditor and that only the single-witness case is disputed, although the two questions depend on each other. It is also difficult that he links this dispute to the Mechaber’s later ruling in seif 53. Once the beginning of this seif establishes that the borrower swears and recovers whenever the creditor lacks a migo, a single witness should necessarily make the creditor unable to swear. If the Rama intended to dispute the beginning of the seif, he should have written there that some say the creditor is believed even without a migo. According to the Rama’s conclusion, which follows the Rashba’s first approach, the borrower prevails without a creditor’s migo, and therefore the creditor facing one witness should indeed be treated as one obligated to swear who cannot do so. Later, with God’s help, I will explain why the practical ruling should nevertheless be that he is not in that category.
