His son too becomes a paid custodian, etc. The benefit is that he holds the collateral against the money, just as an artisan holds the object against his wages. The Nimukei Yosef says this in the name of the Ritva and his teacher. Maharshal disagrees, arguing that this benefit applies only to an artisan who earns wages. Otherwise the Gemara need not have sought another reason why a lender upon collateral is a paid custodian. In my view, the Ritva's distinction is sound. When collateral is delivered at the time of the loan, the lender gains nothing beyond the bargain he chose, since without collateral he would not have lent. When he already lent and later receives collateral, he gains substantial new security for money that was previously unsecured. That benefit can make him a paid custodian. The Gemara's search for another reason concerns collateral taken with the loan, as do Tosafot. The son did not lend anything. When the father's collateral comes into the son's hands, he receives security for money he himself never advanced, and that benefit resembles later collateral. Even according to those who deny that benefit to the original lender after the loan, they may agree regarding the son, who now holds security without having made the loan. There is also an independent basis according to Rav Yosef. The son must spread and air the collateral to preserve it, and while engaged in that mitzva he is exempt from giving a coin to a poor person. Maharshal says this applies only to a finder, because a lender preserves collateral for his own benefit. But Tosafot, the Rosh, the Tur, and the Rif expressly apply Rav Yosef's coin to a lender who spreads or airs collateral, even where the loan itself also benefits him. The same rationale therefore applies to the son. The Rashba's statement that the lender is a paid custodian "at the time of the loan" is not exclusive; each later act of necessary preservation likewise creates the mitzva benefit. Thus the son's paid-custodian status is correct.
