Seif 28. If the two parties do not agree, both must swear. Netivot HaMishpat asks why in siman 138, where neither of two claimants wishes to swear, they divide without an oath. He struggles greatly and derives a novel law. In truth there is no difficulty. There, the basic rabbinic enactment from the outset is that the property be divided. The oath is only an added condition upon a division already imposed by the underlying law. If neither wishes to swear, the underlying division remains. Here, by contrast, the competing claims are unequal. One party claims three dinarim and the other only one. If we say each should simply keep what he presently holds, one side effectively prevails on a claim of three dinarim while the other prevails only on one. The borrower would have no reason to accept such an unequal result without oaths. But to direct an equal division would itself create an entirely new substantive allocation that the Sages never enacted in this case. Since no independent rule of division exists here, the court cannot invent one merely because the parties refuse to swear. The necessary result is that both must swear. This also explains why the Mechaber does not frame the case where each side’s monetary claim is exactly two dinarim. If their claims are equal and neither wishes to swear, each can simply remain with what he holds, just as in the parallel rule of siman 138. The present law is stated where the claims are unequal, so standing on present possession cannot fairly resolve them. Netivot also asks what happens if a lottery determines which one swears first and the other later seeks to turn the oath back upon him. I do not understand the difficulty. Nothing in the Shulchan Aruch requires a lottery. If the parties voluntarily agree to one and accept its consequences, they have chosen that arrangement and need not be protected from a manipulation they knowingly accepted. This seems simple and correct.
