Nimukei Yosef cites Ritva that the lender's son can become a paid custodian because he holds the collateral as security for the money, like an artisan who retains a customer's utensil for his fee. Maharshal rejects this based on Tosafot: a lender does not become paid merely because he holds collateral for his debt, since if he had not made the loan he would never have needed the collateral and has gained no separate compensation from holding it. Shach answers that this objection applies to the original lender only. The son did not make the loan. His father already advanced the money, and the son now receives the independent benefit of having collateral in hand to secure repayment. I do not understand that distinction. If the original lender retains the collateral for decades, we do not say that after the first year the lending act is ancient history and the later years of security create a new paid benefit. The collateral was received as part of one continuing loan relationship. The son steps into his father's place. Had the borrower not provided collateral at the outset, the father would not have made the loan. The son's later possession does not create a new benefit independent of that original condition. The broader question whether holding an object as security for preexisting money makes one a paid custodian is itself disputed. Rosh in Kiddushin expressly says that collateral taken after the loan cannot be classified like an artisan's utensil merely because the creditor holds it for payment. Otherwise R. Yitzchak's special rule of creditor acquisition would be unnecessary. An artisan is different because he has an independent fee earned through his work, and retaining the utensil gives him additional security for that earned fee. Two elements are present: he earned compensation and he retains the object for that compensation. Tosafot in Bava Metzia similarly say that after an artisan has completed the work, mere retention for an old debt is not enough unless his words show the original paid custodianship is still continuing. Thus, according to Rosh and Tosafot, merely holding collateral for an old monetary claim does not create paid-custodian status. This also answers Shach's proof from the damaged ox in Bava Kamma. There the injured party who seizes the ox is to be paid from that very ox. It functions as an apotiki and the claimant's collection is tied to the object itself. That creates a stronger proprietary relationship than ordinary collateral securing a debt collectible elsewhere. Ramban in Kiddushin likewise distinguishes an object formally delivered as collateral from an artisan's mere retention of a utensil. Formal collateral can create an acquisition under R. Yitzchak; simple "holding for my fee" is not the same. There are nevertheless major authorities on the other side. Baal HaMaor and authorities cited in Or Zarua treat possession securing money as enough to create paid-custodian status in some circumstances. Raavad, in the case of heirs who bought an ox that later turned out possibly defective, says the heirs are paid custodians of the ox because their purchase money remains at risk with the seller and the ox stands as practical security for its return. This cannot be based on the perutah of R. Yosef: minors are not ordinary charity obligors, and they believe the ox is their own property. Raavad plainly relies on the benefit of holding security for money. Rosh himself does not accept that theory generally, which also explains Tur's disagreement with Rambam in the later defective-sale case. Ritva's own language can be harmonized with this dispute. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ritva asking why the Gemara needs the perutah of R. Yosef to make a lender on collateral a paid custodian instead of simply saying he holds the object for his money. Ritva gives two answers. One follows Tosafot: absent the loan, there would have been no collateral, so the possession is not independent compensation. The other says that holding for the money can indeed explain paid-custodian status up to the amount of the debt, while the perutah of R. Yosef is needed to impose responsibility for value exceeding the debt. This second answer likely underlies Nimukei Yosef's statement about the son. On that understanding, "he holds it for the money" is closely related to Shmuel's collateral theory. The possession gives security value up to the debt amount, while the mitzvah benefit may be needed for the excess. That also explains Rabbenu Baruch's language that so long as the creditor physically holds the collateral, he benefits by not having to chase the debtor for payment. Accordingly, Nimukei Yosef's rule has an identifiable foundation.
But Ritva elsewhere appears to rule like R. Yosef, making the son's case difficult if the son has no original lending mitzvah. The answer is that Ritva follows his own principle that later spreading and airing produces a mitzvah-based custodial benefit only where the relationship began as a mitzvah. The son's custodianship did not begin with his own mitzvah, since his father made the loan. Ritva therefore needed the separate security-benefit rationale. Even then, the stronger rule should be limited. According to the view that security possession itself creates paid-custodian status, it naturally extends only up to the debt that the collateral secures. Liability for excess value still needs a different basis. Maharshal's practical conclusion is therefore compelling: regarding the lender's son, we should not extend liability beyond the amount of the debt merely from "holding it for the money." Knesset HaGedolah records a doubt whether Nimukei Yosef's rule applies even to a minor son and cites a compromise from Maharashdam. In my opinion a minor plainly lacks ordinary custodial liability. His father's prior custodianship cannot by itself impose a new personal shemirah obligation on the child. Only if one treated Shmuel's rule as an independent debt consequence entirely outside the laws of custodians could there be room to say otherwise, and I have already shown above that practical halachah does not adopt such an unrestricted theory. Thus a minor son should be exempt from personal custodial liability.
