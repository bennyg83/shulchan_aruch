His son also becomes a paid custodian. Nimukei Yosef explains this through the benefit of holding the pledge for collection, as with an artisan holding an item for wages. Maharshal disagrees. Tosafot indicates that an artisan's paid custodian status rests on a combination of benefit from the work and holding the item for wages, whereas Rashi gives greater force to explicit holding for collection. In the son's case the pledge came to him automatically through inheritance, so there is no new act manifesting that he holds it for collection. Thus even according to Rashi the rationale is weak. If the father left estate assets, however, there is room to collect from those assets if the father's custodial liability had already attached. This depends on when a custodian's property becomes encumbered. If liability arises only at the time of negligence or loss, the father's death is itself an unavoidable event preventing further guarding, and the estate may not be liable. If liability attached from the beginning of the custodial relationship, the estate remains bound.
His son becomes a paid custodian. According to Rama in seif 2, the original lender is only an unpaid custodian except to the amount of the debt, yet the Sema and Maharshal say that even the son loses the amount of the debt if the pledge is lost. This requires study. Rama explains the father's loss of the debt through the implied understanding that if the pledge is lost, the loan is lost, not through Rabbi Yitzchak's special acquisition. That implied condition was made with the father at the time of the loan. After the father's death, the son neither received the loan money nor entered that condition. If the father's estate did not become bound before death, why should the son lose the inherited debt? One could say that the original implied condition concerned the continued existence of the pledge itself and was understood to apply even if the pledge later came into the son's hands. If the pledge is lost while the debt passes to the son, the original understanding still says the debt is lost. This is plausible, but remains in need of study.
