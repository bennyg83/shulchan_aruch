If collateral declined in value while held by the lender, and the borrower claims with certainty that it deteriorated through rot, mice, or moths, then if the lender says he kept it in a place protected from mice and rot and aired it properly, the loss was unavoidable. He takes a heses oath to that effect and is exempt. If he admits he failed to protect it or air it properly, he must pay the loss. If they dispute the amount of depreciation and the borrower admits the original value equaled only the debt, as where the loan and value were both twenty and it is now worth ten, while the lender says it was originally worth only fifteen and demands five, the borrower takes a heses oath and is exempt. Although he partially admits, the collateral itself is immediately tendered, and the lender takes no oath because even originally it was worth no more than the debt. If it originally exceeded the debt, as where the borrower claims it was worth thirty against a twenty loan and is now worth fifteen, demanding ten, while the lender says it was worth twenty-five and owes only five, the lender swears it depreciated by only ten, credits five, and collects the remainder from the collateral, because he is believed upon oath up to its value. Certainly, if even after depreciation it equals the debt and they dispute the loss, or the lender says he does not know the amount of depreciation, the lender takes a heses oath and is exempt.
