That its value diminished, and so forth. Sema and Bach ask why the lender takes only hesset. As a custodian, should he not take the formal custodian oath that he was not negligent? They answer that the Torah's custodial oath applies where the object is wholly lost, stolen, or broken, not where the object remains and the parties dispute how a partial depreciation occurred. There is some support from the Gemara's question, "Where do we find the Torah's custodian oath?" The Gemara establishes the case with a deposit evidenced by a document rather than simply saying the object is present and the parties dispute whether its damage occurred negligently. Still, Shach is right that the distinction is difficult. Why should partial destruction differ from total destruction? Shach proposes a broader rule: the Torah imposed the custodian oath where the depositor is uncertain whether negligence occurred. If the depositor says definitely, "You were negligent," and the custodian definitely denies it, the case becomes an ordinary full denial requiring only hesset. Shach supports this from Riva's reading that the Torah's special oath applies to claims characteristic of deposits, where the owner often cannot know what happened, rather than to ordinary face-to-face denials. After examining the Tosafot sugyot, I do not accept Shach's general rule. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 98 distinguish whether a claim about which cow died is part of the custodial oath or an ordinary monetary denial. Their analysis would be unnecessary if every definite claim of negligence automatically ceased to be a custodial oath. Hagahot Maimoniyot states Riva's view explicitly: even where the owner knows the custodian's accident story is false, the special deposit oath can apply. The phrase "for he is this" limiting an oath on total denial belongs principally to ordinary loan-type claims and does not simply eliminate the custodial oath whenever the depositor is certain. Thus Shach's broad principle is not correct. Nevertheless, Mechaber's present ruling can still stand for a different reason. Below in siman 294 Mechaber holds that where there is no principal oath on theft or loss, the custodian does not independently swear a formal oath merely that he was not negligent. That negligence oath arises only through gilgul. Here the collateral is present, so there is no primary oath that it is not in his possession. Therefore no formal negligence oath remains, and hesset is sufficient. For Tur and Rama, who treat the negligence oath more independently, the possessor may still rely on a migo of denial or return where no sighting evidence exists. Accordingly, Sema's result can be correct even though his total-loss versus partial-loss distinction is not the real reason.
Or he did not air it properly, and so forth. The duty to air collateral is itself part of hashavat aveidah, and that is why the lender can receive the perutah benefit of being occupied in a mitzvah. Why then should failure to perform the mitzvah create monetary liability? The answer is that once the lender accepts the collateral into his custody, he accepts the ordinary custodial duties Chazal attach to that possession. Even though the duty to air the object originates in a mitzvah, neglecting the required care is negligence and creates liability. This is true even according to Tur, who generally classifies a lender on collateral only as an unpaid custodian. The distinction is straightforward. No one forced the lender to take the collateral. By voluntarily accepting it, he entered the custodial relationship and its legal duties. The fact that fulfilling those duties is also a mitzvah does not make their neglect nonmonetary. This supports Rama in Orach Chaim siman 443. Rama rules that one holding another's chametz as a deposit who fails to sell it before Pesach can be treated as negligent. Chok Yaakov asks: selling the chametz is an act of hashavat aveidah, so why should failure to perform a mitzvah create damages? The answer is exactly as here. Once the custodian accepted the chametz, he accepted the legally required standard of preservation as defined by Chazal. Failing to sell it when that is necessary to preserve its value is negligence within the custodial relationship and can create liability.
