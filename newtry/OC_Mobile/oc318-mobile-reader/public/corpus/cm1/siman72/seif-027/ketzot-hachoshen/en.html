Its value decreased. The authorities ask why the lender is not obligated to take a Torah oath as a paid bailee. Chacham Tzvi answers that the essential oath of bailees is the oath that the object is not presently in his possession, as explained in Shulchan Aruch siman 294, while the other oath-elements arise through gilgul. Here the pledge is physically present, so the oath that it is not in his possession does not apply. His answer is correct. Yet this creates a difficulty with Shulchan Aruch in seif 30, which rules similarly where the pledge decreased in value and the parties dispute whether the decrease occurred through an unavoidable event. If Shimon admits that it decreased but it is unknown whether by accident or negligence, Shulchan Aruch applies the rule that one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay. But if no oath that the object is not in his possession applies, there is no Torah oath at all. See what I wrote in seif katan 33.
He must pay the loss in value. According to what I wrote in seif katan 9, Shulchan Aruch regards the creditor as a paid bailee because of Rabbi Yosef's perutah benefit. He therefore has the ordinary law of a bailee. The borrower must pay his debt in cash if he has cash, like every debtor, while the creditor's liability for the pledge is paid in land like a bailee's liability. In our case the parties dispute the decrease in value. The borrower admits that initially the pledge was worth no more than the debt. The dispute concerns only the later decrease: the creditor says it was not worth enough to cover the debt and that an additional five is still owed, while the borrower says it was worth enough and that he has a counterclaim against the creditor equal to the entire debt. This is not a case of partial admission. It is like a claimant demanding one hundred while the defendant says, 'I owe the entire one hundred, but you owe me garments and utensils worth the full one hundred,' and the claimant answers, 'I owe you garments only worth fifty.' There is no meaningful way to identify a portion as admission and a portion as denial. Although the garments are only like a pledge and a pledge is not treated as 'here it is,' the underlying debt is fully admitted. The denial concerns the separate claim arising from the garments. Likewise here, because the creditor must answer for damage to the pledge in land while the borrower pays the debt in money, each collects independently from the other. The borrower has admitted the whole debt and asserts a separate counterclaim for the pledge's lost value. The creditor denies part of that counterclaim. There is therefore no partial admission of the loan itself. In an ordinary pledge case, a pledge is not 'here it is' because the debtor must pay the admitted fifty in money and cannot unilaterally discharge it with the pledge. Thus his failure to pay the admitted part creates partial-admission liability. Here, by contrast, he denies none of the debt. The only dispute is over the amount of the damage claim, and that damage claim is payable as a bailee's liability. To the extent the creditor admits that damage, the pledge claim is effectively 'here it is.' Examine this carefully.
The borrower takes a hesset oath. The reason is that they do not dispute the amount of the original loan. The Shach discusses this at length and objects: suppose they disagreed over payment, with the borrower saying, 'I repaid such-and-such amount.' Why would the law differ? It appears, however, from Shiltei Giborim in chapter HaParah concerning the rule that we first address the claimant's action. If Reuven sues Shimon on a document for one hundred and Shimon counters that Reuven holds a certain object or field of his, beit din tells Shimon, 'First pay the document you admit; afterward bring your separate claim.' But if the defendant's counterclaim is itself something suitable to discharge the document, such as money independently owed to him by the plaintiff, it is treated as a payment defense and both matters form one litigation. If he requires the plaintiff to swear, the document is not collected until the plaintiff swears. Here, as explained in seif katan 9, the borrower must discharge his loan in money, while the creditor's liability for damage to the pledge is paid as a bailee in land. Thus the borrower's claim for the decreased value resembles a claim to land, something not itself suitable as payment of the document. Beit din first addresses the creditor's loan claim; the borrower pays the debt, and only afterward sues the creditor for the pledge's decrease. On that latter claim only a hesset oath applies. Baal HaTerumot cites a dispute: Baal HaItur holds that only hesset is required, while Rabbi Yehudah requires an oath while holding a sacred object. Perhaps this dispute follows the authorities whether the creditor is a paid bailee under Rabbi Yosef or under Rabbah. According to Rabbi Yosef, his bailee liability is independent and paid as ordinary bailee damages, so it is not directly set off and only hesset applies. According to Rabbah, the creditor is a paid bailee only to the extent of the loan money, which can be understood as though the parties stipulated that the liabilities are automatically netted against one another. If so, the damage claim is itself suitable as payment against the debt and a stronger oath can apply. But a creditor physically holding the pledge still need take only hesset according to the view based on Rabbi Yosef, since no Torah oath applies beyond that.
