Its value diminished while in the creditor's possession. The Sema understands the case to be one in which the borrower claims that the article deteriorated in the creditor's hands, and the creditor admits the deterioration but says it occurred through unavoidable circumstances. Since there are no witnesses and the creditor could have denied that it deteriorated in his possession, he is believed with a rabbinic oath by virtue of a migo. The Sema further suggests that the Torah requires a custodian's oath only for theft, loss, death, or breakage, but not for ordinary decay. This is difficult. A paid custodian who admits that the deposited object deteriorated while in his possession and claims an unavoidable cause must ordinarily take the custodial oath, even where the owner cannot state the facts with certainty. Nor can he avoid that oath merely because he could have denied the deterioration. The proper distinction is that ordinary deterioration may sometimes be attributable to the article's preexisting condition rather than to an event occurring under the custodian's watch. When the creditor says that he does not know whether the defect arose in his possession, his denial concerns the very inception of liability. But where he admits that the deterioration occurred in his possession and merely alleges an unavoidable cause, he must take the oath required of a custodian. The detailed result therefore depends upon the precise form of his claim and whether the borrower alleges negligence with certainty.

And he demands five from him. This is merely the ordinary form of the case. The same rule applies if the creditor does not wish to accept the damaged pledge toward the debt, but wishes to return it and collect at least the amount that the borrower admits. The creditor is not compelled to accept the article merely because it deteriorated in his possession. Even a borrower or one who directly damaged property may pay money rather than be forced to accept the broken article as payment. Only a thief or robber is subject to the special appraisal rules discussed later in simanim 344 and 403.
Although he admits part of the claim. The Sema says that the pledge is regarded as an immediate tender because it was originally delivered as security, unlike an article now offered as collateral, which is not treated as an immediate tender. The Ran and Nimukei Yosef indeed draw such a distinction. Yet this ruling requires study, because their reasoning follows the view that a creditor acquires a pledge even when it was taken at the time of the loan. I have demonstrated above that the principle that a creditor acquires a pledge applies, in its full sense, only when the pledge is taken after the loan through judicial collection, not to an ordinary pledge delivered at the inception of the loan. If so, the article should not automatically be treated as though the admitted amount has already been paid. The exemption from a Torah oath therefore cannot rest simply on the label of immediate tender, and the matter must be analyzed according to the competing principles governing pledges and partial admissions.
And he credits him five from it. It appears that the word should read “collects for him,” as found in some editions of the Tur. The meaning is that the creditor causes the borrower to receive five from the value of the pledge, while the remainder is applied to the debt. If the creditor instead demands that the borrower pay ten in cash, he may retain even the portion corresponding to the five until the ten are paid, because the deterioration in his possession does not obligate him to accept the pledge itself in satisfaction of the debt.

All the more so if, even after the decrease, it remains worth the debt, he takes only a rabbinic oath. The Sema and Bach explain that only a rabbinic oath is required because the parties do not dispute the amount of the loan, but only the amount of depreciation. In my view that is not correct. The reason an oath while holding a sacred object is required above is that the creditor does not claim ownership of the pledge itself, but seeks to collect his debt from it. The same reasoning applies here. According to the borrower, the creditor is not entitled to collect the full amount from the pledge because part of its loss is chargeable to him. Since the creditor's claim is not to the body of the pledge, he should ordinarily swear the stronger oath. Imagine that the creditor admits receiving a pledge but disputes whether its value was ten or five. The dispute about value directly determines how much he may collect from the article, and it is no less a claim for collection than the earlier cases. The language of the authorities must therefore be understood narrowly. Where the borrower cannot establish that any actionable loss occurred in the creditor's hands, and the creditor merely denies the extent of a possible decrease, a rabbinic oath may suffice. But when the creditor admits facts creating liability and disputes only the amount, the laws of partial admission and custodial oaths apply. Each formulation in the Tur and Shulchan Aruch must be read according to the exact claim, rather than by a general distinction between the original loan and the depreciation.
Or the creditor says, “I do not know how much it decreased,” and the creditor takes a rabbinic oath. This is difficult. Since he admits that the article decreased in value, he appears to admit part of the claim, and because he does not know the amount he should be one who is obligated to swear but cannot, and therefore must pay. The Sema explains that the creditor means he does not know whether it decreased at all while in his possession, since it may already have been decayed. On that reading he is like one who says, “I do not know whether I ever became liable,” though the word “how much” is strained. My revered teacher explained that the clause refers to a pledge now worth less than the debt. The creditor says that before the deterioration it may have been worth the full debt, but he does not know. He therefore swears that he does not know whether he owes the borrower anything. This explanation is correct and preserves the wording.
