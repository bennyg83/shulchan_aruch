That its value decreased while in the lender's possession. In the Perishah I wrote that one may explain the case as follows: the borrower makes this claim and the lender admits the deterioration occurred while the collateral was with him, but says it occurred through unavoidable circumstances, and there are no witnesses. Had the lender wished, he could have denied the matter and said that the decay had already occurred previously and he had not noticed it. He is therefore believed with a hesset oath that it occurred through unavoidable circumstances, by virtue of a migo that he could have said it did not occur in his possession. Otherwise, the lender would have to take a Torah oath that the deterioration occurred through unavoidable circumstances, even against an uncertain claim by the borrower, like every custodian who must take a Torah oath concerning an asserted unavoidable accident, death, or breakage, even where the depositor does not make a definite claim. I also wrote there another possible explanation: perhaps the Torah imposed such an oath only where the custodian claims theft, loss, death, or breakage, which are analogous to death in that the object is completely gone. Here, however, the object still exists but has merely diminished in value and still bears its original owner's identity. Since the borrower does not make a definite claim against the lender, the Torah may not have imposed an oath on a doubtful claim of this kind. See below, siman 294, and the Perishah here, where I discussed this at length.
That it is as he says, and he is exempt. One should not ask that the Tur and Mechaber write at the end of this siman and in seif 14 that if the lender claims the collateral was lost through unavoidable circumstances, and even if the borrower does not know what happened, the lender swears and collects his entire debt. If so, one might think that here too it should have been stated not merely that the lender is exempt, but also that the borrower must pay the full loan if the remaining collateral is worth less than the loan, since the borrower does not assert with certainty that moths consumed it or that it decayed because of the lender's negligence in failing to air it properly. Were he to make such a definite claim, he would indeed be exempt, as explained above in seif 12. One may answer that the present passage is not discussing the lender's affirmative claim for payment, and relies on what was stated earlier in seif 14. Another answer is that the borrower becomes obligated to pay the lender only after the lender first takes a Torah oath that the loss was unavoidable. Since the present discussion is teaching only that the lender is exempt from the borrower's claim through a hesset oath, as explained above, it does not state the borrower's separate liability. Examine carefully.
And if there is a dispute, etc. This refers to the immediately preceding case, where the lender admits that he did not guard it properly. This is clear.
For the collateral is an immediate tender. See the Tur and the Mechaber at the beginning of siman 87, where there is a dispute whether giving collateral is considered an immediate tender, and the Mechaber rules there that it is not. Here is different because the loan was made upon this collateral from the outset and the lender accepted its value in that context. See the Perishah for further discussion.
But the lender does not swear, etc. This is also the wording of the Tur, and the rule itself is obvious. The Tur and Mechaber wrote it only because of the concluding case. The meaning is that in this first case the borrower swears, not the lender, because even according to the borrower's own claim the collateral was worth no more than the amount of the debt. The borrower therefore has no monetary claim against the lender. In the concluding case the reverse is true, because there the borrower does have a claim against the lender, since the collateral was initially worth more than the debt. See the Perishah for further discussion.

And all the more so if even, etc. The Tur likewise writes this. See the Perishah. The reason this is described as 'all the more so,' and also why the passage concludes that the lender is exempt with a hesset oath rather than requiring him to take a stringent oath while holding a sacred object as the Geonim require above in seif 17, and why he is likewise exempt with a hesset oath when he says, 'I do not know how much it depreciated,' may all be explained by recognizing that the laws in this seif differ from the law stated by the Mechaber in seif 17. There, the borrower does not admit that the lender lent him the amount claimed. The lender is therefore considered a claimant seeking to extract money from the borrower and must, according to the Geonim, take a stringent oath in order to retain the collateral in his possession. Here, by contrast, the borrower does not dispute the amount and admits that he owes the full sum of the loan. Rather, it is the borrower who seeks to extract money from the lender by claiming, 'You negligently caused depreciation to my collateral while it was in your possession.' Therefore even the Geonim agree that the lender is exempt from the borrower's claim with a hesset oath. For this reason the Tur uses the language 'all the more so.' In such a case, where the collateral is still worth the amount of the debt and the borrower admits the debt, there is no claimant seeking to extract anything from the lender at all. In the first case, however, the collateral is presently worth less, while the lender states before beit din that he is owed twenty gold coins and the collateral is worth only fifteen. Superficially, it appears somewhat as though he is claiming the balance of the debt. This is especially so because not everyone is expert in the law and aware that the amount of depreciation is to be deducted from the debt. In the Perishah I wrote another reason why this is an 'all the more so' case: the migo that the lender could have claimed purchase is stronger when the collateral remains equal in value to the debt. When it is worth less, one might say that he did not wish to claim purchase because he thought he could also recover another five gold coins on the basis of the loan claim. For the same reason the Tur writes that where the lender says, 'I do not know how much it depreciated at all,' it is possible that the object had already decayed or been moth eaten originally, as stated above. He is therefore like one who says, 'I do not know whether you ever lent me,' and he swears that he does not know and is exempt. The word 'how much' is not precise, and Baal HaTerumot does not contain that word. Baal HaTerumot also cites another opinion that in the first case, where the collateral is presently worth less than the debt, requires the lender to take an unpaid custodian's oath while holding a sacred object. Perhaps the reason is that he appears to be a claimant, as though seeking to extract from the borrower, as explained. See the Perishah for further reconciliation of these rulings.
