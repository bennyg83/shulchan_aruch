And is exempt. The Sema explains that there are no witnesses to what occurred. Had he wished, the lender could have said that the deterioration had already occurred and that he had not noticed it, so it did not happen in his custody. He is therefore believed through a migo with a shevuat heset. Otherwise he would need to take the Torah oath of a custodian that the damage occurred through unavoidable accident, even against the borrower's uncertain claim. Alternatively, the Torah requires a custodian's oath only where he claims an event like total loss, whereas here the item merely deteriorated, and when the borrower has no definite claim the Torah did not impose an oath on a mere doubt. The Shach rejects both explanations. First, Baal HaTerumot, the source of the ruling, does not recognize a migo to exempt from an oath, as the Mechaber rules in simanim 93 and 296. Even according to the Rama, the proposed migo would involve brazenness. Second, there is no source for distinguishing total loss from depreciation. The Shach therefore explains that the borrower asserts with certainty that the pledge deteriorated through the lender's negligence. In such a case the lender takes only a shevuat heset. The Torah's special custodian oath applies where the owner is uncertain and the custodian could otherwise simply claim loss without the owner knowing whether it is false. When the owner makes a definite accusation, the ordinary presumption that a person does not brazenly deny his opponent applies. This also answers the Sema's question why the borrower does not pay based on the lender's oath. Since the borrower asserts a definite contrary claim, the lender's oath does not itself establish a right to collect. The Taz gives another distinction, and Chacham Tzvi defends the Sema on a different basis, arguing that visible moth or rodent damage itself demonstrates accidental loss and the only question is negligence, so only heset applies. He nevertheless regards the Shach's reading of Baal HaTerumot as difficult.
Heilech. The Sema asks how this accords with the Mechaber's ruling at the beginning of siman 87 that a pledge is not heilech. He answers that here the loan was originally made against the pledge and the lender accepted the pledge in place of its value. The Shach notes that the Ran and Nimukei Yosef indeed make this distinction, but questions the law because they follow the view that a creditor acquires a pledge even at the time of the loan. The Shach earlier concluded that we do not follow that view and that a lender does not acquire a pledge taken at the time of the loan as such. If there is no acquisition, the pledge should not constitute heilech. He brings support from the Rambam and other authorities and regards this as the principal law.
Ten. The lender wishes to retain the pledge definitively, arguing that because it was reduced in value through the borrower's negligence, the borrower cannot force him to undertake the burden of selling it. Sema.
From it. The Tur reads "from his debt," which the Sema considers the better text in light of the preceding explanation.
From the pledge. The Sema explains that the remaining ten of the debt cannot be demanded from the borrower in cash while requiring the lender to return the pledge. Rather, the lender collects that amount out of the pledge. As to the additional five by which the lender concedes the pledge exceeds the debt, the lender need not pay cash but may offset it against the debt. The general rule is that the possessor has the advantage and the burden of proof is on the one who seeks to extract. The Shach disagrees. In his view the lender may, if he chooses, retain even the value of the additional five until the borrower pays the entire fifteen in cash. Even if the pledge depreciated while with him, he is not required to accept it in payment of the debt. Even a borrower or direct tortfeasor may ordinarily return the damaged item itself rather than have it appraised as payment, since the rule of compulsory appraisal is limited to a thief or robber. See siman 344 and the beginning of siman 403.
Shevuat heset. The Sema and Bach distinguish the heset here from the more stringent oath in seif 17 on the ground that the parties agree about the amount of the loan and dispute only the depreciation. The Shach rejects this. The reason for the stringent oath in seif 17 is that the lender is not claiming ownership of the physical pledge. Nevertheless, according to the borrower's account the lender is not entitled to collect as much from the pledge, so as one seeking collection he must take the more stringent oath. The Shach therefore explains Baal HaTerumot differently. If the borrower already paid the lender and afterward claims with certainty that the pledge depreciated through the lender's negligence and had been worth more than the debt, the lender takes a shevuat heset that he was not negligent and is exempt. But if the debt has not yet been paid and the dispute concerns depreciation, then if the pledge is still worth the debt the lender takes the Torah-style oath and collects from it. If the lender admits negligence but claims that even before the depreciation it was worth less than the debt, the borrower takes a shevuat heset and is exempt from the alleged balance. Thus whenever the lender seeks to collect the debt from the pledge he must take an oath while holding a sacred object. The Shach states that several decisors agreed with this interpretation.
And is exempt. The Shach asks why, once the lender admits some depreciation but says he does not know how much, this is not a partial admission coupled with inability to swear, requiring payment. The Sema explains that the lender is saying he does not know whether any new depreciation occurred at all, since perhaps the pledge was already deteriorated when received, analogous to "I do not know whether I ever borrowed." The word "how much" is strained on that reading. The Shach cites his teacher's explanation that the alternative clause refers back to the case where the pledge is worth less than the debt: the lender says that before the depreciation perhaps it equaled the debt, but he does not know. He therefore swears that he does not know whether he owes the borrower. The Shach also suggests that because the borrower in any event owes the loan, even the admitted depreciation may be treated as heilech by offsetting it against the debt. This would require the case to be one where the lender does not wish to keep the pledge and leaves it with beit din for return upon payment. Chacham Tzvi argues that even this is unnecessary because until beit din formally awards the pledge it remains a deposit, and a deposit available for return is heilech. The Shach concludes that both readings can stand in law.
