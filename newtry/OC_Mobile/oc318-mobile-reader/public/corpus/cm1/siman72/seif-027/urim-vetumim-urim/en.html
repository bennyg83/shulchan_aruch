And he swears a hesset oath. Why is he not required to take the Torah oath of a custodian that he was not negligent? The Sema and Bach answer that the physical collateral is before us and the dispute concerns only its depreciation, so the case is not governed by the ordinary laws of custodians. The Shach responds that there is no reason to distinguish between loss of the entire collateral and loss of part of its value. I agreed with the Shach in Tumim, although I brought some support for the Sema and Bach from the Gemara in Shevuot. The Shach proposes a broad rule in the law of custodians: if the depositor claims with certainty, "You were negligent," the custodian need take only a hesset oath; a Torah custodial oath is required only where the depositor is uncertain whether negligence occurred. In Tumim I demonstrated at length from Tosafot and the authorities that this rule is incorrect, and that the Torah custodial oath applies in either case. According to the Mechaber, however, there is no difficulty for another reason, because below in siman 294:2 he rules that a custodian does not separately swear a Torah oath that he was not negligent except through gilgul. Here the collateral is physically before us, and the only possible oath is that he was not negligent, so no Torah oath applies. According to the Tur and Rama, who hold that a migo may exempt from an oath except where the alternative plea would require greater brazenness, the case is also resolved. Here the depositor asserts with certainty that the custodian was negligent. Denying negligence is no more brazen than denying the underlying claim, so the custodian has a migo and is believed. See Tumim.
Hesset, etc. And he is exempt. According to what I wrote, the case is one where the borrower claims with certainty that the lender was negligent. It is therefore proper that the lender's hesset oath exempts him and prevents him from collecting the debt, because the borrower says with certainty, "I owe you nothing, since your negligence caused the loss." The reason the Mechaber does not also state that the borrower swears that the lender was negligent and is thereby exempt from payment is simply that this is obvious: where the lender brings a claim and the borrower denies it, the borrower takes hesset. That ordinary rule is not the focus of this siman. See what I wrote above in seif katan 15.
Or he did not air it out properly, etc. See Tumim. The lender's duty to air another person's collateral properly exists only by virtue of the mitzvah of returning a lost benefit to its owner. See there.
And he asks him for five. These words show that both parties agreed that the collateral would be retained absolutely by the lender because the borrower had no other means of payment, and their only dispute concerns the excess value. That is why the borrower demands five. If they had not agreed to such final retention, the borrower would have to take back the collateral even though it depreciated through negligence and pay the lender the debt, while the lender would bear the depreciation. The Shach likewise writes that property is ordinarily not appraised and transferred in satisfaction of a custodian's or tortfeasor's liability; this clause is an exception because the parties agreed that the collateral become the lender's.
For the collateral itself is "here you are." Although below in siman 75 there is a dispute whether collateral is ordinarily treated as immediate tender, here, where the parties agreed that the collateral become the lender's as I explained, all agree that it is treated as payment. The Shach's difficulties therefore do not arise.
And he credits him five against his debt. The Shach cites this as the accurate text, and it is correct according to what I wrote that the entire seif concerns collateral finally retained by the lender. Therefore, where the lender says that the item is worth thirty, the borrower claims only ten rather than demanding that the lender accept less; and according to the lender's own position he must pay the borrower five because the collateral remains with him. It follows properly that five is credited against the debt owed by the lender to the borrower.
For he is believed with an oath. According to my explanation that the collateral has been finally transferred to the lender, it is possible that only a hesset oath is required, because the Geonic rule concerning one who swears and collects on collateral does not apply when the collateral is already absolutely his. Alternatively, the phrase means that as to any remaining amount he collects through the general rule that one holding collateral is ordinarily believed with an oath up to its value. If so, certainly where the collateral has been finally transferred to him the borrower cannot extract it from him. The reader may choose the preferable explanation.
And they dispute the depreciation. The Shach and Sema ask why the lender takes only a hesset oath rather than the sacred object oath required by the Geonim whenever one swears concerning collateral. According to my explanation, the collateral has already been accepted as final payment, so there is no dispute over the collateral itself at all. The only dispute is how much the lender must reimburse the borrower for the depreciation, and plainly this requires only hesset. Baal HaTerumot, who does not use the wording implying final transfer, indeed requires a sacred object oath. In the present formulation, however, all agree that only hesset is required, especially where the lender already holds money and the claim is not directed against the collateral itself.
Or where the lender says, "I do not know." This "or" refers back to the case where, after depreciation, the collateral is not worth the amount of the debt. Even if some depreciation occurred, perhaps its original value never exceeded the debt, in which case the lender owes nothing up to the debt. The lender's uncertainty is therefore like, "I do not know whether I owe you." There is no difficulty that by admitting some depreciation he admits at least a perutah and does not know the remainder, which would ordinarily be a case of one who cannot swear and must pay. The perutah of admitted depreciation is itself offset against the debt and is not an independent amount he must pay. Thus there is no admission creating that Torah oath. So writes the Shach in the name of his father, and this is correct.
