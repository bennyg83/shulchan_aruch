At first glance this is difficult, since the lender nevertheless collects his debt from the collateral, and at minimum he should have to take the mishnaic oath applicable to one who collects on collateral. One must therefore say that the case is where the borrower already paid him, as I wrote in seif katan 121. This also resolves what the Shach wrote in answering the Sema's question in seif katan 80. The Shach appears to contradict what he himself wrote in siman 92, seif katan 12, that there is no case in which a bailee is exempt from the oath of bailees except in the situations he lists there. One cannot answer that here too the case is one of those situations, because that would be very forced, and the poskim would not have stated the rule without qualification. The essential ruling, however, is as Tumim writes, as is evident from many poskim, that even where the claimant makes a definite claim the oath of bailees applies. It is only where the claimant definitely alleges negligence and there are no witnesses that the defendant became a bailee that the bailee is believed through a migo that no such event ever occurred. This is a migo from one audacious claim to another audacious claim, according to the opinion that a migo may exempt from an oath where it is not a migo involving greater brazenness. In my humble opinion Tumim's difficulty can be answered as follows. It is evident below, siman 45 seif 21, that only when each party independently claims against the other are they treated as two claimants. But when one does not assert an independent claim and merely responds, "I have something of yours in my possession corresponding to your claim," it is considered a claim of payment, and there is no law of partial admission. Thus, even if the first party claimed one hundred from the second, and the second claimed a kor of wheat from the first, which is a different claim, and the first admitted part of the wheat, nevertheless, because the second immediately said, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim," and treated it as payment, there is no Torah oath of partial admission. Accordingly, if Reuven sues Shimon on a note for one hundred gold coins, and Shimon says, "I have a pit full of wheat of yours corresponding to the debt," and Shimon has one witness, Reuven is not obligated to take a Torah oath. Since Shimon made that claim as payment, by immediately responding that Reuven has nothing owing because Shimon holds an offsetting claim against him, it is exactly like a claim of repayment. Reuven, the lender, takes the mishnaic oath and collects. This seems clear to me. Accordingly, in our case, where the lender has a note or collateral against the borrower, and the borrower definitely claims, "You were negligent with the collateral and have thereby been paid; therefore you owe me return of the note, or whatever remains of the collateral, without payment," while the lender says, "It was lost through unavoidable circumstances and I have not yet been paid," the oath that the lender must take is an oath in order to collect and to contradict the borrower's claim of payment. It is therefore not a d'oraisa oath. All three d'oraisa oaths share this rule: just as the oath of partial admission and the oath based on one witness are not d'oraisa when imposed in order to collect, as explained in Ketubot 87 and by the poskim, so too the oath of bailees is not d'oraisa where the borrower is not asserting an independent claim but definitely says, "You were negligent," treats that as payment, and the lender must swear in order to collect. In seif 14, where the lender takes a d'oraisa oath, the borrower is claiming from the lender the excess value of the collateral over the debt. There the lender certainly takes a d'oraisa oath regarding the excess, and by gilgul he also swears regarding the debt in order to collect it. Likewise, if the borrower does not treat the matter as payment, but says that he wishes to pay and demands the collateral itself, claiming that perhaps it is still in the lender's possession, the lender certainly takes the d'oraisa oath that it is not in his possession, and by gilgul also swears that he was not negligent, as with other bailees. But where the borrower definitely claims negligence and treats that as payment, and the lender must swear in order to collect, it appears there is no d'oraisa oath of bailees. If one asks that he should nevertheless take the mishnaic oath applicable to one collecting on collateral, that difficulty exists according to all explanations. It therefore appears that the Sema's view is primary, as I wrote in seif katan 87, that only when they dispute the substance of the loan must he take the Geonic oath, whereas when they dispute merely the diminution in value he need not. Some rationale can be offered for the Sema. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 49, part 2, explains that when they dispute repayment involving collateral, the law is like a dispute over a promissory note. Siman 75 explains that a debtor is believed to say, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim," only because he has a migo that he could claim repayment. Thus, when a borrower claims against the collateral because it diminished through the lender's negligence, this is equivalent to saying, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim," as the Sema writes in seif katan 14, and the lender is subjected to an oath only by force of the borrower's migo. Here, however, the lender likewise has a migo that no such event occurred or that he returned the collateral. The borrower's migo is no stronger than the lender's. The rule that a migo is not accepted to exempt from an oath applies only where the oath rests upon him by law. But if the oath arises only because of a migo, we also apply the lender's migo to exempt him. Just as the borrower's migo creates reason to believe he is telling the truth so as to impose an oath, the lender's migo creates reason to believe that he is telling the truth so as to exempt him from that oath. Since the lender is in possession, his migo is stronger than the borrower's migo. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that where collateral diminished in value, the law is that each party collects from the other: the lender collects money as a creditor, while the borrower collects the amount of the diminution from land as one collecting damages. Accordingly, if the borrower says, "I have a corresponding claim against him because the collateral was lost, equal to the entire debt," and the lender says it was worth less, it is as though there were a separate claim against the lender. In my humble opinion this is incorrect, as I wrote above. It is only a claim of repayment by the borrower, since he treats the loss as payment, as I wrote above in seif katan 88. I also do not accept that collateral is governed by the rule that each party separately collects. The Sema, seif katan 85, goes further and writes that even where the collateral merely diminished in value, the lender must accept what remains of it toward his debt. Even the Shach, who disagrees there, agrees in the case before us, because collateral is taken for collection, and where the lender was negligent and does not return it the borrower need not separately pay him the amount of the debt. This seems clear. The proof Ketzot brings from Shiltei Giborim in the chapter HaParah also appears not to be accepted halachah. It accords with one opinion in Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia, but Mordechai there also brings those who disagree. Likewise, siman 75 states regarding an oral loan that if the borrower says, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim," it is treated as a claim of repayment, with no distinction at all between a claim involving an object and one involving money.
The collateral is considered heilach. See Shach, seif katan 118, who raises a difficulty from Rambam. In light of what I wrote above, that with movable collateral everyone agrees the creditor acquires it retroactively, the matter is clear. Rambam discusses a case where the creditor does not collect from the collateral, but the debtor instead gives him money from his house, which certainly is not heilach. Here, however, the case is where the debtor has no other means of payment and the creditor collects from the collateral. Since he acquires it retroactively, such that if the lender had earlier sold it the sale and consecration would take effect, and if he had betrothed a woman with it the betrothal would take effect retroactively, it certainly constitutes heilach. The Shach's reasoning therefore does not apply. Tumim likewise writes that when the debtor agrees to transfer the collateral absolutely to the lender it constitutes heilach, though he does not give the reason. In my humble opinion the reason is clearly as I have written. Since the lender admits part of the diminution but does not know how much, this is a case of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore must pay. Ordinarily, however, the lender certainly does not bear a d'oraisa oath. Rather, when he swears he may collect from the collateral, because the borrower's claim concerning the diminution is equivalent to saying, "I have something of yours corresponding to your claim." In such a claim, even if the lender is uncertain and the borrower makes a definite claim, the lender swears that he does not know and collects his debt, as I wrote above in siman 29, Chiddushim, seif katan 1. Since the perutah of diminution that the lender admits, and for which he credits the borrower from the collateral, is held by the lender as a deposit, it is heilach, as the Shach writes in seif katan 121.
