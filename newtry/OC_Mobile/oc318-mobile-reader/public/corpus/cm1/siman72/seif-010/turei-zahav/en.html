(Seif 27) “The borrower claims with certainty.” This is difficult, for the creditor should be required to take a Torah oath like any custodian who claims that the loss occurred through accident. Moreover, the borrower’s certainty should be unnecessary, since even a doubtful claim can require a custodian’s oath. The Sema answers that the creditor could have said the decay existed before the loan. This is difficult, because a migo is not accepted concerning a custodian’s oath, as stated at the beginning of siman 295. He further answers that there is no Torah oath where the pledged object still exists but has depreciated, since the Torah imposed the oath only in a case like death, where the object is entirely gone. That too is difficult, for the Gemara says, “What difference is there between killing all of it and killing half of it?” It appears to me that a custodian’s oath applies only when the custodian admits the depreciation and claims it occurred through accident. Here he does not admit, or does not know, that any depreciation occurred. He says only: “If your assertion of depreciation is correct, it necessarily occurred through accident, because I guarded properly.” He therefore owes only a heset oath. When the Tur later says, “If he admits that he did not place it properly,” the reason is that in such a location decay is common and depreciation is treated as certain. Sefer HaTerumot’s text reads: “If he admits that it depreciated because he was negligent,” and so forth. The glossator defends the Sema on another ground. Sefer HaTerumot may follow the view in siman 294 that where witnesses establish the accident, the custodian need not swear even that he was not negligent. Here moths, mice, and similar causes are themselves evident accidents; the only uncertainty is negligence, so only heset applies and the borrower must make a definite claim. The Shach’s language does not fit Sefer HaTerumot well, and his underlying ruling requires study.
