Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the lender is disqualified from taking an oath and therefore cannot first swear that the collateral is not in his possession, the case is governed like one where both parties are suspect with respect to oaths, and they divide. In my view this is incorrect. Where both are suspect, the reason for division, as Ran explains, is that the difficulty arose through the defendant's own fault. Here, however, the defendant is not substantively liable at all. Even according to the view that Chazal removed the oath from the borrower and placed it on the lender, if the lender is disqualified he certainly cannot collect anything, because the borrower's inability to swear creates no monetary liability. Chazal withheld the oath from him only because of the concern that he might take an unnecessary oath. This is no worse than a case where a person says, "I know about three and do not know about three," where he is exempt if he was not expected to know. All the more so here. According to Tosafot in Bava Metzia 5, s.v. shekenegdo, even with a suspect party we do not invoke the rule that inability to swear requires payment where he wishes to swear. Even according to Tosafot's second answer, where inability is due to circumstances beyond his control, such as one who admitted part and the subject matter was then slaughtered, the rule should not impose payment. A proof is Shach, seif katan 51, who explains why the rule is not applied to heirs by saying that perhaps if their father were alive he would have made a definite claim. If unavoidable inability to swear itself created liability, the father's death would be the paradigm of unavoidable inability and the heirs should still be liable. Necessarily, where the inability to swear is due to accident or circumstances beyond the party's control, we do not say that inability to swear results in payment.
