Knesset HaGedolah understands Rambam to mean that a lender is not believed to say the pledge was lost through unavoidable accident unless there is independent proof that armed robbers attacked him and some uncertainty only about whether this pledge was among what was taken. He reads Ran's responsum similarly. In my view the lender can swear to an unavoidable accident even where no witnesses establish that robbers came. Since the borrower says in effect, "I do not know whether I repaid you," he remains obligated on the loan. The lender's oath is required not in order to collect the debt, but because the lender is a custodian who must exempt himself from liability for the pledge. Rabbi Akiva's position teaches the same as Rabbi Eliezer's structure: where the lender claims a qualifying accident, he takes the custodian's oath and collects the loan. If witnesses establish that robbers attacked and property was taken, but they do not know whether this particular pledge was among the stolen items, then even a custodian's oath is unnecessary. Siman 294 rules that where witnesses establish an unavoidable event involving the relevant type of object, and the depositor cannot state with certainty that his object was not among those lost, the custodian is exempt. The same applies here. This also explains Ran's responsum: where it was known that gentile raiders pillaged the lender's house and he said the pledge was among the property taken, he could collect the loan without an additional oath. Rambam's language about a pledge "taken by armed robbers" can therefore be read as a substantive example of unavoidable accident, not as a requirement that the attack itself be independently proven.
