(Seif 29) “The borrower takes a heset oath.” The Tur concludes in the name of the Barzeloni that he is believed through the migo that he could say, “I owe you nothing.” Why is a migo needed at all? The Sema gives a strained answer. It appears that the borrower does not accuse the creditor of an unauthorized sale. He admits he authorized the creditor to sell and says only that the sale proceeds remain in the creditor’s possession. The creditor says he never sold the pledge and that it was lost through accident. The creditor has a migo: he could say, “I sold it when you instructed me, but the purchaser later discovered a defect, rescinded the sale, returned the object, and I returned his money.” That claim would make the borrower’s denial uncertain, because he could not contradict it with certainty. The Barzeloni might therefore apply a migo even to extract money. The ruling teaches that the borrower has an opposing migo, namely “I owe you nothing,” which prevents the creditor from converting the borrower’s claim into a doubtful one.
