There is room for doubt, etc. See Tumim, where I explained at length why there is genuine uncertainty here. Yam Shel Shlomo and the Shach ruled that there is no uncertainty and compared the matter to a convert's pledge above in seif 39. I showed that as practical law the matter still requires study.
Whether Shimon acquires. Above in seif 40 the Shulchan Aruch rules conclusively that if Reuven repledged the gentile's pledge to Shimon, Shimon acquires it. Why is the present case of deposit different from pledge? The Sema answers: since Shimon took profit from the arrangement, if he were acting merely through Reuven, how could he receive interest? We must therefore say that Reuven withdrew from the transaction and Shimon became the direct lender to the gentile. Once Shimon is the actual lender to the gentile, it is natural that he should acquire the gentile's pledge as the principal creditor.
To the extent of the pledge, etc. Maharam Alshikh writes in responsum 1: Reuven borrowed money from a gentile and later wished to repay him. Shimon met Reuven and said, "Give the money to me and I will continue paying the gentile the interest just as you did." Reuven did so, and from then on Shimon regularly paid interest to the gentile, while the gentile believed Shimon was merely acting as Reuven's agent. The gentile later died. Maharam Alshikh ruled that since the arrangement between Reuven and Shimon constitutes fixed prohibited interest, Reuven is considered the lender to Shimon. Shimon must therefore repay Reuven what Reuven lent him, and Reuven gains from the gentile's death. Shimon has no independent claim against the gentile. However, the interest that Shimon actually paid to the gentile is deducted, because it was fixed prohibited interest. The Shach cites this. See what I wrote in Yoreh Deah.
