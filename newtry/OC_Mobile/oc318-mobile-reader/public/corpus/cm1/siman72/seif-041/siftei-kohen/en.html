There is room for doubt, etc. The Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot indeed express doubt, but I do not know why. Even where a pledge lies in the lender's unguarded courtyard, if another person takes possession first he acquires the surplus, as is explicit in the Gemara and all the authorities and as stated above in seif 39, although the lender never formally deposited it and it remains in his courtyard. All the more so here, where the pledge was with Shimon from the outset. Years ago I wrote in the margin of my Shulchan Aruch that Maharshal ruled that Shimon acquired it, and that this is the primary ruling. I am presently writing in a village to which we fled because of an epidemic, and Maharshal's work is not before me, but this is surely the correct conclusion. Maharam Alshich ruled concerning Reuven, who borrowed money from a gentile and transferred it to Shimon, who continued paying interest to the gentile while the gentile believed Shimon to be Reuven's agent. After the gentile died, since such an arrangement is prohibited as interest and is treated as a loan from one Jew to another, Reuven acquired the money and Shimon must return the principal. Money differs from a gentile's pledge, because a pledge remains wherever it physically is under its owner's legal dominion, whereas money is given to be spent and did not leave Reuven's ownership merely because Shimon paid interest to the gentile. Shimon may deduct the interest he paid, since it was fixed interest recoverable from the first Jew. Nevertheless, in such cases it is proper to compromise and award Shimon a share, so that the matter not be decided by strict law alone and thereby generate strife.
