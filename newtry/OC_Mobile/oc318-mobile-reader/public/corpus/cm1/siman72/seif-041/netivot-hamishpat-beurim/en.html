There is room for doubt. See Shach, seif katan 163, who is astonished at this. Tumim explains that the doubt is because withholding repayment of a non-Jew's loan is permitted, and according to many poskim even withholding a deposit is permitted. Therefore, he argues, immediately when the Jew lent money upon the collateral it became ownerless and the Jewish lender acquired it through the rule permitting withholding repayment. See there. In my humble opinion, so long as the Jew has not manifested an intention to take it as his own, it does not become his property. Even once he has manifested an intention to take it, it is still not called "yours" for mitzvah purposes, as Magen Avraham writes in Orach Chaim, that even according to the view permitting robbery of a non-Jew, the stolen item is not regarded as "yours." Certainly, before he has taken it, it remains the non-Jew's property and cannot be called the Jew's. A proof is Pesachim 31, concerning a Jew who lent money to a non-Jew against chametz collateral. According to Rava, who holds that a creditor acquires collateral only from the time of collection onward, the Jew does not violate the prohibition of possessing chametz. Why not? If withholding repayment of a non-Jew's loan were itself enough, and according to some even a deposit could be withheld, the Jew should have acquired it immediately. Rather, as long as he has not manifested an intention to appropriate it, it remains the non-Jew's property. In my humble opinion the basis of the doubt is different. The case concerns an interest bearing loan, as Sema writes in seif katan 114, and this law and the law of seif 40 are adjacent in Mordechai. See there. Rashba's responsum, cited by the Shach in seif katan 162, states that even where the non-Jew owes another Jew, that second Jew must pay the first Jew the interest that will accrue until the agreed repayment date, because the first Jew has already acquired that right. See there. This is so even though the non-Jew himself may redeem the collateral before the due date. According to those who disagree there, it appears that even where no term was expressly stated, another person may not redeem the collateral against the first lender's will. The reason is that since the non-Jew fixed an interest obligation with him until the time he redeems the collateral, no other person can acquire it. The first Jew can say, "I acquired an interest in the entire collateral corresponding to the principal and the interest that will accrue until the non-Jew himself redeems it." It is as though the collateral was transferred to him conditionally until redemption. As long as it is not redeemed, he acquires an additional share in the collateral each day until the principal and accrued interest are fully covered. Here all agree that only the person to whom the non-Jew himself is indebted may redeem the collateral, because that creditor has the non-Jew's legal power through Rabbi Natan's lien and stands in the non-Jew's place. Another person, however, cannot redeem it and thereby acquire it. This also explains the Sema's words in seif katan 114 and removes the Shach's question there.
