There is room for doubt. Shach asks why there is any doubt: if collateral of a convert who dies without heirs is held by one Jew and another person acquires the excess value over the debt, the second acquirer wins. Maharshal says the fact that the second Jew originally received the object as a deposit from the first should not matter because the first Jew's right had ended and the excess became ownerless. I have a different and more fundamental doubt. How can the second Jew acquire at all if retaining the gentile's property would ordinarily be theft and a gentile inherits his father by Torah law? Hagahot Rosh addresses this and seems to rely on the rule permitting withholding a gentile's loan obligation where the property came into Jewish hands lawfully. If the collateral came lawfully into the Jew's possession, perhaps the Jew can benefit from the permitted nonpayment rule. But then this case differs sharply from collateral of a convert who dies without heirs. During the convert's lifetime the Jewish creditor never has any ownership in the excess. Only when the convert dies does the excess become ownerless, and a second Jew can acquire first. With a gentile borrower, if lawful possession itself already permits the Jew to withhold repayment, one could argue that the first Jew acquires the collateral immediately when it comes into his hands. If so, when he later deposits it with a second Jew, he is depositing his own acquired property, and the second Jew cannot acquire it against him. On the other hand, Hagahot Rosh may distinguish loans from specific deposited or pledged objects. A loan is given for expenditure and can fall under the permitted nonpayment principle. A specific object remains the gentile owner's property. Then if the gentile dies and his heirs do not know of the collateral, the case is more like a lost object belonging to a gentile. Only at death, when practical loss occurs, does the opportunity to acquire arise. If the heirs know and demand it, retaining it may remain theft. This reading is supported by Rama in Even HaEzer 28, who says kiddushin made with collateral held from a gentile does not automatically take effect. If the collateral were already the Jew's under the nonpayment rule, that would be difficult. It also explains why the Torah needs "his fellow" to exclude gentile property from the laws of custodianship. If any gentile object lawfully reaching Jewish hands became immediately acquirable, ordinary gentile deposits would never create a meaningful shemirah case. Still, the poskim often speak broadly of the permitted nonpayment rule whenever property came lawfully into Jewish hands, without distinguishing money from specific objects, and Mahari Weil strongly implies such breadth. Therefore the issue is genuinely doubtful. My inclination is that the first Jew has the stronger claim unless he clearly relinquished it. This supports Sema's earlier explanation that where the first Jew paid interest to the gentile, he thereby demonstrated that he had removed himself from ownership and the second Jew could acquire. Without such a clear relinquishment, the first Jew's prior right remains substantial. Shach's criticism of Sema is therefore not compelling.
