Reuven claims books from Levi. The Sema cites a responsum of Ran concerning Reuven, who lent a book to Shimon, while Shimon left another book with Reuven as collateral. Marauders entered both houses and took the books. Later they returned to Reuven the book that Shimon had left as collateral. Ran ruled that Reuven must return it to Shimon, because Shimon is not liable for the unavoidable loss of Reuven's book. The Shach writes that Ran's reason is that borrowing a book is itself a mitzvah. Since the lender also benefits from Rabbi Yosef's perutah, the borrower does not receive all the benefit. Ran therefore follows his own ruling like Rav Yosef. According to authorities who rule like Rabbah, says the Shach, the borrower receives all the benefit and is a full borrower liable even for unavoidable accident. Therefore, according to us, this should be an unresolved legal doubt and possession should control. The Sema, he says, should not have cited Ran's ruling as simple law. This objection is not decisive. Even according to Rabbah, who does not treat Rabbi Yosef's perutah as enough to create paid custodian status, he may agree that the lender receives some benefit, only less than a perutah. Tosafot in Bava Metzia explains that even Rabbah agrees that while one is actually engaged with a lost object he may be exempt from charity at that moment, but Rabbah does not make him a paid custodian because that occurrence is uncommon. Paid custody requires a perutah of compensation. By contrast, a borrower is liable for unavoidable accident only where all benefit is his. If the lender receives any genuine benefit, even less than a perutah, it is no longer true that all benefit belongs to the borrower. Thus even according to Rabbah, borrowing a sefer for a mitzvah may fail to create full borrower liability. The Sema can therefore be correct. There is, however, a further difficulty. Ran's reasoning that lending the sefer is a mitzvah seems to rely on the Rabbi Yosef benefit occurring at the time of lending, not on spreading or airing collateral. If so, even an ordinary interest free loan without collateral is a mitzvah and can exempt the lender from giving charity at that moment. Why then does the Gemara in Kiddushin say that everyone agrees a loan is in the borrower's possession for unavoidable accidents because it is no worse than borrowing, which itself incurs unavoidable accident liability? Borrowing itself is not liable where all benefit is not with the borrower, and a mitzvah loan likewise gives the lender some benefit. According to the Shach's approach the answer is straightforward: Rabbah is the one who says that a loan is no worse than borrowing, and Rabbah does not accept Rabbi Yosef's perutah as benefit. This also explains why the other scholars could have thought the dispute concerned liability for unavoidable accident, since they followed Rav Yosef and did not regard the borrower as receiving all benefit. Still, I think there is another and stronger reason why borrowing a sefer is not governed by the ordinary law of a borrower. We rule that mitzvot were not given for personal benefit. For that reason one who is prohibited by vow from benefiting from another may still learn Scripture from him and may receive a shofar blast for a mitzvah. If so, one who borrows a sefer for Torah study does not receive ordinary legal benefit from the act of borrowing. True, Yoreh Deah siman 221 rules that if one prohibits his sefer to another, the latter may not learn from it. Taz and Shach explain that because books are not ordinarily lent freely since they become worn, learning for free saves rental value and therefore constitutes benefit. Yet for borrower liability, the relevant benefit is the benefit of the use itself. The Gemara asks whether one who borrows an item merely to display and be seen with it is a borrower, and leaves the matter unresolved because perhaps we require benefit from the object itself. Here, since mitzvot are not given for benefit, the borrower does not receive the requisite benefit from the borrowed use itself. The saving of rental expense is only collateral benefit, analogous to borrowing for appearance. Therefore the borrower of a sefer is not a full borrower liable for unavoidable accidents. This supports Ran and the Sema.
Since Levi knew. This applies specifically where Levi knew that the books belonged to Reuven. Even if Shimon permitted him to use them, he should not have relied on that permission. If Levi did not know and reasonably thought the books were Shimon's, he is under complete compulsion and is exempt. Although a person is generally responsible for damage whether inadvertent or intentional, one acting under complete compulsion is exempt, as Tosafot explains in Bava Kamma. He need pay only the amount of benefit he actually received. He does not pay the full depreciation unless he knew the books were Reuven's. This resembles the rule where heirs inherit a borrowed cow, slaughter it, and eat it without knowing it was borrowed. They pay only the discounted value of the meat, not full borrower liability. A responsum of Rashi cited by Beit Yosef is therefore very surprising. Reuven pledged an object to Shimon; Shimon pledged it to Levi and told Levi that Reuven had pledged it until a particular time and that if Reuven failed to redeem it by then, he would lose it. Levi then gave the object as a gift to Yehudah. Reuven later demanded his pledge. Rashi rules that Yehudah must swear that it is not in his possession and concerning its value, implying that if it was lost he must pay. Why? If Yehudah thought he had received a valid gift, there is no greater compulsion. Perhaps the case is one where Levi told Yehudah exactly how the object had come to him, but Yehudah erred in law and thought the forfeiture clause was fully effective despite asmachta. A mistake of law is not complete compulsion. He should have asked. This resembles one who nullifies a prohibited mixture because he mistakenly thinks it is permitted. But if Levi told Yehudah nothing and simply gave him the object as a gift, and it was then lost or even damaged by him, Yehudah should certainly be exempt from full damages, no worse than heirs who slaughter their father's borrowed cow without knowledge. This is clear.
