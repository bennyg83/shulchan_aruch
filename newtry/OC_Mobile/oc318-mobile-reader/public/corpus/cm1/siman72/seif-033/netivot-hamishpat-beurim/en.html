He authorized me to study from them. See Shach, seif katan 140, who writes that if the borrower says, "He told me that Reuven gave permission to whoever would lend money upon them to study from them," he is exempt, etc. It appears that this applies even where Shimon has no migo, such as where there are witnesses that the books belonged to Reuven and there are also witnesses who saw them in Shimon's possession. Shimon is believed to assert claims of borrowing, rental, and authorization regarding movable property ordinarily lent out. If he says, "He lent it to me for a long period," and the owner denies this and says he lent it only for a short period, the borrower is believed, because with movable property he is the muchzak. This is unlike land, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 110a, s.v. Pardesi, and as discussed in Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 15 of Hilchot To'en. See there. See Sema, seif katan 101, through the words "through Levi's agency, etc." There is a textual error, and it should read "through Shimon's agency."
He must pay Reuven. See Shach, seif katan 140, who writes that if Reuven authorized Shimon to use the collateral, and Shimon in turn authorized Levi, then although a borrower is generally not permitted to lend the borrowed object to another, after the fact this is not considered negligence and he is exempt. See there. Although Shimon certainly must pay for the diminution, because he cannot swear that the diminution resulted from ordinary use, as explained in seif 30, Reuven is thereby discharged from the amount of his debt to Shimon, as though Shimon had waived it. At first glance one might therefore have said that Levi must return the collateral to Reuven. When Shimon sells the collateral to Levi, Shimon's waiver and payment to Shimon do not help, because Levi acquires the debt itself by taking possession of the collateral, as explained in siman 66. Here, however, Shimon did not sell the underlying debt. He merely borrowed money for himself from Levi against Reuven's collateral, while Reuven's personal obligation remained in Shimon's hands. Once that personal obligation was extinguished by waiver or payment, one might think that the lien upon the collateral likewise falls away. In truth, however, this is not so. Once Shimon pledged that collateral to another, and the second lender acquired a proprietary right in the collateral, the collateral became acquired to the second lender, and neither the first lender's waiver nor payment to him can undo that right.
