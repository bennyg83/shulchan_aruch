Both swear a hesset oath. See Tumim for why the lender does not swear with a sacred object, since his oath does not concern the physical collateral itself.
One swears that he sold it, and the other swears that he pledged it. Although this necessarily places one of them in a false oath, they could instead each swear only that he owes the other nothing, in which case no false oath would necessarily follow. Perhaps it was pledged for three dinars and is worth exactly that amount, or perhaps it was sold and the sale price was already paid. We nevertheless do not avoid the direct formulation merely out of concern for a false oath, because each litigant must clarify and swear to the factual claim he made. We do not rule like Ben Nanas. See Bava Metzia 5b and Tosafot there.
And if they wish to reverse the oath. See Tumim. The correct explanation is as the Taz writes. Ordinarily each swears only to his factual account, one that he sold it and the other that he pledged it, but neither swears affirmatively that the other owes him money, because where he is not presently receiving payment there is no reason for such an oath. If they choose to reverse the oath, however, the one who would receive payment may require the other to swear expressly that no money is owed, and upon that oath he pays. If both wish to reverse, both may swear, meaning each still swears to the factual account, one sale and the other pledge, without affirming an outstanding monetary obligation. See Tumim. If one tells the other, "Swear and take," and the other actually swears, the first can no longer retract and must pay. But if neither swore and they merely cast lots to determine who would swear first, the one selected cannot argue, "Your own mouth admitted that if I swear you must pay." The other may respond, "That was only if you swore without reliance on the lot; now I retract and demand that you too swear." That response is legally effective.
