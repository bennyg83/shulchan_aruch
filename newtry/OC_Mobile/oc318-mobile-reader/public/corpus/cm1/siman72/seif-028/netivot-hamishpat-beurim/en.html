And if they did not agree, both swear. This is difficult to me. In siman 138 seif 1, regarding two people holding a garment, where both must swear, it is explained that if one says to the other, "Swear and take the whole garment," we heed him. If the other also refuses to swear, they divide it without an oath. Why then does the Mechaber write here that if they did not agree, both swear? There is a further difficulty. Since the oath here is a shevuat heset, which may be reversed onto the opposing party, when the opposing party reverses it back and then refuses to swear, the first party is exempt without an oath. If so, because each one reverses his oath onto the other and the other refuses to swear the reversed oath, each should thereby become exempt, and both should be free of any oath. Moreover, if they ultimately cast lots to determine which one swears first, and the one selected swears and then seeks to compel the other to swear, the other should certainly be able to reverse his oath onto the first party, because the first can no longer reverse it back after having already sworn. It therefore appears that when Reuven reverses his oath onto Shimon, thereby exempting Reuven from his own oath, if Shimon then reverses his own oath onto Reuven so that Reuven must swear and collect, Reuven automatically once again becomes obligated in the first oath that had rested upon him, by way of gilgul, even though he had already been exempted from it. Gilgul applies even to a matter that independently carries no oath obligation, and even to an oath that comes through reversal, as the Shach writes in siman 94, seif katan 2. The same applies to Shimon. Once he becomes obligated in the oath that Reuven reversed upon him, he also becomes obligated, by gilgul, in the first oath that had originally rested upon him. A gilgul always follows the law of the principal oath through which the secondary oath was imposed. Some poskim therefore hold that where one cannot swear the principal oath and must pay, he must pay also with respect to the gilgul, even where he makes a definite claim concerning the gilgul. See Sema, seif katan 91. Here, because his principal oath arises from his fellow's reversal and can no longer itself be reversed, the gilgul likewise can no longer be reversed. Although with ordinary gilgul one may say that he reverses the secondary oath, as explained in siman 94, here, since he cannot reverse the principal oath, he cannot reverse the gilgul either. Therefore, even after one party has already sworn, the other cannot then say that he reverses his oath, because that oath was imposed upon him by gilgul from an oath that came upon him by reversal and can no longer itself be reversed. This also resolves the other difficulties. Although regarding the money for which Shimon must swear in order to be exempt, Reuven is also obligated by gilgul to swear in order to collect, siman 87 seif 9 explains that one who must swear and be exempt, if he refuses to swear, may be placed under nidui, whereas one who must swear and collect was never included in this enactment of coercion. See Sema, seif katan 22. Therefore, his fellow can compel the party who must swear in order to be exempt, by nidui, to take his oath, even though the other party also has an oath obligation concerning that same money, because the latter has no judicial coercion to swear, whereas one who must swear and be exempt does have an enacted form of coercion. Each party can therefore compel the other by nidui to take the oath required for his own exemption. This differs from siman 138, where both parties must take a mishnaic oath and collect. Neither is subject to the enactment of coercion by nidui. Therefore, when each imposes upon the other by gilgul an oath concerning the other portion of the garment, each has an oath obligation concerning every part of the garment. If one refuses to swear, he cannot compel the other to swear, because he himself is likewise obligated to swear and collect with respect to that same portion, just as his fellow is, and their oath obligations are equal. One oath is no stronger than the other. Therefore, when both refuse to swear and neither can compel the other, the law is that they divide without an oath. According to what I have written, it appears that even there, in siman 138, after they divided without an oath, if one then seeks to impose a shevuat heset upon the other, each can compel the other, just as here. This seems clear.
