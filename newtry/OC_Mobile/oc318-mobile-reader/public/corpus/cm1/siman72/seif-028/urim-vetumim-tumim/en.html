Both take a hesset oath. Why does the lender take only hesset? According to the Geonim, a lender claiming money against collateral ordinarily takes a formal oath because he is not claiming ownership of the object itself but money secured by it. There are two answers. One possibility is that the collateral is no longer extant, as the wording "it was worth" suggests, so the present dispute concerns only its former value. More likely, the special facts themselves remove the Geonic rule. The lender says the object is collateral. The borrower says the lender sold it. According to the borrower's own claim, he no longer seeks return of the object. He seeks only its monetary value. Thus the borrower does not place the lender in the classic position of one holding another's object and seeking to collect through it. The borrower's own pleading treats the controversy as an ordinary money dispute. For that reason only hesset applies. Baal HaIttur likely formulated the case specifically as "one says pledged and one says sold" to teach this distinction. Even though Baal HaIttur himself does not follow the Geonic oath rule generally, his formulation works even according to them.
And if they wished to reverse the oath, and so forth. Sema explains that each party can require the other to swear that the object was sold rather than pledged, or vice versa. Shach correctly finds this strained, because "sold" and "pledged" are direct opposites. Bach and Shach explain instead that each says to the other: "You swear and collect from me." But the wording "reverse the oath" still does not sit naturally, and another problem arises. If both insist that the other swear first, one must eventually be selected to swear. Once he does so, why must the second party also swear when he had already offered to pay if the first swore? The simpler explanation is based on the rule that one does not swear about money one will not receive. The person who swears that the object was sold does not also swear that the other party owes him the purchase price if beit din is not awarding him that price. Likewise, the person who swears it was pledged does not swear to some additional debt beyond the value actually being adjudicated. That is the principle of the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia: an oath is framed around the property the litigant can actually obtain. Therefore, if the parties voluntarily choose to reverse the ordinary structure, each may say, in effect, "Swear that I owe you the dinar and I will pay," or the parallel claim on the other side. If they do not agree to such a reversal, each takes only the oath attached to his own affirmative position: one that it was sold, the other that it was pledged. Neither swears to a further monetary conclusion that beit din is not directly awarding. This is the cleanest reading of the law.
