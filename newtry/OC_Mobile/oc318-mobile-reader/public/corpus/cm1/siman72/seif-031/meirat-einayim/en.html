Your minor son came, etc. The Mechaber uses the wording of the Rosh's responsum, which was written about an actual incident of this kind. The Rosh answered that even had the son been an adult, handing it to him would still be considered negligence.
Shimon was negligent because he handed it to Reuven's son, etc. See below in siman 298, seif 21, where Rama writes in a gloss that just as we say that anyone who deposits does so with the understanding that the custodian may entrust it to his wife and children, so too if the custodian returned the deposit to the depositor's wife, he is exempt. In siman 340, seif 8, however, Rama writes in a gloss: 'Some say that the same is true if he returned it to the borrower's wife and an unavoidable accident occurred, he is liable.' Thus he distinguishes between a deposit and a borrowed item. This also appears from the Rosh's words cited by the Tur here in seif 25. The Rosh writes that although we say anyone who deposits does so with the understanding that it may be placed with the custodian's household, that applies specifically to a deposit. One who takes collateral or borrows an object from another must return it to the owner himself, as we learn from the Mishnah concerning one who borrowed a cow and sent it back through the owner's son. He explicitly states that a borrower must return it to the owner, and he compares one holding collateral to a borrower. The reason appears to be that with a borrower all benefit is his, so he must exercise greater care in guarding the item. One holding collateral is also treated as receiving compensation because of the benefit of Rav Yosef's perutah, unlike an ordinary deposit custodian, who is not liable for airing and spreading out the deposited object, as the Tur writes at the end of this siman in the name of a responsum of the Rashba. Even according to the opinion that one holding collateral is only an unpaid custodian, one may still distinguish that the part of the collateral corresponding to the money lent upon it is regarded as though acquired by him. At redemption it is as though the pledgor purchases it anew from him. Since this resembles a new acquisition, the transaction should be completed directly with the depositor himself rather than with his son or wife, unless they were authorized. The same reason applies to a borrowed object, which is acquired for the borrower's use and stands fully within his domain, unlike a deposit. This appears to be the Mechaber's view here. See what I wrote in the Sema to siman 340, seif 8, in the name of the Rashba, that even a borrower who returns the item to the owner's wife remains liable for it. As for Rama's statement at the end of siman 120 concerning a loan, that one who gives payment to the lender's wife is exempt from liability, I explained it there. In the Perishah I explained the Rosh's wording differently, based on close reading. According to that explanation, there is no distinction between a deposit, a borrowed item, and collateral. In all three cases, while the object is being guarded it may be placed with the custodian's son or wife, but the act of returning it in all three cases must be directly from hand to hand. The words of the Mechaber and Rama in the simanim cited above, however, do not appear to support that explanation. Moreover, the Beit Yosef's novellae to siman 121, seif 1, prove that with a deposit there is no distinction between the original custody and its later return to the depositor through his wife. See there. The Ir Shushan's explanation of the Rosh follows what I wrote in the Perishah, distinguishing between custody by members of the custodian's household and return through members of the depositor's household. Yet he does not mention at all the borrower and holder of collateral that appear in the Rosh's wording and in the Mechaber. See there and examine carefully.
