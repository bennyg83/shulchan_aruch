This also appears from the Rosh, who writes that although we say that anyone who deposits does so with the understanding that the custodian may entrust it to his wife and children, one who gives a pledge or lends an object to another must return it to the owner's own hand. Thus he explicitly requires personal return by a borrower and compares a pledge to it. The Sema explains that because a borrower receives all the benefit, and one holding a pledge is treated like a paid custodian because of Rav Yosef's perutah, they must exercise greater care than an ordinary custodian of a deposit. Even according to the view that a pledge holder is merely an unpaid custodian, one can distinguish because the portion corresponding to his money is regarded as acquired by him, and at redemption the owner is in effect buying it back. Therefore the transfer back must be made directly to the depositor, not through his wife or child. The same applies to a borrowed object, which is acquired by and fully in the borrower's domain during the borrowing. A deposit is different. In the Perishah the Sema offered another explanation, that there is no distinction among deposit, loan, and pledge regarding custody, which may be through the custodian's wife and children, but return must always be directly from hand to hand. The Shach rules that this latter distinction is correct: the difference is not between custodian and borrower but between custody and return. A custodian is exempt after returning to the owner's wife only when she conducts the affairs of the household, as stated at the end of siman 120. Maharshal rules likewise, and this appears primary. See responsum Maharshdam, siman 133. The Bedek HaBayit in siman 291 cites the Ritva that the rule permitting entrusting to one's household applies only to an unpaid custodian, but Nimukei Yosef explicitly applies it also to a paid custodian, as do the students of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef.
