Because it is like interest. He was precise in writing 'like interest,' because actual interest exists only where the substance of the loan is increased, such as where one lends a dinar and receives back a dinar and a perutah, which is the very meaning of interest. See Yoreh Deah at the beginning of siman 166, where the Tur writes in the name of Ramban, and the Mechaber likewise rules, regarding one who lends money to another, that he may not live in the borrower's courtyard for free. They write there that this prohibition applies initially, but if he already lived there, since the courtyard is not normally rented out, even if he himself is a person who would ordinarily rent a dwelling, he need not pay even to satisfy the requirements of Heaven. It appears that the same applies here. If he already violated the rule and used the pledged item, he need not pay for that use if the item is not normally rented out. This is all the more so here, because he is using the very item upon which he lent the money and upon which the responsibility for his loan rests. This is more lenient because he is exposed to loss, for if the item is stolen or entirely lost, his debt is lost, unlike a case in which he did not lend upon that item, where if the item he uses is stolen he may collect his debt from elsewhere. This distinction is necessary in Yoreh Deah to reconcile the Rosh's rulings in siman 166 with those in siman 172 concerning a loan made on a field. Rama likewise writes this at the beginning of siman 172, seif 1. See there and examine carefully.

But if he stipulated with the borrower from the outset, it is permitted. This applies specifically where the lender deducts compensation for the use from the debt. Rashi explicitly explains this in the chapter HaUmanin, and I cited his language in the Derishah. The novelty is that there is then no concern of appearance, because anyone who knows that the item is held as collateral will also know that the lender stipulated that he may use it in return for a deduction, since he made that condition immediately at the time of the loan. Without a deduction, however, it is prohibited like any form of interest or rabbinic interest, even if the borrower wishes to give the benefit to him as a gift at the beginning or at the end, as the Tur writes in Yoreh Deah, siman 160.
He misappropriates a deposit. A practical consequence is that from that point onward he is liable for the item even if an unavoidable accident subsequently occurs. Also, if the pledgor says that the books deteriorated by a certain amount because the lender studied from them, while the person who studied from them says that they did not deteriorate, the lender is not believed by an oath merely to exempt himself. Rather, the pledgor swears as to the amount of depreciation and collects it. This applies specifically where there are witnesses that the lender studied from the books. Otherwise he is believed with an oath by virtue of a migo that he could have said, 'I did not study from them,' since a person does not render himself a robber and disqualified witness by his own admission. The Mordechai writes this there in the name of Raavan. He also records that Raavyah wrote that although initially it is prohibited to use pledged books without a deduction, nevertheless, if he did so and read from the books, he is not considered one who misappropriated a deposit so as to become disqualified from taking an oath. Since he thought he was performing a mitzvah, because one who lends his books to others to read from them performs a mitzvah, and the reader thereby creates merit for the lender, the same applies to the pledgor or depositor. Rather, he swears that he did not damage the books and is exempt. All this is in the Mordechai. This is what Rama means here by 'some distinguish, etc., because he thinks he is performing a mitzvah,' referring to the pledgor's merit. This applies only after the fact. Initially it is prohibited without a deduction. It requires study why Rama wrote in his glosses to Yoreh Deah, siman 172, seif 1, that some permit lending on books and studying from them, and on synagogue seats and sitting upon them, even without a deduction, since this Raavyah, who disagrees and permits, appears to permit only where there is a deduction. Perhaps Rama there speaks where he stipulated from the outset, at the time of the loan, that he would study from the book or sit in the synagogue place. He holds that such a stipulation is effective because a mitzvah is involved, whereas Raavyah discusses a case where there was no prior stipulation. See what I wrote further there in Yoreh Deah.
Because he thinks he is performing a mitzvah, etc. See below at the end of siman 292, where Rama writes in a gloss concerning one who deposits books: 'If he deposited them with a Torah scholar, the scholar may read and copy from them, because he certainly deposited them with him on that understanding.' This too is from the Mordechai. That applies specifically to a deposit. With collateral, however, even a Torah scholar may not use it because of the concern of interest. With a deposit as well, permission exists specifically where it was deposited with a Torah scholar, since one may say that it was deposited on that understanding, as stated. But another person may not do so according to Raavan, who considers such conduct a misappropriation of the deposit. All this applies only where the book came into his possession with the owner's consent. To enter another person's house and read from his book without his knowledge is prohibited even for a Torah scholar, and even without any concern of interest, because the owner may object out of concern that his book will be torn. Rama rules accordingly in Orach Chaim at the end of siman 14.
