Interest. If the lender actually used the collateral, the Sema writes that where the item is not ordinarily rented out, even if this particular lender is a person who normally rents such items, whether he must pay in order to fulfill his duty before Heaven depends upon the dispute in Yoreh Deah, siman 166. I wrote in Tumim that if no such condition was made at the time of the loan and the lender simply uses the collateral on his own, beit din compels him to pay the rental value of the use. If the condition was made at the time of the loan, I already wrote in the previous seif katan that it depends upon whether the benefit is treated as fixed interest recoverable by beit din or only as rabbinic interest.
Without the owner's permission. Because ordinarily the owner is pleased with the arrangement, and one may acquire a benefit for a person even outside his presence.
But not for himself, because of suspicion. The Bach asks: the entire reason he should rent it out is to restore a lost benefit to the owner, and concerning one who returns a lost object we rule that he may even sell it to himself because there is no suspicion. The Shach answers that there is no suspicion there because, had he wished, he could have denied finding the object altogether. Here, however, once he is seen using the collateral he cannot conceal the use, and we suspect that he rented it to himself for less than the proper amount. According to this explanation, if he uses the collateral privately in his home so that no one knows that he is using another person's collateral, the concern disappears, because he could have denied using it at all, and the case would resemble a finder of lost property. But according to the Sema, seif katan 3, the concern is different. The suspicion is not that he set too low a rental price, but that an observer who sees him using the collateral will think that he uses it without deducting any rental value from the debt. On that basis the Bach's difficulty never arises. With a lost object, observers know that he is engaged in restoring another person's property and therefore have no reason to suspect him. Here, by contrast, they may suspect that he is not engaged in restoring any benefit to the owner at all, and that very suspicion is the concern.
Specifically where he did not stipulate, etc. This refers to a case where the value of the use is deducted from the debt, for otherwise it would be rabbinic interest according to all opinions. The Shach questions even this. See Tumim for what I wrote in explanation of the Rama.
He is considered to have misappropriated a deposit. If there are witnesses, he becomes disqualified like a robber, because a person is not rendered suspect on the basis of his own admission. In many areas there is a practical difference whether his act is classified as unauthorized use of entrusted property, as explained below in the laws of deposits.
Because he thinks he is performing a mitzvah. He errs in thinking so, and therefore his act is not classified as deliberate misappropriation, although it is still prohibited. See Tumim. I concluded that the case is one where no condition was made at all, so there is no issue of interest. The problem is theft, because he uses another person's property without permission. Where a condition was made, by contrast, there is no theft, only interest. Mahari Weil, as cited by the Rama in Yoreh Deah, holds that where the use is for a mitzvah it is permitted even initially. Here, however, where the issue is unauthorized use of another's property, it is prohibited initially according to all opinions. As for the ruling below in siman 292 that a Torah scholar entrusted with books may use them and there is not even a concern of theft, see Tumim. I distinguished between books of which he already owns comparable copies, which is the type of case discussed here, and other books; alternatively, one may distinguish between a deposit and collateral.
