Because of suspicion, etc. The Bach argues that since the Mishna permits renting the collateral because doing so is comparable to returning a lost object, there is no concern that the lender will be suspected. Regarding a lost object, the Gemara permits the finder, after the prescribed period, to appraise it and retain it, and Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh explain that he may appraise it himself without beit din, because one who returns a lost object is not suspected. The Tur rules accordingly below in siman 267. In my view the cases are not comparable. There, the finder already demonstrated his honesty by bringing the object into his home and identifying it as lost property. Had he wished to steal it, he would not have reported it. Here, by contrast, the lender is not returning a lost object until the very act of renting it out, and there is therefore a proper concern that he may rent it to himself below market value.

In my view this is incorrect. Rashi cannot be discussing an express stipulation, for then the lender is a hirer and is a paid custodian according to everyone. Yet the Gemara clearly states that according to the authority who rejects Rav Yosef he is an unpaid custodian, while according to Rav Yosef he is a paid custodian because he performs a mitzva. The case must therefore be one without an express stipulation, as explained below in seif 2. This does not contradict the Tur, since the Gemara there addresses the custodian's status, not the concern of suspicion. Alternatively, the Tur may follow Rabbeinu Chananel's interpretation cited by Tosafot. The Rama's ruling in a case of express stipulation nevertheless requires study, because even then the Tur may still be concerned that observers know the item is collateral but do not know of the stipulation.

He misappropriates the deposit. The practical result is that from that time onward he bears liability even for unavoidable accident. Furthermore, if the pledgor claims that the book was damaged by a stated amount through the lender's study and the lender denies any damage, the lender cannot free himself merely by an oath. The pledgor swears to the depreciation and collects. This applies only where witnesses saw him use the book. Otherwise he is believed upon oath through the migo that he could have denied using it, because a person does not render himself a robber and thereby disqualify himself through his own admission. This is the Mordechai in the name of Raavan. Raavyah writes that although it is initially forbidden to use pledged books without deducting value from the debt, one who did so is not considered to have misappropriated the deposit so as to become disqualified from taking an oath, because he thought he was performing a mitzva. Even according to Raavyah this is only after the fact; initially such use without a deduction is forbidden. The Sema therefore asks why the Rama in Yoreh Deah 172 writes that some permit lending upon books and studying from them, or lending upon synagogue seats and sitting in them, even without a deduction. He suggests that the Rama discusses a stipulation made at the outset, while Raavyah concerns a case without one. I cannot accept this. If Raavyah prohibits the benefit as interest, an express stipulation only strengthens the interest problem. As I explained in Siftei Kohen there, the Rama's permissive authorities are Maharil and the Aguda, who permit even without a deduction because the use is for a mitzva. The Rama knew of Raavyah's contrary view, and therefore added that it is proper to be stringent and make a deduction. A scholar recently suggested to me that Maharil and the Aguda too may permit only with a deduction. That is impossible. Maharil never mentions a deduction, and from the Aguda he derives permission to lend orphans' money with interest for a mitzva purpose. The Aguda itself asks how one may lend upon synagogue seats and use them after first stating that one may not lend upon a house and live in it even with a deduction; he answers that perhaps a mitzva purpose is different, referring to his earlier ruling that lending with interest for a mitzva is permitted. The permissive view therefore clearly allows study from the books even without a deduction, contrary to the Sema. I later saw that my teacher and father likewise challenged the Sema and identified the permissive authorities as Maharil and the Aguda.

Because he holds that he is performing a mitzva, etc. The Rama writes at the end of siman 292 that when books are deposited with a Torah scholar, he may read and copy from them, because the depositor presumably entrusted them to him with that understanding. That ruling, based on the Mordechai, applies only to a deposit. A pledged book may not be used even by a Torah scholar because of the concern of interest. Even for a deposit, the permission is limited to one entrusted specifically to a Torah scholar, since only then may one infer the depositor's consent. According to Raavan, another person who uses it is considered to have misappropriated the deposit. The Sema adds that all this concerns a book which came into his possession with the owner's knowledge. Going into another's house and reading his book without permission is forbidden even to a Torah scholar and even where no interest is involved, because the owner may object lest the book tear. The Rama rules similarly in Orach Chaim 14. The Sema's attribution specifically to Raavan is imprecise, since Raavyah also agrees that such use is initially forbidden. The rule concerning unauthorized use is explicit in the Gemara and Mordechai, but it is limited to books, which may tear. The Rama permits taking another's tallit or tefillin without his knowledge, and the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Semak likewise permit temporary use of another's tallit because a person ordinarily wishes a mitzva to be performed with his property. In such a case there is no misappropriation according to any view. The Shulchan Aruch's source reference to the Nimukei Yosef appears surprising, since the Nimukei Yosef records an objection: if one who uses another's property without permission is a robber, it should be forbidden to wear another's tefillin or tallit. His teacher nevertheless permits it for a mitzva, but he himself questions this from the law that one who finds tefillin appraises and retains them, which seems to imply that use before acquisition was forbidden, and from the rule that a borrower may not lend a Torah scroll to another. In my view the objection is answered by distinguishing temporary use from taking the object permanently for regular use. Temporary use of tefillin is permitted, while the finder who wishes to keep and use them regularly must first appraise them. Books differ because even temporary handling commonly tears or blurs them. This also accords with the Rosh's preferred interpretation that the finder leaves their assessed value with himself. The Gemara singles out tefillin because they exist for their mitzva use, and it would be pointless to leave them boxed. Since replacements are readily available and the owner suffers no loss, the finder may appraise and use them. The Rambam and Semag emphasize both points, that tefillin are readily purchased and are made solely for their mitzva. The Nimukei Yosef himself distinguishes in Hilchot Tzitzit between a tallit, which may be borrowed briefly in a synagogue because no principal loss is expected, and books, which may be damaged through extensive handling. The principle may also be learned from the Gemara in Pesachim that a person is pleased to spend his property for the performance of a mitzva. The Darkei Moshe independently resolves the Nimukei Yosef's objection in substantially the same way and notes that the accepted custom plainly permits temporary use of another's tallit and tefillin.
