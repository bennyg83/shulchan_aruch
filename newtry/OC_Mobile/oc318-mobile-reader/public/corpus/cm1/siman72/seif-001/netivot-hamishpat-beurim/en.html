Like interest. See Sema, seif katan 1. The proper explanation is that actual ribbit exists only when the lender receives a benefit in return for extending the loan. Here the relevant benefit is not itself the stipulated interest payment, but an ancillary benefit arising from possession of the collateral. One must therefore distinguish carefully between the prohibition of ribbit and the law that possession of collateral can make the lender a paid custodian. The latter may apply because the lender receives a legally cognizable benefit through the collateral even where the transaction does not constitute actual ribbit. See the authorities cited there.
After the loan. Where collateral is taken after the loan, the rule depends on whether the creditor had a legal right to obtain collateral at that time. When beit din could have authorized seizure, whether because the borrower lacks means of payment or because the debt has matured and he evades payment, a creditor who takes collateral, even by force, is treated as exercising a lawful right, and if the borrower voluntarily gives collateral it is as though beit din delivered it toward collection. But when beit din would have no right to take collateral, such as before the due date where the debt is secure, a voluntary delivery by the borrower has the same status as collateral given at the time of the loan. The verse governing return of a poor borrower's pledge does not apply to an unauthorized seizure, since the lender could not compel the borrower to give security after having relied on his personal credit. This also answers Ketzot HaChoshen. The lender's proprietary right in collateral is only a lien right, not full ownership of the object. The object itself remains the borrower's, while the Torah gives the lender a right in it sufficient to transfer the secured debt through delivery of the collateral. Since the lender derives a benefit from the borrower's object by being able to transfer his claim through it, that benefit can make him a paid custodian. Maggid Mishneh's proof that one who seizes without authority is not a robber is also incorrect, because the end of Maggid Mishneh's discussion proves from Makkot that such seizure violates the prohibition of robbery. The distinction suggested regarding liability for unavoidable loss when the lender seizes only up to the amount of the debt rather than beyond it is likewise unpersuasive.
He is a paid custodian. The law below distinguishes between the period in which the lender has fixed a time for the borrower to come retrieve the collateral and the period after that time. Sema and Shach explain that while the fixed period continues, the lender remains obligated to air and shake out the collateral when required, and because of the perutah of Rav Yosef he is a paid custodian. After the appointed time, he resembles an ordinary custodian whose depositor is free to come take the article and care for it himself, so the special duty no longer makes him a paid custodian. Shach equates Rashba's explanation, that the benefit of the perutah arises at the time of the loan, with the Rosh's explanation, that it arises when the lender performs the acts of airing and shaking. It follows that even according to Rashba there is a distinction between before and after the appointed retrieval time. The reason is that after that time he resembles a paid custodian whose term of paid custody has ended. When he originally accepted the collateral he undertook to guard it only until the loan arrangement ended, and by fixing a retrieval time he in effect says that he does not accept compensated custody beyond that point. Tumim seeks to distinguish the two explanations, but in my view the law does not differ. Even if the borrower has permission to take the collateral home whenever it needs airing and shaking, the lender can still qualify as a paid custodian, because circumstances may arise in which the mitzvah obligation devolves upon him, such as when the borrower is absent or when the lender is actually occupied with guarding the collateral. The Rosh's intent is that the perutah of Rav Yosef applies specifically when the lender entered into the duty of caring for the collateral as a result of the Torah's mitzvah of lending on security. By contrast, an ordinary custodian's duty to care for a deposited article does not arise from a Divine command to accept deposits, and a craftsman's duty arises from his own benefit. Since their care of the object is not generated by the compulsion of the mitzvah, they do not thereby receive the perutah benefit that would change their custodial status. This also explains the case of one who was an apostate at the time of the loan and later returned, unless the lender extended the loan after the return. Ritva, cited by Tumim, similarly writes that where the beginning of the relationship was not a mitzvah, the later care does not acquire the character of a mitzvah. His stated rationale differs, but the reason given here appears more persuasive. The same framework explains the craftsman who has completed his work. After completion, his ordinary paid custodianship has ended. If he says, "Bring the money and take your article," his continued paid status is based only on his expressed intention to retain the article for his fee. If he does not express that intention, he is an unpaid custodian after the term of his paid service ends. A lender holding collateral because of the perutah of Rav Yosef, however, remains a paid custodian as long as that mitzvah based custodial relationship continues.
A paid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 11, who writes that even where the lender needs the collateral, making the loan is a mitzvah and therefore he is a paid custodian. It appears that this is true only where there is no special deduction arrangement and the borrower ultimately pays the full agreed return. If they stipulated a deduction under which the borrower provides only a small benefit in exchange for use, the lender has a personal benefit from the transaction and his liability is that of a renter rather than one based on the mitzvah. The practical difference is when the rental term ends. A renter's compensated responsibility ends with the term, while one who is a paid custodian because of the perutah of Rav Yosef remains so while the collateral stays with him, even after payment, as Shach writes. Taz writes that where one sells on credit and takes collateral, in an ordinary sale the seller is a paid custodian according to everyone. He seeks to prove this from the laws of vows, but that proof is not decisive. In the laws of vows even a benefit worth less than a perutah can be prohibited, including customary waivers, while paid custodianship requires a benefit worth a perutah. Conversely, the perutah of Rav Yosef can make a lender a paid custodian even though lending remains permitted to one from whom benefit is forbidden. The explanation is that the latter prohibition concerns a newly conferred benefit, whereas being spared a possible expenditure for charity is only avoidance of a loss. For custodial law, however, avoidance of a loss worth a perutah is sufficient compensation, just as reciprocal guarding can create paid custodianship. In an ordinary sale, since the seller benefits from making the sale, taking collateral for the purchase price resembles a craftsman retaining an article for his fee and makes him a paid custodian. Where the goods are unusually marketable and the seller derives no particular benefit from extending credit, the result for one who accepts the perutah of Rav Yosef rationale depends on the circumstances and the judgment of the dayan. And there is no distinction. See Sema, seif katan 10. The Geonim also hold that where the collateral is lost, the lender does not thereby lose the debt. Although their principal proof against Shmuel is from the Gemara's statement that all reject Shmuel, in a case where the collateral equaled the amount of the debt, and this indicates that even then loss of the collateral does not extinguish the debt, Sema's explanation is stronger where the parties expressly stated their arrangement. An express statement can make it as though the lender received the collateral for collection, whereas without such a statement it may have been taken only as a memorial or evidence of the debt. And if it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. See Shach, seif katan 14 and seif katan 126, who rules that when the borrower has no migo, such as a debt evidenced by a note, a claim made within the due period, or a stipulation that repayment be made only before witnesses, then even if the borrower makes a definite claim, the lender swears and collects. Tumim strongly objects. Since it is known that the lender held collateral and the borrower claims that the lender accepted it in satisfaction of the debt, why should the borrower not be believed? Likewise, if the collateral was lost and they dispute its value, with the lender saying it was worth a shekel and the borrower a sela, the borrower is believed even without a migo. Why then, when the lender says the loss was unavoidable, which is effectively a claim that nothing is owed for the loss, and the borrower says it resulted from negligence, which would satisfy the debt, should the borrower not be believed without a migo? In my view Shach is clearly correct. A claim of "I have property or a counterclaim of yours in my hands" against a debt that is not yet due is a weak claim, even though the borrower does not claim ordinary early payment but rather that the creditor himself has already received value from property under his control. The poskim explain that only an ordinary repayment claim is strong, because every loan stands for repayment. A claim that the creditor owes the borrower money from another source is weak and stands against the ordinary presumption. Thus even where it is known that the creditor once held a deposit, a claim that he sold it or negligently lost it is essentially a claim of a separate counterclaim. We do not presume people to be thieves or negligent without proof. Accordingly, where the borrower says that the lender sold the collateral, which would make him a wrongdoer, or negligently lost it, the claim is weak and is not accepted without a migo. But if the borrower says that the collateral is still in the lender's possession and the lender claims that he returned it, the borrower appears to be believed, because here the lender's assertion is the weaker one. Why would he have returned security after taking it? The circumstances support continued possession. If it is established that the lender was negligent and the only dispute concerns value, the borrower's claim is strong and he is believed without a migo. The notation of collateral in the debt instrument functions like a receipt even if no value was stated. In the case of an heir producing a note where the collateral is mentioned, the reason the defendant swears and is exempt is that even according to those who permit us to plead unavoidable loss for heirs, such a plea is used only to retain property, not to extract it. See Shach, seif katan 143. Ketzot HaChoshen makes the matter depend on whether each party has an independent right of collection against the other, but that analysis cannot be accepted. Tur follows Rava and Shmuel and nevertheless requires a migo. Moreover, Tumim's objection from a dispute over the value of the collateral remains, and the poskim's general treatment of a separate counterclaim as weak does not depend on the formal rule that each side collects from the other. See Shach, seif katan 17, who holds that where collateral was taken after the loan, the lender is a paid custodian even for the value exceeding the debt, because of the perutah of Rav Yosef and the benefit that he can use the collateral to effect kiddushin. I am surprised by this. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 29 state explicitly that according to the perutah of Rav Yosef rationale, the lender cannot be liable beyond the amount of his debt. Ritva likewise writes that because the lender holds the collateral for his debt, he is a paid custodian only to the extent of the debt, since he receives no benefit from the excess. This differs from a craftsman, whose benefit relates to the whole article according to the nature of the work. Shach's proof from Bava Kamma, where one who holds an ox for damages is treated as a paid custodian even for the owner's share, was already rejected by the Rosh in Kiddushin. There the claimant can collect only from that ox. Since Tosafot, Rosh, and Ritva all state plainly that the lender is not a paid custodian for the excess even when the collateral was taken after the loan, we cannot extract money on the basis of our own contrary reasoning. One can, however, infer that where the collateral is an express apotiki, the lender is a paid custodian even for the excess, like the case of the ox seized for damages. Shach's proof from Bava Kamma is additionally distinguishable because that passage concerns liability for damage caused by the ox, and Tosafot in Bava Kamma 56 write that for purposes of damages, whoever has the power to guard an animal is treated as its owner. Rather, an unpaid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 18, who cites a responsum of Rashi stating that if Yehudah claims the collateral is no longer in his possession, he must swear that it is not in his domain. At first glance this is difficult, because Yehudah never accepted a custodial obligation, since the object was given to him as a gift. We must therefore understand the case as one where Yehudah knew that the gift was given only through an asmachta and thus knew that the purported gift had no binding force, since he was expected to know the laws of asmachta, as Shach's citation itself indicates. Tumim correctly answers Shach's difficulty by explaining that Rashi's statement concerns the consequence where the defendant cannot swear. Rashi's point is that the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear does not ordinarily apply where the defendant genuinely did not know and was not expected to know, whereas here he was expected to know. In practice, however, we rule that even where he knew it was collateral, if he cannot take the required oath he must pay, as explained in this siman. He loses his money. Tosafot in Shevuot, in the sugya of the handle of a sickle, write that according to Shmuel it is as though the lender expressly stipulated that if the collateral is lost, his money is lost. They state that this applies even in a case involving a non Jew, who is outside the ordinary laws of custodians. It follows that if land or documents were given as collateral, although they are not subject to custodial liability, the lender nevertheless loses the debt under Shmuel's rationale because the transaction is treated as containing an express condition. According to the Mechaber, by contrast, the lender is a paid custodian because of the perutah of Rav Yosef, and ordinary custodial rules do not apply to land or documents. Although Sema in siman 66 writes that with documents, where custodial law does not apply, liability cannot be created without a kinyan, Tosafot's ruling shows that Shmuel's implied condition operates even where ordinary custodial law does not. The loan itself is made subject to the condition that loss of the collateral extinguishes the debt, and such a loan condition can be effective without a separate kinyan. Thus, if a house was mortgaged and the structure burned through negligence or an event comparable to theft while the land remained, if the document says only that the house was pledged, the lender loses the debt. If it states that the property was pledged from the depth of the earth upward, thereby including the land, part of the collateral remains and the debt is not entirely lost. If a mortgage document is written in sale terminology but it is understood by local practice that repayment of the money requires return of the property, its law is that of collateral, as explained in Yoreh Deah in the laws of ribbit. The same applies in contexts ordinarily excluded from custodial liability. For example, if one receives an iska and takes collateral, even though the custody is formally custody with the owner because the recipient is obligated to manage and guard the deposit share, Shmuel's implied condition can still cause loss of the debt. Tumim attempts to reject Tosafot, but his objections are readily answered, and established practice follows Tosafot. Tumim's doubt therefore cannot displace Tosafot's certainty. As to the amount by which the collateral exceeds the debt, however, the lender is exempt even according to the Mechaber because that excess is subject to the rule of custody with the owner, and seizure does not help. The general law of custody with the owner will be explained later in the laws of borrowing. Ketzot HaChoshen's statement that according to Shmuel the lender would not be liable for a theft occurring through an unavoidable circumstance is also unpersuasive, since the Mishnah calls him a paid custodian, indicating that all the ordinary laws of a paid custodian apply. Shach's ruling that the recipient of an iska is not a paid custodian because his profit is uncertain was correctly challenged by Tumim. It is no worse than one who lends produce, who is treated as a paid custodian. Moreover, because the loan portion serves the recipient's own needs, he has the benefit of holding the collateral for money advanced for his benefit. Tosafot's answer in Bava Metzia, that a lender does not benefit from making a secured loan more than from not making the loan, does not apply where the transaction itself is for the borrower's commercial benefit. Such a case resembles a craftsman and creates paid custodianship. This is the primary ruling. Because of doubt we do not extract money. See Shach, seif katan 23, who writes that if one party has seized property he may say that he follows the poskim who support his position. This is very difficult, as Tumim notes, because the two rationales are interdependent. If the law does not follow Shmuel, then it must follow Rav Yosef, since the Mishnah expressly states that one who lends on collateral is a paid custodian. Conversely, if Rav Yosef is rejected, Shmuel supplies the basis for liability. Although one can find isolated views rejecting both approaches and reading the Mishnah differently, the overwhelming majority of poskim hold that a lender on collateral is a paid custodian either because of Shmuel or because of Rav Yosef. Therefore seizure should not be effective to alter the ruling. There is also a textual error in Shach there. The correct reading is that Rif and those who follow him do not accept that rationale, and the reference to Rav Yosef in that clause should be corrected accordingly.
