Because it resembles interest. The Sema explains that it is only "like" interest rather than actual fixed interest because the principal of the loan itself did not increase. This is incorrect. If one lends money on condition that the borrower permit him to live in a courtyard, the principal also does not numerically increase, yet that can still be fixed Torah interest because the lender gains rental value through the loan. The reason for the softer expression here is that there was no explicit condition at the time of the loan that the lender could use the pledged object without payment. Even if there had been such an express stipulation, most authorities hold that because a pledge is acquired to the creditor and a pledge held for a day has aspects of a sale, the benefit is not necessarily fixed interest. Rambam distinguishes between a house and a field, and according to his approach an express condition concerning movable collateral comparable to a house could amount to fixed interest. See Yoreh Deah, siman 172.
Like interest. The Sema writes that if the creditor actually used the pledged object, and it was an object not ordinarily rented although this particular person would have rented such an item, liability depends upon the dispute in Yoreh Deah whether he must at least satisfy Heaven. The Shach says similarly. I do not understand the comparison. A house may not depreciate through ordinary use, but movable collateral almost inevitably depreciates through use. Below in siman 363 it is explained that where an object suffers even partial depreciation, the user must pay the full benefit even if the object is not ordinarily rented. The same applies here. Even if the benefit is not classified as fixed interest, the lender must pay through beit din because he used another person's property and thereby caused depreciation. This is a form of monetary taking, unless the borrower expressly authorized the use. If he did authorize it, the classification may then depend upon the dispute whether the arrangement is rabbinic or fixed interest.
But if he stipulated initially, it is permitted. Meaning, with a deduction from the debt, as the Sema explains. The source is Rashi's explanation of a creditor who "needs the pledge," meaning he uses it and continually deducts from the debt. The Shach asks that this would make him an ordinary renter, so why does Rabbi Eliezer consider him an unpaid custodian? He therefore forces a different reading of Rashi. The Rama's rule is nevertheless explicit in Ritva and Asifat Zekenim. The baraita says that one may rent out a poor person's pledge, while renting it to oneself is prohibited because of suspicion unless the right of use was stipulated at the time of the loan. In that case the arrangement is permitted. Raavad had already raised the Shach's question against Rashi. Ritva cites the answer that the lender's permission to use the object was for a limited period. During that period he indeed resembles a renter, but once the period ended and the pledge remained in his hands, his later custodial status is the subject of the dispute. Ritva calls this answer somewhat forced, but it explains Rashi. There is also a simpler answer. The Gemara explains elsewhere that an artisan can be a paid custodian because of the benefit that customers choose him over others, and asks similarly regarding a renter whom the owner chose over other possible renters. According to the opinion that a renter is a paid custodian, that incidental benefit itself can create the higher custodial status. But here the owner did not simply choose this person as renter for ordinary rental value. He pledged the object because he needed a loan, while the creditor agrees as an act of accommodation to use the object in a manner that gradually reduces the debt. Such lenders are not so readily available that one can say he receives the ordinary benefit of being preferred over many others. Therefore he need not become more than an unpaid custodian after the special use period. In any event, the Rama's practical ruling is well founded.
And some distinguish, etc. The Sema asks from Yoreh Deah, siman 172, where the Rama permits lending on books or synagogue seats and using them without deduction, and answers that there the arrangement was stipulated at the outset. He also asks from siman 292, where a Torah scholar who receives books on deposit may read or copy them, and answers that a pledge is worse because of the concern of interest. The Shach argues that if interest is the concern, an express condition at the time of the loan should be more severe, not more lenient, because it approaches fixed interest. He therefore says that the view permitting use for a mitzvah in Yoreh Deah is not Raavyah but Maharil and Agudah, who allow it even initially because of the mitzvah. The better explanation is that the Rama here is not discussing interest at all. Yoreh Deah concerns a case where the borrower expressly authorized the use. There is therefore no issue of misappropriating another person's property, and because the interest is only rabbinic, Maharil permits it for a mitzvah. Here, however, there was no authorization. The issue is ordinary unauthorized use of the borrower's property. Initially that is certainly forbidden. The dispute is only whether, after the fact, a Torah scholar who used books because he mistakenly believed he was performing a mitzvah is treated as one who unlawfully sent his hand into a deposit. Raavyah says he is not, because his error was connected to a perceived mitzvah. Thus the two rulings address different questions. As for siman 292, there are further distinctions. Mordechai permits a Torah scholar to read books deposited with him because by choosing specifically to deposit them with a Torah scholar, where the owner could have deposited them elsewhere, the owner is presumed to have accepted that use, comparable to one who deposits loose money with a moneychanger. This presumption does not apply to a pledge. A borrower takes a loan from the first suitable lender available, and Torah scholars are particularly likely to lend on books. There is therefore no inference that the borrower consented to use merely because the creditor is a Torah scholar. If the books are of a kind that the scholar already possesses, the permission is even weaker. Accordingly, there is no contradiction.
