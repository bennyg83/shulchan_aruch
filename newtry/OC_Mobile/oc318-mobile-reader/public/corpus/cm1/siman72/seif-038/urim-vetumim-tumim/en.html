And some say the same applies to money, and so forth. The later commentators object strongly to Beit Yosef's ruling because loan money is given for expenditure. Rama therefore says that once the money reaches the borrower, even if the identical coins remain in his hand, the lender no longer owns those coins as a deposit. Shach even suggests Beit Yosef had a corrupt text in Rabbenu Yerucham. I am not convinced. Beit Yosef has substantial support. Bach cites Kiddushin 46, where the Tannaim dispute whether a loan remains in the lender's domain for purposes of retraction. The first Tanna says one who betroths with an outstanding loan does not effect kiddushin. R. Shimon ben Elazar in R. Meir's name treats the loan more like a deposit. The Gemara explains that they dispute whether the lender can demand return of the identical money while it remains unspent. This proves the question is not conceptually absurd. One Tannaitic view treats unspent loan money as still sufficiently in the lender's domain that he can retract and reclaim it. The halachah follows the first Tanna regarding kiddushin, but we must determine whether this is Torah law or a rabbinic acquisition rule. Tosafot in Bava Kamma explain that concerning a borrowed utensil there are three views: before actual use, Rav Huna allows retraction; R. Elazar says Chazal enacted meshichah for custodians so that after taking possession the owner cannot retract; R. Ami treats the acquisition even more strongly. The accepted halachah follows the rabbinic meshichah rule. If so, it is possible that on a Torah level even loan money still standing intact could remain retractable, with the borrower's stronger ownership arising from rabbinic rules adopted for ordinary Jewish transactions. That creates an important difference when a Jew lends to a gentile. Why should Chazal enact a rule specifically strengthening the gentile's ownership and weakening the Jew's Torah claim? If the money remains entirely unspent and identifiable, one can argue that on the Torah level it still belongs to the Jew for this purpose. On that basis, if the money is lost from the gentile before being spent, it can retain the character of Jewish property and its finder may have to return it. There are objections. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ritva that Rav Huna's rule regarding a borrowed axe depends on whether meshichah itself is a Torah acquisition. If gentile acquisitions work through meshichah on the Torah level, the Jew may indeed lose the right of retraction immediately. But even then there is room to distinguish acquisition of the body of an object from a loan, where only its use or expenditure is granted. Ritva himself makes this type of distinction. The issue therefore cannot simply be dismissed as a textual error. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 43 do provide real support for Rama's side. Their discussion suggests that once money may be used and need not be returned in the identical coins, the depositor or lender no longer has the same proprietary claim, and this interacts with whether the custodian is liable for unavoidable accident. No major Even HaEzer authority, moreover, seems to treat an unspent loan in a gentile's hands as automatically available for kiddushin through the lender's continuing ownership. Still, Beit Yosef's view has a coherent Talmudic basis and should not be called a simple mistake. The question turns on the precise acquisition status of unspent loan money, especially in a Jew-gentile transaction.
And they had already fully left his possession, and so forth. Sema cites Maharam that where a Jewish borrower's collateral is held by a gentile and the accumulated principal and interest exceed the collateral's value, so that under gentile law the creditor looks only to the collateral, the pledge is effectively forfeited to the gentile and one who later finds it may acquire it. Sema says this supports Rama all the more in the case of found money. Shach says Mechaber omitted Maharam because Mechaber follows Rabbenu Yerucham that even found loan money may still belong to the Jew. I do not see the comparison. In the money case, the whole question is whether the identical coins have yet left the Jewish lender's ownership. Here the debt already exceeds the collateral and, under the governing law, the gentile can look only to the pledge. The object is functionally absorbed into the gentile's claim. Maharam cannot mean that the Jew expressly said, "The collateral shall be yours in payment," because then the gentile's ownership would be obvious and there would be no need for the special reasoning about gentile creditors looking to the security. Rather, forfeiture occurs automatically because the gentile cannot proceed against the Jew personally and the pledge is his sole recourse. Still, under our law this conclusion is not automatic. Mechaber rules here and below in siman 109 that even an heir who is not personally liable for the debt can redeem collateral by paying its actual value rather than the entire excess debt. If so, the fact that the debt exceeds the pledge does not necessarily make the pledge fully forfeited. Perhaps Maharam's result instead rests on despair of recovery. If under the gentile courts the Jew has no realistic way to recover the collateral except by paying more than its value, he effectively gives up hope of recovering it. The finder can then acquire through ye'ush. Mahari Weil appears to support this distinction by differentiating collateral actually in the gentile's hands from collateral held by a Jew merely as security for the gentile. If the reason is ye'ush, the physical holder matters. If the reason were automatic legal forfeiture, it should not. There is one further historical limitation. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah siman 170 note that contemporary gentile legal systems do not always follow the older rule of looking only to the guarantor or pledged property. Where that premise is absent, Maharam's original legal-forfeiture rationale weakens. The ye'ush explanation, however, can still apply wherever actual practical recovery is hopeless.
