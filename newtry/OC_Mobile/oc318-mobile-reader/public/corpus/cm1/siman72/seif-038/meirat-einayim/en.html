He must return it to the Jew. The Tur concludes: because the body of the collateral belongs to the Jew. The non-Jew had only a lien upon it. Once it fell from the non-Jew and another Jew found it, the non-Jew's lien ceased, because the finder has no obligation to return a lost item to a non-Jew. If the finder says that he wishes to return it to the non-Jew for kiddush Hashem, as the Tur and Mechaber write below at the beginning of siman 266 that in such circumstances it is a mitzvah, he should sanctify God's name with his own property, not another person's property without that person's consent. So writes the Tur in the name of Ramah at the end of siman 183.
He must return it to the Jew. Responsa Maharam concludes, as cited by Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, that the finder fails to acquire the collateral specifically where its value exceeds the non-Jew's debt, because the pledge was not absolutely transferred to the non-Jew. But if it had become completely forfeited to the non-Jew, for example principal and interest had risen above its value, the non-Jew acquired it and the finder likewise acquires it when he finds it. Under non-Jewish law the creditor proceeds against the guarantor, and a person's property is his guarantor, all the more so collateral already in his possession. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted this.
And it had already completely left the giver's domain. See Hagahot Darkei Moshe, where I brought proof from Maharam's law that even collateral on which a non-Jew had lent money, when principal and interest increased beyond its value and it was lost from him, is acquired by the Jewish finder because it had presumptively become absolutely the non-Jew's. All the more so here, where these coins had completely and irrevocably entered the non-Jew's ownership.
