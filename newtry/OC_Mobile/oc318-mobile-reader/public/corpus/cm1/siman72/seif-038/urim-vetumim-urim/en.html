He must return it to the Jew. The body of the collateral belongs to the Jew, and the non Jew had only a lien upon it. Once it fell from the non Jew's possession, that lien terminated and the item reverted fully to the Jew, so the finder must return it. If the finder says, "I will return it to the non Jew for the sake of sanctifying Hashem's Name," he should sanctify Hashem's Name with his own property, not with another Jew's. Tur. It follows that if he nevertheless returns it to the non Jew, he must reimburse the Jewish owner as one who directly caused damage and transferred another's property.
And some say the same applies to money that he lent, etc. See Tumim, where I discussed at length how to reconcile the Beit Yosef. The case is where the non Jew has not yet begun spending the money. But if he already began spending even part of it, the entire amount is established as belonging to the non Jew, and it is no longer considered Jewish money. See there.
It appears to me that the money belongs to the finder, etc. The Shach agrees and writes that Beit Yosef had before him a corrupt text of Rabbeinu Yerucham. See Tumim. Although I found some support for the Beit Yosef's rule, as practical law the Rama's ruling appears correct, particularly because the finder is in possession and the burden of proof rests upon the claimant.
And it has already completely left his ownership. Maharam writes that the same applies to the collateral itself if accrued interest together with principal exceeds the collateral's value, so that under their law the collateral is absolutely forfeited to the non Jew. The finder then acquires it. See Tumim. I explained that the Mechaber omitted this because he holds like the opinion that the lender cannot say, "Give me the collateral's value and forfeit the remainder of the debt," by arguing that the collateral is worth the entire debt to him. According to the Mechaber, the non Jewish creditor therefore still cannot retain the collateral absolutely, so the collateral is not yet fully his and Maharam's ruling would not apply. One can alternatively say that because under non Jewish law the Jew cannot compel return of the collateral, the rule rests on the owner's despair of recovery, and therefore the finder acquires it despite Maharam's wording not sounding this way. Maharit therefore writes that if the collateral is not actually in the non Jew's possession but in a Jew's possession, Maharam's rule does not apply and the finder never acquires it. See Tumim. I further discussed a convert who lent a Jew on collateral worth less than the debt, and the Jewish borrower died leaving an heir who is not obligated to pay the excess. Does Maharam's rule apply because the collateral is effectively absolute property of the lender, so that when the convert dies the person in possession acquires it? If the rule is based upon despair of recovery, that reasoning would not apply to the property of a convert. Consider carefully.
