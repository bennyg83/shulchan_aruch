And includes. See siman 295, seif 1, where the Tur and Mechaber formulate the order oppositely: he swears first that the item is not in his possession and then includes by gilgul that it was not worth more. The Shach writes that there is no practical difference, so the authorities were not exact in the order; alternatively, there the primary claim concerns the item itself.
And is exempt. The Sema writes that the rule of "since he cannot swear, he must pay" does not apply here, as stated in siman 75, seif 15, because that rule applies only to a Torah oath, contrary to the Ir Shushan's formulation. The Shach notes that the Ir Shushan himself repeatedly states that the rule does not apply to rabbinic oaths. His point was instead that siman 75 records differing views concerning a gilgul claim of "I do not know": one view says he takes a shevuat heset on the gilgul or reverses it, while another applies the rule that inability to swear requires payment. Here, however, the ruling says that the lender includes his uncertainty by gilgul and is exempt. The Shach suggests that because the oath that the pledge is not in his possession arises from the claimant's uncertainty, out of concern that the lender may have set his eyes on it, he may likewise swear on the gilgul that he does not know and be exempt. This would accord with the latter opinion in siman 75. According to the first opinion there, which does not impose gilgul on an uncertain matter because gilgul must resemble the Sotah procedure, the present ruling is difficult. It is straightforward only according to the Rambam and the Mechaber at the end of siman 344, who allow gilgul even concerning uncertainty. The Shach leaves the Tur's position requiring substantial analysis.
Obligated. The Shach questions the Rama and Sema because in siman 388 the Rama rules like the Raavad that the rule "since he cannot swear, he must pay" does not apply in such a case, and at the beginning of siman 298 the Sema likewise says we follow the Raavad because most authorities agree. Why then do they repeatedly state the Rambam's view here? The Shach distinguishes that there the custodian commonly does not inspect what is inside sealed sacks and bundles, whereas here the pledged item is not concealed and he should have known its value. He then develops the law at length and concludes: where the claimant does not know whether the defendant himself knows, whether in a deposit, pledge, or damage case, inability to swear results in payment. But if the claimant concedes that the defendant could not know the amount, such as a sealed sack whose contents the defendant never saw, and the defendant admits part and does not know the rest, he does not pay by this rule. Likewise, where he denies everything he need not take a shevuat heset on whether he knows, since the claimant himself concedes that he does not. The same applies where everyone knows he does not know, or where one witness establishes that he does not know, except in cases of an informer, fire, or a custodian where the Sages instituted the injured-party oath and collection. In those cases one witness can allow collection without an oath. Thus, if a person has one witness that another's animal caused various damages, the owner need not pay when the witness also establishes that the owner did not know, because no oath applies. The same principle governs comparable cases.
Also. The Sema questions this ruling from seif 15, where the Tur and Shulchan Aruch say that when the borrower must swear to the pledge's value, the lender can never avoid swearing that it is not in his possession, even if the borrower trusts him. He suggests a distinction: here the lender must in any event take a shevuat heset that the pledge was worth no more than one sela. If the pledge were actually in his possession, he would produce it rather than expose himself to an oath. Accordingly, the lender should swear first, and afterward the borrower takes his shevuat heset.
