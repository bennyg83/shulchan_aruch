And he should include in his oath that he does not know, and so forth. This belongs principally to the laws of gilgul shevuah discussed in simanim 75 and 94. Since I explained the rules of gilgul shevuah at length in siman 94, I will not repeat them here. See there for the detailed analysis.
And he pays, and so forth. The early authorities object that this appears not to be a claim defined by measure, weight, or number. It resembles the Mishnah where one says, "I gave you a house full of produce," and the other replies, "Whatever you left there is what you may take," which does not create the ordinary partial-admission oath. Ramban answers, and Shach correctly explains him as Rivash did before him, that the collateral case is different. The collateral has been lost. The lender says it was worth one sela. The borrower says, "I know it was worth more than one shekel, but I do not know how much more." That uncertainty can never later be clarified from the object itself because the object is gone. The lender's own admission therefore establishes only the minimum monetary amount contained in his words: one perutah above the stated shekel. Beyond that he has made no definite admission. The remainder is consequently treated as a defined denial or uncertainty concerning a fixed monetary claim, allowing the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear. The "house full of produce" case differs because the factual quantity can later be determined from the remaining produce or the condition of the house. At the time of pleading it is simply unclear how much is admitted and how much denied. The claim is not yet expressed in defined measure. Rosh does not accept Ramban's formulation and gives another explanation. When the lender says, "I know the collateral was worth more," the minimum legal meaning of that definite statement is one perutah, because less than a perutah has no monetary significance. His unspecified admission therefore equals one perutah. If he had definitely denied the rest, the remainder could supply the required two-silver denial. Since instead he says "I do not know," he is obligated to swear and cannot, and therefore pays. Shach understands Rosh as merely contrasting a one-perutah admission here with the house case, where perhaps no perutah is definitely admitted. He objects that the produce can easily be valuable enough to include a perutah. That misses Rosh's central point. Rosh is answering two separate questions. First, how do we have the minimum monetary admission and denial? His answer is that an unspecified admission of "more" legally includes only the minimum meaningful sum. Second, why does the requirement of measure, weight, or number not prevent the oath? Here the decisive distinction is between a definite and an indefinite defense. In ordinary monetary law, a definite claimant does not automatically defeat a doubtful defendant because the burden of proof remains on the claimant. But where the Torah would have imposed an oath had the defendant made a definite denial, the defendant's own statement "I do not know whether I owe" creates the classic unable-to-swear rule. If the lender here had said definitely, "The collateral was worth only this amount," his denial would concern a clear monetary valuation and he would have to swear. Now that he says, "I do not know," his uncertainty cannot improve his position. The borrower makes a definite claim and the lender admits he cannot deny it under oath. The house-full-of-produce case is different. There the defendant does not say, "I do not know." He says definitely: "Nothing of yours is missing. Whatever you left is still there." He denies owing even a perutah. The parties are not litigating a defined partial admission at all. They dispute how much produce was originally present and how much, if anything, diminished. The defendant's position is a complete definite denial, not an inability to swear about a fixed admitted balance. Thus there is no rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear. This distinction is even clearer if some of the remaining produce rotted. The court dispute is not about the value of the admitted rotten portion. It is about whether anything beyond that was lost. The defendant says definitely no additional loss occurred. So too with collateral: if the lender says, "The collateral originally contained six cubits of material, or some amount I do not recall, but here is the same object and nothing was lost from it," he is not automatically in the unable-to-swear category. He makes a definite defense that he owes nothing because the object itself has not diminished. This, in my opinion, is the plain meaning of Rosh, and his words are precise and compelling.
And if the borrower trusted him. Sema and Bach ask: if the borrower must swear, what does it help that he trusts the lender? Beit din should not permit the borrower to swear because the lender might afterward produce the collateral and reveal that the oath was false. They answer that because the lender too must take a hesset oath, he will not later produce the object. This needs explanation. Why exactly would the lender refrain? The entire concern that the borrower may swear and the lender later produce the collateral is itself puzzling. We generally assume Jews do not swear falsely. If the borrower sincerely appraises the collateral and swears according to his belief, why should we fear desecration of Hashem's Name? Ritva asks similarly: whenever a defendant denies one hundred and the claimant says, "I have witnesses overseas but do not want to wait; let him swear now," would beit din prevent the oath because witnesses may later appear? We do not ordinarily do so. The special problem is valuation. People often disagree honestly about an object's value. One person says it is worth this amount, another says more or less. It is difficult to find universal agreement on a single appraisal. The borrower can therefore swear sincerely according to his own valuation. Yet if the lender later produces the object, other appraisers may reject that valuation and people will say the borrower swore falsely. Hashem's Name will appear desecrated and the borrower may be treated with suspicion even though his heart was truthful. That is the danger Chazal sought to avoid. This also answers Ritva's question why the Mishnah is not established with an object whose kind is readily available in the market. Where there is a known market value, the dispute may concern only which recognized type the collateral was, and the special problem of subjective valuation is weaker. The ordinary foreign-witness analogy returns. Once we understand the concern this way, Sema and Bach's answer is clearer. The same valuation uncertainty applies to the lender. His hesset oath is also based on his own good-faith appraisal. If he later produces an object that the public appraises differently, he too can appear to have sworn falsely and cause desecration. Therefore he has no incentive to hide the collateral now and later reveal it. The reciprocal oath protects against the feared manipulation.
