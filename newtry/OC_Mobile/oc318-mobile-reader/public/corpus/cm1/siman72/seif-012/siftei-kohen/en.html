And he shall swear that it was not worth more, etc. This means that he takes a Torah oath, as one who admits part of the claim. The Mechaber used the wording of the Tur, rather than that of the Rambam, whose wording is the reverse: he first swears that the pledge is not in his possession, and by gilgul he is made to swear that it was not worth more. The Sema writes that, as I explained regarding this in the Derishah, it appears that they do not disagree as a matter of law. The Bach writes that the Rambam speaks where the lender says, concerning the amount he admits, "Here it is for you." This does not appear from the Rambam, however, since he concludes, "and he shall pay the dinar that he admits." Rather, since there is no practical difference in the law, the Rambam was not exact about the order. Below, siman 295, seif 1, the Tur and the Mechaber themselves write in the Rambam's order, and there too it appears that they were not exact for this reason. Alternatively, there the principal claim concerns the object itself. In any event, there is no practical difference. See there carefully.
And he shall include in his oath that he does not know that it was worth more, etc. The Sema writes that the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay does not apply here, as the Tur writes below, siman 75, seif 15, because that rule was said only with respect to a Torah oath, and not as the Levush wrote. This is surprising, for the Levush himself writes many times in this siman and in siman 75 that the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay does not apply to a rabbinic oath. His point here is entirely different. He writes that this is the opinion of the Tur without dispute, that these are the words of the Rambam in chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh, and that the Shulchan Aruch likewise brings them without qualification as the law. He then asks that this should not be difficult in light of what we write below, siman 75, seif 16, where one opinion holds that when an uncertainty arises through gilgul we do not say that one who cannot swear must pay, but rather he takes a hesset oath on the matter added by gilgul, or the oath is reversed, while another opinion holds that one who cannot swear must pay. Here, however, we wrote that we impose by gilgul an oath that he does not know, and by swearing that oath he is exempt, which seemingly does not accord with either view in siman 75. The Levush answers that the Maggid Mishneh explained this ruling as follows: if the oath that the pledge is not in his possession had arisen from a definite claim, then, if he answered "I do not know" concerning the matter added by gilgul, he would have to pay. Here, however, the oath that the pledge is not in his possession itself arises only from uncertainty, meaning that the other party requires him to swear because perhaps the pledge is still in his possession and he has set his eyes upon it. Therefore he swears, also as to the gilgul, that he does not know, and is exempt. Thus, the Levush says, our ruling here accords with the latter opinion in siman 75. His meaning is that according to the first opinion in siman 75, an uncertain response cannot be made the subject of gilgul at all, since gilgul must resemble the case of Sotah, where she makes a definite denial, as explained below in siman 94, seif 2. If so, why is an oath imposed by gilgul here, where the defendant's answer is uncertain? It therefore must follow the latter opinion, that even an "I do not know" may be imposed by gilgul. Nevertheless, he does not pay here when he cannot give a definite oath, for two reasons: one, the oath is rabbinic; and two, the principal claim itself is uncertain. This is also necessarily the meaning of the Maggid Mishneh, for the truth is that even if the oath that the pledge is not in his possession were a Torah oath, the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay would not apply when the principal claim is itself uncertain. The Mechaber rules so below at the end of siman 344, even regarding a Torah oath, as I explain there. Thus the Levush did not mean to ask why we do not make him pay here because he cannot swear. His question is why gilgul is imposed here at all, since according to the first opinion an uncertain claim cannot be imposed by gilgul because it does not resemble Sotah. Consequently, under that view he should swear separately that he does not know, rather than include that statement within the oath that the pledge is not in his possession, with the practical difference that the separate oath would not require holding a sacred object, as explained below in siman 87. The Sema has not answered this question at all, and the Levush's question is powerful and requires serious study. Certainly, regarding the Rambam, it can be answered as the Levush and the Maggid Mishneh explain, and this must likewise be the Mechaber's view at the end of siman 344, where he rules that even with a Torah oath one may impose gilgul upon an "I do not know." Although in siman 94, seif 2 and seif 9 he writes that an "I do not know" is not imposed by gilgul, his rulings there are independently inconsistent with his ruling in siman 344, as I explain there. But the Tur cannot be explained this way, for the Rosh holds in the chapter HaShoel and at the end of Kol HaNishba'in that an "I do not know" is not imposed by gilgul at all. Moreover, in siman 75 the Tur writes that the Rosh agreed with the first opinion, and in siman 94 the Tur likewise writes, based on the Rosh, that an uncertain claim of the defendant is not imposed by gilgul at all, since only a definite claim of the defendant, like Sotah, may be imposed by gilgul. We must therefore say that this case is different: because the oath that the pledge is not in his possession is rabbinic, we may impose upon him by gilgul an oath that he does not know. The Rosh and the Tur said there that one does not swear by gilgul upon an "I do not know" only where the gilgul would generate a Torah oath, since a Torah oath cannot be taken upon an uncertain response. For that reason they also do not say there that he is obligated to swear and cannot swear and therefore must pay. Here, by contrast, he takes a rabbinic oath, while holding a sacred object, that he does not know. This explanation is somewhat forced. See carefully.
That he does not know that it was worth more, etc. One may ask: since the lender has the legal status of a custodian, why should we not let the borrower swear and collect, by virtue of the enactment made for one who was robbed in a deposit, as explained below in siman 90, seif 10 and at the beginning of siman 298? In siman 90, that enactment applies even where the custodian does not admit part of the claim. It is difficult to answer that the case there is different because the deposited property was in a sack. If anything, the opposite seems more reasonable, as I will write shortly. It appears that the enactment for one who was robbed was made specifically regarding an ordinary depositor, in order that the custodian be careful in guarding the property. Here, however, because the lender advanced money against the pledge, no such enactment was made, since even without it he will be careful in guarding the pledge on account of his loan. According to this, the law is the same even if the object pledged was in a sack. See carefully.
I know that it was worth more than the amount of the debt, etc. The Rosh writes in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim that since he says, "I know it was worth more than the amount I am owed," he has admitted that it was worth at least one perutah more, because less than a perutah is not treated as a value in a single object. The Ramban, Ran, and Rivash write similarly, and I cite them in the next seif katan. This supports the latter view below in siman 75, seif 19. See what I write there.
He is one who is obligated to take an oath. And since he cannot swear, he must pay. This is the ruling of the Tur, Rambam, and the other poskim. The Maggid Mishneh, chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh, writes that this is the view of Rabbi Yosef ibn Migash in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. The Rambam writes similarly in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah concerning one who deposited unmeasured produce with another and the custodian mixed it with his own produce without first measuring it; the Rambam adds that his teachers, Rabbi Yosef Halevi ibn Migash and his teacher, ruled likewise. The Tur and the Mechaber rule similarly below in siman 292, seif 14, siman 298, seif 1, and siman 388 at the end of seif 1, as well as elsewhere. In the next seif katan I discuss the Raavad's objection that the rule should not apply because the custodian was not expected to know, the poskim who agree with him, and why the Rambam and those who follow him are primary. The Raavad raises an additional objection in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah: a partial admission sufficient to generate a Torah oath must be an admission of something defined by measure, weight, or number, as explained below in siman 88, seifim 23 and 24. The Semag, positive commandment 88, after citing the Rambam's ruling in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah, which the Mechaber quotes below in siman 298, seif 1, likewise writes that in his view only a rabbinic oath is present, since the defendant has not admitted a defined number of dinars and said that he does not know the remainder. Since it is only a rabbinic oath, one who cannot swear is not made to pay. This is the same objection as that of the Raavad. The Shiltei Giborim at the end of HaKones and in Chezkas HaBatim, folio 177, calls this a powerful objection against the Rambam and writes that he does not know how to answer it. It appears that he did not see the Rosh and Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim, as I explain below, for he does not mention them. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 38, part 3, folio 169, also asks this question against the Rambam, discusses it at length, and leaves it unresolved. I am surprised that he did not notice that the Raavad, Semag, Maggid Mishneh, and Rosh had already addressed it, as I explain below. Because this issue affects many places, we must examine it thoroughly. In my view there is no difficulty at all with the Rambam. The Rosh writes in Shevuat HaDayanim as follows. Regarding one who lends to another upon a pledge, after citing the concern that one party might swear and the other later produce the deposit, Rabbi Yosef Halevi writes that where the lender says, "I do not know how much it was worth, but I know that it was worth more than the amount I was owed, and I do not know how much more," he is obligated to take an oath and, being unable to swear, must pay. Some challenge this: since he does not know how much it was worth, there should be no Torah oath because he has not admitted something defined by measure, weight, or number, and it resembles the Mishnah's case where the defendant says, "I do not know, but whatever you left, you may take." Furthermore, he has not denied two silver ma'ot, since he says that he does not know how much it was worth. Nevertheless, all the great authorities agreed with Rabbi Yosef. The Rambam writes in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah that if one deposited unmeasured produce with another, and the custodian mixed it with his own produce without measuring it, he was negligent; if the owner says, "There were such and such an amount," and the custodian says, "I do not know," he pays without an oath, because he has made himself liable to pay but does not know how much he owes, and he is thus obligated to take an oath but cannot swear. The Rambam adds that his teachers, Rabbi Yosef Halevi and his teacher, ruled so. The Ramban answered the objection, though the Rosh says that his words were not understood. The Rosh himself answers that when the lender says, "I know it was worth more than the amount I am owed," he has necessarily admitted at least one perutah of additional value, since less than a perutah is not treated as an independent value in one object. Thus there is an admission of money. Since the admission is unspecified, it contains only the smallest monetary amount, one perutah. It is therefore as if he expressly admitted one additional perutah. As to the remainder, he says, "I do not know." If he had denied the remainder, the denial would amount to two silver ma'ot, and since instead he says that he does not know, he is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must pay. The Rosh distinguishes this from the Mishnah's case of "I delivered to you a house full of produce," where the defendant says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take." There, says the Rosh, the defendant does not give an uncertain answer. Rather, he says that he did not pay attention to whether the house was literally full, and the plaintiff's assertion is not so exact either, since ordinary speech calls a house nearly full of produce "a house full." But the defendant knows definitely that whatever was left remains for the plaintiff to take. Thus there is no admission of a defined measure, weight, or number. By contrast, if the depositor says, "I deposited such and such an amount of produce," and the custodian says, "You deposited produce with me, I know it was worth one perutah, but I do not know how much more," then, if the depositor's claim is worth two silver ma'ot, there is an obligation of an oath. But if the custodian merely says, "You deposited produce," without admitting a perutah's worth, there is no implication in his admission of even one perutah, since the term produce can apply even to less than a perutah. These are the Rosh's words. With due respect to the Rosh, however, I say the reverse: his explanation is difficult, whereas the Ramban's words are readily understood. The Rosh appears to answer the Mishnah's case by saying that only in a loan upon a pledge has the lender necessarily admitted one perutah, while in the Mishnah's case of "whatever you left you may take" he has not admitted even a perutah because even less than a perutah may be called produce. To the other objection, that there is no denial of two silver ma'ot, he answers that as to the remainder there is a denial of two silver ma'ot, unlike the case of a full house because there the defendant's response is not uncertain. I cannot reconcile these words. What does it mean that there the defendant does not answer uncertainly? And what does it mean that the plaintiff's claim is not definite? We rule like Rava that even if the plaintiff says definitely, "I delivered to you this house full," or "I delivered to you up to this beam," and the defendant says, "Whatever you left you may take," the defendant is exempt because he has not admitted anything defined by measure or number. Moreover, the plain meaning of the Mishnah surely includes valuable produce such as etrogim, where even one fruit is worth a perutah. If the defendant says, "There was produce in it, but I do not know how much," he has certainly admitted at least a perutah. The Mishnah also teaches, "I delivered to you a purse full of coins," and the defendant says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take," and he is exempt. One might explain that he means, "I do not know whether it contained money or dross," but that is not the plain sense of the Mishnah. The Rambam at the beginning of chapter 4 of Hilchot To'en, the Semag, and the Tur and Mechaber below in siman 88, seif 23, likewise clearly indicate that even if the defendant admits that the purse certainly contained coins, such as dinars or perutot, he is exempt because he did not admit a defined number. This is also proved from the Tosefta at the end of chapter 5 of Shevuot: "You have ten lamps of one hundred litra each of mine"; "I do not know, they are before you." "You have ten garments of mine"; "I do not know, they are before you." "You have ten kor of wheat of mine"; "I do not know, they are before you." He is exempt because he has not admitted a measure. But if one says, "I have a house full of produce in your possession," or "a purse full of coins," and the defendant says, "I have only half or a third," he is liable because a house is a measure and its half or third is a measure, and a purse is a measure and its half or third is a measure. Even one lamp or garment is surely worth a perutah, yet the Tosefta implies that although he necessarily admits at least one lamp or one garment he remains exempt because the admission is not in a defined measure. The plain sense of the Gemara in Shevuat HaDayanim is likewise so. I further find difficult the Rosh's distinction that when one merely says "produce" there is no implication of a perutah because produce can refer to less than a perutah. The Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah expressly speaks of one who deposited produce and of a custodian who says, "I do not know." It appears that the Rosh would have to read the Rambam as speaking where the custodian says explicitly, "I know it was worth a perutah, but I do not know how much more." This is not a sound reading. The Rambam says simply, "the custodian says, I do not know," which implies that he says only that he does not know how much there was, and no more. Had the law depended on a distinction between one who merely says "I do not know" and one who says "I know it was worth a perutah but do not know whether it was worth more," the Rambam should have stated that distinction explicitly. As for the Rosh's statement that the Ramban answered but his words are not understood, in my humble view the Ramban's words are clear and fundamental, as I shall explain. The Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim writes that one may still question Rabbi Yosef Halevi's ruling concerning a custodian who lost the deposited object and says, "I do not know how much it was worth." Why do we regard him as one obligated to swear who cannot swear and therefore must pay? He has not admitted something defined by measure or weight, and his case seems like one who says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take," who is exempt. Furthermore, he has not denied two silver ma'ot, for he says he does not know how much it was worth. The Ramban answers that the cases are different. When one says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take," he has admitted everything presently in the house, something that can be ascertained, and therefore it is not an admission of a defined number and he is exempt. But a custodian who says, "I do not know how much it was worth," has admitted only the minimum monetary amount, namely one perutah, and as to the remainder he is treated as a complete denier because his statement itself cannot establish any further liability, since the value can no longer be ascertained. It is like one who says, "Fifty I know, and fifty I do not know," whom we judge as one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Here too it is as though he says, "I know it was worth one perutah, and the remainder I do not know." This applies only where he says, "I know it had monetary value, but I do not know how much." If he says, "I do not know whether it was worth anything at all," he is exempt because there is no admission whatsoever. Maharam of Tiktin altered the Ran's text in this passage to refer to "the same type as the claim," but that emendation is incorrect, for the issue here has nothing to do with whether the admission is of the same type as the claim. It appears that he too did not reach the Ramban's intention. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 38, part 3, folio 169, also seems not to have seen the above words of the Rosh, or the Raavad and Semag whom I cited. He writes that he was troubled by the Rambam's ruling, discusses the difficulty at length, and leaves it unresolved. He then says that after writing all this he found the Ran's citation of the Ramban, but that in his humility he did not understand the Ramban. He argues that when the defendant says, "Whatever you left you may take," and the plaintiff says, "I delivered to you this house full," the defendant certainly admits everything now found in the house, and as to the balance up to a full house he says that he does not know whether there was more. Why is that different from a custodian who says, "I do not know how much it was worth," where we say that his admission is only the minimum monetary amount, one perutah, and that as to the balance he is a complete denier? In the house case as well, his admission should be only the minimum possible, namely what is now found there, and as to the rest he should be deemed a complete denier. The fact that we can now inspect and know how much is in the house, he argues, should make no difference, and there is no "here it is" defense here. But, in my view, this too fails to understand the Ramban. His meaning is clear. When the defendant says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take," although his admission necessarily includes at least one perutah, he has admitted whatever is actually there, and that amount is not fixed by his words. There may be more than fifty in the house. He has not admitted precisely one perutah, since he says, "Whatever you left you may take." If there are several perutot there, the plaintiff takes them all. Thus his admission has no fixed amount. It is as though he said, "I do not know whether there is one perutah there or several." Even if in fact only one perutah is later found in the house, because he made his admission depend upon whatever presently existed before us, he did not give his admission a fixed amount. At the time of the admission, beit din could not know what amount to impose upon him. When we later inspect the house and see a fixed amount, that amount is fixed by the existing property itself, not by his admission. His admission therefore was not of something defined by number. By contrast, where a custodian says, "I do not know how much it was worth," it is as though he immediately admitted exactly one perutah and no more, for an unspecified monetary admission contains only the smallest monetary amount. The object has been lost and is no longer before us, so one cannot say that if there turns out to have been more the owner will take more. The custodian can be made liable only by virtue of his own admission, and that admission establishes exactly one perutah and no more. At the time he speaks, beit din therefore knows a fixed amount to charge him: one perutah. His admission has a fixed measure and is an admission of something defined by number. In the Mishnah, by contrast, beit din cannot fix his liability from his words, but only says, "Go and take whatever of yours remains there." This is the Ramban's meaning. I have explained it at length only because the Rosh wrote that the Ramban's words were not understood, and later authorities likewise did not understand them. In my view the Ramban's words are clear, compelling, and correct. Examine them carefully and you will see that this is so. It appears that this is what the Maggid Mishneh intended in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah when he wrote that the Raavad relied upon the other argument, that there is no denial of something defined by number, but that the Ramban had already rejected this argument with a sound reason in his novellae to Shevuot. The Shiltei Giborim, at the end of HaKones and in Chezkas HaBatim, folio 177, writes regarding this objection that the Maggid Mishneh says the Ramban answered it well in his novellae to Shevuot, but that he himself did not know the answer. It seems that he did not see the Ramban's words as quoted by the Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim. The Rivash, siman 381, likewise cites Rabbi Yosef Halevi's rule that a custodian who says, "I do not know how much it was worth," is one obligated to swear who cannot swear. The Rivash writes that if one were to say that because it is not known that the pledge was lost the parties are litigating over the pledge itself rather than over the amount of the loan, then there is another reason why no oath would arise: when the lender says, "I do not know," he resembles one who says, "Whatever you left you may take," and we do not treat him as one who admitted something defined by measure, weight, or number. When the dispute is over money, by contrast, if he says, "I do not know how much it was worth," we treat him as having admitted the smallest monetary amount, because no greater amount can be ascertained from his words. But where one says, "I do not know, but whatever you left you may take," in the Mishnah's case of a house full of produce, he admits everything in the house, which can be inspected; he has therefore admitted an amount that is not fixed by his words and is exempt. These words appear to be drawn from the Ramban, for the Rivash also cites the Ramban's further qualification that the custodian is liable only if he says, "I know it had monetary value, but I do not know how much." If he merely says, "I do not know," perhaps he has not admitted even one perutah. These are exactly the Ramban's words as quoted by the Ran. The ruling of Rabbi Yosef Halevi, the Rambam, and those who follow them therefore stands, and the objections of the Raavad, Semag, and the later authorities cited above are rejected. It appears that the Semag himself later retracted his objection, for in positive commandment 70, folio 148 at the beginning of column 2, he writes in accordance with the Rambam, as the Mechaber quotes below at the end of siman 388, seif 1, and in positive commandment 94, folio 176, column 4, he writes in accordance with the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber here. Thus the Semag's statement on folio 171 contradicts his own rulings in two other places, and the consensus of the poskim in many places follows the Rambam. This is the ruling we adopt. See Responsa Maharshadam, siman 69.
He is obligated to take an oath and cannot swear, etc. The Raavad objects to this as well, and the Beit Yosef cites his words: "He follows his own approach, but we do not agree with him, because this defendant's 'I do not know' does not come in response to the plaintiff's definite assertion that 'you know it was worth two.' If the plaintiff says that, the law is indeed as the Rambam writes." The meaning is that the Rambam follows his general position that, in a deposit case, when a custodian says "I do not know," he is treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear, as explained below in siman 90, seif 10, siman 292, seif 14, and at the beginning of siman 298. The Raavad objects in all those places and holds that the rule applies only in a case like "fifty I know and fifty I do not know," where the uncertain answer appears deceptive because the defendant should have known. In an ordinary deposit, however, the custodian may genuinely not know and was not expected to know. So too here, concerning a pledge, the lender may truly not know its value, and his claim is not deceptive because he was not expected to know. But if the borrower says definitely, "You know that it was worth two," then the Rambam's law applies: he is obligated to swear and, being unable to swear, must pay. This is the structure of the Raavad's position, and the Baal HaItur likewise explains this explicitly in his laws of deposits, folio 47, column 4. According to this, however, the Rosh and Tur are difficult, because below in siman 298 they rule like the Raavad, while here the Tur rules without qualification like the Rambam, and the Rosh in Shevuat HaDayanim likewise rules like the Rambam concerning a pledge. The Rashba too seems inconsistent. In chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah the Maggid Mishneh cites him as holding that where the defendant was not expected to know we do not apply the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and the Beit Yosef cites this below in siman 292, seif 16. In Responsa Rashba, siman 571, concerning a moser, he likewise rules that where one was not expected to know the rule does not apply, as the Beit Yosef cites below in siman 388, seif 6, and as the Darchei Moshe and Sema cite there. Yet here, in this siman, the Beit Yosef in his fourteenth additional note cites a Responsa Rashba concerning one who lent money upon a ring set with a precious stone, the ring was lost, and the lender does not know its value, where the Rashba rules that he is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must pay whatever the borrower claims. It is forced to say that a lender is always expected to know the value of a precious stone. The Rama and Sema are likewise difficult. The Rama rules below at the end of siman 388, seif 1, like the Raavad, and the Sema at the beginning of siman 298 writes that we rule like the Raavad because most poskim agree with him. How then can they rule here, and again below in seif 16 and several other places in this siman, without qualification like the Rambam and Mechaber? It is difficult to say that every one of these cases assumes that the borrower says, "You know it was worth two." At first glance one might distinguish between an ordinary deposit and a loan upon a pledge. In an ordinary deposit the custodian may not be expected to know the contents or value, but one who advances money on a pledge normally examines what it is worth in relation to his loan. The Mabit, part 1, second collection, siman 133, makes a similar distinction. He writes that if one borrows from another a ring containing a precious stone and loses it, we do not treat him as one obligated to swear who cannot swear because he was not expected to know the value of the stone, whereas the Beit Yosef's citation here from Responsa Rashba concerns a lender who advanced money upon such a ring and therefore should have known its value. A better distinction appears to be that only in the ordinary deposit case do they follow the Raavad, for the reason given by the Rosh and cited by the Tur in siman 298: daily practice is that a custodian does not carefully inspect what is deposited with him in sacks and packages. A pledged object, by contrast, is not ordinarily enclosed in such a sack and the lender should know its value. We are compelled to make some such distinction, for the Rosh in Shevuat HaDayanim rules like the Rambam concerning a custodian who was negligent and does not know the value. One could, however, distinguish that case because the custodian mixed the deposited produce with his own, and after doing so he should have known the amount. The Sema below, siman 292, seif katan 36, and the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah make this distinction. As a matter of law, however, it appears to me that the Rambam and those who follow him are primary, rather than the Raavad, as I will explain. The Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot She'eilah writes that his teacher Rabbi Yosef Halevi and his teacher's teachers ruled likewise. The Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim cites the same ruling in the name of Rabbi Yosef Halevi and the Rif, notes the Raavad's objection, and says that the Ramban resolved their position. It appears that the principal source for the Raavad and Rashba's distinction, that the rule does not apply where the defendant was not expected to know, is the Yerushalmi cited by the Rif at the end of HaKones concerning a person who deposited a tied bundle with another and an unavoidable accident occurred. This is explicit in the Raavad's gloss at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot She'eilah and in the Maggid Mishneh's discussion in chapter 5 in the name of the Raavad and Rashba, who explain the Yerushalmi this way because they were troubled why that custodian was not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear. In my view, however, that Yerushalmi proves nothing here. First, its primary subject is liability for concealed property damaged by fire, as the Ran writes at the end of Shevuat HaDayanim, the Nimukei Yosef at the end of HaKones in the name of the Raah citing the Ramban, the Rivash at the end of siman 381, and the Rosh at the end of HaKones. If so, it has no connection to our issue. Even according to the Rosh's report that the Geonim interpreted the Yerushalmi as dealing with a deposit and applied to deposits an enactment analogous to the enactment for one who was robbed by fire, there is still no proof. The Raavad says that the custodian at least admitted the sack, but perhaps the sack itself was not worth a perutah, or perhaps the owner did not claim for the sack and waived it. Indeed, the Raavad himself appears to have been driven to say that the sack constituted a partial admission only because otherwise he could not explain why the owner swears and collects. He then avoided the rule of one who cannot swear by saying that the custodian was not expected to know. But if the Yerushalmi concerns concealed property in a fire, or a special enactment giving the depositor the status of one robbed, then it is entirely possible that the sack does not constitute the relevant admission. All this appears clear to me. Although the Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim does not expressly discuss the distinction between one who should know and one who should not, and his words therefore do not conclusively show whether the Ramban follows the Rambam and his teachers on that point, the fact that the Ran says the Ramban resolved the position of the Rambam and his teachers implies that the Ramban accepted their ruling fully. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh, after citing the Raavad's gloss that one who was not expected to know is not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear, writes that the Ramban's view inclines to that of the Rambam. The Beit Yosef cites this at the end of siman 6. I likewise found in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 39, part 2, a responsum of the Ramban stating: "Do not be misled by what some authors have written, that this rule was said only concerning 'you have one hundred of mine,' where the defendant should have known. That is an error. The chapter HaBayit VeHaAliyah proves that even where he was not expected to know, he is liable, and the rule that one who cannot swear must pay applies; the same is true in the chapter HaShoel and elsewhere." Although Tosafot at the beginning of Shor Shenagach Et HaParah and in HaShoel write that the case of HaBayit VeHaAliyah also concerns one who should have known, since a person normally recognizes his stones, the plain sense supports the Ramban. The Nimukei Yosef in HaBayit VeHaAliyah likewise writes in the name of the Ran that from that passage we learn that even with an untainted uncertain claim, where the defendant was not expected to know, one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. I have indeed seen that Tosafot at the beginning of HaParah follow the Raavad, and the Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri, Nimukei Yosef there, and later authorities follow them. But I found no proof in their words sufficient to overturn the Rambam and those who follow him. Tosafot there and the Rosh at the end of HaKones offer one argument: in Kol HaNishba'in the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay is derived from the verse "the oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them," which the Gemara expounds as between the litigants but not between their heirs. The Gemara explains the heirs' case as one where an heir says, "Fifty I know, and fifty I do not know." The father in that situation would be liable. They argue that there must be a reason why the father is liable and the heirs exempt, for otherwise there would be no basis for the distinction, just as the Gemara says that if the heir said, "Fifty you have and fifty you do not," there is no difference between him and his father. The reason, Tosafot say, is that the father should have known whether he owed the full hundred, whereas heirs cannot be expected to know their father's affairs. They therefore extend this reasoning to a case where one could not be expected to know whether the cow gave birth before or after it was gored. Based on this, the Rosh at the end of HaKones likewise says that a custodian does not ordinarily inspect the contents of sacks and packages and therefore should not be treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear. In my humble view, however, there is never such a distinction regarding the original defendant. The heirs are exempt because the dispute was not originally with them but with their father. Perhaps, had the father been alive, he would have said, "You have fifty, and I know definitely that you do not have the other fifty," and would have sworn and been exempt. The heirs cannot be made to pay merely because they do not know their father's defense. On the contrary, careful analysis of Kol HaNishba'in supports the Rambam. Rava says that Rabbi Abba's position is reasonable because Rabbi Ami taught, "the oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them, but not between the heirs." If the heir says, "Fifty you have and fifty you do not have," there is no difference between him and his father. Rather, it must be where he says, "Fifty I know and fifty I do not know." If the father in such a case would be liable, a verse is needed to exempt the heirs, but if the father would also be exempt, why is a verse needed for the heirs? If one says that the father too is exempt whenever he was not expected to know, the Gemara's question returns: why is a verse needed for heirs? According to Tosafot one must say that the verse reveals a special rule for heirs and those like them, who ordinarily do not know, but this is not the plain sense of the Gemara. Furthermore, their view makes the rule fluctuate from case to case, requiring us to determine whenever uncertainty arises whether the defendant should have known. It is difficult to say that the Torah left the law dependent upon such varying assessments, and the Gemara nowhere expressly makes this distinction. The Rambam's view is therefore preferable. The case of Shor Shenagach Et HaParah also presents no difficulty. Although Tosafot offer two other answers there that can reconcile the passage with the Rambam, in my view there is no question to begin with. The rule that one obligated to swear but unable to swear must pay applies only where the claimant seeks an oath from the defendant and the defendant cannot swear definitively. If the claimant himself knows, just as the defendant does, that the defendant lacks the relevant knowledge, no oath applies at all. Suppose one says, "You took my sealed purse and threw it into the sea in my presence. You did not know what was inside, while I know there was a dinar in it." Would the defendant be obligated even to take a hesset oath that he does not know? Or suppose the plaintiff says, "In your presence I put one dinar into the purse, and earlier, outside your presence, I put another dinar into it, which you did not know about," and the defendant answers, "That is true. I saw you put in one dinar, and I do not know about the other that you say you put in earlier." Can one say that he is obligated to swear but cannot swear merely because he does not know? He is not obligated to swear about his lack of knowledge at all, because the plaintiff admits that he does not know. Beit din can say to the plaintiff: what do you seek from him? You do not contradict him. If you want him to swear that he does not know, such an oath would be in vain, since you yourself admit that he does not know. An oath applies only where there is some contradiction. If the plaintiff makes an ordinary claim and does not concede that the defendant lacks knowledge, then even though it is possible, or even more likely, that the defendant truly does not know, the defendant must swear that he does not know, because the plaintiff's position is that perhaps the defendant does know. But where the plaintiff himself knows that the defendant does not know, there is no oath at all. Accordingly, in Shor Shenagach Et HaParah the injured party himself knows that the owner of the ox does not know whether the fetus was expelled before the goring or as a result of it. No oath applies there according to any opinion. The Rambam himself so rules in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon, and the Mechaber below in siman 400. With this principle, the ruling in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Kinyan, siman 18, and in Responsa Maharam, Prague edition, siman 299, can also be understood. Those responsa seek to prove a distinction between one who should know and one who should not from Kol HaNishba'in and Shor Shenagach Et HaParah, and then say that otherwise a single witness could impose enormous liability by saying that another person's animals caused many different types of damage that no one could be expected to know about. At the end, the responsum says that the proof from HaParah can be rejected because that sugya does not accept Rabbi Abba's rule from Kol HaNishba'in, but it does not reject the other two arguments. According to what I have written, however, there is no proof from Kol HaNishba'in, and the concern about a single witness is likewise no proof. A single witness cannot make another liable for innumerable acts of damage done by his animals outside his presence, because the witness himself concedes that the owner does not know what happened, so the witness does not contradict the owner's lack of knowledge and no oath arises. This appears clear to me. The conclusion is therefore as follows. In a case of "fifty I know and fifty I do not know," or where a defendant says "I do not know" and one witness contradicts him, the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay applies whenever the claimant does not know whether the defendant actually knows or not, even if it is more reasonable to assume that the defendant does not know. Thus, if one deposits a purse of gold coins with another, the custodian negligently loses it, admits that it contained gold coins, but does not know how many; or one lends money upon a pledge and the pledge is lost and he does not know its value; or one borrows a ring with a precious stone and does not know its value, in each case he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear and must pay. If he had denied everything by saying, "I do not know whether it was worth even a perutah," he would at least have had to take a hesset oath that he truly did not know. Now that he admits part and is thereby subject to a Torah oath, he is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must pay. The same applies to one who snatches a purse of gold coins from another and throws it into the sea, while not knowing how many coins it contained. But if the claimant admits that the tortfeasor or custodian does not know how many gold coins were present, for example because he handed him a sealed sack and knows that the custodian or tortfeasor never saw its contents, or because he gave the lender a sealed pledge whose contents the lender never saw, then where the defendant admits part and does not know the rest, he is not treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Even if he denies everything, he does not take a hesset oath that he does not know, for the plaintiff himself admits that he does not know. The same is true where it is universally known that the defendant cannot know. A single witness likewise cannot create the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear in such a case. The only exception is where the Sages enacted that the opposing party swears and collects, such as in certain cases of one robbed, a wife, or a depositor. There, a single witness may serve to exempt the claimant from the oath and allow him to collect without swearing. Thus, where a single witness says that another person's animals caused various forms of damage, the owner is not automatically liable to pay if the witness concedes that the owner does not know what occurred, because no oath applies at all. Similarly, where an ox gored a cow and caused it to miscarry in the injured party's domain, even though the owner admits that his ox gored it, if the injured party concedes that the owner does not know when the miscarriage occurred, he is not one obligated to swear who cannot swear. The same principle applies in all comparable cases.
And if the borrower trusted him that it was lost, etc. The lender too takes only a hesset oath, etc. Although whenever the borrower must swear, the lender is not spared the oath that the pledge is not in his possession even if the borrower believes him, as stated below in seif 15 in the gloss, that case is different because we fear that the lender may later produce the deposited object and thereby cause a Divine oath to have been taken in vain. Here, however, since the lender must in any event take a hesset oath that the pledge was not worth more than one sela, we do not have that concern. If the pledge were in his possession, he would immediately produce it rather than subject himself to an oath. According to this, the lender must first take his hesset oath, and afterward the borrower takes his own hesset oath. This is how the Sema and Bach explain the matter.
