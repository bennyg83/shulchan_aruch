The creditor takes a heset oath. See Responsa Veshav HaKohen, siman 79, concerning Reuven who had a loan relationship with Shimon secured by a pledge, after which Shimon placed the pledge with Levi and it was lost from Levi. Reuven claimed that the pledge was worth a stated amount more than the loan, while Shimon denied that valuation. Veshav HaKohen explains at length the responsum of Rashi cited by the Shach above, seif katan 18, and concludes that where Reuven and Shimon dispute the value of the pledge, Shimon himself must swear as to its value. He cannot exempt himself by an oath taken by the second creditor or custodian, because Reuven may say that he does not trust that second person regarding the pledge's value. Even if Shimon regularly deposits property with that person and trusts him in other matters, Reuven may say that he does not trust him specifically on this valuation, because the first creditor does not have definitive knowledge of the second person's reliability in that respect. He concludes that the practical ruling still requires study. See there and Shaar HaMishpat, seif katan 14.
And he shall swear, etc., and include, etc. See Responsa Veshav HaKohen, siman 79. As explained above, seif 3, if the creditor returned the pledge to the debtor as a deposit and it was then lost from the debtor, the creditor remains liable because the debtor is only a gratuitous custodian. If in such a case the debtor claims, "I borrowed one sela upon it and it was worth two," while the creditor says it was worth only five dinarim, one cannot simply have the creditor swear and be exempt. There is concern that the creditor may swear and then the debtor will produce the deposited pledge. Therefore the debtor must first swear that the pledge is not in his possession, and only afterward does the creditor swear that it was worth no more than five dinarim and become exempt. This follows Rashi and the explanation of Rabbeinu Chananel and Rif concerning the concern that the other party may later produce the deposit. According to Tosafot's alternative reason in Bava Metzia 34b and Shevuot 43b, namely concern that the pledge itself may deteriorate, that concern does not apply where the creditor is swearing only that it was not worth more than the stated amount. On this basis Veshav HaKohen explains precisely the Mishna's wording, "Whoever has the deposit in his possession shall swear first, lest this one swear and the other then produce the deposit." Why did the Tanna formulate it in terms of who has the deposit, rather than simply asking who swears first, and why use the word "deposit" rather than "pledge"? This also properly explains Mahari Weil, cited by the Rama in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 217, who derives from this Mishna that one who vows not to accept deposits is also prohibited, if that was his intent, from lending on collateral, because the term "deposit" can encompass both an ordinary deposit and a pledge.
