Whether he lent him produce. In that case the lender benefits from the loan, because the produce might have rotted in his home, while the borrower must return good produce at the end of the loan term. It is therefore obvious that the lender becomes a paid custodian over the collateral. With paired expressions such as 'whether...whether,' the simpler case is commonly listed last. The Tur, however, writes the cases in the opposite order.
Whether he took the collateral at the time of the loan or, etc. The Tur writes the order oppositely: 'whether he took it not at the time of the loan,' etc. In Perishah I explained that this is a progression: certainly if he took the collateral not at the time of the loan, where he acquires it under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule, or alternatively because it is certainly being taken for collection, he has a stronger status. Even where it was given at the time of the loan, where one might have thought he merely holds it as a reminder, the rule still applies. The Mechaber uses the neutral 'whether...whether' formulation, whose last member is often simpler than the first.
He is a paid custodian. This is the view of the Geonim. The reason is that one who lends on collateral is performing a mitzvah. If a poor person were to appear at the moment he is extending the loan, he would not have to give him bread or other charity, because one occupied with one mitzvah is exempt from another. Because of that perutah of benefit received when he lends money on the collateral, he becomes a paid custodian over it for as long as it remains with him, just like a paid custodian whose compensation is received at the beginning or end. So writes Rashba. See Perishah for another rationale.
There is no distinction whether, etc. This gloss is printed in some copies on the Mechaber's statement that he is a paid custodian, but it belongs later, on the rule that if the debt exceeds the value of the collateral, the borrower must pay the excess. It teaches that even if the lender accepted the collateral without specification, we do not say he accepted it against the entire debt, like Shmuel, who holds that if a borrower leaves as collateral the handle of a sickle and the handle is lost, the entire debt is lost. The Geonim do not rule like Shmuel. All the more so, if the lender explicitly stated when taking the collateral that he accepts it only up to its value and no more, then if it is lost the borrower must pay the balance of the debt. The Tur explicitly states this in the clause where the debt exceeds the collateral: 'There is no distinction whether the lender expressly said he accepts the collateral only to the extent of its value or accepted it without qualification.' In Derishah I wrote that the Tur follows Rashi's version of the Gemara, which understands Shmuel's rule, 'if the sickle handle is lost, one thousand zuz are lost,' to apply even where no express statement was made. Rather, the lender simply accepted the collateral without qualification, even though it was worth less than the debt. In that specific case the halacha does not follow Shmuel. All the more so if he expressly said he accepts it only to the extent of its value. But if at the time the collateral was handed over he expressly said, 'Although it is not worth the amount of the debt, I accept it for the entire debt,' even the Geonim agree that if the collateral is lost, the money is lost. This is what Rama writes in the following gloss, that some say if he expressly states he accepts it for the entire debt, the entire debt is lost. Rama uses 'some say' because Tosafot reject Rashi's version and read the Gemara so that Shmuel's rule applies specifically where the lender expressly said, 'Although it is not worth the amount, I accept it.' In Derishah I wrote that the Geonim also appear to follow Tosafot's version. Under that version, since the Geonim do not rule like Shmuel, even in such a case only the value of the collateral is lost. But because Rif also adopts Rashi's reading, the Tur and Rama follow Rashi and Rif in presenting the Geonim's ruling so as to minimize disagreement. According to this, the Geonim and Ri, who rules like Shmuel, agree that where the lender said, 'Although it is not worth the amount, I accept it,' the entire debt is lost. One should not explain Rama as holding that the Geonim follow Tosafot's reading, so that even such a statement does not forfeit the excess debt, and that Rama's 'some say' refers only to a still stronger statement, 'if the collateral is lost, my money is lost.' If that were his meaning, the principal distinction would be missing from the text; he should have written explicitly, 'I accept it and my money will be lost.' Moreover, he should first have stated the greater novelty that even saying, 'Although it is not worth the amount, I accept it,' does not forfeit the entire debt. Accordingly, Rama's later unqualified citation of Ri and his conclusion that money is not extracted in doubt also make sense. If the Geonim held the broader contrary position, Rama should have also applied that leniency for the defendant where the collateral is worth less than the debt. Rather, as explained, Rama relied on the immediately following gloss that some say an express acceptance for the whole debt loses the whole debt. Understand this carefully. See Derishah further.
And some say that if he expressly said, etc. This gloss belongs immediately after the preceding gloss, and both belong here, as I explained above. I also explained why Rama presents this view as 'some say.'
If the collateral was lost through unavoidable accident, etc. A paid custodian is exempt from unavoidable accidents. Even if the lender expressly said at the time of the loan that he accepts this collateral against the entire debt, his presumed intent covered only theft and loss, which are common, not unavoidable accidents, which are uncommon. The Tur and Mechaber write a parallel rule below in siman 225 concerning liability.
The owner of the collateral must pay him the debt. This applies specifically where the borrower does not contradict him with a definite claim. The Tur in seif 9 and the Mechaber in seif 14 explain: since the borrower does not know how the collateral was lost, it is like one who says, 'You lent me, but I do not know whether I repaid you.' This implies that if the borrower says with certainty, 'The collateral is still in your possession,' or, 'You sold it and received its price,' the lender cannot extract money merely by swearing that the collateral was lost by unavoidable accident. The Mechaber writes this explicitly below in seif 29: if the borrower says with certainty that the lender sold it and received the money, the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt. The Tur likewise writes it in seif 23. Yet the Tur there explains that the borrower is exempt through a migo that he could have said, 'I owe you nothing,' or, 'I paid you,' implying that where no such migo exists, for example the loan was made before witnesses who know it was not repaid, the lender would be believed. We must therefore say that the Tur invokes the migo only to explain why the borrower is exempt with a mere hesset oath. Where he lacks the migo, the lender still cannot extract against the borrower's definite denial, but the borrower may have to take a stringent oath against the lender's claim, because witnesses establish the loan and nonpayment, and ordinarily the Torah grants force to the lender's claim. Since the borrower now asserts a definite counterclaim, he is at least protected against extraction on the lender's oath. Therefore the Mechaber below in seif 29 simply says that the borrower takes a hesset oath and is exempt, without mentioning migo.
He is only an unpaid custodian, etc. This view does not regard the occasional possibility of exemption from charity at the moment of lending as payment for custodianship. This applies specifically to collateral belonging to a Jew, received for the Jew's benefit without interest. But if one Jew delivers to another Jewish lender collateral belonging to a non-Jew and pays him interest from it, it appears obvious that the lender is a paid custodian and, if it is stolen or lost, must pay the full value of the collateral. First, with such a substantial benefit it is reasonable that everyone agrees he is a paid custodian. Second, the case is treated as though he lent directly to the non-Jew who pays interest and for whom he would be liable for the collateral. The Jew who brings the non-Jew's collateral acts as the lender's agent to receive it from the non-Jew and bring it at his responsibility, and to carry the money to the non-Jew on his behalf and at his responsibility, as the Tur cites from the Rosh in Yoreh Deah, siman 169. Thus it is as though the lender dealt directly with the non-Jew. This seems clear according to the Rosh, unless the non-Jewish law provides that a lender who receives interest on collateral and loses it need not pay any excess of the collateral's value. In such a jurisdiction the Jewish intermediary likewise would be exempt for the reason stated. I heard that Responsa Ibn Lev is uncertain about this; what appears correct to me I have written.
Rather, his money is lost, etc. The Tur explains the reason. Although this opinion holds the lender is only an unpaid custodian, he nevertheless loses from the debt the amount equal to the collateral's value, even when the loan and collateral were given without special terms and even when the collateral was given at the time of the loan so that Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition rule does not apply, for Rabbi Yitzchak spoke only of collateral taken after the time of the loan. The reason is that Ri and those who follow him rule like Shmuel, who says that if the lender expressly stated at the time of the loan, 'Although this collateral is not worth the amount of my debt, I accept it,' then if even a mere sickle handle is lost, the whole debt of a thousand zuz is lost. Since the halacha follows Shmuel, they also say that even without any statement the lender at least accepted responsibility for the collateral up to its value. This is explicit in Tosafot, and we must interpret the Tur accordingly, as I wrote in Perishah, unlike Eir Shushan who explains that this view makes the lender a paid custodian only to the extent of the debt because of the mitzvah. That is incorrect. According to Ri, the halacha does not follow the view that the lender becomes a paid custodian because of the mitzvah; proof is that regarding a found object Ri rules like Rabbah that the finder is only an unpaid custodian, even though he performs a mitzvah with the lost object itself. Rather, the reason is as stated. See Derishah.
And because of the doubt, we do not extract money. Rama means to teach that we rule according to these 'some say' that the lender need not pay the excess value of the collateral. Know that the Tur further concludes according to Ri, who rules like Shmuel, that if the debt exceeds the collateral, the borrower pays the lender the excess where the loan was made without special terms. But if they expressly stipulated, certainly if the lender said that if the collateral is lost the whole debt is lost, the borrower does not pay the excess. Even if he merely said, 'Although it is not worth the whole debt, I accept it,' then even if he lent one thousand zuz on a sickle handle, loss of the handle loses the whole debt. Likewise, if he lent one thousand on two sickle handles, or on a sickle handle together with another pledge, and one is lost, half the debt is lost; if the second is also lost, the remainder is lost. As I explained, in the case where an express acceptance causes the entire debt to be lost, there is no difference between the Geonim and Ri according to the Tur and Rama's presentation. Therefore Rama did not repeat it. Nevertheless he should have stated the useful distinction where the loan was secured by two separate items and only one was lost, because the Gemara contains differing views on that point. See Perishah.
