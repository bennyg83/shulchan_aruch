Whether he took the collateral at the time of the loan, etc. The Mechaber follows his position in the Beit Yosef that all authorities, apart from Rashi, hold that even collateral taken after the loan renders the creditor only a paid custodian, whereas Rashi holds him liable even for unavoidable accidents. In my view the primary talmudic interpretation is Rashi's, and many leading authorities agree. The matter requires extended clarification because Tosafot and numerous great authorities disagreed with him. The Gemara repeatedly states in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak that a creditor "acquires" collateral. This language implies a genuine proprietary acquisition, not merely the duties of a paid custodian. Rabbi Yitzchak derives the rule from the verse that returning the collateral "shall be righteousness for you": if the creditor does not acquire it, what act of righteousness is involved in returning the debtor's own property? A custodian's return of a deposit is not charity. Tosafot and the Rosh themselves ask that if the acquisition is complete, the creditor should be liable for accidents, while if it is incomplete it should not even make him a paid custodian. Their answer, that the acquisition suffices to betroth a woman or acquire slaves and land with the collateral, is itself difficult. If he lacks full ownership, how can he effect those acquisitions? The better conclusion is that collateral seized after the loan becomes fully his. Passages in Gittin and Pesachim apply Rabbi Yitzchak's rule to collateral taken at the time of the loan for the laws of remission and chametz. This does not disprove Rashi. Tosafot explain that because later collateral is fully acquired, collateral taken with the loan is at least acquired sufficiently to prevent remission and to be called the creditor's property for chametz. Rashi can use the same distinction, and his position is actually more coherent. If even later collateral is only partially acquired, why should that limited right extend to remission and chametz but not to betrothal and other acquisitions? According to Rashi, later collateral is fully owned, while contemporaneous collateral is merely held strongly enough that the debt is not considered collectable by demand and the chametz is attributed to the creditor. Full title is still required for betrothal and ordinary conveyances. This answers the Ramban and Rashba, who inferred that Rabbi Yitzchak spoke even of collateral taken with the loan and treated the Gemara's distinction as uncertain. The plain language of the Gemara is definite: Rabbi Yitzchak spoke only of collateral taken after the loan. The passages in Kiddushin likewise can and should be read as later collateral. The case of betrothal through remission of a debt secured by collateral is explained by Tosafot as involving the woman's own collateral, and it derives from Rabbi Yose bar Chanina rather than Rabbi Yitzchak. Indeed, had the Ramban's reading been correct, the Gemara should have cited Rabbi Yitzchak directly. Many authorities, including Tosafot, the Rosh, Mordechai, Semag, Tosafot Rid, and the responsa of the Rosh, expressly limit Rabbi Yitzchak to collateral taken after the loan. The principal objection to Rashi is that the Mishna calls the lender a paid custodian rather than one liable for accidents. This is not decisive. The Gemara could have asked that question but instead attacks the proposed explanation of the baraita more fundamentally, since the baraita itself concerns collateral taken at the time of the loan while Rabbi Yitzchak concerns later seizure. Alternatively, the initial assumption may have been that complete acquisition of later collateral creates only paid-custodian liability for collateral taken with the loan, just as Tosafot extend the later acquisition partially for remission and chametz. The Gemara rejects that partial extension: Rabbi Yitzchak said later collateral is acquired; he did not say contemporaneous collateral is partially acquired. There is no sound reason to divide ownership in this fashion. The Ramban also argues from Rabbi Akiva's wording that when the collateral "is lost," the debt is lost, supposedly implying ordinary loss rather than unavoidable accident. Yet the Gemara elsewhere regularly explains such language as a ship sinking at sea, an unavoidable accident. Rabbi Akiva may simply have used Rabbi Eliezer's wording. Rabbi Eliezer stated the case broadly to teach that even where the object is lost, he swears and collects because, in his view, the lender is not even a paid custodian. None of these formulations disproves Rashi. A more satisfactory reading removes the supposed conflict between Rashi and the passages concerning contemporaneous collateral. Raavan explains that the Tannaim in Pesachim may disagree with Rabbi Yitzchak and hold that even collateral taken with the loan is acquired. More fundamentally, there are two distinct legal grounds. Collateral taken by beit din after default, where the debtor has no other means of payment, is effectively appropriated toward the debt. The creditor's lien is concentrated upon that object, and the Torah orders him to return it at times of need as an act of charity. Because it stands in place of payment, he acquires it fully to the extent of his debt and bears accidental loss. Collateral voluntarily delivered at the outset is not payment and remains the debtor's property, though the creditor has sufficient possession for certain laws and may become a paid custodian through the mitzva benefit described by Rav Yosef. This distinction also answers the comparison to a damaged ox seized by the injured party. Before judgment, especially where half-damages are a fine, the claimant has not yet obtained an adjudicated monetary right. Even according to the view that half-damages are compensatory, his claim has not been established by beit din. He therefore does not acquire the ox as payment, though the benefit of holding it securely can make him a paid custodian. Moreover, according to Rabbi Yishmael the injured party is merely a creditor; the tortfeasor can redeem the ox with money, and the injured party's consecration or sale is ineffective. That case is unlike later collateral, which Rabbi Yitzchak says is fully acquired and may be used for betrothal or the purchase of land. The Ramban further argues that the creditor lacks all benefit because he must return the collateral for the debtor's use, may not use it himself, and has his money tied up. These considerations do not negate ownership. When the Torah commands return of a plow by day or bedding by night, it imposes an act of charity upon the creditor. While the item is in his possession he may arguably use it, although ordinary patterns of use make that uncommon. Even if use is forbidden, the prohibition serves to preserve the object so that the required charitable return remains meaningful; it does not show that title remains with the debtor. The comparison to an artisan who acquires the improvement of a vessel is also no proof. Where there is an actual improvement, the artisan who acquires it has genuine proprietary rights and can betroth a woman with it. The Mishna's general statement that artisans are paid custodians concerns cases without an acquired improvement, such as payment by the blow or mere delivery work. A paid custodian who has no proprietary right cannot betroth a woman with the entrusted object. Thus the talmudic reasoning strongly supports Rashi: collateral seized after the loan through beit din is fully acquired, including liability for accidents, at least to the amount of the debt. Nevertheless, many authorities rule otherwise. Therefore one should not simply extract money against them. If the debtor has not yet paid, he may say that he relies upon Rashi and the authorities who agree with him. If he paid and then retook property from the creditor, even before witnesses, his seizure is effective because possession is effective in a dispute among early authorities. Rashi is supported by the Raavad, Rav Ephraim, Baal HaMaor, Baal HaItur, Raavan, Tosafot Rid, Rabbeinu Baruch, and other great authorities. The Ran and Migdal Oz describe the issue as an old dispute; the Aguda and Rabbeinu Yerucham record both positions. Later responsa likewise treat reliance upon Rashi and the Raavad as a valid claim. It is also possible that the Rif has been misunderstood. He cites Rabbi Yitzchak and the Gemara's distinction between collateral taken with and after the loan, even though he independently rules like Rav Yosef that a lender is a paid custodian because of the coin he saves while engaged in the mitzva. If Rabbi Yitzchak merely created paid-custodian liability, the entire discussion would be unnecessary. The Rif may mean that later collateral is fully acquired for accidents, while contemporaneous collateral creates paid-custodian liability through Rav Yosef. Rav Yosef's rationale also applies to later collateral when the creditor spreads or airs it, and therefore the Rif states that in either case he is a paid custodian. This matters for value exceeding the debt. Proprietary acquisition can cover only the amount owed, whereas paid-custodian liability covers the excess as well. The Rif's subsequent statement that unavoidable loss leaves the lender free to collect his debt may therefore refer specifically to a loan originally made upon collateral. Although the other authorities' broad language makes this interpretation uncertain, it is entirely consistent with the Gemara. All of this concerns collateral taken lawfully after the loan, through beit din or with the debtor's consent, as in the verse requiring charitable return. A creditor who seizes collateral without authority is a robber and is liable for accidents according to everyone, not because Rabbi Yitzchak grants him title but because wrongful seizure places the object entirely at his risk. He is liable even for value beyond the debt. This resolves the Gemara in Makkot, which assumes liability when a creditor enters the debtor's house and takes collateral unlawfully. The Rambam and Maggid Mishneh likewise hold that such an unlawful taker is responsible for accidents, even though they generally exempt a lawful creditor from accidental loss. The Ramban's forced distinction is therefore unnecessary. In conclusion, lawful later collateral is, in my view, acquired as Rashi explains, while unlawful seizure independently creates full accidental liability as robbery.
He is a paid custodian. A lender upon collateral performs a mitzva. If a poor person approaches him while he is occupied with it, he need not give the poor person a coin, because one engaged in one mitzva is exempt from another. The benefit of that saved coin makes him a paid custodian for as long as the collateral remains with him, until it is returned to its owner, even after the debt was repaid. This is clear from the Rashba, the responsum of the Rosh, and the rulings below in seif 43. Maharashdam questions this from the law of an artisan who says, "Take your item and bring the money," where he becomes an unpaid custodian, showing that paid-custodian status depends on intending to retain the object for payment. There is no difficulty. An artisan is paid only because he holds the item against his fee; once that intention ends, so does the paid status. Here, the status began as though the lender received a coin at the outset of custody through Rav Yosef's benefit, and therefore it continues throughout the custody.
A paid custodian, etc. The Gemara concludes that even where the lender needs the collateral for permitted use, he performs a mitzva and is a paid custodian according to Rav Yosef. This appears from the Rif, Baal HaMaor, and the other authorities, who make no distinction. Rashi and Tosafot explain that the lender uses the collateral while deducting its value from the debt, as do the Nimukei Yosef and Raavan. One cannot object that he should then be a hirer even according to the authority who rejects Rav Yosef. He is a hirer only when the parties expressly stipulate that he may use it. When he merely has the legal ability to use it with an accounting, he does not become a hirer until he actually uses it.
Some say, etc. The Bach argues that no Gaonic authority holds this view. In my opinion, the Rama did not intend the Geonim but Rabbeinu Yitzchak's position, cited by the Tur and the authorities, who rule like Shmuel. The Sema's reading would make the Tur's presentation of the dispute between the Geonim and Rabbeinu Yitzchak pointless if they agreed in practice. Rather, according to the Geonim, even if the lender said, "Although it is worth less than the debt, I accept it," the statement is treated like an unstated case. This is Rabbeinu Chananel's version, which rejects Shmuel. If the lender expressly says that loss of the collateral will cancel the entire debt, Tosafot indicate that even the Geonim agree that the whole debt is lost. The Beit Yosef says likewise. The Bach suggests that even this may be an unenforceable undertaking according to the Geonim, and the Hagahot Ashri in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel supports that understanding. In practice every formulation remains a monetary doubt, and the burden of proof rests on the claimant, as the Bach rules.

If it was lost through unavoidable accident, etc. The owner of the collateral must then pay the debt. The Sema limits this to a case where the borrower does not assert with certainty that the collateral remains in the lender's possession or was sold. He infers this from seif 14, which compares the borrower to one who says, "You lent me, and I do not know whether I repaid," because the borrower does not know how the collateral was lost. Where the borrower says with certainty that the lender sold it and received its proceeds, seif 29 states that the borrower swears a rabbinic oath and is exempt. The Tur there adds that the borrower has a migo that he could have denied owing anything or claimed payment. The Sema therefore suggests that without such a migo, as where witnesses establish the loan and nonpayment, the lender still cannot collect merely by swearing, but the borrower must take a more severe oath against the lender's claim. The Mechaber omitted the migo because it affects only the kind of oath. I disagree. Where the borrower has no migo, the lender swears and collects. Baal HaTerumot, the source of this law, states this explicitly, and the Bach also indicates it. Seif 14 must therefore concern a borrower who does possess a migo.

Some say, etc. According to this view the lender is only an unpaid custodian. The Tur makes clear that even where he lent produce, he remains only an unpaid custodian, and the Rosh says the same. At first this is difficult, for perhaps Rabbeinu Yitzchak disagrees with Rashi only because he rules like Rabbah rather than Rav Yosef, while produce that tends to decay may independently make the lender a paid custodian through the benefit of the transaction, as Rabbi Yehuda says in the Mishna. The Rosh and Tur evidently reason that according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, the first Tanna's paid-custodian liability only to the amount of the debt rests on an implied stipulation that loss of the collateral cancels the debt. If produce independently created full paid-custodian liability, the first Tanna should have distinguished produce from money and held the lender liable for the entire value. Since he did not, he rejects Rabbi Yehuda's rationale altogether. There is therefore no distinction between money and produce, as the later authorities and Rama also imply.
He is only an unpaid custodian, etc. The wording implies that even when the collateral exceeds the debt, and even when it was taken after the loan, the lender is not liable beyond ordinary negligence. The authorities do not distinguish between collateral taken with the loan and collateral seized later. This supports the view that those who reject Rashi understand Rabbi Yitzchak only as creating a limited acquisition sufficient for collection, remission, and similar laws, not liability for accidents. Nevertheless, as explained above, the debtor may retain or seize the amount of his claim by asserting reliance upon Rashi and the many authorities who agree with him. Where the collateral was taken unlawfully, however, the creditor is liable for accidents according to everyone as a robber. The value exceeding the debt is included, because wrongful possession places the entire object at his risk.
Only an unpaid custodian, etc. The authorities imply no distinction between a loan evidenced by a note and an oral loan, contrary to Halachot Gedolot. The Tur and Mechaber likewise make no distinction. The lender's status depends upon the legal character of the collateral and the mitzva benefit, not upon the form in which the debt was documented. Where the parties made an express condition concerning loss of the collateral, the condition governs subject to the dispute discussed above. Otherwise the ordinary rules stated here apply equally to written and unwritten debts. The creditor's ability to prove the debt by a note does not itself turn him into a paid custodian, nor does the absence of a note reduce his custodial obligation.
An unpaid custodian, etc. According to this view, once the borrower repays the debt, the lender still bears only the liability of an unpaid custodian for the collateral. This is stated by the Beit Yosef and by the Rama below in seif 43. See what I write there.
Rather, he loses his money. The law treats the transaction as though the lender expressly stipulated that if the collateral is lost or stolen, his debt will be lost. In an unavoidable accident, however, he is exempt and may still collect. Mahari ibn Lev discusses one who receives collateral worth less than the debt and says that if the collateral is lost the debt is lost. The effect depends on the exact language and on the dispute whether the statement is a valid allocation of risk or an unenforceable contingent undertaking. Where the meaning is clear and effective, the loss is limited to the amount the parties assigned to the collateral; any remainder of the debt may still be collected.
And no more, etc. The Sema writes that this limitation applies to collateral belonging to a Jew and pledged for an interest-free Jewish loan. If one Jew pledges collateral to another Jew for a loan from a non-Jew, or where the arrangement involves permitted interest, the allocation may differ according to the parties' undertaking and the amount for which the collateral stands. In my view the governing point is whether the lender accepted the collateral as satisfaction only up to its value or expressly undertook broader responsibility. One cannot impose liability beyond the debt merely because the collateral passed through another person's hands. Conversely, an express and valid stipulation that the collateral stands for a particular amount is enforced. Any excess value remains governed by the ordinary laws of custody, and any deficit remains a debt unless the lender clearly accepted the collateral in full satisfaction.
Money is not extracted because of a doubt. The Sema understands the Rama to be teaching that the law follows the cited opinion, so that the lender need not pay the excess value of the collateral because money cannot be extracted in a doubt. In my view the Rama does not definitively decide the underlying dispute. He merely applies the general rule that the party seeking payment bears the burden of proof. Therefore, when the borrower demands the excess from the lender, he cannot collect it without establishing the lender's liability. The converse also follows where the lender seeks to collect the debt and the borrower relies upon the opposing authorities. Possession and the direction of the claim determine who must prove his position.
We do not extract money. If the lender seized property corresponding to the amount of his debt, it is not removed from him. Tosafot and the other authorities indicate that the Rif and those who follow him did not conclusively reject the opposing view. At minimum, this is a dispute among early authorities in which possession is effective. Thus, where either party already holds the disputed amount, the other cannot recover without proof. This is especially so in light of the many authorities supporting Rashi's view, as explained above.
