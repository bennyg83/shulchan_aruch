Whether he took the pledge at the time of the loan. See Shach, seif katan 9, and Korban Netanel, first chapter of Kiddushin, entry 53.
Whether he took the pledge after making the loan. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 152, who writes that even if the item was first deposited with him under the laws of an unpaid custodian, and he later seized it for his debt without the borrower's permission and without permission from beit din, he nevertheless becomes a paid custodian. This is unlike Responsa Perach Mateh Aharon, part I, siman 71. See there.
He is a paid custodian. In the glosses of Rabbi Akiva Eiger to Shulchan Aruch it is written: If the pledge was delivered to him before the loan, he does not become a paid custodian of it until he actually lends the money, as stated below in seif 52. End of quote. See Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, entry 20, who writes in the name of Maharashach, part II, siman 169: Even according to the opinion that the pledge holder is a paid custodian, the rule in the chapter HaSocher Es HaPoalim that a paid custodian cannot defend himself by saying, 'What more could I have done?' because the owner can say, 'I paid you so that you would perform superior guarding,' applies specifically where he receives explicit payment for guarding. In the case of a pledge, however, where he is considered a paid custodian only because of Rav Yosef's perutah benefit, we certainly do not treat him as an ordinary paid custodian in this respect. If he can say, 'What more could I have done?' he is exempt even without a stipulation. End of quote. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 10 of Hilchos Sechirus, law 1, cites these words of Maharashach and challenges them, asking who divided the laws in this way. If he becomes a paid custodian because of this benefit, all laws of a paid custodian should apply to him. Responsa Maharik, shoresh 155, likewise indicates this. See there. Ketzos HaChoshen, seif katan 5, also cites Maharashach and discusses the matter. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 16, who appears to agree with Maharashach. The same principle, he writes, applies to all cases where a person is treated as a paid custodian by presumed intent, such as because of the benefit that the owner passed over everyone else and hired him, or because a reputation for trustworthiness results, and similar benefits. Such benefits do not obligate the custodian in the extraordinary level of additional guarding applicable to one who is directly paid for guarding. Chatam Sofer writes that this distinction is hinted to in the wording of Bava Metzia 93a, 'Who is the Tanna who holds that a renter is like a paid custodian?' He further suggests that this is the view of the Raavad cited by the Rosh in chapter 7 of Bava Metzia, siman 16. See there, and see what I write below in siman 303, seif katan 4.
A paid custodian. See Baer Heitev and Shach, seif katan 10, who resolves Maharshdam's question against the Rashba, according to whom even after repayment the pledge holder retains the status of a paid custodian. See there. See Responsa Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Orach Chayyim, siman 11, regarding what he writes on this matter.
Only an unpaid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 18, who questions Responsa Rashi. See Responsa Veshav HaKohen, siman 78, who gives a sound resolution. See there.
And no more. See Baer Heitev, who writes that if a Jew pledges to another Jew a non Jew's collateral and pays him interest, etc. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 216, who writes that it is even more obvious that the same applies to a wicked Jew who lends another Jew at fixed interest. Such a substantial benefit renders the lender a paid custodian. This is certainly true in our times, when unfortunately the borrower cannot practically recover the forbidden interest through judges and would not even think to sue for it. But even if our authority were strong enough to compel the lender to disgorge the interest, the fact that the lender holds and uses the interest money until it is recovered through litigation itself renders him a paid custodian. See Shach, siman 292. The Sema and Shach mention a non Jew only because they do not discuss wicked Jews. See there.
