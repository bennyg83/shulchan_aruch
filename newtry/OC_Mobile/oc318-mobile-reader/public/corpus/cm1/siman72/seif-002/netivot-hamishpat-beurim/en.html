Whether he took the collateral other than at the time of the loan. See Shach, seif katan 9, who writes at length to answer Tosafot's difficulty with the Gemara in Perek HaOmanin, which says that Rav Yitzchak's law applies only to collateral taken other than at the time of the loan, while passages in Gittin regarding shemittah, Pesachim regarding chametz, and Kiddushin indicate that Rav Yitzchak's proprietary rule applies even to collateral given at the time of the loan. Shach also labors to prove Rashi's view that the lender is liable even for unavoidable loss. His explanations are strained, conflict with the rulings of the Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim regarding chametz and in Even HaEzer regarding kiddushin, and oppose the consensus of the Rishonim and Acharonim. The way I propose to reconcile the authorities is as follows. Shach appears to understand that collateral taken after the loan becomes the lender's actual property. In my view this cannot be correct. He cannot sell it, as explained in siman 96, seif 22, and he cannot use it, as Shach himself writes in his second answer. In what sense, then, is it his property? We do not find an article that one may neither sell nor use and yet is called fully his. Rather, the lender's acquisition in collateral has two legal effects. Rashi in Pesachim 31 explains that when the due date arrives and the debt remains unpaid, where the collateral was already delivered to the lender and no further act of collection is needed, it becomes clear retroactively that it belonged to him from the time it was pledged. The Gemara there identifies this as the full acquisition derived from Rav Yitzchak. The first effect of the acquisition is therefore retroactive collection. If the lender consecrated or sold the collateral and eventually collected it, the consecration or sale takes effect, as the Gemara states according to Abaye. A second effect follows: through delivery of the collateral the lender can transfer the secured debt itself, even though ordinary assignment of debts is only rabbinic. Tosafot in Bava Batra 77 ask why a lien on land cannot be transferred by money, document, or chazakah just as the land itself can, and answer that because collection is only prospective, the lien resembles property not presently in one's control. With collateral, however, collection operates retroactively. The lien can therefore be transferred through delivery of the collateral just as ownership of an object can be transferred by meshichah. Even if one says that a land lien cannot be transferred by money even according to Abaye because the specific land is not fixed and the debtor may discharge the debt with other property, collateral is different. The Torah specifically strengthened the lien in identified collateral so that delivery of the collateral can transfer the secured right. These two consequences, retroactive collection and transferability of the lien, are interdependent.
This framework answers Tosafot's difficulty. Once the Torah reveals that a creditor acquires a proprietary right in collateral, there is no reason in principle to distinguish, for these proprietary consequences, between collateral given at the time of the loan and collateral taken later. Thus the chametz case in Pesachim is readily understood: because collateral is collected retroactively, the Mishnah follows the same logic as Abaye. Kiddushin likewise presents no difficulty. Since the lender has a proprietary right enabling him to transfer the secured debt by delivery of the collateral, when he hands the collateral to a woman she acquires that right, and the kiddushin can take effect. Even an ordinary promissory note belonging to another can effect kiddushin when assigned by writing and delivery, because the woman acquires the debt. Here too she acquires the debt through delivery of the collateral. The view in Even HaEzer that a woman is not betrothed through collateral given at the time of the loan, despite the lender's ability to transfer the debt, must either understand the attempted kiddushin as made with the physical object itself, or, more fundamentally, follow what I wrote in siman 64: the lender cannot transfer the borrower's personal obligation itself so as to enable the transferee to sue the borrower. The lender's right in the collateral is only sufficient to permit the transferee to retain the collateral until the loan is repaid. The personal obligation remains nontransferable and can be waived. According to that view, the woman never acquires a claim that she can independently enforce for money, and this may be insufficient for kiddushin. The opposing view holds that the proprietary interest in the collateral is itself worth money and therefore suffices. The same explains shemittah. Because the lender has a proprietary lien in the collateral, the debt is considered one in which the borrower's property is already in the lender's hand. The Gemara in Perek HaOmanin limits Rav Yitzchak's rule to collateral taken after the loan only with respect to liability for loss, as Pnei Yehoshua explains. There is no inherent reason that acquisition of a lien should itself make the lender responsible for accidental loss. An ordinary lien on assets is only security, and the creditor may still sue the debtor personally even while holding security. Why should accidental loss of the security extinguish the personal obligation? Collateral taken after the loan is different because, as explained in siman 90, certain collateral that the Torah requires the lender to return cannot be permanently sold even through beit din until the lender first returns it in full. So long as he retains it he cannot proceed with collection. Baal HaTerumot, gate 49, indicates that where the collateral can never be sold while retained, it resembles an express apotiki, and if swept away the creditor cannot collect elsewhere. That consequence stems from the special proprietary right coupled with the mitzvah to return the collateral. By contrast, collateral taken at the time of the loan, or collateral not subject to return, does not block the lender from suing and selling the security, and therefore it resembles an ordinary apotiki. The acquisition alone does not impose liability for unavoidable loss.
Baal HaTerumot proves that the rule against permanently selling collateral taken after the loan applies specifically to items that must be returned, such as a pillow or plow. Otherwise, if every item taken after the loan could never be sold, every such pledge would be equivalent to an express apotiki and the lender should bear even unavoidable losses, which conflicts with the rule that he is merely a paid custodian. On this basis, Rashi's position and the other authorities can be reconciled. Rashi speaks of articles that must be returned because of the mitzvah, and for those the lender bears even unavoidable loss. The other poskim speak of ordinary collateral, for which he is only a paid custodian. Collateral taken at the time of the loan, and collateral taken later that need not be returned, likewise creates only the liability of a paid custodian according to everyone, because of the benefit that the lender can use the proprietary right to effect kiddushin. Another possible rationale is that since collection can take effect retroactively, and an attempted sale or consecration by the lender will become effective if he ultimately collects, the possibility that the article may become fully his from the outset resembles the law in siman 186 and supplies compensation for guarding. Yet a difficulty remains. The Gemara says that Rav Yitzchak's rule applies only outside the time of the loan even with respect to paid custodianship, while Even HaEzer rules that a lender can effect kiddushin with collateral of another even at the time of the loan. If so, why distinguish for paid custodianship? One can answer that at the time of the loan the ability to effect kiddushin is not an additional benefit, because the lender could have effected kiddushin with the money before lending it, as Tosafot write. Tosafot's second answer, that the collateral provides a sudar for chalipin, need not be accepted, for a person is not presumed to be without any cloth of his own. According to Rashi the passage is especially simple: whenever collateral is taken for collection the lender is a paid custodian, whereas Rabbi Eliezer understands the collateral as held only as a reminder. Thus the Gemara's statement that all reject Shmuel means that all agree the pledge is held merely as a reminder and dispute only Rav Yitzchak's rule. The challenge, "Does Rav Yitzchak say this at the time of the loan?" then follows, because if the pledge is merely a reminder there is no proprietary acquisition at that time. According to Shmuel, however, who treats it as held for collection, no such distinction is necessary. One may also explain the Gemara's distinction in light of Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. Collateral taken after the loan is acquired through the collection act of beit din, while collateral given at the time of the loan is acquired through the loan money itself. In the former case the lender derives an immediate benefit from delivery into his hand, because beit din can grant him the right only by physically delivering the collateral. The benefit of acquiring a right at that moment, one he can use to effect kiddushin, is worth a perutah at the beginning of the custodial relationship. At the time of the loan, by contrast, the lender's right arises through the loan money rather than through receipt of the collateral, and even if the pledge were still in the borrower's hand the borrower could transfer it by chalipin. Receipt itself therefore gives the lender no new benefit and does not make him a paid custodian on that basis.
After he lent him the money. See Shach, seif katan 9, who writes that all of this concerns collateral taken after the loan with authority, through beit din or with the borrower's consent. Siman 96 likewise treats seizure by the creditor, forcible seizure, and voluntary delivery by the borrower under the rules applicable to a poor borrower. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 10 of Hilchot Sechirut, understands collateral taken other than at the time of the loan to mean specifically collateral taken through beit din. In my view, the key is whether beit din had the legal authority to obtain collateral. In the circumstances described in siman 96, such as when the borrower has nothing with which to pay or the due date has arrived and he evades payment, beit din may take collateral. Therefore, even if the lender himself seizes it by force, he is viewed as one who lawfully enforces his own right, and if the borrower voluntarily delivers it, it is as though beit din transferred it toward collection with his consent. But where beit din would have no right to seize, such as before the due date where the debt is secure, voluntary delivery by the borrower has the same legal status as collateral given at the time of the loan. The verse concerning returning a poor man's pledge does not apply. This is evident from siman 90, which also speaks of forcible seizure by the lender. If the lender seizes at a time when beit din itself has no right to do so, the verse clearly cannot apply, and the lender acquires no special pledge right. He could be compelled by beit din to return the object, because he originally lent on the borrower's personal credit and had no basis to compel security afterward. The Mechaber's case must therefore involve a time when beit din could lawfully have taken collateral. Then even forcible self help seizure creates the pledge right, and the same must be true where the borrower voluntarily delivers it in those circumstances. This answers Ketzot HaChoshen.
Ketzot also asks why the lender is considered a paid custodian because he can effect kiddushin with the collateral. Since he acquired it, he is using his own property and should resemble a purchaser for thirty days. There is no difficulty. The lender acquires only a lien right, not the substance of the object. The object itself remains the borrower's. The Torah revealed that by drawing the borrower's object into his possession, the lender acquires a transferable right in the debt. Pnei Yehoshua in Gittin 37 and Ritva in Kiddushin 8 both explain that the lender has only a lien right in the collateral. He therefore derives a benefit from the borrower's physical object by using possession of it to transfer his debt to another, and because he benefits from the borrower's object he becomes a paid custodian. Ketzot's further proof from Maggid Mishneh that unauthorized seizure is not robbery overlooks the end of Maggid Mishneh's words, where he proves from Makkot that one who does so violates the prohibition of robbery. Ketzot's distinction regarding unavoidable loss between property taken only to the amount of the debt and property taken in excess also does not appear sound.
He is a paid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 10, who cites the ruling below, seif 43. There a distinction is drawn between the period before the fixed time for the borrower to retrieve the collateral and the period afterward. Sema and Shach explain that because the rationale is the lender's obligation to air and shake the collateral, during the fixed period he must perform that care and is a paid custodian because of the perutah of Rav Yosef. After the fixed period, however, the collateral resembles an ordinary deposit whose owner is free to come take it and care for it himself, so the custodian is no longer obligated to air it and is only an unpaid custodian. Shach treats Rashba's rationale, that the lender was spared the charitable perutah at the time of the loan, and the Rosh's rationale, that the perutah arises when he airs and shakes the object, as leading to the same result. Even according to Rashba, the distinction between before and after the retrieval date remains. After the date, he resembles a paid custodian whose term of employment has ended and who thereafter is only an unpaid custodian. When he originally accepted the collateral, he did not undertake compensated guarding beyond the end of the loan; by fixing a time he effectively states that after that time he will no longer guard for the original compensation. Tumim seeks to distinguish Rashba and Rosh, but in my view the law is as stated.
Tumim proposes another practical difference: if the lender gives the borrower permission to come take the article home whenever he wishes to air and shake it, the Rosh's rationale would disappear while Rashba's would remain. I do not believe there is any legal difference. If the Rosh's entire rule were simply that the perutah arises whenever the lender himself airs and shakes the pledge, difficult cases would result. A lender who needs the collateral and therefore is only an unpaid custodian according to Rabbi Eliezer, because he did not perform a mitzvah by making the loan, should nevertheless be a paid custodian like any ordinary deposit if he refuses to let the borrower take the article home for airing. A craftsman who says that his work is completed, and who retains the object for his fee, would likewise become a paid custodian whenever he prevents the owner from taking it home for airing. So too an ordinary custodian whose depositor is out of the country must air and shake the object, yet remains only an unpaid custodian. The Rosh's meaning must therefore be narrower. A lender on collateral, having performed the mitzvah of lending and having thereby voluntarily entered into the Torah imposed duty to care for the pledge, may be exempt from giving a perutah to a poor person while occupied with that mitzvah. An ordinary custodian is not commanded by the Torah to accept deposits in a manner that would create such a duty, and a craftsman accepts his duty for his own benefit. Their later care of the property did not arise from the compulsion of a mitzvah, so it does not generate the perutah of Rav Yosef. For the same reason, even if the secured lender allows the borrower to take the object home for airing, he can remain a paid custodian because there may be times when the borrower is absent or the lender is otherwise occupied in guarding the pledge and obtains the perutah benefit. Likewise, if the lender was an apostate when the loan was made and later returned, he did not originally enter the duty of airing and shaking through a mitzvah obligation, and therefore does not become a paid custodian unless he extends the loan after returning. This, I believe, is the Rosh's intent. Ritva, cited by Tumim, also writes that where the relationship did not begin as a mitzvah, later airing and shaking is not treated as a mitzvah act, although he gives another reason. Tumim questions Ritva's rationale, and the explanation given here appears sounder.
This also clarifies Maharshal's difficulty cited by Shach from the law of a craftsman who says, "Take your article." The main question is from a craftsman who merely says, "I have finished it." After completion he is an unpaid custodian even though he retains the object for his wages and may not allow the owner to take it. The answer is that a craftsman, once the work is completed, is like a paid custodian whose paid term has ended. He remains a paid custodian only if he expressly reveals that he is retaining the article for the money owed him, as explained in Bava Metzia and siman 306. Without such a declaration he is an unpaid custodian. The lender on collateral is different. Because his status arises from the perutah of Rav Yosef, as long as the collateral remains with him under the mitzvah based relationship he is like a paid custodian whose term has not yet ended. Ketzot HaChoshen misunderstood Shach on this point and therefore raised an objection that does not apply.
A paid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 11, who says that even a lender who needs the collateral performs a mitzvah and is a paid custodian. It appears that this is only where there was no special deduction arrangement and he receives full payment of what is owed. If they agreed on a deduction so that the lender receives a small benefit in exchange for the loan, the lender has a personal economic benefit and his liability is that of a renter rather than a mitzvah based paid custodian. The difference is where the stipulated term ends. A renter is no longer liable as a paid custodian after the rental term, whereas a lender whose status derives from the perutah of Rav Yosef remains a paid custodian for as long as the pledge remains with him, even after the debt is paid, as Shach writes in seif katan 10.
Taz writes at the end of this siman that a seller who sells on credit and takes collateral is a paid custodian according to everyone in an ordinary sale. He proves it by an a fortiori argument from vows: one who is forbidden to derive benefit may nevertheless lend money, and the possible saving of the perutah of Rav Yosef does not make the loan prohibited, yet in custodial law that same perutah can make the lender a paid custodian. Therefore, an ordinary sale, which would constitute a prohibited benefit under a vow, should certainly count as compensation for custodial law. In my view this proof is not decisive. We see the reverse distinction as well. Under vows even a benefit worth less than a perutah may be prohibited, including customary waivers, while paid custodianship requires compensation worth a perutah. Moreover, it seems surprising that the perutah of Rav Yosef can make the lender a paid custodian yet remain permitted under a vow if the benefit in the transaction itself is worth a perutah. The explanation is that the law of vows treats avoidance of a loss differently from receipt of a new benefit. Tosafot in Nedarim ask why the perutah of Rav Yosef is not merely driving away a lion, which is permitted where benefit is forbidden. They answer that when the finder himself is occupied with another's lost property, thereby saving his own charitable perutah, it is as though he took money belonging to the owner of the lost property and gave it to the poor. That answer does not apply to an ordinary lender, who uses his own money to perform the mitzvah and merely avoids a possible expenditure. In Bava Kamma 58, one who merely drives away a lion is not liable without both voluntary action and a loss. A vow forbids a newly conferred benefit, not mere avoidance of loss. An ordinary sale can involve a benefit too small to be worth a perutah and therefore not create paid custodianship even though it could be prohibited as a waiver under a vow. By contrast, the secured lender's avoidance of a charitable expenditure is worth a perutah and is sufficient compensation for custodial law, just as reciprocal guarding makes each party a paid custodian even though the benefit is merely avoidance of an expense. In the underlying rule, however, Taz's conclusion can be sustained on another ground. In an ordinary sale the seller benefits from the sale itself. If he retains collateral for the unpaid purchase price, this resembles a craftsman holding an article for his fee, and he is a paid custodian because of the benefit of holding security for money arising from a transaction that benefited him. Tosafot's explanation that a lender at the time of the loan derives no extra benefit from holding collateral because he gains nothing from having made the loan rather than not making it does not apply where the seller benefited from the sale, or wherever the lender derives an independent benefit from extending the credit. A particularly advantageous sale is different. If the seller derives no special benefit from the transaction and extends credit solely for the buyer's benefit, then according to the opinion that applies the perutah of Rav Yosef, he can still be a paid custodian, because there is no distinction between lending money and lending merchandise when both constitute a mitzvah. The practical determination depends on the circumstances as assessed by the dayan. And there is no distinction. See Sema, seif katan 10. The Geonim likewise hold that the lender does not lose his debt if the collateral is lost. Their principal proof that the law does not follow Shmuel is from Bava Metzia, where the Gemara says that all the opinions there reject Shmuel in a case where the collateral equals the debt. That indicates that even where the values are equal, loss of the pledge does not automatically extinguish the loan. Nevertheless, Sema is correct that an express statement is stronger. If the lender expressly receives the collateral for collection, the arrangement can be treated accordingly, whereas absent such language the pledge may have been taken only as a reminder. Tumim strongly objects. Since it is known that the lender possessed collateral and the borrower says, "You accepted the collateral in payment of the debt," why should he not be believed? This is the reasoning behind the ruling in seif 34 regarding an heir who produces a note in which the collateral is mentioned, where the defendant swears and is exempt. Tumim further asks that when collateral is lost and the parties dispute its value, the borrower is believed even without a migo. If the lender says the collateral was worth only a shekel and the borrower says it was worth a sela, the borrower's valuation is accepted. Here, the lender's claim that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances is effectively a claim that nothing is due for the loss, while the borrower's claim of negligence is equivalent to saying that the debt was paid through the lender's liability. Why should the borrower not be believed even without a migo? In my view Shach's ruling is clear. Even if a borrower claims, against a loan still within its term, that he has a deposit or some other money in the lender's hands that should offset the loan, he is not believed merely because the doctrine that one does not repay early is not literally applicable. The poskim explain that a claim of "I have money of yours in my hands" is a weak claim and is not accepted without a migo. An ordinary claim of repayment is strong because every loan is made to be repaid. A separate counterclaim, however, is weak and opposes the ordinary presumption. For this reason one who is uncertain whether he ever became obligated can be exempt. Likewise, even if it is known that the lender once held a deposit, a claim that he sold it or negligently lost it is simply a claim that the lender owes the borrower money from another source. We do not presume a person to be a thief or negligent. The Mechaber in siman 294 therefore holds that where witnesses testify that a deposited object was stolen without negligence, the custodian need not take an additional oath. Accordingly, if the borrower says the lender sold the collateral, making him akin to a robber, or negligently lost it, the assertion is a weak separate claim and is not believed without a migo. If, however, the borrower says the collateral remains in the lender's possession and the lender claims that he returned it, the borrower appears to be believed. Here the lender's claim is the weaker one, because why would he have returned security after taking it? The circumstances indicate that it remains with him, as Sema writes in siman 294, seif katan 2. If negligence is established and the parties dispute only the value of the collateral, the borrower's claim is a strong one and he is believed without a migo. Even if the note merely records that collateral existed without stating its value, the notation functions like an unstated receipt that can defeat the note. In seif 34, regarding an heir, the reason is that even according to the view that beit din may plead unavoidable loss for heirs, such a plea is made only to retain property, not to enable an heir to extract money. See Shach, seif katan 143, who does not plead for heirs even that the collateral secured the full debt when doing so would permit collection. Ketzot HaChoshen makes the result depend on the rule that each party collects independently from the other. According to him, if Rav Yosef governs and the lender is a paid custodian, the debt is payable in money while the custodial liability may be collected from land, and the borrower cannot offset the two without a migo. According to those who follow Rava and Shmuel, however, and treat the arrangement as though the lender expressly agreed that loss of the collateral extinguishes the debt, the two claims are not independent and the borrower should be believed without a migo. This cannot be correct. Tur rules in accordance with Rava and Shmuel and nevertheless requires a migo. Tumim's objection from a dispute over the value of collateral likewise remains. Moreover, the poskim state generally that a separate counterclaim is weak, without making the matter depend on the formal doctrine of mutual collection. I will explain in seif 27 that the doctrine of separate collection does not properly apply to collateral in any event. The primary rule is therefore as I have explained. At first glance this is puzzling because Yehudah never accepted custody, as the property was given to him as a gift. We must understand the case as one where Yehudah knew the purported gift rested on an asmachta and therefore knew it had no legal force, since he was expected to know the law of asmachta, as appears from Shach's quotation. Tumim correctly answers Shach's difficulty by explaining that Rashi was addressing whether failure to swear triggers payment. Tosafot in Shevuot, in the sugya of the handle of a sickle, write that according to Shmuel the secured loan is treated as though the lender expressly stipulated that loss of the collateral would extinguish the debt. They apply this even to a non Jew, who is not included in the ordinary Torah laws of custodians. It therefore appears that if land or documents are given as collateral, although ordinary custodial liability does not apply to them, the lender nevertheless loses the debt according to Shmuel because of the implied condition. According to the Mechaber, whose liability is that of a paid custodian because of the perutah of Rav Yosef, land and documents remain outside ordinary custodial liability. Although Sema in siman 66 writes that liability for documents cannot be imposed without a kinyan because custodial law does not apply to them, Tosafot's rule under Shmuel is different. The implied agreement is a condition in the loan itself, that the loan money is advanced on terms that loss of the collateral causes loss of the debt. Such a condition is effective without a separate kinyan. Thus, if a house was pledged and the structure burned through negligence or through an event comparable to theft while the land remained, if the document says merely that the house was pledged, the lender loses the debt. If the pledge was described from the depth of the earth upward, so that the land itself was included, some collateral remains and the debt is not lost. If the mortgage document uses sale language but local practice is known to require return of the property when the money is repaid, it is treated as collateral, as explained in Yoreh Deah in the laws of ribbit. The same principle applies in other contexts outside ordinary custodial liability. Thus, one who receives an iska and also takes collateral can lose his money under Shmuel's implied condition even though the arrangement may constitute custody with the owner, because the iska recipient is obligated to work with and guard the deposit portion. Tumim seeks to reject Tosafot's position, but his objections can readily be answered, and established halachah generally follows Tosafot. Tumim's uncertainty therefore cannot displace their definite ruling. As to value of the collateral beyond the amount of the debt, however, the lender is exempt even according to the Mechaber because that excess is subject to the rule of custody with the owner, and seizure does not alter this. The general laws of custody with the owner will be explained later, with Hashem's help, in the laws of borrowing. Ketzot HaChoshen's suggestion that Shmuel's rationale would exempt the lender from a theft occurring through unavoidable circumstances also does not appear correct, because the Mishnah calls him a paid custodian, implying that all the laws of a paid custodian apply. Tumim correctly challenges this, for it is no worse than a loan of produce, which makes the lender a paid custodian. Tosafot's answer in Bava Metzia, that a lender derives no benefit from having made a secured loan rather than not lending, does not apply where the transaction is undertaken for the recipient's own commercial advantage. In such a case he resembles a craftsman and is a paid custodian. This is the primary ruling. And because of doubt we do not extract money.
