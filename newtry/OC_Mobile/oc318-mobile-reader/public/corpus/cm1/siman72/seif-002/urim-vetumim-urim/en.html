Whether he lent him produce. Rabbi Yehudah in the Mishnah distinguishes a loan of produce, where the lender benefits because the produce would otherwise spoil, from a loan of money, where the lender receives no comparable benefit. The ruling teaches that we do not follow Rabbi Yehudah, and the lender is always treated as a paid custodian.
Whether he took the collateral after making the loan. Even if the collateral was taken after the loan under authority of beit din by an officer of beit din, as explained below in siman 97, the lender is not liable for unavoidable accidents. He is only a paid custodian, liable for theft and loss, because of Rabbi Yitzchak's principle that a creditor acquires a possessory interest in collateral. The Shach holds that the party in possession may claim that the law follows Rashi and those who agree with him, who impose liability even for unavoidable accidents. See Tumim, where I wrote that the general course of the authorities is not like that opinion. If, however, the lender forcibly seized collateral after the loan in a manner prohibited below in siman 97, the Shach holds that he is a robber and according to all opinions is liable even for unavoidable accidents. See what I wrote below in siman 97.
He is a paid custodian. This is because of the perutah of Rabbi Yosef: one engaged in one mitzvah is exempt at that moment from another mitzvah. While the lender is occupied with the mitzvah of lending and taking collateral, had a poor person arrived he would have been exempt from giving charity at that moment, and this saved perutah is considered compensation. By virtue of that benefit he remains a paid custodian for as long as the collateral is in his possession. So writes Rashba. The Shach writes that this remains true even after the debt was already paid. In Tumim I discussed at length that the Rosh disagrees. If the debt was paid and the borrower may retrieve the collateral whenever he wishes, or even if the debt has not yet been paid but the borrower may take the collateral home briefly and leave in its place a handwritten acknowledgment or similar security, the lender is no longer a paid custodian. Conversely, if he lent to a Jewish apostate, so that the initial loan was not a mitzvah and Rabbi Yosef's perutah does not apply, but the borrower later repented while the collateral remained with the lender, according to Rashba the lender does not become a paid custodian, whereas according to the Rosh he does. See there, where I discussed this at length. I wrote that the language of the Mechaber, the Tur, and Tosafot tends toward the Rosh, though the matter requires study.
A paid custodian. Even where the lender needs the collateral, meaning that he uses it and deducts the rental value from the debt, he is nevertheless a paid custodian, because the loan itself remains a mitzvah. So writes the Shach, correctly, unlike Bnei Shmuel, who understood the Rif and Rambam to hold that where the lender needs the collateral he is not a paid custodian. See Tumim, seif katan 3.
And some say that if he specified, etc. The Sema explains that this is according to the Geonim who rule like Rabbi Yosef that a lender holding collateral is a paid custodian, but he understands them not to rule like Shmuel's principle that if the handle of the sickle is lost the entire thousand zuz debt is lost. Rather, they hold that Shmuel spoke of an ordinary unspecified case according to Rashi's text, whereas if the lender expressly accepted the collateral against the entire debt, then even according to us, who do not generally follow Shmuel, the entire debt is lost. The Bach and Shach already objected to this explanation, and I agreed with them in Tumim. We have found no authority who says that one who expressly accepted a small item against an entire debt loses thousands of zuz upon loss of the item while simultaneously rejecting Shmuel's rule. The correct explanation is therefore that of the Shach: these authorities do rule like Shmuel, but understand Shmuel to apply only where the lender expressly says that he accepts the collateral against the entire debt. In an unspecified case he does not lose the entire debt. See Tumim, where I wrote that it requires study whether one can consistently rule both like Rabbi Yosef and like Shmuel.
That he accepts the collateral for the entire debt. If he expressly says that if the collateral is lost he will forfeit the entire debt, see Tumim. Beit Yosef and Rama appear to hold that all agree such a stipulation is effective. The Bach and Shach hold that even this remains a doubt in law, because according to those who reject Shmuel the condition may be an asmachta and acquire nothing. I discussed at length in Tumim why the Gemara does not refute that view. See there.
If the collateral is lost, the entire debt is lost. If there are two small collateral items and one is lost, half the debt is lost; if both are lost, all is lost. If there is one small item together with a larger item of collateral, meaning the latter is worth more, the Mordechai also rules that loss of either item causes loss of half the debt. This is difficult for me. If the lender says that both the larger collateral and the small item secure the debt, and the larger item is worth more, why should we treat the small item as securing an equal half? For example, if an ingot is worth three dinars, a small handle one dinar, and the loan is ten, it is unreasonable that loss of the one dinar handle should reduce the debt to five, while loss of the three dinar ingot should also reduce it to five. Surely the lender relied more heavily on the more valuable collateral. It therefore appears that the four dinars of actual value should first be attributed to the items according to their value, and only the remaining six dinars of unsecured debt should be divided equally as the special additional undertaking. Thus if the one dinar handle is lost, the lender loses that dinar plus three of the remaining six, a total of four, and the borrower pays six. If the three dinar ingot is lost, the lender loses its three dinars plus three more, a total of six, and the borrower pays four. This is correct, because as to the additional amount beyond value there is no reason to distinguish whether the physical collateral is worth much or little. The underlying ruling itself, however, requires study. The Tur rules like the Sages of Nehardea, while the Rosh omits their ruling and in his abridged rulings explicitly rules otherwise, yet the Tur cites no disagreement. Perhaps one can answer that authorities who rule like Shmuel also rule like the Sages of Nehardea, whereas the Rosh, in practical ruling, considered that many authorities reject Shmuel and therefore declined to extend the rule further to the Nehardean formulation. This requires great study.
The lender must swear that it was lost through unavoidable accident, etc. Even where the lender expressly accepted responsibility, his acceptance is understood to cover only theft and loss, not unavoidable accidents. This applies where the borrower does not claim with certainty that no accident occurred. If the borrower does assert with certainty that the loss was not accidental, the borrower swears a hesset oath and is exempt. The Sema, and apparently the Bach, hold that this is so even where the borrower has no migo of payment, such as where the loan was made before witnesses and remains within the due date, or where the lender said, "Do not repay me except before witnesses." The Shach holds that the borrower is believed only where he has such a migo; without it, the lender swears that an accident occurred and collects the debt. In Tumim I clarified that the Sema is correct and that this is also the Mechaber's view.
And he shall pay. Even if the lender had held a formal debt instrument and returned it when the borrower gave him collateral, and the collateral was then lost through unavoidable accident, we do not say that the lender accepted the collateral as actual collection of the debt. The borrower must still pay. Maharik, shoresh 80, cited by the Shach.
He is only an unpaid custodian. According to this view we do not accept Rabbi Yosef's saved perutah, because it is uncommon that a poor person will arrive at the very moment the lender is occupied with the collateral. The lender is therefore only an unpaid custodian. On the other hand, this view rules like Shmuel that if the small handle is lost the entire debt may be lost where the lender said, "I accept it for the entire debt," and to the extent of the actual value of the collateral even an unspecified acceptance is treated as though expressly stated. Consequently there is ordinarily no practical difference whether the lender is called a paid custodian because of Rabbi Yosef's perutah or because of Shmuel's rule, since in either event he loses at least the value of the collateral. The differences arise where the collateral is worth more than the debt, for according to the Mechaber, who follows Rabbi Yosef, the lender must pay the excess, whereas according to this alternative view, which follows Rabbah, he does not. Likewise, if the debt exceeds the value of the collateral, the dispute may determine whether an express undertaking causes forfeiture of the entire debt. The Rama already addressed this above. There is no distinction between a documented and an undocumented loan. Shach, and this is clear.
He gives the borrower. This applies whether the lender lent money or produce. In either case he is treated as a paid custodian only up to the amount of the debt, not for the value exceeding it. Shach. The Taz, at the end of this siman, discusses one who sold merchandise on credit at an intermediate market price and took collateral, and holds that he is a paid custodian even for value above the debt. He overlooked the explicit words of the Rosh and Tur that even when produce was lent, the lender is not a paid custodian beyond the value of the loan. That is the principal ruling.
The excess. If the collateral was taken after the time of the loan, Maharam holds that even then the lender does not pay the excess value. The Shach disagrees, and I agreed in Tumim. When collateral is taken after the loan, the creditor acquires a possessory interest in the entire item by virtue of Rabbi Yitzchak's principle, and is a paid custodian even for value exceeding the debt. See there.
Rather, if it was lost, etc. If the collateral is worth less than the debt and the lender stipulated that he accepts it against the entire debt, so that under this opinion loss of the collateral causes loss of the entire debt, Beit Yosef holds that this applies only where collateral was given at the time of the loan. If it was taken later, the condition is ineffective. The Bach disagrees. See Tumim, where I clarified the Beit Yosef's position. The matter remains a doubt in law and requires study.
His money. The Shach cites a responsum of Rashi: Reuven gave an item as collateral to Shimon, Shimon transferred it to Levi, and Shimon claimed that he had stipulated with Reuven that if Reuven did not redeem it by a fixed date it would become absolutely Shimon's. The date passed, and Levi gave the collateral to Yehudah as a gift. If Reuven later comes to redeem it, Yehudah must return it, because the stipulation making it absolutely the lender's was an asmachta. Yehudah receives the money Reuven actually owes on the collateral. If Yehudah claims that the item is no longer in his possession, he must swear to that effect and also swear to its value, because he must pay Reuven any value above the debt. See Tumim. If Yehudah says that he does not know its value, and he admits at least once that the collateral was worth more than the debt, he must pay whatever value Reuven claims. We do not say that he had no reason to know. The Shach concludes that neither Levi nor Shimon must pay Reuven even a perutah. Yehudah, who actually held the collateral, alone must pay the excess from his own pocket. Although Shimon and Levi were negligent by transferring entrusted property to another custodian, once Yehudah is present and personally liable to restore the entire excess they are exempt. This is correct and clear. If, however, Reuven sues Levi claiming that the collateral was worth substantially more, Levi admits that it was worth at least a perutah above the debt but says that he does not know the remaining value, then even if Yehudah knows the value and is willing to swear, I concluded in Tumim that Levi cannot rely on Yehudah's oath. Reuven can say, "I do not trust him on oath," and Levi must pay. See there for the proof.
And no more. If the collateral was very valuable, part of it was stolen, and what remains is still worth the entire debt, the lender is likewise exempt for the lost excess. According to this view he is an unpaid custodian regarding whatever value exceeds the debt. Mahari ben Lev, and this is clear. So writes the Shach.
More. The Sema writes that if a Jew lends with interest to another Jew upon collateral belonging to a non Jew, then, since the Jewish borrower acts as the non Jew's agent, without which structure the interest would be prohibited, the lender is a paid custodian of the collateral. The Shach writes likewise. In Tumim I wrote that according to the opinion rejecting Shmuel and ruling only like Rabbi Yosef, the lender is plainly exempt, and even according to those who follow Shmuel it appears to me that as practical law he is exempt even up to the debt. See there at length. If a Jew lends another Jew funds as an iska and the recipient gives collateral, I likewise concluded in Tumim that the lender is not a custodian at all because it is a case of custody while the owner is in service to him. See there at length. But if Reuven gives Shimon collateral so that Shimon will lend money to Levi as an iska, then Shimon is a paid custodian of the collateral according to all opinions. Likewise, if Reuven borrows from Shimon on collateral and Levi gives Shimon money in order that Shimon lend to Reuven, Shimon is a paid custodian of Reuven's collateral. See Tumim.
And because of doubt we do not extract money. There is no definitive ruling whether the law follows Rabbah together with Shmuel or Rabbi Yosef. See Tumim. I concluded that even when the lender is in possession he cannot claim conclusively that the law follows Rabbah against those who hold that an ordinary receipt of collateral is equivalent to an express acceptance against the debt. Up to the value of the collateral, all agree that the lender bears the loss. Therefore, to the extent of the collateral's value within the debt, money cannot be extracted from the borrower. Likewise, if the debt exceeds the collateral and the lender expressly said that he accepted it against the entire debt, the excess debt cannot be extracted from the borrower because of the doubt. But if the collateral is worth more than the debt, the excess value cannot be extracted from the lender. See what I wrote above.
