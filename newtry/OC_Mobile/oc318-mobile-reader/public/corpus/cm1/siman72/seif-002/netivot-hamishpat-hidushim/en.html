Where he took the collateral after lending. The Shach rules that collateral taken not at the time of the loan makes the lender liable even for unavoidable accidents. See Beurim. This is specifically with items necessary to the borrower, such as a pillow or plow, as explained in siman 96. Since the lender cannot sell such collateral or demand repayment while it remains with him, it is like an express apotiki and he is liable for unavoidable accidents according to everyone. But with items he need not return to the borrower under the law of returning collateral, he is only a paid custodian. "Taken not at the time of the loan" means where beit din had authority to seize it, or he seized it himself, either forcibly or with the borrower's consent, at a time when beit din would have had authority, such as after the due date when the borrower was delaying payment, or even before the due date if the borrower was dissipating his assets. But if at a time when beit din had no authority to seize the property the borrower voluntarily gave it as collateral, even though it was after the loan, the lender is only a paid custodian. Even where he is liable for unavoidable accidents, his liability is only up to the amount of the debt, not beyond it. If he seized without legal authority and is therefore a robber, he is liable for unavoidable accidents for the entire collateral. Shach.
He is a paid custodian. This is because of the benefit of the perutah of Rav Yosef: one who is occupied with a mitzvah is exempt from another mitzvah. At the moment the lender is engaged in the mitzvah of lending or in airing and caring for the collateral, he performs a mitzvah and is thereby exempt from giving a perutah to a poor person. Because of this benefit he is treated as a paid custodian for as long as the collateral remains with him, even after the debt has been repaid, as explained in seif 43. Even if the lender gave the borrower permission to take the collateral home whenever needed to air and care for it, the lender remains a paid custodian. See Beurim, seif katan 7: the essential point is whether the duty of airing and caring for the collateral was imposed involuntarily by a Torah command. Here, because the Torah compelled the lender to lend and the obligation to care for the collateral followed from that Torah imposed duty, the perutah of Rav Yosef applies. But where a person voluntarily enters a custodial duty not imposed by Torah, that principle does not apply. Therefore, where the lender also derives ordinary benefit from the loan, for example where he lawfully receives permitted interest by deduction or another permissible arrangement, or he took collateral against the extension of merchandise on ordinary commercial terms, the perutah of Rav Yosef does not apply. Nevertheless, he may still be a paid custodian because of the separate benefit that he holds security for his money, and even according to Rama he is then a paid custodian. Likewise, if at the time of the loan he said he would lend only for interest and later retracted, he does not become a paid custodian through the perutah of Rav Yosef because the original obligation did not arise from a mitzvah, unless he later extends the payment term after retracting. Even where the lender needs the collateral for use and deducts its use from the debt, according to the Mechaber he remains a paid custodian through the perutah of Rav Yosef so long as the collateral is with him, even after repayment. But this applies specifically where the entire rental value is deducted from the debt, such as collateral from a poor person in seif 1. With a mortgage by partial deduction, where he receives only a small amount as rent, he is not performing the lending mitzvah in that manner, so he is only a paid custodian until repayment and no longer. See Beurim.

The lender swears that it was lost through unavoidable accident. Even if he expressly said he accepts responsibility, that does not ordinarily include unavoidable accidents but only theft and loss, because unavoidable accidents are uncommon. This applies specifically where the borrower does not assert with certainty that no unavoidable accident occurred, in which case the lender's claim is like, "I do not know whether you repaid me." But if the borrower asserts with certainty that no unavoidable accident occurred, the borrower swears hesset and is exempt. The Sema holds that even where the borrower has no migo of payment, such as a documented loan or a debt still within its term, the borrower is nevertheless believed through a serious oath. The Shach disagrees and holds that in such a case, where no migo is available, the lender swears and collects. See Beurim, where the Shach's view is primary. However, if the lender claims, "I returned the collateral to you," and the borrower denies it, the borrower is believed even without a migo. Likewise, if it is known that the lender was negligent and they dispute the collateral's value, the borrower is believed even without a migo. Also, if heirs hold a document that states there was collateral, we do not advance for them the claim that perhaps the collateral was lost through unavoidable accident, and they cannot collect, as explained below in seif 54.
He is only an unpaid custodian, because the perutah of Rav Yosef is uncommon. Nevertheless, this opinion holds like Shmuel that if the lender expressly accepted the collateral against the entire debt, loss of the collateral causes loss of the entire debt. Up to the value of the collateral it is as though he expressly said that if the collateral is lost, that amount of the debt is lost. When collateral is taken not at the time of the loan, the Shach holds that the lender is a paid custodian even regarding any excess value. See Beurim, seif katan 11, where the primary law is that there is no distinction. With store credit secured by collateral, the matter depends on the judge's assessment. If extending the credit primarily benefits the lender, he is a paid custodian because of the benefit of holding security for his money. If the benefit is only to the borrower, it resembles an ordinary loan secured by collateral and depends upon the dispute between the Mechaber and Rama. Even if he lent produce, he is only an unpaid custodian according to this view. Shach.
The excess belongs to the borrower. If the collateral was taken not at the time of the loan, the Shach holds that the lender is a paid custodian even for the excess. See Beurim, where the primary law is that even then he is only an unpaid custodian for the excess. However, if the collateral was taken not at the time of the loan and was made an express apotiki, he is a paid custodian according to everyone. Likewise, where the lender derives benefit by receiving permitted interest through a lawful arrangement on this loan, he is a paid custodian according to everyone even for the excess.
He loses his money. See Beurim, seif katan 13. This applies even to collateral to which ordinary laws of custodians do not apply, such as land, a debt instrument, custody in the owner's presence, collateral belonging to a gentile, despite the rule that ordinary custodial law does not apply to gentiles, or where the loan was structured as an investment and is therefore comparable to custody in the owner's presence because just as this party must guard the collateral, the other must guard the deposit portion. Nevertheless, the lender loses his money under Shmuel's principle, because it is as though he expressly agreed that if the collateral is lost, his money is lost. The Shach cites a responsum attributed to Rashi: Reuven pledged an object to Shimon, Shimon repledged it to Levi, telling him that he had stipulated that if it was not redeemed by a certain time it would become absolutely his, and Levi then gave it as a gift to Yehudah. If Reuven later comes to redeem the collateral, Yehudah must return it because the original forfeiture condition was an asmachta. If Yehudah claims that the object is no longer in his possession, he must take the oath of custodians. This applies specifically where Yehudah knew of the condition. He should have learned the laws of asmachta and was therefore treated as knowing that the object was not truly Levi's, making him a custodian. But if Yehudah thought that Levi was giving him his own property, he is not a custodian at all. See Beurim, seif katan 12. If Yehudah admits the object was worth at least one perutah more than the debt but does not know how much more, he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must therefore pay. If Yehudah claims it was worth only one perutah more than the debt, while Levi admits that one perutah and concerning the remainder says he does not know how much was borrowed, Levi is likewise one obligated to swear but unable to swear. Yehudah's oath does not help him. Shach.
And no more. If part of the collateral was stolen but what remains is sufficient to cover the debt, the lender may collect the debt from what remains, because regarding the excess he is only an unpaid custodian. If the collateral is land, then even if the document uses language of sale, so long as the custom is to return the property when the money is repaid, it remains legally only collateral. Therefore, if a house burns through negligence or an accident comparable to theft but the land remains, then if the document says that he pledged from the depths of the earth to the height of the heavens, thereby pledging the land itself as well, the lender may collect most of the debt from the land. But if the document says only that the house was pledged, he loses his money. A land mortgage too can cause loss of the loan as explained above.
