A paid custodian. The Sema explains that one who lends against a pledge performs a mitzvah. If a poor person appeared while he was making the loan, he would be exempt at that moment from giving tzedakah because one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from another mitzvah. By virtue of that small benefit he becomes a paid custodian of the pledge for as long as it remains with him, like any paid custodian whose compensation is received either at the beginning or the end. The Rashba writes this. The Shach discusses these laws at great length in the Gemara and the authorities; one who wishes to study his analysis should see it there.
And there is no. The Sema writes that this gloss does not belong here but later, in the case where the debt exceeds the value of the pledge. Its point is that even if the lender accepted the pledge without qualification, we do not say that he accepted it as payment of the entire debt. All the more so, if at the time he accepted it he expressly stated that he accepted it only up to its value, the borrower must pay the balance. If, however, when the pledge was given he said, "Although it is worth less than the debt, I accept it," then according to all opinions he loses the excess of his money. The Rama's formulation of this as "some say" is discussed there.
And he shall pay. Even if the lender expressly said at the time of the loan that he accepted the pledge against the entire debt, ordinarily he meant only theft or loss, which are common, and not an unavoidable accident, which is uncommon, similar to the rule in siman 225 concerning liability. This is only where the borrower does not assert with certainty that the pledge is still in the lender's possession or that the lender sold it and received its proceeds. If the borrower makes such a definite claim, he takes a shevuat heset and is exempt, as the Mechaber writes in seif 29. The Sema questions this because the Tur there bases the borrower's credibility on a migo that he could have said, "I owe you nothing," or "I paid you," implying that where he lacks such a migo, such as where witnesses know he did not pay, the lender should be believed. The Sema is forced to suggest that even without a migo the borrower is believed, but must take a more stringent oath, whereas with a migo a shevuat heset suffices. The Shach rejects this. In his view, where the borrower lacks a migo, the lender swears and collects, as Baal HaTerumot clearly indicates and as the Bach also appears to hold. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 14 must therefore be speaking where the borrower has a migo. See also seif 29. Maharik writes that even if the lender returned the promissory note after receiving the pledge, if the pledge is later lost by unavoidable accident the borrower must still pay the debt.
As a lender. The Shach discusses at length whether there is a distinction between a pledge taken after the loan and a loan originally made against a pledge. He concludes that perhaps the Rama's wording, "some say that one who lends against a pledge," rather than simply "some say he is only an unpaid custodian," indicates that the dispute concerns only a pledge taken at the time of the loan. Where the pledge is taken later for an existing debt, all agree he is a paid custodian, and he is therefore liable even for the excess value. The Shach regards this as the principal ruling.
More. The Sema writes that this applies specifically to a Jewish borrower's pledge given for the borrower's benefit without interest. But where a Jew pledges to another Jew collateral belonging to a non-Jew and passes along the interest received from the non-Jew, it seems clear that the second Jew is a paid custodian liable for the full value. First, such substantial benefit should make him a paid custodian according to all opinions. Second, it is as though he lent directly to the non-Jew, for otherwise taking interest from the Jew would be forbidden, as stated in Yoreh Deah 169. This should be true unless the governing non-Jewish law provides that a lender who receives interest on a pledge is not liable for value above the debt if the pledge is lost. The Shach notes that the responsum of Mahari ibn Lev cited by the Sema does not actually mention a non-Jewish pledge bearing interest, and suggests that Mahari ibn Lev might agree with the Sema in that case because the Jew acts as the non-Jew's agent. The Shach also distinguishes this case from a broker in siman 185 and from a person given merchandise to sell for a share of the profit in siman 186.
We do not remove. The Shach writes that later authorities generally ruled in practice that the lender is a paid custodian, whereas the contemporary common practice was to treat the issue as a doubt in law, as Responsa Maharshach II, 169 also states. If the lender seized an amount equal to his loan, it is not removed from him. The Shach reasons that Rav Yosef and those who follow him apparently do not accept the principle that accepting the pledge is as though the lender expressly agreed to lose his loan to that extent. Since, as the Shach establishes elsewhere, a possessor may say, "I rely on these authorities regarding this point though not regarding another," the lender may say, "I rely on the authorities who regard me as an unpaid custodian, and I do not accept the view that I implicitly agreed to lose the debt." The Sema adds that the Rama should also have cited the Tur's distinction where the loan was made against two knife handles or against one handle and another pledge, and one of them was lost, and should have ruled among the differing opinions in the Gemara.
