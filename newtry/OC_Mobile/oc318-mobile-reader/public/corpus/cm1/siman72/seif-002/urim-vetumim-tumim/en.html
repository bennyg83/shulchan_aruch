Whether he took the collateral after the loan, and so forth. Shach argues at length for Rashi that where collateral is seized after the loan through beit din, the creditor acquires such a strong proprietary interest under R. Yitzchak's rule that he may even be liable for unavoidable accidents. Shach therefore says the borrower can say kim li like Rashi and those who agree with him. Although I am not worthy to decide among these great authorities, the general direction of the poskim appears otherwise. Rashi follows his own theory of the baraita distinguishing between a loan with a formal note and one without it. According to Rashi, if the creditor holds the collateral for actual collection, everyone treats him at least as a paid custodian; R. Eliezer's leniency applies only where the collateral was taken merely as a memorandum. A creditor who seizes collateral after the loan through beit din is obviously taking it for collection. Why then would we need R. Yitzchak's verse that "to you it shall be righteousness" to establish that the creditor acquires the collateral? On Rashi's theory, ordinary reasoning would already make him at least a paid custodian. Rashi therefore interprets R. Yitzchak's rule as creating a more complete acquisition, even enough to support liability for unavoidable accidents and perhaps to permit kiddushin with the collateral. That fits Rashi's system. Tosafot and most authorities, however, do not accept Rashi's distinction based on whether collateral is held "for collection." For them, being a paid custodian does not itself make the object the creditor's property. R. Yitzchak's rule can therefore be read in its simpler sense: collateral taken after the loan gives the creditor a sufficient proprietary interest to make him a paid custodian and to create other collateral rights, but not full borrower-like liability for accidents. There is another proof. Rashi reads the baraita's clause "but if he lent him with a note and placed collateral upon it" as collateral given at the time of the loan. Tosafot object and explain it as collateral taken afterward. Most later authorities never codify Rashi's special rule distinguishing a loan with both a formal note and contemporaneous collateral from a loan with collateral but no note. If they followed Rashi's theory, that distinction would have important practical consequences, especially in cases where the mitzvah-benefit rationale does not apply, such as lending to a Jewish apostate. Their silence strongly indicates they follow Tosafot's reading that the note language is not the operative distinction. Once the baraita is read like Tosafot, "lost" cannot mean ordinary theft in the first clause but unavoidable accident in the second clause without any indication. It is unnatural to give the same word two radically different meanings within one baraita. The simpler reading is that the creditor is a paid custodian, not an insurer against unavoidable accident. Therefore Knesset HaGedolah is correct to cite several major authorities who say one cannot confidently plead kim li like Rashi here. The mainstream halachic structure follows Tosafot and the majority rather than Rashi's stronger liability theory.
He is a paid custodian. Sema cites Rashba's reason: at the time of the loan, had a poor person requested charity, the lender would have been exempt from giving that perutah because he was then engaged in the mitzvah of lending. That benefit makes him a paid custodian of the collateral for as long as it remains with him. Shach says that according to Rashba, even if the loan has already been repaid and the lender simply has not yet returned the collateral, he remains a paid custodian. Shach thinks Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber agree. I do not think Rashba and Rosh are identical. Rashba bases the status on the one-time mitzvah benefit at the moment of lending. Once that benefit attached, the custodial classification can continue throughout possession, even after repayment, unless the legal relationship clearly ends. Rosh gives another reason. So long as the collateral remains with the lender, he must periodically spread and air it. At those recurring moments he is engaged in a mitzvah and may be exempt from giving a perutah to a poor person. The recurring care obligation produces the paid-custodian status. That theory depends on whether the lender still has exclusive responsibility for the object's care. If the borrower can come at any time and take back the collateral or personally spread and air it, the lender no longer has the same recurring mitzvah obligation. The case then resembles an ordinary deposit, where the owner remains practically available to care for his own property. But if the parties fixed a future time for return and the borrower cannot take it earlier, the lender remains responsible for spreading and airing it until that date, and Rosh's rationale continues. Thus there is a real difference between Rashba and Rosh. Mechaber below, in seif 43, appears to follow Rosh by distinguishing whether a return time was fixed. That suggests Rashba's broader continuing-status theory is not fully accepted. Several practical differences follow. If at the time of the original loan there was no mitzvah benefit, for example because the borrower was then outside the mitzvah framework, but later circumstances changed, Rashba's one-time theory would not create paid-custodian status. Rosh's recurring care theory could. Conversely, if the lender expressly permits the borrower to come whenever he wants to spread and air the collateral, Rosh's rationale weakens even though Rashba's original benefit remains. There is, however, a complication from the authorities' explanation of a lender who needed the collateral for temporary use. After the permitted use period expires, why should R. Eliezer regard him as only an unpaid custodian? Under Rosh's theory he still has to care for the object and should obtain the recurring mitzvah benefit. This can be answered by the distinction cited from Ritva: where the original loan itself was not performed as a mitzvah because the lender acted primarily for his own use, later spreading and airing does not retroactively transform the custodial relationship into one founded on that mitzvah. That answer also limits the case where the borrower was outside the mitzvah framework at inception and later changed status. There is another subtle distinction in Tosafot. Tosafot initially say that if the lender's benefit is the mitzvah of lending, there should be no difference between collateral worth the full loan and collateral worth less than the loan. But in another explanation they imply that under R. Yosef's perutah theory, a distinction can exist. The reason is this. If the collateral equals the debt and is clearly held for collection, the lender does not entrust it back to the borrower for care. He himself must repeatedly spread and air it, so the recurring mitzvah-benefit theory applies strongly. If the collateral is worth far less than the debt and functions only as a reminder, the lender may be willing to hand it briefly to the borrower for airing. Since he is not relying on the object as real collection security, his own recurring care obligation is weaker. This explains the otherwise puzzling distinction cited by Maharsha and Shitah Mekubetzet. It also helps reconcile Rashi's language that full-value collateral is taken for collection while lesser collateral may be only a reminder. The broader result is that the precise paid-custodian rule depends on the reason for taking the collateral, the lender's ongoing control, and whether the underlying mitzvah benefit is present. Mechaber's broad language should therefore be read with these distinctions in mind.
And some say that if he explicitly stated, and so forth. Sema explains that this opinion follows the Geonim who rule like R. Yosef and not like Shmuel, while accepting Rashi's reading that Shmuel's rule concerns an unstated arrangement. Thus they reject Shmuel in the ordinary case, but if the lender expressly says, "Even though the collateral is worth less, I accept it against the entire debt," then everyone agrees that if the collateral is lost, the whole debt is lost. Derishah understands Mechaber as speaking of a more explicit acceptance of liability, while Sema says the wording does not support that and interprets it as acceptance of the collateral against the entire debt. Bach and Shach object that no clear authority holds exactly this combination. One could defend Sema through Rashi's own logic. Rashi explains R. Eliezer's unpaid-custodian status by saying the collateral was not taken for actual collection but merely as a reminder. Where the lender also holds a formal note, however, the collateral is clearly being held for collection, and even R. Eliezer can agree to paid-custodian status. Accordingly, if the lender expressly says he accepts an under-value collateral against the whole debt, he has declared that it is being held for collection. This can strengthen his liability even if ordinary Shmuel is not accepted. But that reasoning proves only responsibility up to the value of the collateral, not necessarily loss of a thousand zuz for loss of a tiny object. To reach the full Shmuel result, one needs either Shmuel himself or an explicit statement that the entire debt will be lost if the collateral is lost. Therefore Sema's precise formulation is difficult.
Therefore, Shach's explanation is preferable: the "some say" follows Ri, who rules like Shmuel, but follows Rabbenu Chananel's version that Shmuel applies where the lender expressly says, "Although this collateral is not worth the whole debt, I accept it against the entire debt." Even this raises a difficulty because Rama places the gloss within Mechaber's ruling that a lender on collateral is a paid custodian through the perutah of R. Yosef, apparently even where the lender needs the collateral for his own use. How can we simultaneously rule like R. Yosef and like Shmuel if the Talmudic explanations of the baraita seem to set those theories against one another? The answer may lie in distinguishing ordinary collateral from collateral the lender needs for use. When the lender has no special need for the collateral, a collateral worth the debt is naturally taken for collection. A lesser collateral may still be treated as a memorandum unless there is express language. But where the lender specifically needs the collateral for his own use, his taking it does not itself prove collection intent. In that setting Shmuel's presumption need not arise in the absence of express language, while the perutah-of-mitzvah theory can still determine custodial status. This prevents multiplying unnecessary Tannaitic disputes. The larger Talmudic discussion is complex. According to Rashi's text, the Gemara first asks that if the collateral equals the debt, why would R. Eliezer exempt the lender? Such full-value collateral is plainly held for collection. Later the Gemara can still say the Tannaim disagree even with full-value collateral under another theory, because the case has shifted to collateral taken for the lender's own need, where collection intent is no longer obvious. This also answers Maharsha's question why the initial questioner and later answer seem to reverse positions on whether full-value collateral is automatically "for collection." The contexts are different. It also explains why Tosafot's questions against Rashi from Shemitah are not decisive. In the Shemitah case, the collateral may be ordinary collection collateral, while the baraita can involve collateral taken for use. Ramban offers another way to understand Shmuel: the statement that "if the handle is lost, a thousand zuz are lost" does not itself determine whether the lender is an unpaid or paid custodian. It only says that whatever level of custodial breach would make him liable for the collateral also extends that liability to the full debt. Under Rabbah the trigger may be negligence; under R. Yosef it may be theft or loss. This allows Shmuel to coexist more flexibly with the Rabbah/R. Yosef dispute. Still, the Gemara's failure to settle on Shmuel as the final explanation is significant, and most poskim do not rule like him in the unstated case. Accordingly, the safest practical rule is: Without an explicit undertaking, liability does not extend beyond the ordinary value and custodial rules. If the lender expressly says he accepts the collateral against the entire debt, there is substantial authority to bind him to that stipulation. But where the language is merely "I accept this collateral" without a clear statement that loss of the object cancels the whole debt, the broader Shmuel consequence remains doubtful.
That he accepts the collateral against the entire debt. Rama adopts the Tosafot interpretation of Shmuel: the case is where the lender expressly says that he accepts the collateral against the whole debt, rather than the alternate interpretation that he explicitly says, "If it is lost, I lose the entire debt." In the latter formulation, there is strong reason that everyone should agree because an explicit monetary condition is generally valid. Beit Yosef says so directly. One might object that such a condition is an asmachta. Beit Yosef follows his view below in siman 207 that a pure "I waive what is mine" clause is not asmachta. Rama is more willing to find asmachta even in some waiver settings, but here the obligation falls only on the lender and functions as a present waiver of his debt, which is stronger. Bach and Shach nevertheless treat the matter as a safek because Tosafot can be read as saying even an explicit undertaking to lose the full debt may remain disputed by R. Eliezer as asmachta. That requires explanation. Tosafot themselves say that, at least up to the collateral's value, ordinary acceptance is treated almost like an express assumption of responsibility. If so, why would R. Eliezer reject an explicit acceptance up to that nonexcessive value? A custodian can validly accept a higher level of responsibility, such as an unpaid custodian agreeing to be liable as a borrower, and that is not asmachta where the undertaking is not exaggerated. At first I thought perhaps a lender on collateral differs from an ordinary custodian because there is no special reliance benefit from the borrower: the lender gave the money only after taking security, so the ordinary rationale that a custodian obligates himself because the owner trusted him may be weaker. But this cannot be the whole answer. Shmuel's own rule is discussed without requiring a kinyan, and Tosafot do not make such a distinction. The better explanation is that a condition made at the moment of the loan can operate as an immediate waiver of the debt itself. If the collateral is lost under the stipulated circumstances, the loan was from the outset subject to that limitation. A present waiver does not require the same kinyan as a new custodial undertaking. This also helps explain Behag, who distinguishes between a collateralized oral loan and a formal note. If a written debt cannot be waived in his system without additional formality, then the "waiver through loss of collateral" mechanism is weaker when a formal note exists. There is some Talmudic support for this distinction. In Shevuot, the Gemara challenges Shmuel from the later clauses of the Mishnah. The exact choice of which clause to challenge can be explained if Shmuel's mechanism depends on the type of debt and whether the obligation is embodied in a note. Returning to Tosafot, the core idea is this: If Chazal regard the lender as genuinely and wholeheartedly accepting responsibility when he expressly ties the whole debt to the collateral, then ordinary full-value collateral can also be understood as carrying at least that level of responsibility by implication. But if even an express excess undertaking is viewed as asmachta, then one cannot infer from silence that the lender accepted full-value responsibility. This explains why the ordinary and explicit cases are conceptually linked. It also helps make sense of the differing texts in Bava Metzia and Shevuot. In one formulation the Gemara's question "if the collateral equals the debt, what is R. Eliezer's reason?" concerns an explicit, nonexaggerated acceptance, where there should be no asmachta. In another formulation, where the statement is only "I accept it," the issue is whether that language itself conveys a sufficiently definite assumption. Thus the halachah of an express condition remains stronger than the ordinary presumption and should not be rejected merely because Shmuel's broad unstated rule is not accepted.
And if the collateral was lost through unavoidable accident, and so forth. Sema says the borrower is liable only where he does not contradict the lender and simply does not know whether the loss was unavoidable. If the borrower says definitely that the lender was negligent or that the collateral is still in his possession, the borrower can take a hesset oath and be exempt. Sema infers this from Tur and Mechaber's reason for liability: the borrower is in the position of one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid." That implies a definite defense would be different. Baal HaTerumot and Tur elsewhere say that when the borrower claims definitively that the lender misappropriated the collateral, he is believed through a migo that he could have said the debt was already paid. Shach infers that without such a migo the borrower would not be believed. Bach and Shach therefore disagree with Sema. Baal HaTerumot's wording does indeed sound like Shach. Still, I do not understand the conceptual basis. Why should this collateral case be treated as though the debt were fully established and only payment were doubtful? The very existence and status of the collateral are part of the debt relationship. If the lender holds collateral and the borrower says definitively, "You destroyed or wrongfully retained the object that secured this loan," the amount still collectible on the debt is itself doubtful. Tur later rules that when an heir produces a note expressly mentioning collateral but does not know what became of that collateral, we do not simply say the note is certain and the collateral issue is a doubtful payment. The debt cannot be separated from the security that accompanied it. The same logic should apply here. There is also no clear reason to distinguish between a dispute over all of the collateral and a dispute over part of its value. If the lender says the lost collateral was worth one shekel and the borrower says one sela, the lender cannot simply extract the difference from the borrower merely because the original loan is certain. This is so even without a separate migo. The borrower's definite valuation defense matters. Why should it be different where the lender says the whole collateral was lost by accident and the borrower says the lender was negligent? The lender is effectively saying the collateral contributes nothing against the debt; the borrower says it discharges the corresponding amount. The same uncertainty exists. Bach explains why a migo is mentioned in the specific case of the borrower claiming the lender sold the collateral. That is an inherently weak factual claim: if it was sold, to whom and where is the purchaser? A migo may be needed to support such an unusual allegation. But where the borrower makes an ordinary definite claim such as negligence, no such migo is essential. This means Bach actually supports Sema's basic distinction more than Shach's. Mechaber later restates the rule without mentioning any migo, which strongly indicates the migo was not the foundation of the borrower's credibility. Had the entire rule depended on it, Mechaber would not have omitted the central requirement. Therefore the better practical reading is Sema's: a borrower who makes a definite ordinary defense concerning the collateral can be believed with the appropriate oath, while a borrower who says only "I do not know" bears the consequence of an uncertain payment.
The lender does not pay the excess, and so forth. Shach discusses whether, when collateral was taken after the loan, the lender is responsible even for value above the amount of the debt. Maharam in Mordechai limits the lender's paid-custodian responsibility to the amount of the debt. Shach disagrees. Shach cites Shaarei Rif, which treats collateral taken after the loan as a paid-custodian matter even according to R. Eliezer, who otherwise regards ordinary loan collateral as only an unpaid custodianship. The broader sources support Shach. As explained above, collateral taken after the loan does not derive paid-custodian status from the perutah of R. Yosef. The original lending mitzvah already occurred earlier. Yet Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber still rule that the lender is a paid custodian both for contemporaneous collateral and for collateral seized afterward. If liability after the loan extended only to the amount of the debt, the two cases would not truly have equal custodial status. Their equal formulation suggests that R. Yitzchak's rule gives the creditor a proprietary relationship to the collateral as a whole, including value above the debt. This conclusion does not depend on Shmuel's rule that loss of a small item can extinguish a much larger debt. The scope of R. Yitzchak's acquisition is a separate question. Ritva likewise indicates that the rationale "he is holding it against his money" does not by itself explain liability for value above the debt. That is precisely why the stronger proprietary rule of R. Yitzchak is needed. An artisan differs because the entire object is naturally entrusted to him for the work, and his compensation relates to the whole item. A creditor would have been satisfied with adequate security only up to the debt amount. Thus ordinary possession for money does not automatically explain excess-value liability. R. Yitzchak does. There is also a textual proof from the Mishnah in Shevuot. The Mishnah's phrase "the collateral was lost" governs both the clause where the lender claims the collateral was worth less than the debt and the clause where the borrower claims it was worth more. If the lender were a paid custodian only up to the debt amount and merely an unpaid custodian on the excess, "lost" would need to mean theft or loss in one clause and negligence in the other. It is unnatural for one undifferentiated phrase in the same Mishnah to shift meanings that way. The better reading is that the lender has one paid-custodian status over the entire collateral. Accordingly, Shach's conclusion is the stronger one: collateral taken after the loan can create responsibility for the full value of the object, including the excess over the debt.
To the borrower the excess. If the collateral is worth less than the debt, the lender ordinarily does not lose the balance unless he expressly accepted the collateral against the entire debt according to the Tosafot version of Shmuel. Beit Yosef holds that even if such an express condition is made after the loan, it does not produce Shmuel's full result. Hagahot Rosh in Perek HaOmanim explicitly says that where collateral was taken after the loan, loss affects only the value of the collateral through R. Yitzchak's rule, while Shmuel's broader "loss of the handle loses the whole debt" belongs only to collateral given at the time of the loan. Bach asks what substantive reason could justify this timing distinction. Tosafot provide the answer through asmachta. They explain that Shmuel's broad condition is not asmachta because the borrower too is exposed to loss of the collateral. Since the borrower places his property at risk and the lender accepts a corresponding risk to the debt, the lender's commitment is made wholeheartedly. This is different from an exaggerated conditional penalty such as "if I fail to cultivate I will pay one thousand," where the promisor does not make a balanced present exchange of risks. But that reasoning is strongest at the moment of the original loan. Without the lender's condition, the lender would then be only an unpaid custodian under the relevant view and the borrower truly risks losing the collateral. The lender's acceptance of broader debt loss directly balances that exposure. Collateral seized only after the loan is different. Under R. Yitzchak, the lender already has paid-custodian responsibility at least for the collateral's value. The borrower is not exposing himself to the same uncompensated collateral risk. An added condition that the lender will lose debt far beyond the object's value is therefore more one-sided and more readily classified as asmachta. This explains Hagahot Rosh and supports Beit Yosef. It also clarifies a Tosafot discussion that otherwise seems repetitive. In Shevuot, Tosafot ask both why the Mishnah cannot follow R. Eliezer when the condition is explicit and why the Gemara does not simply say everyone accepts Shmuel when the condition is explicit. The questions are not identical once one distinguishes the asmachta theory. If the Mishnah itself follows a Tanna who elsewhere accepts asmachta in certain contexts, an explicit condition may work more readily. But for the general halachic conclusion, where asmachta is usually not acquired, the broad Shmuel undertaking needs the special reciprocal-risk rationale. Accordingly, there is genuine basis to distinguish collateral given at the time of the loan from collateral taken later. The practical law therefore remains doubtful where a post-loan collateral agreement expressly purports to make loss of a low-value object cancel the entire debt.
The excess. Shach cites a responsum of Rashi in Mordechai. Reuven pledged an object to Shimon. Shimon pledged it to Levi. Levi then gave the object as a gift to Yehudah after Shimon told him that Reuven had been given a redemption deadline and that failure to redeem would make the object absolutely Shimon's. Rashi ruled that this forfeiture clause was asmachta. Yehudah must therefore return the object to Reuven, and Reuven must pay Yehudah the amount properly owing through the chain. If Yehudah says he already sold the object or no longer has it, Rashi says he must swear how much it was worth. Shach is puzzled. The Gemara in Perek HaMafkid already says that a lender who no longer has the collateral can swear its value and be discharged. Why does it matter that Yehudah mistakenly thought the object had become his by gift? The key is not the placement of the oath but what happens if Yehudah says he does not know the value. R. Ashi rejects entirely transferring the valuation oath away from the lender in the ordinary collateral case because a lender may genuinely not know the precise value of an object that is not his. Chazal should not force a person into an oath on a matter he was never expected to know. Thus where the lender says, "I do not know its value," one must consider whether the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear applies. If the lender was never expected to know, that rule may not apply. Yehudah is different. He believed the object was absolutely his gift. A person normally knows the approximate value of his own property. Therefore when Rashi says Yehudah "must swear how much it was worth," the practical consequence is that if he cannot state the value, he can be treated as one obligated to swear who cannot swear and must pay. This is why Rashi emphasizes Yehudah's belief that the object was his. The passage is concise, but this explains it well. It also explains why Reuven may prefer to sue Yehudah rather than Levi. Levi merely held collateral and may reasonably say he never knew its exact value. Yehudah, who treated it as his own gift, was expected to know. According to the authorities below who hold that even a lender is expected to know collateral value, the distinction narrows and Levi himself may face the same "unable to swear, therefore pay" rule. But within Rashi's framework, the gift-belief point is essential.
More, and so forth. Sema discusses a Jew who lends to another Jew on collateral belonging to a gentile and receives interest in a permitted structure because the Jewish borrower is acting only as the gentile's agent. Sema says the lender may be a full paid custodian and liable even for value above the debt, unless local law limits such liability. Shach says Mahari ben Lev's actual doubt concerned a different case: collateral given in connection with a permitted iska, where the lender's profit is uncertain and comes from the financing arrangement rather than from the collateral itself. Both discussions require clarification. If the Jew is really safeguarding property belonging to a gentile, ordinary Torah custodial law may not apply at all because the Torah speaks of "his fellow." Rambam and Mechaber below in siman 301 exempt custodians of gentile property from the ordinary shemirah framework, and according to Tosafot even negligence may sometimes be outside those rules absent direct damage. There are sources that sound stricter, such as Ran's discussion of chametz deposited by a gentile with a Jew. But those can involve explicit acceptance of liability rather than ordinary default custodianship. Tosafot Shevuot are revealing. They ask why, if we rule like Rabbah and Shmuel, a Jew who lends to a gentile on chametz collateral would need R. Yitzchak's special collateral-acquisition rule to create Pesach responsibility. Under Shmuel, the lender already appears liable as a paid custodian. Tosafot answer through several possibilities, including that the mitzvah benefit of R. Yosef does not apply to a gentile and that ordinary custodial obligations may themselves be excluded regarding gentile property. This suggests Shmuel is still connected to the laws of custodianship and is not an entirely independent universal debt rule. Accordingly, where the collateral is truly a gentile's property, the lender may not be a paid custodian at all absent an express undertaking. There is, however, a separate route. If the lender expressly accepts the collateral against the debt and thereby undertakes liability as a monetary condition, such a stipulation can operate even where ordinary shemirah law would not. That may explain some Tosafot passages applying Shmuel in a gentile context. But this is not universally agreed and should not be treated as automatic.
However, the chametz sugyot also require care because ownership, responsibility, and the "from now" acquisition formula interact differently for Pesach. Once the mainstream halachah is that a Jew can violate chametz restrictions on his own chametz even when a gentile bears responsibility, some earlier proofs based on the opposite assumption fall away. The cleaner conclusion is that ordinary default paid-custodian liability does not automatically attach to gentile collateral merely because a Jew holds it. Now to the iska case. If Reuven gives Shimon money as an iska and Shimon gives collateral, one might say Reuven is a paid custodian because he hopes to earn from the transaction. But an iska is half loan and half deposit. At the very moment Reuven receives Shimon's collateral, Shimon simultaneously receives Reuven's entrusted investment half. This can create shemirah beba'alim or a reciprocal-custody structure, weakening ordinary custodial liability. The matter is therefore not simple. If, however, a third party Levi gives Reuven collateral to induce Reuven to finance Shimon's iska, there is no reciprocal custody between Reuven and Levi. Then Reuven clearly receives a benefit from holding Levi's collateral: without it he would not enter the profit-bearing transaction with Shimon. Shach says the profit is uncertain and is earned from the loan, not the collateral. Neither point is persuasive. An uncertain profit opportunity itself has definite monetary value. People pay money for a chance at profit. The classic perutah of R. Yosef likewise depends only on the possibility that a poor person may appear during the mitzvah. And the profit is economically linked to the collateral because without the collateral the lender would not extend the financing. This resembles the rule concerning one who lends produce and receives collateral: the benefit arises from the loan transaction, yet the collateral still connects the benefit to the custodial relationship. Therefore, where third-party collateral induces a profit-bearing iska loan, the lender should be a paid custodian of that collateral. Likewise, if Reuven receives a fee from Shimon specifically to lend money to Levi and takes Levi's collateral, the collateral is part of the transaction that produces Reuven's benefit and paid-custodian status is strongly indicated.
And because of doubt we do not remove money. Shach applies his general theory of kim li and says that a litigant can adopt one authority's view on one issue without adopting that authority's entire legal system on another issue. That is true where the issues are genuinely independent. But where one proposition logically depends on the other, a person cannot coherently say, "I follow this authority here but reject the premise that makes his conclusion possible." That would be walking in darkness. If we rule like Rabbah concerning the lender's custodial level and then explain R. Akiva's contrary result through Shmuel, Tosafot themselves ask why the Gemara did not simply say everyone agrees with Shmuel. Their answer depends on the principle that ordinary collateral up to its value is treated like an implicit acceptance. Thus the positions are interconnected. We should not invent a novel kim li combination that no recognized authority actually maintains. Accordingly, if one says halachah follows Shmuel, he must also account for the ordinary-collateral implications that the major commentators derive from Shmuel. And if one says halachah follows Rabbah in a way inconsistent with those implications, he cannot selectively invoke Shmuel merely to preserve possession. Therefore Shach's proposed combination requires study. Kim li permits choosing among established halachic positions, not constructing a new hybrid position from mutually dependent fragments.
