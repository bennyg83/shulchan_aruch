When he took the collateral at the time of the loan. Mishneh LaMelech criticizes a responsum that treated every collateral given after the loan as collateral taken not at the time of the loan for purposes of the special acquisition and liability rules. He says those rules apply only where the creditor takes the collateral later through beit din, which also creates the Torah obligation to return a poor debtor's clothing by day or night. In his view, collateral voluntarily given by the debtor later is like collateral given at the time of the loan and creates only paid custodian liability. I am astonished. Rambam explicitly rules that the obligation to return necessary clothing applies whether the creditor took the collateral through beit din, seized it by force himself, or received it voluntarily from the debtor, so long as it was taken after the loan. The Shulchan Aruch rules the same in siman 97. Once the Torah return obligation applies, Rabbi Yitzchak's rule applies as well, because the verse says, "and it shall be righteousness for you," which presupposes that the creditor has acquired a real interest in the collateral. Therefore, according to Rashi and those who hold that this acquisition creates liability even for unavoidable accident, collateral voluntarily given after the loan can produce that liability. Rabbi Yosef Halevi, however, appears to hold that Rabbi Yitzchak's special acquisition after the loan applies specifically where the collateral reaches the creditor through beit din collection, not where the debtor voluntarily hands it over. Nimukei Yosef cites his view regarding partial admission: if the defendant gives a pledge for the admitted portion, that is not "here it is," because ordinary meshichah does not acquire a mere pledge when no new money is given. A pledge given at the time of the loan can be acquired through the loan money, and a later pledge collected through beit din is acquired through the Torah's special decree of collection. But a later voluntary pledge has neither new money nor beit din collection, and in Rabbi Yosef Halevi's view meshichah alone does not acquire a pledge because the body of the object is not transferred. This position is difficult. The sugya of a gentile who lent to a Jew on chametz shows that a pledge can be acquired even at the time of the loan, and when ordinary meshichah is unavailable, money itself can serve as acquisition. More seriously, Kiddushin says that a woman can be betrothed with collateral held from another person because of Rabbi Yitzchak. When the creditor hands that collateral to the woman, she can acquire his rights through ordinary transfer. If meshichah could never acquire pledge rights because the body remains the debtor's, how could the woman acquire the pledge from the creditor? Likewise the Torah requires return even of collateral taken without permission, proving that a creditor can acquire pledge rights without formal beit din collection. Therefore the better rule is Rambam's: a creditor acquires a pledge taken after the loan whether through beit din, by force, or voluntarily from the debtor. Tosafot and Rosh explain the kiddushin case of a man who merely places new collateral for a promised maneh differently: "there is no maneh and therefore no collateral," because no personal debt exists yet. Ramban and Rashba explain it as the pledge remaining too attached to the giver. Neither rationale supports Rabbi Yosef Halevi's broad denial of meshichah for an existing debt.
After he lent to him. Rashi holds that collateral taken after the loan makes the creditor liable even for unavoidable accidents. Ramban in Milchamot seeks proof against this from the case of an innocuous ox seized by the injured party for collection. The Gemara calls him a paid custodian, not one liable for unavoidable accidents. According to Rabbi Yosef Halevi the proof would be easy, because the seizure there is not through formal beit din collection. Even according to the broader view that Rabbi Yitzchak applies to forceful or voluntary seizure, the sugya can be explained. The creditor acquires under Rabbi Yitzchak only to the extent of his monetary claim. In the case of an innocuous ox, the injured party's claim is only against half the body of the ox. His acquisition therefore reaches only his own half. The Gemara's statement that he is a paid custodian concerns the owner's remaining half. He is responsible for guarding that part because of the benefit that the ox remains available for collection and is not hidden. Thus Rabbi Yitzchak can still create full acquisition and unavoidable accident liability with respect to the claimant's own half, while paid custodian liability governs the owner's half. Tosafot similarly explains why Rava had to establish paid custodian status according to Rabbi Yishmael. According to Rabbi Akiva the parties are already partners and each ordinarily guards his own share.
He is a paid custodian. The usual reason is the benefit of Rabbi Yosef's perutah, because by engaging in the mitzvah of guarding and returning collateral he may be exempt from giving charity at that moment. The Shach says this makes him a paid custodian throughout the period he holds the pledge, even after the underlying debt was repaid, until he returns the object. A responsum challenged this from the rule that an artisan who says, "Take your object and bring my money," can become only an unpaid custodian. The cases differ. An artisan's paid custodian status derives from the benefit of earning through the work or from holding the object for his wages. Once the work is complete and he no longer holds it for that purpose, the source of paid custody can end. A creditor who became a paid custodian through the mitzvah benefit is treated as though he received compensation at the beginning of the custodial relationship. That status continues until the object is actually returned. There is, however, an additional issue. Tosafot and Rosh hold that a paid custodian is liable for ordinary theft even where it occurs close to unavoidable accident. The Torah's liability for theft is broad. This includes a finder of a lost object who is a paid custodian only because of Rabbi Yosef's perutah. Therefore the same broad theft liability applies to a creditor whose paid custodian status arises from that benefit. According to Rama, who treats a lender on collateral differently and says his liability to the extent of the debt rests upon an implied condition that loss of the pledge means loss of the debt, the scope is narrower. An implied contractual condition does not ordinarily include true unavoidable accident. If the pledge was stolen despite extraordinary protection, or the creditor entered his home at the normal time and a wolf took it, the implied condition should not make him lose the debt, because people do not ordinarily condition upon such accidents. This differs from a true paid custodian, whose liability for theft is a Torah rule. Therefore where the creditor's liability rests only on the presumed agreement and not on actual paid custodian status, extraordinary accidental theft should not cause him to lose the debt.
A paid custodian. Nimukei Yosef explains that a person holding property against money owed to him can become a paid custodian from the benefit of holding it for collection, just as an artisan can be a paid custodian over the object he holds for wages. Maharshal disagrees and limits that reasoning to an artisan. Tosafot in Perek HaUmanin indicates that mere holding for wages is insufficient unless the artisan also receives the benefit of the work itself. Rashi, however, appears to say that explicit holding for collection can itself suffice. This explains Baal HaMaor's view that an injured party who seizes an ox can be a paid custodian even where the ox is forewarned and collection is not limited to the body of the ox. According to Rashi, the stated purpose of holding for the claim itself creates paid custody. According to Tosafot, the stronger benefit of having a specific collectible object available is needed. In the present case of a son who merely finds his father's pledge in his possession, there is no new manifestation that he is holding it for collection. Therefore even Rashi's approach does not readily make the son a paid custodian. If the father left property in the estate, however, the estate itself may remain liable if the father's custodial obligation had already attached before death. That depends upon whether a custodian's property is encumbered from the beginning of the custodial relationship or only from later negligence or loss.
Only an unpaid custodian. The Shach argues that collateral taken after the loan is a paid custodian even beyond the amount of the debt, because of Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition and the benefit of being able to use the pledge for legal acts such as kiddushin. In my view Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition reaches only the amount of the debt. The creditor can therefore use only that portion. Responsa Maimoniyot explicitly says that collateral taken after the loan creates paid custodian liability only to the extent of the money owed. The Shach's second rationale, that the creditor benefits simply by having the debt secured, is also insufficient in an ordinary loan because he chose to lend voluntarily and could collect from other assets. The case of a tort claimant who seizes an ox is different because the damage occurred against his will, and if the ox were hidden he might lose his collection source. Therefore the principal rule is that even collateral taken after the loan creates special paid custodian status only up to the amount of the debt unless some additional benefit exists.
If part of the collateral is lost. Mahari ibn Lev, cited by the Shach, rules that if some of the collateral is stolen but what remains is still worth the entire debt, the creditor loses nothing. The implied understanding that "if the collateral is lost, the debt is lost" exists only so that the creditor will not have to pursue the borrower after losing the security. Since the remaining collateral still fully secures the debt, that rationale disappears. This is clear for collateral given at the time of the loan, where liability rests on implied condition. I was uncertain concerning collateral taken after the loan, where Rabbi Yitzchak gives the creditor an acquisition interest. If he acquires only to the amount of the debt, and part of the physical object is lost, perhaps the lost portion should be proportionally attributed to the creditor's acquired share rather than entirely to the borrower's residual share. In my view the loss is shared proportionally. If the collateral is worth one hundred and the debt ten, and part is stolen, the creditor bears one tenth of that loss and the borrower nine tenths. According to Rashi, who treats Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition as a full property interest for liability, this follows directly. According to Tosafot, who say the creditor is a paid custodian because he benefits from the ability to use the pledge for kiddushin and other acquisitions, one can argue that he is effectively compensated for guarding the portion corresponding to the debt and should therefore bear the full loss of that portion. Thus even under Tosafot the creditor loses proportionally to the value of his claim.
Because of doubt we do not extract. Even though everyone agrees the creditor is at least liable as a paid custodian to the amount of the debt, there is still a practical difference between the competing explanations. According to Rabbi Yosef's perutah, creditor and borrower have separate claims: the creditor collects his debt in money, and the borrower collects compensation for the lost pledge according to ordinary custodian law, which can be from land. According to the view that the pledge was accepted on the implied condition that its loss cancels the debt, the two values are directly offset and the borrower simply owes less. There is also a difference where the loss occurred through an extraordinary theft approaching unavoidable accident. A true paid custodian can remain liable, while the implied condition may not extend that far. Since the authorities disagree, possession controls and we do not extract in doubtful cases.
We do not extract. The Shach writes that if the creditor has already seized an amount equal to the debt, we do not remove it because he can say he follows the opinion rejecting the implied condition. This is difficult. The Mishnah itself rules that a lender on collateral is a paid custodian. The Amoraim disagree only in explaining why. One says the reason is Rabbi Yosef's perutah; the other says it is the implied condition of offset. A litigant cannot defeat the undisputed law of the Mishnah by combining the rejected rationale of one authority with rejection of the other's rationale. The creditor is certainly liable as a paid custodian to the extent of the debt. Tumim likewise rejects the Shach on this point. The isolated view in the Geonic "Gates" that a pledge taken at the time of the loan creates only unpaid custody cannot support a claim of "I follow this authority" against the entire consensus of the decisors, especially where that view's Talmudic route is itself obscure. Therefore the creditor cannot retain contrary to the Mishnah's established rule.
