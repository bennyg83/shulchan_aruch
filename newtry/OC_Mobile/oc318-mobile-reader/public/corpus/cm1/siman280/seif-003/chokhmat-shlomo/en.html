Seif 6. The other says, "I do not recognize you," and is believed. Note: I am surprised that the codifiers did not cite Tosafot in Bava Metzia, chapter 3, which is the source of this rule. Tosafot imply that the denial is effective specifically where there is some adverse circumstance, for example where one previously saw the claimant without a beard and now he appears with a beard. Where there is no such defect, Tosafot imply that one may be believed to say, "He is my brother." Perhaps Tosafot wrote this only to explain the sugya according to the opinion that where one claims a definite debt and the other says "I do not know," the definite claim prevails. According to that view, absent an adverse circumstance, the claimant would be believed. We, however, rule that where one says, "You owe me a maneh," and the other says, "I do not know," the defendant is exempt. Accordingly, even without an adverse circumstance the claimant cannot recover on the basis of uncertainty. At minimum Tosafot would then prove that according to the view favoring a definite claim over an uncertain one, liability applies even where the defendant had no reason to know. By analogy, according to our law that one who says "I do not know whether I repaid you" is liable, liability should apply even where he had no particular reason to know. Consider this carefully.
