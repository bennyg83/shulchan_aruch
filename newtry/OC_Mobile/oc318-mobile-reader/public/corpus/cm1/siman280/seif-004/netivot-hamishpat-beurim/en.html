Shimon shall not inherit. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 4 of Hilchot Nachalot, asks: why do we follow Shimon's admission that Levi is not his brother rather than Reuven's admission that Levi is his brother? He brings a similar question from Gittin 40. In my view the cases are not comparable. There, both statements concerning the field operate as admissions of litigants. If there had been only one such admission, even witnesses contradicting it would not ordinarily defeat it because a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Here, Shimon's statement that Levi is not his brother is indeed an admission against Shimon's own claim. Even witnesses cannot necessarily overcome that admission as to his own share. But Reuven's statement that Levi is his brother is not an admission against Reuven's own property as to the disputed half. The question there is whether that half belongs to Shimon or to other unknown heirs if Levi is not a brother. Reuven is therefore more like a witness regarding that half, not a litigant admitting against his own property. Likewise Levi's own statement is not a litigant's admission against property already his after death. At that point the estate may already lie before other heirs. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that even if Reuven says to Shimon, "You are my brother and may take part of our father's estate," while Shimon says, "I am not your brother," we would follow Shimon's admission. Contradictory admissions are treated as a doubt, and where Reuven is a definite heir while Shimon is doubtful, the doubtful party cannot remove from the definite. This reasoning is plausible. Still, one must explain why Shimon does not later inherit from Levi. Siman 47, seif katan 1, indicates that where a person's statement was intended only to push Levi away from Jacob's inheritance, that statement need not bar him from inheriting Levi afterward. Perhaps the distinction is that here Shimon could have said, "I do not know," and that too would have prevented Levi from taking Shimon's share. By choosing instead to state with certainty, "He is not my brother," we treat the statement as a full admission rather than a mere litigation tactic.
