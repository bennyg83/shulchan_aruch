Where it is uncertain whether they had a son, we do not fear that they gave birth. Terumat HaDeshen asks why we do not follow the majority, since most women conceive and give birth. He answers that even though majority can overcome many presumptions, it does not ordinarily extract money from established possession. Here the estate stands in the presumptive family line of the known brother, like the case of the doubtful child and the levir, where a doubtful claimant cannot remove inheritance from a certain heir.
I am not satisfied that this inheritance presumption defeats majority. Rosh's language in the doubtful child and levir case rests on the principle that a doubt does not remove certainty: the levir is certainly an heir, while the other claimant is uncertain. But where a majority makes the birth of a child legally certain, the case is no longer one of certainty against doubt. A majority can be treated as certainty for this purpose. Rosh himself rules that where a scholar or upright man had a son from a maidservant, the son can inherit because we presume the father freed the woman rather than maintain a prohibited relationship, even though the son may remain restricted for marriage. This shows that a sufficient presumption can establish heirship against the otherwise certain family line. The stronger reason not to rely on the majority here is that the majority of women who conceive includes a significant minority who miscarry. Where the minority arises within the very majority relied upon, it can weaken the majority more fundamentally. Thus "most women conceive and give birth" may not supply the kind of decisive majority needed to displace the known heir. This is the better explanation.
