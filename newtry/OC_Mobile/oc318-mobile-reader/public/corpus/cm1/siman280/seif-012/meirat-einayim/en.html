And there was a ketubah obligation against him, etc. Meaning, the son owed either his wife's ketubah or his own creditor, and he had no property from which to pay them except the assets he stood to inherit from his father. The father's heirs say the son died first and never inherited anything, so the wife or creditor cannot collect from these assets, because where the son died in his father's lifetime they may collect only from property he already possessed, not from property merely fit to come to him by inheritance. The creditor or wife says the father died first, so the son inherited and was legally in possession, enabling collection from those assets.
The property remains in the possession of the heirs. The reason is as stated in seif 10: we leave the property in its prior possession, namely the father's. Moreover, these heirs are definite heirs. Even if the father died first and the son afterward, they are still the son's heirs. The wife and creditor, by contrast, have a right in the property only if the father died first. A doubt therefore does not remove property from definite heirs.
And they inherit everything. We must say that the wife was the mother of the daughters. Otherwise, if one of the daughters died last and the husband died during his wife's lifetime, the wife's ketubah would remain in the possession of her own heirs. Why would the text say that his heirs inherit everything? Rather, the wife was their mother, so if the daughters died after her they inherit their mother as well. The husband's heirs have a claim only if she died last.
Nevertheless, they divide. Here we do not simply leave the money in its prior possession, because the wife also had an established ketubah claim during her husband's life. This differs from the inheritance case above.
Because anything fixed in place is treated as half and half. We therefore do not follow the numerical majority, even though the husband's heirs derive from five possible people, the man and his four daughters. Mordechai adds: if they were not fixed in place when they died but died after going out, we apply the rule that whoever separates is presumed to have separated from the majority. We therefore assume that four from Reuven's side died first, leaving one from his side and one from his wife's, and the rule returns to division. Although the wife had not sworn on her ketubah, we do not suspect that issue where death was sudden, because in such a case there is no concern that the husband had placed money with her in advance. End of his words.
But if the daughters are married, etc. The word "but" is somewhat awkward, because if six claimants stand before us and each seeks a share, it is obvious that the estate is divided into six portions. Mordechai and Darkei Moshe state the cases in reverse order, which is clearer. They first say that if the daughters are married the estate is divided into six portions, and then say, "but if they are unmarried, etc., they divide." That latter ruling is the novelty: although the heirs of Reuven have five possible avenues of entitlement, they nevertheless divide because a fixed doubt is treated as half and half.
