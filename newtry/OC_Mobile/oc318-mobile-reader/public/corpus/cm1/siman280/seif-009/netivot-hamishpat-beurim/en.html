He left sons, daughters, and a tumtum. The Tur writes that if the estate is ample, so that the sons inherit and the daughters receive support, the sons can push the tumtum toward the female status. Nevertheless, the tumtum does not reduce the daughters' portion. Rather, the daughter's support until adulthood is first set aside, and only afterward is the tumtum given a similar amount. These words are very difficult, as Beit Yosef notes. In my humble opinion, the Tur's intent can be explained as follows. Suppose there is one son, one daughter, and one tumtum. If the tumtum is treated as a son, the estate is sufficient to support everyone until the daughter reaches adulthood. For example, the daughter has only half a year until adulthood, and her support for that period is ten gold coins. Supporting all three until she reaches adulthood would therefore require thirty gold coins. The Tur says that the sons push the tumtum toward the female status. This means that if the tumtum requires only a small amount for support, he is treated as female, receives only his support, and the son takes the remainder. The Tur then says, "nevertheless, he does not diminish," meaning that if the tumtum requires more support than the daughter because he has much longer until adulthood, and his support until then would cost fifty gold coins, the son and tumtum cannot say to the daughter: perhaps the tumtum too is female, in which case support for the daughter and tumtum totals sixty gold coins, while the estate contains only fifty, so ten is lacking and each ten should be reduced proportionally, leaving the daughter only eight. Rather, the tumtum does not diminish the daughters' share. The daughter can say to the tumtum: I am a definite creditor, while you are only doubtful, and your doubt cannot weaken my definite claim. This is like the case of a daughter and a tumtum in a small estate, where the daughter excludes the tumtum, as the Tur and Mechaber write.
If the estate is small, the tumtum receives nothing. This rule, and the following rule that with a son and tumtum the tumtum receives three portions, are very difficult to me. Where there are sons, daughters, and a tumtum, the sons are obligated to pay the debt created by the ketubah condition supporting the daughters, so the doubtful tumtum cannot diminish the daughters' definite debt. A doubt cannot displace a certainty. But where there is only a daughter and a tumtum, by what right can the daughter collect from the tumtum based on doubt? If the tumtum is also female, the tumtum has no obligation toward the daughter, because the beit din condition was imposed only in the daughter's favor against sons, not against other daughters. Daughters' support is not taken by inheritance, so that one could say one heir is certain and the other doubtful. It is taken by way of gift and debt, as explained in Bava Batra 131. Whenever the recipient's entitlement to a gift is doubtful, the recipient does not acquire it. The same applies to a doubtful debt, which cannot be collected from heirs, as explained in seif katan 12. It therefore appears that Rambam, whose words are those of the Mechaber, is correct that a daughter and tumtum inherit equally, without distinguishing between a large and small estate. I am also troubled by Rama, for at minimum the tumtum should receive one quarter, as in a case of a son and tumtum.
The tumtum takes three portions. If there is a son, daughter, and tumtum, with an amount remaining beyond the daughter's support until adulthood, the Tur writes that the son and tumtum divide the remainder, which sounds like an equal division. See Beit Yosef, who labors to explain this. The primary view appears to me to be that of the Bach: even here the tumtum takes three portions of the remainder. The Tur did not state it explicitly only because the rule is not uniform in every factual arrangement. See Bach.
