Why can Reuven not argue: either Levi is truly our brother, in which case Shimon is holding one sixth that belongs to Levi, my brother, wrongfully, and I should receive my share from it? The answer appears to be the principle of kam dina, that the earlier judgment stands, as explained in Yevamot 36 concerning a doubtful heir and the sons of the yavam. This proves that kam dina can apply even against a single person, unlike what Shach writes at the end of Tekfo Kohen. If the disputed one-sixth is still physically extant, however, Reuven can recover against it. Kam dina applies principally where he seeks to remove property from the person who already acquired it through the earlier legal resolution. There beit din's power makes the prior acquisition stand. But if the very one-sixth remains extant, or something directly traceable to it remains, it is treated as though the original object were still present. Reuven can then say: either way, this remaining object belongs to me to the extent of my claim. There is no distinction in these laws whether Levi himself claims doubtfully or with certainty. Bava Batra 135 establishes Rav Huna and R. Yochanan's case even where Levi also claims uncertainty. One might ask more fundamentally: a decedent who says, "This is my heir," can be believed through a migo that he could have given him the property as a gift. But how is Reuven himself believed to say Levi is his brother so that Levi becomes heir to an estate? Every heir ordinarily requires witnesses. The answer is that witnesses are needed specifically where there is already a known heir and a newcomer claims a closer status that would displace him, such as where brothers exist and a man says, "I am the decedent's son." But where a person dies and no heir is known, one who comes and says, "I am his relative entitled to inherit," is believed to take the estate. If another person then claims to be a closer relative and they contradict one another, the rule between them is kol d'alim gavar. This is the case of Rav Idi bar Avin in Bava Batra 33 under Tosafot's text, and Nimukei Yosef likewise says their dispute is governed by kol d'alim gavar.
