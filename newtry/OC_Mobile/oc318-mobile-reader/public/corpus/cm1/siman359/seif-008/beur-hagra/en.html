What is oppression, etc. Although Bava Metzia 111a records Rava's formulation, this does not mean robbery and oppression are identical. The Gemara itself distinguishes the prohibitions and elsewhere Rava differentiates oppression from robbery. Tosafos ask about this, and Bava Kamma also defines a robber separately. The plain meaning of the verse is actual robbery and actual oppression. Nevertheless, the prohibition of “you shall not rob” is also expounded regarding withholding a worker's wages, and the Gemara asks in what manner one violates each prohibition. As in Pesachim, where multiple prohibitions can apply even though each has its own primary subject, so too here the verse can impose two prohibitions on the withholding of wages while retaining its ordinary application to robbery. This resolves Tosafos's difficulty.
(Collected note) What is, etc.; what is, etc. The statement “What is oppression?” and Rava's answer are not redefining the ordinary word “robbery,” which means taking another's property by force, while oppression means withholding a worker's wages. Rather, the baraisa teaches that withholding wages can also violate “you shall not rob,” and the Amoraim dispute the precise form in which each prohibition applies. Rashi therefore explains that robbery ordinarily implies taking by force, while Rava teaches that withholding wages can nevertheless be included under “you shall not rob,” so that the Torah added “you shall not oppress” to make the employer violate two prohibitions. This fits the entire sugya and the Sifra, which distinguishes robbery, where property is taken from the owner's possession, from oppression, where wages are withheld without first being taken from the worker's possession. The Rif explains that Rava does not fundamentally dispute Abaye, because the two prohibitions have a common rationale.
This one who comes, etc. Bava Metzia 61a and 111a-b, and the Sifra on Kedoshim, explain that “you shall not oppress” includes one who withholds a worker's wages. The Rambam learns that the same principle applies to a loan, which resembles an obligation already owed, even though the Torah distinguishes categories regarding a korban, for the Gemara elsewhere recognizes multiple forms of deposit liability.
