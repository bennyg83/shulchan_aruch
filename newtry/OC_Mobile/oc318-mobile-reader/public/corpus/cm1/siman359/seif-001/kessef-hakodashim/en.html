It is forbidden to rob. It appears to me that a doubt concerning theft of another's property may be treated more leniently at the Torah level than an ordinary doubt concerning a Torah prohibition, because Scripture itself establishes the monetary rule that the claimant must approach and prove his claim. The Torah was given for all levels of Israel, including the most righteous and pious, and yet monetary doubts are governed by possession and burden of proof. Thus, where both sides are uncertain, one generally need not pay even to discharge a heavenly obligation, except in a case such as 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' where a prior presumption of debt exists. Even there the precise heavenly obligation where the debtor could not reasonably have known requires study. The reason may be that the Torah did not require a person to surrender his own property beyond the line of monetary law merely because of doubt. Therefore the principle, 'What is hateful to you, do not do to another,' does not itself require one to treat every unresolved monetary doubt as theft, for that would simply create the reverse doubtful taking from himself. Where one party claims with certainty and the other is uncertain, however, there may still be an obligation to satisfy the claim under the laws of Heaven.
