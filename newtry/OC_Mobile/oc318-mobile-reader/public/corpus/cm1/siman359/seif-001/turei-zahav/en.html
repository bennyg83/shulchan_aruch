[Seif 2] Since they are better. The Rosh explains the incident where David's men wished to take a stack of barley and later replace it with lentils. They did not yet possess the lentils, but planned to take a Jew's barley and replace it when Philistine lentils became available. Had the lentils already been available, they could have acquired them on the owner's behalf because they were more valuable than the barley. The Tur adds that this applies only where it is known to be beneficial, such as property intended for sale, but not household articles. The barley in David's incident was not shown to be intended for sale, and Jews dwelling on their land may have needed it for their households. The Tur therefore appears to mean not literally that it must be listed for sale, but that the property must be commercial produce rather than household articles retained for use. Still, the Rosh's rule is difficult in light of Bava Metzia 38a, which says that one may not touch deposited produce even when it is deteriorating, because a person prefers one measure of his own to nine measures of another's. All the more so, the owner may prefer the species he labored over to another, better species. One may answer that when the loss exceeds the ordinary amount, the custodian may sell. Here the superior replacement must provide a benefit greater than the owner's ordinary expected loss. Yet Kiddushin 45a teaches that if vinegar was sold and found to be wine, the sale is void, because some prefer vinegar and others wine; similarly, a woman betrothed with silver found to be gold may not be betrothed, since she may prefer silver. The same concern should apply to barley and lentils. Moreover, one may not take another person's animal for work without permission, even when it is ordinarily rented, as shown by the law of forcibly taking another's ship. The owner may demand depreciation as from a robber, and we do not presume that he prefers rental payment. Maharshal therefore rejects the Rosh: one may not trade with another's property without permission, for perhaps the owner values the barley more or does not wish to sell. The principle of benefiting a person in his absence applies only where the benefit contains no loss. Maharshal's ruling is correct. Nevertheless, where there is a great communal or mitzvah need, one may take property without prior permission, because the owner is presumed pleased that a mitzvah be performed with his property. In such a case equivalent produce may be returned; it need not be superior. This appears to me the practical law.
