And there is one who says, etc. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 49, concerning an artisan who is given wood, silver, or gold to fashion into an object, while the artisan has comparable material of his own and presently needs the material given by the owner. May he take the owner's material for himself and substitute equivalent material? Beit Ephraim first notes Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 35, seif katan 21, who writes that where an agent betroths a woman with the sender's money in a matter where the sender has no reason to care which particular coins are used and the agent can substitute equivalent money, this is not theft. Beit Shmuel similarly cites Shiltei Giborim that where money is sent through an agent there is no concern if the agent exchanges it. Sefer HaMakneh questions this and suggests that substitution is permitted only with loose deposited money where the bailee has permission to use it. Beit Ephraim objects. Permission to use deposited money and permission to exchange specific property are separate issues. A money changer who receives loose coins is allowed to use them because the deposit was made with that understanding. Even before use he may therefore have paid custodian status, but he does not yet become a borrower liable for unavoidable accidents. If the owner appears before use and demands his coins, the holder must return those very coins, because he has acquired no property right in them and the owner may insist on his own money. But if the owner is absent and the holder needs the coins, and he does not wish to borrow them but rather credits the owner with equivalent coins in their place, that may be permitted under the principle that one person benefits while the other loses nothing, where the owner has no particular interest in those specific coins. This logic may apply even to one who is not a money changer. Still, if the owner is present and objects, the holder may not force such an exchange, just as one who lives in another's courtyard that is not normally rented need not pay after the fact, yet the owner may prevent him from living there initially. Beit Ephraim then compares the rule of one who consumes another's produce and substitutes superior produce, which is allowed because the replacement is objectively to the owner's benefit. Maharshal disputes even that where there may be some personal preference for the original produce. But where the substitute is exactly equivalent and people ordinarily have no preference between the items, there is neither objective gain nor loss to the owner, and the principle that one benefits while the other loses nothing permits substitution so long as the owner does not protest. He brings support from Bava Batra 99b concerning one who relocates a public path through his field, and explains that even according to authorities who limit that case, their objection concerns forcing an exchange against the owner's will. Where no one objects, equivalent substitution is permitted. This is the basis of Beit Shmuel's ruling that where there is no customary concern over the specific item and the agent can replace it, the act is not theft. The principle that a person prefers his own small measure to another's larger measure applies to produce and similar deposits where replacement quality may differ, but not where people ordinarily have no concern and the substitute is truly equivalent. Therefore, if an owner gives an artisan silver or similar material with no particular individualized significance, the artisan may use it when needed and replace it with equivalent material for the owner's object, without committing theft. The same may apply to wool, wood, and many comparable materials where the owner is indifferent between equivalent units. See there.
