And there is an opinion that says, etc. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat siman 49, discusses a craftsman who receives wood, silver, or gold from an owner to make a vessel and himself owns identical material, but presently needs the owner's material. May he take the material for himself and substitute equivalent material? At first glance this follows Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 35, who writes that where an agent was given money and the sender is not particular about the particular coins, the agent may exchange them and this is not theft. Sefer HaMikneh questions that ruling and suggests that only where deposited money was left unsealed in circumstances allowing the custodian to use it may he exchange it, as discussed by Shach in siman 292. Beit Ephraim questions HaMikneh. Beit Shmuel based himself on Shiltei HaGibborim, who speaks even where permission to use the money was not given, and Chacham Tzvi had already challenged Shach's proof from siman 292. Beit Ephraim therefore distinguishes the right to use a deposited item from the right to exchange it for an equivalent. Permission to use unsealed money rests on an implied understanding at the time of deposit, especially with a moneychanger, and makes the custodian a paid custodian even before use. Yet before actual use he has not become a borrower liable for unavoidable accidents, and if the owner appears and demands his own coins, those very coins must be returned because the custodian acquired no ownership in them. If, however, the owner is absent, the custodian needs these coins, and he does not wish to borrow them but instead transfers equivalent coins to the owner's ownership in exchange, Beit Ephraim argues that he may do so not because of implied permission to use the deposit but because this is a case where one benefits and the other suffers no loss. Where people are not particular about the specific coins, substituting identical coins creates no loss. The same reasoning would apply even to a person who is not a moneychanger. Nevertheless, if the owner is present and objects, the custodian cannot force the substitution. The rule that one who dwells in another's courtyard that is not normally rented need not pay because one benefits and the other loses nothing does not permit a person to enter against the owner's objection; Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri state that the owner may prevent him in advance. So too, substitution is allowed only while the owner does not protest. Rosh in Bava Kamma, regarding Israelite stacks of grain, permits one who has lentils available to take barley and confer the superior lentils upon the owner through another person, because the substitute is better and therefore clearly a benefit. Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 359, rule similarly. Yam Shel Shlomo disputes this, arguing that one may not trade in another's property without permission because perhaps the owner particularly wants his barley. Beit Ephraim explains that Rosh permits substitution of a different type only when the substitute is objectively better, so the benefit outweighs the possible disadvantage. If the substitute is not better, there may be a disadvantage without a corresponding benefit and substitution is forbidden. But where the substitute is truly identical and people are not particular, there is neither benefit nor disadvantage to the owner, and the principle that one benefits while the other loses nothing permits it so long as the owner does not object. He brings support from Bava Batra 99b concerning a public path that crossed a private field. Even according to the authorities there who reject Rashbam and hold that the owner cannot exchange the public path, that is because the public was actively asserting its right and the owner cannot impose an exchange against the rights-holder's will. Where no one objects, all agree that an equivalent exchange can be made when needed. This, he says, is the meaning of Beit Shmuel: where the sender is not particular and the agent has an equivalent replacement available, there is no theft. The principle that a person prefers his own measure of produce applies to produce and similar deposits where the replacement may differ in quality and the owner may reasonably be particular. It does not apply where people are not particular and the replacement is truly identical. Accordingly, if an owner gives a craftsman silver or similar material about which owners are not particular, the craftsman may take it for his own need and substitute equivalent material to complete the owner's item. The same may apply to wool and wood where market practice shows no particularity about the identical material.
