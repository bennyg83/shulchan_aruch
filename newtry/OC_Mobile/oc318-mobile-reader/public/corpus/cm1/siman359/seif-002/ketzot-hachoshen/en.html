Rosh writes that if Jewish barley stacks were not yet taken and lentil stacks belonging to the enemy were not yet available, one may not seize the barley intending later to repay with the more valuable lentils. But if the lentils are already available, one may acquire them for the barley owners even without their presence, because that is clearly beneficial to them. Maharshal objects that one may not trade with another person's property without consent merely because the substitute is worth more. Perhaps the owner prefers his barley or does not wish to sell at all. The rule that one may acquire on another's behalf applies only where there is no possible disadvantage. Rosh's position can nevertheless be understood where the replacement is objectively superior and the transaction avoids an immediate loss. His proof from the verse implies that the prohibition concerned taking now and repaying only later. Where the superior replacement is already present and can immediately be vested in the owner, the act is a direct rescue rather than speculative commerce.
