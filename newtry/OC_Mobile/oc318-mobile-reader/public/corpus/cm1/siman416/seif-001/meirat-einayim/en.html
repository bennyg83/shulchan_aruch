Even though he abandoned them. At first glance it would seem that the text should read, "even though he did not abandon them," because since they are not lying in the public domain and still belong to him, having not been abandoned, there is greater reason to collect from him than if he had abandoned them. Rama likewise writes in the gloss, "if he did not abandon them, he is liable." According to the text before us, one must explain: even though he abandoned them, so that everyone has permission to pass through that place and might be injured by them, he is nevertheless exempt for the reason that follows.
After the allotted time he is liable. The Tur writes: after the allotted time, if the wall or tree caused damage either while falling or after it fell, it has the law of a pit. Rosh likewise writes this and adds that this is unlike a stone, knife, or load placed at the top of a roof that falls through an ordinary wind and causes damage while falling, which has the law of fire. Those objects become "fire" through an outside force, namely the wind that knocks them down. A wall or tree, by contrast, falls because of its own deterioration. The owners saw that it could no longer stand and beit din warned them to remove it. Although it travels and causes damage and thus does not resemble a stationary pit, that does not lessen the owner's liability. All the more so liability should apply, just as one who ties a stone to his animal's leg and the animal walks into the public domain and causes damage is liable. If one objects that the wall or tree was not originally made for the purpose of causing damage, the category of ox proves otherwise. End of Rosh's words.
