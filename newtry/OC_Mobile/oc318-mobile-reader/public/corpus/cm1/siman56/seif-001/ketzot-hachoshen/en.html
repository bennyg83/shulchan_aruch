A shalish. Hagahot Mordechai writes that although the creditor denies payment, once the shalish says with certainty that the debt was paid, the creditor cannot now retract from the arrangement because the act has already been completed through payment and the creditor admits that he had trusted the shalish for that purpose. This implies that before the relevant act occurs a party can retract. Rashba likewise writes that unless the parties said, "Acquire on behalf of both of us," they can retract. It appears, however, that this is so only before the shalish has stated his testimony. Once the shalish declares the terms or outcome entrusted to him, the parties can no longer retract even if no external act has yet occurred. His testimony itself is the operative conclusion, just as one who accepts his father or another otherwise disqualified person as judge cannot retract after that person has given the ruling, because that is the final judgment. Therefore, if the parties merely trusted the shalish but he has not yet announced his version, they can still retract unless they said, "Acquire on behalf of both of us," which functions like a kinyan and makes the arrangement binding immediately. After the shalish has given his account, however, they can no longer retract in any case. Later authorities wrote too generally on this point.
Like two witnesses. The shalish is believed because the parties trusted him. Even if he no longer has a present migo, once he previously had a migo at some stage they are regarded as having entrusted him. But if he never had any migo at any time, they never gave him that special credibility. Knesset HaGedolah raises a doubt whether a shalish might be believed even if he never possessed a migo and says Hagahot Mordechai may imply so. The primary rule follows the Shach: the special credibility of the shalish depends upon there having been a stage at which his possession and control gave him a migo.
