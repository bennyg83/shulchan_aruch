A shalish is believed. Even if he no longer has a migo, so long as at one time he had the power to act as he now says, the parties entrusted him to be believed even if he were to lie. Therefore, even with objects ordinarily lent or rented and where there are witnesses who saw them in his possession, he is believed if the parties admit they appointed him as shalish. Even if he later became established as one who falsely denies claims, his credibility as shalish remains. If the person who deposited the matter with him retracts from the arrangement, that does not help if the shalish says the act has already been completed. If the shalish admits that it has not yet been done, the depositor may retract and appoint another shalish. If the shalish is uncertain, then with movables, if both parties deposited them with him, both are treated as in possession and they divide where division can be true. If only the giver deposited them, the property remains in the original owner's possession even if both giver and recipient assert certainty. With a document, if the shalish is uncertain, it remains deposited even if both parties entrusted it to him. If the shalish dies and the document is found with his heirs, and division can be true, they divide because it is like two parties holding the document.

He denied the shalish arrangement. Not only where the opposing party claims the item was merely deposited, since by entrusting it to the shalish he believed him regarding whatever he would say. Even if he claims robbery or loss, the shalish is believed because we do not ordinarily suspect robbery or loss. The Shach disagrees and holds that where the borrower denies the shalish arrangement, the shalish is not believed even if the borrower claims mere deposit, because otherwise anyone could conspire with another and say, "I am the shalish," thereby exempting him from an oath. The Gemara's rule that the shalish is believed where the husband says the get was only deposited applies where he admits a shalish arrangement but claims it was subject to a particular condition. Accordingly, whether with movables or a document, even if the shalish already gave the item to the recipient, if the giver denies the shalish arrangement and the recipient knows only from the shalish, it must be returned to the giver. All the more so if it is still in the shalish's hand. But where the giver admits the shalish arrangement, the shalish is believed even if the recipient knows only from him. The Beurim concludes that the law of a shalish parallels the law of a purchaser. With movables not ordinarily lent or rented, where a purchaser would be believed and the other party could not claim loss or robbery, the shalish likewise is believed. But with things ordinarily lent, animals that wander, or a document, where a purchaser would not be believed, a claim of shalish likewise is not believed unless supported by a migo. If the recipient asserts certainty, the migo helps. If the recipient knows only from the shalish, it must be returned. The Tumim too concludes that with an ordinary object the law is not like the Shach. The shalish must nevertheless swear where the shalish arrangement itself is denied.
