A third party, and so forth. The source is Sanhedrin 31b. There are two versions of R. Nachman's ruling in the case of a woman who produced a document from her possession and said it had been paid. In one version R. Nachman believed her because she had a migo: she could have burned the document. In the other version he did not believe her once the document had already been established before beit din. Rava challenged R. Nachman from a baraita: a receipt bearing witnesses must be validated through their signatures; if it bears no witnesses but is found in the possession of a third party, or if it appears after the signatures on the note, it is valid. This shows that a third party is believed. The Gemara concludes with a refutation of R. Nachman's second version. Rashi explains: "It bears no witnesses and is found in the possession of a third party," meaning it is not in the debtor's own possession. Both parties admit that this person was appointed as their third party, and therefore the receipt is valid. Nimukei Yosef adds an explanation to Rashi. If the receipt literally bore no signatures at all, what evidentiary significance could it have? It would be a mere scrap of paper. He therefore explains that the receipt is in fact signed, but the signatures cannot presently be authenticated. The creditor admits that genuine witnesses signed it. Were the receipt in the debtor's own hands, it would not help because a party who relies upon an unvalidated writing must validate it. But because the creditor admits that he entrusted this signed receipt to the third party, and perhaps at the time it was deposited the witnesses could still have been authenticated, he thereby admitted that he trusted the third party. The third party is therefore believed even though the signatures cannot now be validated. On this interpretation, the third party's position is stronger than the debtor's own possession. If the receipt were in the debtor's hands, it would be worthless until authenticated. In the third party's hands, because the parties initially trusted him, he is believed despite the present lack of authentication. This also explains the Gemara's challenge to the version of R. Nachman that requires a current migo. Here the third party has no current migo capable of making the unvalidated receipt effective in the debtor's hands, yet he is believed because the original entrustment created independent credibility. Tosafot explain the phrase "it bears no witnesses" literally. In their first explanation, both the note and the receipt are in the third party's hands. Since he can burn the note, he has a migo and is believed to say it was paid. They add that the baraita sounds as though the same is true even after the document was established before beit din, paralleling the clause where the receipt appears after the signatures on the note. Under Tosafot's approach, the third party has no greater legal status than the debtor would have if both the note and the receipt were in the debtor's own hands. The debtor would obviously be believed if he possessed the original note together with the receipt. So too the third party. Even if at the moment of litigation the third party no longer has a current migo because the note has already been established before beit din, he was trusted when the note was first deposited with him, at which point he could have handed it to the debtor or destroyed it. Tosafot in Gittin similarly explain the rule "the third party says it was given for divorce": even if he lacks a present migo, since when the item was deposited with him he had power to deliver it in the claimed manner, the depositor trusted him. Tosafot then give another explanation: the receipt may bear witnesses, but those witnesses do not know whether actual payment occurred. The receipt is in the third party's possession, and the third party is believed to say it records a real payment. Shach is troubled by this second explanation. He asks: perhaps here the third party is believed only where the document was never established before beit din and he has a current migo to deliver it to the debtor. If Tosafot intend that the second explanation also parallels their statement in the first explanation that the case resembles a receipt appearing after the signatures on the note and therefore remains valid even after court establishment, why did they insert the second explanation in the middle rather than after completing the first? From this, Shach infers that the second explanation must instead mean what Nimukei Yosef said: the receipt has signatures that cannot be authenticated, and yet the third party is believed even though handing it to the debtor would not have helped. He reads Tosafot's phrase that the witnesses "do not know whether it was paid" as shorthand for inability to validate the receipt, because if they cannot remember the underlying matter, according to certain views they cannot authenticate their signatures. Shach therefore concludes that Tosafot too agree that the third party's possession can be stronger than the debtor's own possession. He criticizes Bach, who understood Tosafot as holding that the third party is never stronger than the debtor. He likewise criticizes Maharsha. Maharsha writes that Tosafot did not accept Rashi's interpretation because if the receipt had no witnesses and the third party had no migo, even Rava would not believe him. Tosafot therefore had to explain that both note and receipt are in the third party's hands, giving him a migo. Shach responds that, according to Nimukei Yosef, even without a usable present migo the initial entrustment itself is enough. In my view Shach's reading does not justify rejecting Bach, and certainly not Maharsha. His explanation that Tosafot's words "the witnesses do not know whether it was paid" secretly mean "the signatures cannot be authenticated" has no basis in the language. Tosafot could simply have said, as Nimukei Yosef does, that the receipt is unvalidated. Why speak obscurely about the witnesses not knowing whether payment occurred? Rather, Tosafot are referring to the preceding clause of the baraita, "a receipt bearing witnesses is validated through their signatures." As in Bava Metzia, the creditor can claim that the witnesses merely prepared a receipt in anticipation of payment but actual payment never occurred. In the next clause, the receipt is signed, but the witnesses themselves do not know whether payment ultimately took place. The third party is believed because when the parties originally placed the documents with him, he could have handed the note to the debtor. That initial migo shows they trusted him. This is exactly Maharsha's point. The relevant migo need not exist at the present moment after the note was established before beit din. It is enough that the third party possessed such power at the moment of entrustment. What Tosafot reject is the idea that a third party is believed more strongly than the debtor would be if the item were in the debtor's own possession. If at the time of entrustment the third party had no effective power to confer the claimed result upon the debtor, there is no basis to say he was trusted. Shach asks: if the second explanation depends on the same "parallel to after the signatures" premise, why did Tosafot not place it after the entire first explanation? This is too weak a textual inference to support a forced reading. Tosafot may simply have inserted an alternative understanding of "it bears no witnesses" before returning to the broader point that the third party's credibility derives from his prior migo. Indeed, the structure of the baraita supports Tosafot. If the first case means a receipt literally without signatures but both note and receipt are in the third party's hands, then if the note has not been established before beit din he has a current migo to destroy or hand it over. One might ask why the baraita did not distinguish within the third-party case itself: before establishment he is believed, after establishment he is not. But that distinction would not teach the next clause, concerning a signed receipt in the creditor's own hands. The baraita's progression is sensible: an unsigned receipt can work in a third party's hands because of his control over the note, while a signed receipt in the creditor's own papers can also work because of the circumstances of its placement. Under the second Tosafot explanation, where witnesses signed but cannot say whether actual payment occurred, it is even clearer that current establishment before beit din does not destroy the third party's credibility. At the time of entrustment he could have given the document to the debtor. Therefore, the depositor trusted him to determine the significance of the receipt. This remains true even if the note is later presented to court. The crucial principle is thus: the third party need not have a current migo at litigation, but when he was entrusted with the item he must have had practical control enabling him to bring about the result he later asserts. Otherwise there was no reason to infer that the parties granted him special credibility. This is consistent with Tosafot in Gittin concerning a third party who says a get was given for divorce. Even when the current migo is gone, the original power to deliver the get as claimed is enough. But if the item could not have had the claimed legal effect even had the third party handed it to the beneficiary at the outset, the initial entrustment proves nothing. Accordingly, the halachah follows Maharsha and Bach: the third party's hand is not intrinsically stronger than the debtor's own hand. His special credibility derives from the fact that the parties knowingly placed the matter under his practical control. Therefore, if from the outset the receipt would have been ineffective even had it been in the debtor's hands, the third party does not gain some new superior power merely by holding it. This also explains why Rashi and Nimukei Yosef differ. Rashi can be understood as assigning the third party a stronger formal status because the parties expressly acknowledged him as their third party, while Tosafot ground his credibility in practical entrusted control. Our general halachic framework, following Tur and most authorities, is that the third party has no greater substantive claim than the debtor himself; he is believed because he was trusted with the ability to act. Therefore Bach's conclusion is correct. Shach's elaborate discussion does not overturn this reading. The straightforward language of Tosafot and the underlying principle of third-party credibility support Maharsha and Bach.
He is believed like two witnesses. Shach explains that this is not literally equal to two witnesses. If two witnesses contradict the third party, they are believed over him. Rather, the phrase means that his credibility is stronger than that of a single witness: if one witness contradicts the third party, the party benefiting from the third party's statement need not even take a rabbinic oath. I do not understand this. We have adopted, following Tur and most authorities, the principle that the third party has no greater substantive force than if the item were in the debtor's own hands. If the debtor himself possessed the item and one witness testified against him, then if the third party is personally qualified to testify, we would have one witness against one witness and the debtor would still need to take a rabbinic oath, as explained below in siman 87. If the third party is disqualified from testifying, the debtor would face a Torah oath based on the opposing witness. Why should placing the item in the third party's hands improve the debtor's position beyond what possession by the debtor himself would accomplish? Rather, the statement that the third party is "like two witnesses" concerns the third party's authority to carry out the entrustment. If he says, "Return this to the debtor," and one witness contradicts him, we do not freeze the object as a matter of unresolved doubt and apply division or shuda. For purposes of administering the deposited item, the third party is trusted and his hand is treated as the debtor's hand. He returns the item to the debtor. Afterward, if the creditor sues the debtor and produces the one contradicting witness, the ordinary laws of testimony and oaths apply. If the third party is a valid witness, it is one witness against one and the debtor takes the appropriate rabbinic oath. If the third party is not qualified to testify, the opposing single witness can trigger a Torah oath. The phrase "like two witnesses" therefore means only that a lone witness cannot displace the third party from carrying out the entrusted disposition of the object. It does not abolish the ordinary oath consequences in a later lawsuit between the parties.
And even if they wrote the terms of the entrustment, and so forth. There are three distinct cases. First, if the parties wrote the terms of the third-party arrangement on a paper and handed that paper itself to the third party, then the third party may certainly say that the actual arrangement was different. He could simply conceal or destroy the written memorandum. Since the parties placed the memorandum under his control, they trusted him from the outset. Second, if they wrote and signed the terms and delivered the signed memorandum to one of the litigants, Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef implies that if the third party directly contradicts the writing, he is not believed. For example, if the document says there was no condition at the time of deposit and he later says there was a condition then, he contradicts the written evidence. But if he says a new condition was made afterward, or otherwise explains his statement in a way consistent with the written memorandum, he is believed. Shach rules likewise. There is reason to question this. The entire special credibility of a third party arises because the parties initially trusted him. Tosafot and Rashba in Gittin therefore struggle with the case where the husband says a get was deposited merely for safekeeping while the third party says it was for divorce. If the husband delivered it before witnesses, what evidence of special trust exists? They answer that the witnesses may later die or travel overseas, so the depositor still placed meaningful practical control in the third party's hands. That reasoning does not readily apply when the signed written terms of the entrustment were delivered to one of the litigants. What special trust remains? The writing itself preserves the evidence. Why should the third party be believed even to say that a later condition was added, if the parties no longer needed to rely on his memory or word? Perhaps where the deposited object is one for which a custodian could claim unavoidable loss, we can still infer trust. The third party could have claimed that the object was lost through circumstances beyond his control. Even though such a claim requires an oath, he still possessed meaningful evidentiary power. But where no such defense is possible, I do not know what basis remains for special credibility. One could say the case concerns a handwritten memorandum rather than a formally witnessed instrument. Then the third party could perhaps say, "I returned the memorandum," and because the writing does not itself impose a monetary obligation upon him, he might not have been expected to retain it carefully. Although Rashba holds that a debtor generally cannot claim payment against his own handwritten obligation, that is because a debtor who pays knows to demand return of the writing. A third party's memorandum is different. Since it does not itself concern a debt owed by him, people may sometimes return or release it without insisting on recovering the paper. He might therefore be believed to say it was returned. Still, this requires study. There is another point in the Gittin sugya. Tosafot and Rashba explain the husband's entrustment despite witnesses by saying the witnesses might die or travel overseas. Why not give the simpler explanation that the third party could hand the get to the wife, and if the husband later sued him before the witnesses, the third party could say, "I returned it to you"? A custodian is not generally required to return before witnesses. Mordechai in Perek HaChovel indeed says a third party can remain believed despite witnessed deposit because he could claim he returned the item. One cannot answer that the husband trusts the wife not to brazenly say she received the get from him directly. If, under Rav Huna's view, the husband himself would be believed to say the get was only a deposit, then even if the original witnesses later died and the third party delivered the get to the woman, if she admitted she received it from the third party the husband could still say he had deposited it for safekeeping and she would need proof. So merely imagining future death of the original witnesses does not always generate enough trust. It appears that the concept of special trust is needed principally where the parties are in one city, in which the presumption "if it were so" can operate. But where they are in different cities and that presumption does not apply, the third party holding the get is believed by ordinary possession principles even without special proof of entrustment. We see this in the case where the husband says the agent was appointed only to deliver, while the agent says he was appointed to receive on the woman's behalf. Even according to authorities who otherwise favor the husband, the agent can be believed where the normal "if so" presumption does not operate. There is little meaningful entrustment there: the husband wanted him to take the get to the woman in any event. If the agent falsely says he was an acceptance agent, the burden would ordinarily rest upon him. Yet the possession structure itself gives him credibility. Accordingly, in one city the third party's credibility can still be understood through potential future circumstances. Even if the wife would not dare claim she received the get directly from the husband, the third party and wife may later travel to another city. There the ordinary "if so" presumption disappears. If she receives the get there and truthfully says she received it from the third party, the third party can be believed regarding the nature of his appointment. Since the husband knows the third party has that practical power, entrusting him with the get even in the present city demonstrates trust. This may be the deeper meaning of Tosafot and Rashba. There is also another answer concerning the case of an agent for delivery versus acceptance, which I discuss in my chiddushim to Even HaEzer. In any event, the present ruling must be divided carefully. A third party who physically controls the only written evidence of the arrangement has broad credibility because he could suppress it. Where the writing was delivered to a litigant, he cannot contradict its express contents. His credibility as to later developments depends on whether meaningful entrusted control still existed after the writing was created. In cases where such control can be shown, he may be believed to explain subsequent conditions; where it cannot, the matter remains difficult.
And even if he contradicts him, and so forth. Tur and Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, make clear that the third party is believed not only where the owner contradicts him by saying, "I gave it to you merely for safekeeping." Even if the owner says, "You stole it from me," or, "It fell from me and came into your hands; I never delivered it to you at all," the third party is nevertheless believed and does not even need to take an oath. See Baal HaTerumot there. Shach argues at length. His basic position, as I summarized in Urim, is that whenever the owner denies having appointed this person as a third party, the holder has no special third-party credibility unless he has a migo. If he has a migo, he is believed merely like an ordinary litigant and must take the appropriate oath; without a migo he is not believed at all. In my opinion his arguments are not clear. He says that if the owner claims, "I entrusted this to you merely as a deposit," the holder should not have the law of a third party. Otherwise, every depositary could collude with another person and say, "I was appointed to deliver it to him." Baal HaTerumot proves the contrary from the dispute where the husband says, "I gave the get to the third party merely as a deposit," and the third party says, "It was given for divorce." In monetary matters, everyone agrees the third party is believed. Shach explains that "deposit" there does not mean an unconditional deposit. Rather, the husband admits that it was really delivered for divorce, but says it was subject to a condition that was not fulfilled. Shach further says that this is the intent of the authorities cited by Baal HaTerumot who hold that if the owner denies the entire character of the third-party arrangement, the holder is not believed. They do not disagree with an explicit Gemara; they interpret the Gemara as Shach does. This interpretation cannot stand. First, it does not fit the language. The Gemara says simply, "the husband says, for safekeeping." If it meant, "for divorce subject to a condition," it should have said so, especially because the legal difference is crucial. According to Shach, an ordinary safekeeping claim would actually defeat the third party. Why would the Gemara use the unqualified word "safekeeping" for a case where that very claim is not legally meant? Second, the Gemara later discusses a woman who says, "Receive my get for me." It requires two sets of witnesses, one set testifying that she appointed the agent and another that the agent received the get. The Gemara asks why witnesses to receipt are needed; let the third party be believed. According to Maharsha's reading of Tosafot, the husband is there contradicting the third party. Even according to Rosh, who says the question is directed mainly according to R. Huna and according to R. Chisda concerns a case where the husband does not contradict him, why did the Gemara force the answer that the baraita follows R. Elazar and concerns a get with no signature witnesses, contrary to the enactment of Rabban Gamliel the Elder? It could have answered far more simply that the husband denies the third-party appointment and says, "I gave it to you only as an ordinary deposit." According to Shach, even R. Chisda should then agree that the holder is not believed. The fact that the Gemara does not give this simple answer shows that an ordinary deposit claim does not automatically defeat third-party credibility. The same follows from Tosafot there. Tosafot ask what the Sages gained by requiring the agent in the Diaspora to say, "It was written and signed before me." The husband can still contradict him and say, "I gave it to you merely for safekeeping." Tosafot answer that those who require the declaration even within one locality hold like R. Chisda that the third party is believed. But according to Shach, the husband could simply say, "I gave it to you as a complete deposit and not under any conditional divorce arrangement," and even R. Chisda would agree. Tosafot's answer would accomplish nothing. The plain text therefore contradicts Shach. Agudah and Tosafot write explicitly concerning an item normally lent or rented that if the owner says, "I gave it to the third party for safekeeping," the third party is believed. It is impossible to read their words as meaning a special conditional third-party arrangement. Their language is straightforward. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 39, part 2, cites Ramban that if one says to another, "This vessel in your hands is mine; I deposited it with you," and the holder says, "It belongs to so-and-so; let him come and take his property," the holder does not even owe an oath. The Geonim rule that he is believed without an oath because he has the law of a third party. This directly contradicts Shach's premise. There is, admittedly, a responsum of Maharam in Mordechai, Perek Chezkas, where Reuven claimed that he deposited a belt and household articles with the defendant, and the defendant answered that they belonged to the heirs of his wife because his wife had deposited them with him. Maharam required the defendant to swear that his statement was true. That sounds unlike Ramban. It requires study. The case may differ because the defendant had some personal connection to the heirs and therefore some interest, or because Maharam follows those who require an oath when the entire third-party status is denied. But it cannot erase the explicit contrary ruling of Ramban. Likewise, a responsum of Rosh, klal 13, siman 15, concerns one who deposited books with his sister's son and died. The nephew said the deceased had instructed him to sell them and devote the proceeds to charity. Rosh required the nephew to swear that the deceased had indeed so instructed him. At first this seems to contradict Ramban's rule, because the nephew is not keeping the books for himself but says they are to go to charity. But the charity there means poor people, which is like our ordinary communal charity. As a resident of the city, the nephew benefits from the charitable fund and is therefore considered interested, just as residents of a city cannot serve as judges concerning their own communal charity. Since he has an interest, ordinary third-party credibility falls away and he is believed only through a migo such as "there was never a deposit" or "the goods were lost," which requires an oath. The case therefore does not disprove Ramban. Rambam in Hilchot Gerushin also simply records the case, "the husband says I gave it for safekeeping," and rules that the third party is believed, with no suggestion of Shach's qualification. There is therefore no basis for saying that the third party is believed only if the owner admits a conditional third-party arrangement. Shach seems simply not to have seen Ramban's explicit ruling in Baal HaTerumot. Had it been before him, he could not have written that perhaps Ramban also agreed with his approach. Shach further attempts to distinguish a claim of loss. He argues that even if one who admits depositing an item cannot defeat the third party by saying, "It was only for safekeeping," the owner should at least be believed to say, "I never entrusted you at all; it fell from me and you found it." He draws support from discussions of an agent where the husband denies appointing him. In my opinion that too is untenable. In general, we do not suspect a document found in another's possession to have fallen from its owner, as explained above in siman 50 concerning a note written "from you." If we do not ordinarily suspect loss there, why should a third-party case be weaker? The mere fact that the owner now claims loss cannot create a concern that halachah normally rejects. There is also no basis to distinguish theft or other similar claims. If the item stands in the third party's possession and he gives a coherent account of the entrustment, ordinary law does not assume that he stole it merely because the owner now says so. This is especially true where the holder's conduct is consistent with a third-party role and he claims no personal ownership. Shach seeks proof from the laws of an agent for a get. He assumes the husband can deny that the bearer is an agent and claim that the get fell from him. But the authorities themselves were uncertain how far that claim goes, and Beit Yosef does not rule as Shach assumes. Rosh, responsum klal 45, discusses whether a delivery agent in the Diaspora must bring witnesses establishing his agency. Rosh initially suggests that perhaps he does, because if the husband later says, "I did not appoint this man," the agent's statement "it was written and signed before me" proves authenticity of the get only if he is first established as an agent. If he is not an agent, his declaration is merely one witness and cannot validate the document. Beit Yosef gives two possible resolutions. The first is that Rosh later agreed with Ramah that once the get is authenticated, the husband cannot deny the agency. The second is that perhaps the special enactment concerning an agent in the Diaspora itself gives the bearer enhanced credibility.
Therefore, the first answer of Beit Yosef is the correct one, as Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer siman 142, seif katan 15, and as appears from Rama. Even Rosh agrees with Ramah that in Eretz Yisrael, or whenever the get is independently authenticated, the bearer is believed regarding his agency. There was no separate rabbinic enactment specially believing an agent as such. The rule follows ordinary law. When the parties are in different cities, the husband cannot deny the agency, because we do not suspect that the get fell into the bearer’s hands, and the ordinary concern that it was given merely as a deposit also does not apply where the "if so" reasoning is unavailable. Therefore, whether in Eretz Yisrael or the Diaspora, once the get is independently authenticated the husband cannot simply deny that the bearer is his agent. Rosh's concern in the Diaspora was narrower. There the get is not independently authenticated. Its validation rests solely upon the agent's declaration that it was written and signed before him. But that declaration is legally effective only from an authorized delivery agent. An ordinary person cannot validate a get merely by giving such testimony; he would be only one witness. Therefore, if the husband says, "I never appointed him and the get is forged," the very foundation for treating him as an agent collapses. His declaration cannot authenticate the get until his agency is established, and his agency cannot be proved from a get whose authenticity is itself uncertain. That circular concern explains why Rosh considered requiring proof of agency. Once independent authentication exists, none of this remains. The husband cannot say the get fell from him, because ordinary halachah does not suspect such loss. Nor can he undermine the bearer through a general theft claim. This is the straightforward law. This also explains Tur in Even HaEzer siman 141. Tur writes that an agent for delivery should be appointed before witnesses, and in Eretz Yisrael the bearer may need to validate the written authorization, while in the Diaspora he need not. At first this sounds as though in Eretz Yisrael the husband can deny the agency. Bach and Perishah already note the tension with Tur in siman 142, where he rules like Ramah that the agent is believed. The explanation is that in Eretz Yisrael, if the bearer wants to avoid any later need to authenticate the get should the husband challenge it as forged, he can instead authenticate his written authorization. Once that is validated, his agency is certain. In the Diaspora there is no need for such a procedure because in any event he must say, "It was written and signed before me," which already supplies the necessary authenticity. There is therefore no contradiction in Tur. We need not invent an unmentioned enactment dividing Eretz Yisrael from the Diaspora, nor say that the agent's formula is somehow stronger than full independent authentication of the get. No such distinction appears in the Gemara. This point has major practical consequences, and Beit Shmuel was uncertain in several places. The proper rule is as stated: by ordinary law, once the document is authenticated, the husband cannot deny the agency merely by claiming loss or theft. Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Ishut chapter 3, also supports this. It says that once kiddushin money or a get reaches the woman's hand, the husband can sometimes deny the agency because the third-party item is no longer in the agent's possession. The implication is that while the item remains in the agent's possession, the husband cannot deny the agency. Beit Shmuel in siman 141, seif katan 13, also notes that Hagahot Maimoniyot does not accept concern for loss in this context. This is directly against Shach. One could still attempt to say that the laws of gittin are different. Maggid Mishneh, Hilchot Gerushin 12:3, writes that a husband is not believed to say, "The get fell from me," even where he has a migo that he could have said the document was forged, because laws of prohibition are not the same as monetary law. Above in siman 46 I explained that one reason may be that a husband is especially careful not to lose a get. Perhaps Rosh therefore rejects concern for loss only with a get, while monetary documents remain different. That is forced. We already rule in siman 50 that ordinary monetary documents are not suspected to have fallen merely because someone raises such a possibility. There is no source for assuming Rosh would nevertheless create a special concern for loss precisely against a third party. There is also strong evidence from Rosh's own responsa. In klal 105, siman 3, Reuven sued Dona Velidira. A promissory note that Reuven held against his grandmother, Dona Lidisia, had been lost and later came into Dona Velidira's hands. She returned the note to the grandmother. Reuven demanded compensation for the loss she caused. Dona Velidira answered that the grandmother herself had deposited the note with her subject to a condition and that she returned it in accordance with that condition. Rosh ruled that she must swear that the facts were as she said. He added: she cannot be exempted from the oath merely on the ground that a third party is believed without an oath, because the third-party matter is no longer in her possession and she therefore has the law of any other person. This is decisive. Rosh says that had the deposited matter still been in her possession, she would have had full third-party credibility, even though Reuven expressly denied ever appointing her and said the note had been lost from him and merely found by her. Why would Rosh then exempt her even from an oath? Clearly he does not accept Shach's loss theory. There are two ways to reconcile this with another Rosh responsum, klal 68, siman 22, cited earlier, where Reuven, Shimon, and Levi were in the same city. Levi claimed that Reuven gave him a note to tear and return to Shimon, while Reuven denied that arrangement. One possible explanation, suggested somewhat by Perishah, is that even if we reject concern for loss and believe Levi concerning the physical note, his third-party power extends only over the note itself. Suppose Levi says, "I was told to tear it." Then we believe him that the note should not be used. But remove the document. Reuven can still sue Shimon personally and say: "True, I willingly instructed Levi to destroy the note, but I never forgave the debt itself. I merely chose to abandon the written evidence. You still owe me the underlying money as an oral debt." If Levi additionally testifies that Reuven forgave the underlying debt, Levi is only one witness concerning money that is no longer embodied in an item under his control. Since Shimon himself admits he never paid, the debt remains. If Reuven had admitted appointing Levi as a third party, we could reason that by entrusting the note with power to hand it to Shimon, Reuven intentionally placed himself in a position where Shimon could later boldly claim payment, and therefore he must have intended the full arrangement. But where only Levi says he was appointed and the item no longer remains under his control, his special credibility cannot extend to extinguishing the underlying oral debt. This somewhat accords with Perishah.
But a more straightforward and satisfying explanation is available from the Gemara itself. R. Huna holds that where the husband says, "I gave it only as a deposit," and the third party says, "for divorce," we apply the argument, "if it were really so, he would have given it directly to the wife." Tosafot explain the challenge from the baraita "a third party is believed" as concerning one city, where that same argument applies. The Gemara answers that monetary matters differ because they are capable of waiver. Rashi and Tosafot explain: by trusting the third party and giving the item into his hands, the owner effectively accepted the consequences if the third party later says it belongs to the other person. Rosh can understand the "if it were so" argument as especially powerful. In a context where it applies, it does more than allow the owner to say, "I merely deposited it." It can even support a claim that the item fell from him and was found by the supposed third party. The reasoning is: if the third party's account were true, why did the owner not simply give the item directly to the beneficiary? The circumstances strongly suggest the holder's story is false. Once we have that strong indication, we consider all plausible ways the holder might have acquired possession, including accidental loss, even though in ordinary cases we do not suspect loss. This resolves a conceptual difficulty. If R. Huna admits that entrusting a third party ordinarily grants him special credibility, why does the "if it were so" argument defeat him? The owner really could have deposited the item and intentionally trusted the holder to later determine its disposition. The answer is that in the particular factual setting where direct delivery to the beneficiary was natural and easy, the owner's failure to do so creates such strong contrary evidence that he may say not only "deposit" but even, "I never trusted you at all; perhaps it fell from me." The normal inference of entrustment is displaced. This also explains the baraita. The baraita says, "the third party says this and one litigant says that," meaning both sides admit the item was intentionally placed with the third party; they dispute only what he was to do with it. In that case monetary matters are capable of waiver and the third party is believed. The baraita does not address a claim that the item fell and was never entrusted at all. According to R. Huna, then, there is a distinction even in monetary law. If the owner admits, "I deposited it with you," but says the purpose was different, the holder is believed because monetary rights can be waived. If the owner says, "I never entrusted you; it fell from me," and the circumstances support him through the "if it were so, I would have given it directly" argument, then the owner is believed, because there was never any act of entrustment from which waiver could arise. This is why Rosh, who rules like R. Huna because R. Huna was the teacher of R. Chisda, can distinguish cases. In different cities, or even in one city where the "if it were so" reasoning does not fit, as in the Dona Velidira case where the item was allegedly deposited conditionally for later disposition, we do not suspect loss and the third party is believed. Had the third-party matter still been in Dona Velidira's possession, Rosh would have exempted her even from an oath. In the klal 68 case, however, all three lived in one city and Levi said Reuven gave him the note simply to tear and return to Shimon. There the question screams: if Reuven truly meant to deliver the note to Shimon, why did he need Levi at all? He could have handed it directly to Shimon. Therefore the "if it were so" reasoning applies, the third-party claim is weakened, and Reuven's denial can prevail. This understanding also clarifies Baal HaTerumot. Baal HaTerumot cites authorities who hold that if the owner denies the third-party status entirely, the holder is not believed. He proves the opposite from the dispute concerning the husband saying "deposit" and the third party saying "divorce," and then notes that Rif rules like R. Chisda. Why does he need to invoke Rif's ruling? In monetary matters, even R. Huna admits that rights can be waived. The answer is exactly this. The disputed case is not where the owner merely says "deposit"; it is where he says, "It fell from me," in a setting where the "if it were so" argument supports him. R. Huna would then allow the owner to deny entrustment. R. Chisda rejects the "if it were so" argument altogether, and therefore does not create concern for loss. Baal HaTerumot cites Rif's ruling like R. Chisda in order to show that we do not accept such a denial. Rosh, by contrast, follows R. Huna and therefore does recognize the distinction. This is clear and correct for practical halachah. Shach also brings a supposed proof from Tosafot concerning items normally lent or rented. Tosafot say that if the owner claims, "I deposited it with him," the third party is believed, but if he says, "I lent it," the holder is not believed. Shach treats borrowing as comparable to loss. I do not understand the comparison. Loss is uncommon. Borrowing an item that is normally lent is the most ordinary thing imaginable. Rashba writes explicitly in his chiddushim to Gittin: even according to R. Huna, the only type of owner claim that ordinarily deserves weight is one such as deposit, which people commonly do. But claims of loss and similar uncommon events are not suspected even according to R. Huna. This directly opposes Shach's comparison. More importantly, an item normally lent is by definition one whose lending is common. How can that teach anything about a rare loss claim? Tosafot's distinction is instead based on entrustment. If a person willingly deposits property with another, we say: you must have considered him trustworthy; by placing it in his control, you accepted his handling of the item. It is as though you said, "I trust whatever you later say concerning its disposition." But with an item that people routinely lend or rent, the owner may lend it even to someone he does not deeply trust. As Tosafot Bava Batra 45 explain, one cannot demand witnesses every time one lends ordinary objects without damaging one's relationships and reputation among neighbors. Ramban adds that a person may lend because he expects the borrower to lend him something tomorrow, so the decision does not reflect a special judgment of reliability. Therefore, lending does not imply the same extraordinary entrustment. This is why the third party is weaker against a borrowing claim involving an item normally lent. That is the meaning of Tosafot and it actually supports the opposite of Shach. If not for the fact that lending such items does not demonstrate trust, the third party would be believed. A rare claim of accidental loss certainly cannot be equated with this ordinary borrowing pattern. Accordingly, the correct law is as follows. Ordinarily, a person who holds an item as third party is believed even if the owner says, "I deposited it merely for safekeeping," and, in a setting without the special "if it were so" presumption, even if the owner says, "It fell from me and you found it." We do not normally suspect loss. Where the factual circumstances themselves generate the strong R. Huna inference that if the holder's story were true the owner would have acted directly, and the owner therefore denies ever entrusting the item, Rosh's view permits that denial. Since Rif and Baal HaTerumot follow R. Chisda and reject that inference, there is a genuine dispute in such a case. But Shach's broad rule stripping the third party of credibility whenever the owner denies appointment has no foundation.
He is believed. The wording implies that he is believed even without an oath. Shach cites many authorities who require the third party to take an oath when the owner contradicts his account. It is true that Responsa Maharam and Hagahot Maimoniyot contain many such opinions. See Hagahot Maimoniyot there. It is not entirely clear, however, whether Rabbenu Tam, who holds that no oath is required, says so even where the owner denies the entire third-party arrangement. On the other hand, the responsum of Rosh cited above, klal 105, siman 3, strongly implies that had the third-party matter still been in the woman's possession, she would have been exempt from an oath even though the owner denied the entrustment and claimed the note had been lost from him. Baal HaTerumot in the cited gate likewise brings Geonic rulings that if the owner says, "I gave it to you only as a deposit," while the holder says he is a third party, the holder is believed without an oath. Still, I do not know the source for exempting him from an oath in every such case. Baal HaTerumot cites Rav Hai Gaon, who infers from the elderly woman whom R. Nachman believed that no oath was imposed. But there the litigants did not deny her status as third party. Both agreed she had been entrusted between them and disputed only what she was to do. Where the owner says, "I gave it to you merely as a deposit and you breached your trust by handing it to another," why should he not at least be able to impose a rabbinic oath? There is no proof from the get case, because no rabbinic oath applies to gittin. Nor is there proof from the baraita saying that the third party is believed in monetary matters, because the baraita predates the enactment of the rabbinic oath altogether. This provides a strong defense of the authorities cited by Baal HaTerumot who hold that where the owner denies the third-party arrangement, the holder does not have ordinary third-party status. Shach's explanation of their position, that the Gemara's "deposit" really means a conditional third-party arrangement, is very forced, as explained above. There is another, simpler explanation. Those authorities may agree with the responsa that a genuine third party is ordinarily believed. But they also hold that where the owner makes a definite claim that the holder violated an ordinary deposit, the holder must take a rabbinic oath. Until he does so, he is an interested litigant under oath rather than a neutral third party. Baal HaTerumot derives from the principle "these are his witnesses" in Kiddushin that one who is himself subject to an oath is not treated as a qualified third party. A person who must swear in order to defend his own handling of the item is no longer the detached third person whom the parties trusted. This distinction also explains the get sugya. With a get, no rabbinic oath applies, so if the husband says "deposit" and the holder says "divorce," the third party remains believed in monetary-style reasoning because there is no oath transforming him into an interested litigant. Likewise, the Tannaitic baraita concerning money predates the rabbinic oath and therefore treats the holder as an ordinary third party. But after Chazal instituted the rabbinic oath, if the owner says definitively, "You breached my deposit," the holder may have to swear. Under the view that a third party who must swear loses his special status, the result changes. The same reasoning can explain those authorities who rule like the second version of R. Nachman and require a migo for the third party. They may hold that even an acknowledged third party is subject to a rabbinic oath when one litigant definitely says he acted improperly. Once an oath rests upon him, he no longer possesses special third-party credibility. If he has a migo, however, he can prevail as an ordinary litigant despite the oath. Then the baraita and the get case are no difficulty, because again there was no rabbinic oath in those settings. These two explanations may depend on the two versions of R. Nachman's enactment of a rabbinic oath. One version holds that the rabbinic oath was instituted specifically when the defendant says, "I paid," but not where he says, "There was never any debt," absent prior monetary involvement. Under that approach, the first version of R. Nachman can believe the elderly woman without any oath where no preexisting monetary claim against her exists. The other version imposes a rabbinic oath even where there is no prior monetary relationship. Under that version, once the owner definitely accuses the third party of violating the deposit, the holder must swear, and his special status is weakened. This fits well. There is nevertheless some evidence for the lenient view. The statement that "the third party is believed more than both litigants" sounds as though even a single witness contradicting him does not force him into an oath before carrying out the entrustment. If a Torah oath cannot be imposed in that configuration, perhaps Chazal likewise did not institute a rabbinic oath against him. This may be the reasoning of those who exempt him entirely. Still, for practical halachah, when the owner denies the third-party arrangement itself, I remain uncertain whether the holder is exempt from oath. The authorities are divided and the matter does not emerge from doubt. See my kuntres on possession and the laws of kim li concerning uncertainty in oath obligations.
He is believed. Responsa Rosh, klal 105, siman 4, records the following case. Leah deposited with Shimon a note for one thousand gold coins that she owed her son Reuven, instructing Shimon not to deliver the note during her lifetime. After Leah died, her heirs and Reuven came before the third party so that he would carry out the entrustment. The note was produced in beit din and an erasure was found on its reverse. The third party said that the terms of the third-party arrangement had been written there and had accidentally been erased. Leah's heirs claimed that perhaps the erased text recorded payment or some other condition, and the third party had erased it. Rosh answered that we do not suspect an unspecified additional condition that is not mentioned in the note. Nor do we suspect payment so long as the document remains in the third party's possession. In addition, the due date of the note had not yet arrived, and a person does not normally pay within the term. Darkei Moshe and Sema infer from this that a third party is believed concerning an erasure on the back of a note and may say that it merely contained the terms of the entrustment rather than a payment notation or some other condition. Shach and Taz object. A third party has no stronger substantive credibility than the litigant himself would have if the document had already been returned to him. If the note were in the creditor's own hands with an erasure on its reverse, Rosh above in siman 45, seif 23, would invalidate it or at least treat the erasure as suspicious. Why should the third party be stronger than the owner himself? Shach therefore explains the case as one where local practice was not to write payments or conditions on the back of notes. Rosh then ignored those possibilities because of custom, not because of special third-party credibility. This is strained. The essential qualification is missing from Rosh's responsum. More importantly, Rosh says expressly, "We do not suspect payment so long as the note is in the third party's possession." According to Shach, we would never suspect it even if the note were elsewhere. Rosh's wording makes the third-party possession itself relevant. R. Tzvi, in his gloss to Taz, says the third party is certainly believed because both parties appointed him consensually. But that too is not fully clear. The whole question is how far third-party credibility extends. We ordinarily say the holder is no stronger than if he had returned the item to the litigant. Moreover, in Rosh's case both parties did not jointly appoint him. Leah alone deposited the note. Her heirs can therefore say perhaps the note was lost from her and found by Shimon. This incident itself supplies another proof that Rosh does not ordinarily suspect accidental loss, although one could answer that we do not plead for heirs a highly uncommon event. The correct explanation appears from Rashba's view cited above in siman 44, seif 11, and from what I wrote there. Rashba holds that erasures and insertions can be validated even by one witness. Responsa attributed to Ramban, siman 94, likewise states that we do not ordinarily suspect a single witness of falsifying such a matter. A single witness may be believed at least to require an oath, and in some documentary matters even for monetary effect, to identify and authenticate an erasure. Once that is so, the third party here functions at minimum like one witness concerning the erased portion. Even if the special third-party status had ended, he remains a competent witness. All the more so while the matter remains in his possession. He can therefore say that the erased writing was not a receipt or disqualifying condition but merely part of the third-party terms. He is effectively authenticating the erasure. This explains Rosh precisely. "So long as the note is in the third party's possession" means that he remains positioned as a witness concerning what happened to the memorandum. He testifies that there was no payment notation and that the erasure related to something else. His statement has at least the force of a single witness sufficient under Rashba's approach to uphold the writing.
Accordingly, because Mechaber above in siman 44 rules like Raah that one witness is not sufficient to authenticate erasures, he properly omitted this ruling of Rosh. Under Mechaber's halachic framework, the third party's statement is no stronger than one witness for this particular purpose and therefore cannot validate the erasure by itself.
Provided there is no dispute between them, and so forth. Shach at the end of seif katan 5 questions this rule. The source compares the case to the rule, "these are his witnesses," on the theory that once the third party is contradicted he becomes someone who must swear and therefore cannot serve as a witness. Shach asks: there, the witness's testimony itself concerns the disputed money. Here, why should contradiction concerning one part destroy the holder's credibility concerning a separate part about which no one contradicts him? For example, the owner says, "I deposited one hundred," and the third party says, "Only fifty." Why should the third party not be believed concerning the fifty that are undisputed? Sefer Bnei Shmuel and Tiferet Shmuel raise the same problem. One might attempt to answer by distinguishing whether both litigants agree that the third party is lying concerning the disputed amount. If both claimant and defendant agree that he is wrong, his credibility is undermined. Even on the claimant's own version the third party has wrongfully withheld or misdescribed money. How can the original doctrine of entrustment continue? One entrusted an apparently honest person, not someone now shown to be a wrongdoer. If a person was considered trustworthy when appointed as third party and later became known as a thief, his special credibility is surely weakened. If, however, only one litigant knows the third party is lying and the other is uncertain, the situation is different. There may also be a personal interest. If the third party returns the disputed item to one side, the other side may impose an oath upon him; if he follows his own version, he avoids that burden. The identity of the person holding the property can also matter under the rule that one litigant may be easier for him to deal with than another. Therefore some degree of personal interest may exist. Still, the principal issue troubles me. Why not simply require the third party to swear that his account is true, remove the concern of personal interest, and then continue to believe him concerning the third-party arrangement? Baal HaTerumot says that a witness who must swear cannot then serve as a witness, citing Kiddushin, "these are his witnesses." But that rule applies where formal kosher testimony is required. Chazal received a tradition, as Tosafot and Ran explain, that "if he does not tell" and ordinary witness status apply only to a person who is not personally subject to an oath. A witness who must swear lacks the formal category of qualified witness. Third-party credibility, however, does not rest on ordinary witness qualification at all. A relative, a woman, or even an unlearned cattle herder may serve as a third party and be believed because the litigants entrusted him. His credibility is like a party saying, "My father is trusted upon me," not the Torah law of witnesses. If, after swearing away the personal concern, he is no worse than a woman or relative, why should the technical rule that an oath-bearing person is not a formal witness matter? This point is strongly supported by Hagahot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, Responsa Maharam in the Prague printing, Mordechai Perek HaOmer, and many other authorities cited by Shach. They debate whether a third party must take an oath. Those who require the oath never say that by taking it he thereby loses his third-party credibility. The entire discussion assumes that one can both swear and remain the trusted third party. They even infer from the case of the elderly woman whom R. Nachman believed that perhaps no oath was required. No one asks the stronger question: if an oath were required, how could R. Nachman believe her at all? Evidently being an oath-bearing person does not automatically remove the special credibility of a third party. The issue therefore depends on two explanations of the Gemara's rule concerning litigants who are also witnesses. Tosafot and Ran say that a witness who must swear is simply not a person fit for the Torah category of witness. Under that explanation, our case is different because a third party need not be a formal witness at all. A relative or woman is enough. Therefore, after he swears he should retain his third-party credibility. Rashba in Kiddushin gives another explanation. He says a person who must swear is a litigant, a baal davar, and a litigant is not within the category of witness at all. If we extend that concept to third-party law, there is more force to Baal HaTerumot. The very moment the third party becomes an interested litigant whose own conduct is under challenge, he may cease to be the neutral third person whom the parties trusted. Baal HaTerumot's language is precise: once there is a dispute affecting him, "the litigant has returned among them and he is no longer called a third party." Most authorities, however, follow Tosafot and Ran's first explanation. That is why they can require a third party to swear without saying he thereby loses his credibility. Accordingly, where the entire third-party appointment is not denied and the dispute concerns only an amount or detail, the stronger view is that he swears as required and remains believed concerning the entrusted matter. There is also a practical argument. If any owner could destroy a third party's credibility merely by asserting, "I deposited more with you than you admit," the entire institution of third-party entrustment would become meaningless. Every litigant who disliked the third party's statement could manufacture a monetary dispute, force him into an oath, and thereby erase his special credibility. Chazal could not have established a law so easily defeated by unilateral pleading. Therefore, the ruling should be limited as Bach suggests. Where the owner denies the very third-party arrangement, there are already many authorities who hold that the holder has no special third-party status. Add to them the view that because he is now subject to an oath he is an interested litigant rather than a neutral third party. Together these create a strong majority, and his special credibility should not be applied freely. But where the parties admit the third-party arrangement and the only dispute concerns an amount or some collateral detail, the majority view is that an oath does not destroy the entrustment credibility. In particular, Shach and those who agree with him maintain that the "oath-bearing witness" problem applies only to the matter on which he must swear, not to separate undisputed matters. The correct law therefore appears to be that he takes the necessary oath and remains believed concerning the third-party disposition. This conclusion is also the most reasonable administratively. Otherwise, third-party law could always be nullified by strategic allegations. The institution would serve no purpose. Thus the distinction stated above should be followed.
And he produced it before beit din, and so forth. Sema, citing Tur, explains the case as follows: the woman produced the document in beit din without immediately saying anything. Only after it had been authenticated did she say that it was paid. Shach objects. Baal HaTerumot indeed gives this explanation according to the second version of R. Nachman, which does not believe the woman once the document was established before beit din. Baal HaTerumot asks: if she said immediately upon producing it that it was paid, why should she not be believed? There is no stronger migo than this; before the court takes control she could have destroyed or withheld the document. Therefore, he explains that she first produced it and remained silent. After the court had authenticated it, she then said it was paid. At that stage R. Nachman did not believe her. But since we rule like the first version of R. Nachman, Shach argues, we do not need this special factual reconstruction. We can read the incident simply. According to the accepted version, she remains believed even after the document has been established in court because her original entrustment gave her credibility. Still, even those who follow the first version would presumably agree that if she intentionally remained silent until the court completed authentication and only afterward suddenly claimed payment, her behavior could weaken her. Why did she allow the court to authenticate a note she knew was paid? On Shach's reading, then, the two versions of R. Nachman do not really disagree about law in this specially suspicious case. They disagree only about what happened historically: according to one version she asserted payment early enough to retain credibility; according to the other she waited until after authentication. This is difficult. The language of the poskim and Baal HaTerumot treats the two versions as a legal dispute with practical consequence. According to Shach, there would be no difference in law at all, only a different reconstruction of the incident. That is not how the sources read. The Gemara's challenge from the baraita concerning a third party also becomes problematic. Either the baraita describes a person who did not remain silent until after authentication, in which case it does not challenge R. Nachman's ruling in the unusual silent-then-authenticated case. Or it describes the same type of silence and later statement, in which case the challenge should apply equally to the first version of R. Nachman if Shach says both versions agree such conduct destroys credibility. Why does the Gemara refute only the second version? One might try to say that according to Shach the authorities who rule like the second version simply do not read the Gemara's conclusion as a decisive refutation, perhaps changing "refutation" to "difficulty." But this is not a satisfactory reading of Baal HaTerumot. He says the second version cannot be explained except by the case where she was silent until after authentication. His whole reconstruction is part of the legal distinction, not merely a textual possibility. The more likely conclusion is that even in that case, the first version of R. Nachman still believes the third party. The baraita's plain rule that a third party is believed supports this. Her silence while the court independently authenticates the document need not mean she approved the debt. Beit din may have ordered authentication on its own as part of processing the note, under the ordinary instruction, "Authenticate your document and descend to litigation." She did not necessarily request authentication or affirm the note's validity. Therefore her later statement that the note was paid does not inherently destroy her credibility. There is, however, another textual issue. Baal HaTerumot may follow Rashi's explanation of "established before beit din." Rashi in Sanhedrin understands it to mean that the document already bore a formal court certification. Rashbam in Bava Batra 159 similarly interprets "established before beit din" as a note bearing a court authentication. Tosafot challenge Rashi: why does a court certification prevent the woman from burning or withholding the physical note? The certification itself does not remove the paper from her control. To answer, Rashi's case must mean that she produced the note to beit din and stood silently while the court validated it, perhaps even writing a formal henpek on the instrument. Only after that public judicial action did she say it was paid. Under that reading, Baal HaTerumot's explanation has force within Rashi's system. But Tosafot reject Rashi's interpretation. They explain "established before beit din" to mean only that the court saw the document in a manner that removed the current migo of burning it, not that any formal authentication occurred. According to Tosafot, there is no legal root for saying that the third party's silence while formal certification was performed destroys credibility, because formal certification is not even part of the case. Had Tosafot accepted Baal HaTerumot's law, they could easily have defended Rashi: the reason court certification matters is precisely that the woman stood by and allowed the court to create a formal authentication before asserting payment. Since Tosafot instead reject Rashi and explain the phrase merely through court sight of the note, it appears they do not accept this special rule. Perishah recognizes the difficulty and tries to reinterpret Tur's phrase "until it was authenticated" as meaning only that the document became fully established through the court's possession or recognition, not that a formal henpek was written. But this itself shows how difficult it is to align Tur with Tosafot's language. Therefore, as Shach says, this ruling requires further study. I agree with his conclusion that the law is uncertain, though not for the same reason he gives. The real problem is that the special silence-until-authentication rule emerges only from Rashi and Baal HaTerumot's construction of the rejected version of R. Nachman, while Tosafot's interpretation gives it no support. One should therefore be cautious about using it to defeat an otherwise valid third party.
And likewise if the third party died, and so forth. Shach correctly explains Rambam's source for this ruling. Rambam follows Tosafot's interpretation of the baraita concerning a receipt found in a third party's possession: both the loan note and the receipt are in the third party's hands. Tosafot ask why the receipt is needed at all. If the living third party holds the note, he can simply say it was paid, because he has a migo to burn or conceal the note. Rambam therefore establishes the case where the third party has died. His heirs now possess both papers but do not know the history. Without the receipt, the mere fact that the note was found among the deceased third party's papers would not establish that it was paid. The receipt is therefore necessary. Shach adds that Tosafot themselves can be understood this way. That is difficult. Tosafot ask why the Gemara's baraita is a refutation; perhaps it concerns a case where the note had not yet been established before beit din and the third party has a current migo. They answer that the clause should be read parallel to the next clause concerning a receipt written after the signatures, which implies the note had already been established. If Tosafot meant the case of a deceased third party, there is no stronger absence of a current migo. The third party is dead, and the heirs innocently bring the papers to beit din and ask what to do. There is no present migo at all. The entire point is that the original third party had a migo during his lifetime and therefore had been trusted from the outset. This would directly support the first version of R. Nachman without Tosafot needing the parallel-clause argument. Indeed, this may be a proof for Rambam's interpretation. It neatly answers Tosafot's question: the baraita necessarily concerns a situation with no present migo, because the third party died. Yet the receipt found in his possession is still effective, showing that original entrustment can create credibility beyond the life of the current migo. Perhaps Tosafot agree with Rambam's substantive law but do not interpret the baraita that way because the phrase "found in the possession of a third party" sounds like the third party is alive. If he had died, one would expect the baraita to say it was found among the papers of a third party or in the hands of his heirs. Therefore Tosafot retain the living-third-party reading and use the parallel clause to show the case extends beyond a current migo. There is, in any event, no serious difficulty with Rambam's law. One might ask why a receipt was deposited at all if the living third party could always say the note was paid. The answer is simple. The parties may have wanted a written memorial of the arrangement in case the third party died, exactly as in Rambam's case. The receipt tells the heirs what the deceased knew. Mordechai also notes that documents are often left with a third party as a record for future contingencies. Therefore the presence of a receipt is meaningful and the ruling is correct.
