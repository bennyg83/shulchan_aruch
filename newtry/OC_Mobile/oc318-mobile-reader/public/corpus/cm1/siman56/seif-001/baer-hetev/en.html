Believed. The Sema explains that a third party entrusted with the instrument is believed against both lender and borrower with the force of two witnesses. If actual witnesses contradict him, however, the witnesses are believed over him, as the Tur and Mechaber state in seif 2. The Shach writes that his status as equivalent to two witnesses means that if one witness contradicts him, the party supported by the third party does not have to swear a Torah oath against that witness, and indeed need not even take a shevuat heset. We do not treat the case as one witness against another, which would ordinarily require the defendant to take heset under siman 87, seif 6. Rather, the third party is treated as two witnesses until two actual witnesses contradict him. See the Shach's extended analysis of the Gemara and Tosafot in Zeh Borer and his disagreement with Maharsha. See also Responsa Rashdam, siman 193.
To swear. Meaning that the third party is believed without taking an oath. Sema.
They wrote. Meaning that the parties wrote down the terms of the deposit or arrangement, but that writing was later lost, or a new matter arose that was not recorded there. The third party is still believed, because we assume the writing was intended only as a memorandum. Sema.
Believed. If the written terms of the arrangement state that the document was deposited with him only on a particular condition, he is not believed to say that there was an additional earlier condition that was omitted from the writing. He is believed only if he says that after the writing was prepared the parties added another condition. If he says the omitted condition already existed beforehand, he is not believed, because even a litigant who wrote such terms in his own handwriting could not later contradict his writing. The third party has no greater credibility than the litigant himself. Shach.
Still. If the lender placed his document with a third party and empowered him to make a compromise or issue a ruling by a specified time, and that time passed without the third party doing so, he must return the document to the lender. So writes the Rashba in a responsum cited by Darkei Moshe, siman 54, seif 12, where the matter is discussed at length. Sema.
Denied. The Tur writes: not only if the owner says, "I did not appoint you as a third party; I merely gave it to you for safekeeping," is his denial ineffective, because once he trusted him with possession the custodian is believed regarding the terms. Even if the owner says, "You stole it from me," and the holder says, "No, you appointed me as a third party between you and so-and-so," the holder is believed. See siman 41, seif 10, where the Tur also says that if the owner claims, "It fell from me after I paid it and you found it," he is not believed. The reason, according to the Sema, is that we do not ordinarily suspect people of outright theft, nor do we fear an uncommon accidental loss. The Shach finds this law highly problematic: if so, no one's property would be secure, because anyone could steal another's property, claim to be a third party, conspire with someone else, and divide it with him. The Shach cites many poskim who disagree and concludes that the primary law follows them: a third party is not believed when the true owner directly denies having entrusted it to him. See his lengthy discussion.
Both of them. The Rosh writes similarly regarding an erasure found on the document. The Shach explains that this concerns a circumstance in which, had the instrument been in the lender's possession, it would not be invalidated by that erasure or scraping, such as a locale where people do not customarily write payments or conditions on the reverse. In a place where payments are customarily written on the back and some poskim invalidate a document bearing such an erasure, as discussed in siman 44, seif 6, the same defect applies when the document is held by a third party. The third party has no greater power than the litigant himself. Even according to poskim who say that if at some point the third party had the power to make the document valid this can help, who says he had that power here? Perhaps the condition or payment notation existed from the outset. See the Taz, who raises strong objections to this responsum of the Rosh and says it should not be relied upon for practical halacha. Chacham Tzvi in his glosses there resolves the difficulty.
Interested. Even if there are witnesses that the parties appointed him as third party, or both parties admit that fact, if he now has a personal interest he must swear against the claimant who contradicts him. This is unlike the Bach. Shach. The Taz agrees.
Believed. The Shach writes that Rama's wording is awkward. If the holder lacks the special credibility of a third party but has an ordinary migo, then although the migo may help his own claim, he remains an interested person and cannot serve as a witness. Rama's word "believed" therefore does not mean the same thing as the word "believed" used by the Tur and Mechaber regarding a true third party. A practical difference is that the opposing party may still demand an oath. The holder's support does not count as a supporting witness that exempts from an oath.
To make him swear. The Sema explains this as an oath imposed on the third party regarding the contradiction between their accounts, comparing it to siman 93, where partners and agents may be required to swear even on a doubtful claim when suspicion exists. The Shach finds this astonishing. Why should an ordinary third party swear on a doubtful claim? Agents are different because they may rationalize taking extra compensation, and some opinions there require an oath only where the agent was paid. Even according to those who require an unpaid agent to swear, he was handling the owner's property in a purchase or sale and may have profited more than he admits. Here, why would mere possession of another's property cause a person to rationalize denying its owner? The Shach therefore explains the case differently. Suppose the third party received one hundred twenty to deliver to the lender in payment, while the lender says he received only one hundred. The third party must swear to the borrower regarding the remaining twenty. Although the borrower cannot claim with certainty what was actually delivered to the lender, in such a case an oath can be imposed on a doubtful claim, analogous to the shopkeeper's ledger, where workers and shopkeeper swear despite the employer's uncertainty because the two groups contradict each other. Here too the relevant parties contradict each other. Although in that case the oath is taken to collect while here it is taken to be exempt, no distinction is made where the parties directly contradict each other, as explained explicitly in siman 121, seifim 8 and 9. Perhaps Rama himself intended this case, contrary to the Sema.
And produced. The Tur's wording is that even if he was already seen in beit din producing the document from his possession without explanation, remained silent until it was authenticated, and only then said, "It has been paid," he is nevertheless believed. Thus the Sema. The Shach disagrees and leaves the Tur's statement requiring study. See the Taz's discussion.
Wished. The Sema writes that even though the document is now visibly in his hand before beit din, since the parties initially trusted him with it and regarded him as reliable not to tear or burn it or hand it to the wrong party, he remains credible regarding all his statements about it. The Mordechai discusses a third party who burned a document and said the lender instructed him to burn it while the lender denied this. The third party is believed.
A writing. Even if the note is in someone else's handwriting, it may be relied upon if there is some identifying feature showing that it was written with the third party's knowledge or if the lender admits that the third party authorized the note but claims the debt was nevertheless unpaid. Otherwise we must fear that someone else placed the note there, or that the borrower persuaded the third party's heirs to place such a note with the papers. The special rule is that the third party himself is believed because the parties trusted him, not that his heirs receive the same credibility. See below, siman 65, seifim 18 and 19. Shach.
The claimant. The Sema writes that whoever receives the benefit and advantage from the arrangement must pay the third party's fee. The Taz finds this statement surprising. Why should that always be true? On the contrary, the claimant may prefer that the document be given directly to him rather than deposited with a third party. Hagahot Mordechai speaks of the messenger's fee in a case where it was necessary to send a question to the Great Beit Din. Although in siman 14 the Mechaber says both parties pay that messenger's fee, Hagahot Mordechai apparently disagrees. As to the present issue, the straightforward rule is that the person who requests the third-party arrangement pays the fee. Ordinarily we may presume the arrangement benefits both parties.
