An escrow holder, etc. See Tumim. The principal law follows the majority of authorities: even if the escrow holder no longer has a present migo that he could hand the item to the party he says is entitled to it, he can still be believed, provided that originally, when the item was placed with him, he had a meaningful migo to deliver it to that party. By entrusting him under such circumstances, the parties gave him credibility. But where the item delivered into escrow was an unauthenticated deed, even if he had immediately handed it to the lender, the lender would still have needed authentication. The escrow holder's position cannot be stronger than the lender's own. Therefore the deed must be authenticated even while in the escrow holder's hands. In general, an escrow holder is never stronger than the litigant whose rights he asserts.
Witnesses, etc. The Shach holds that if one witness contradicts the escrow holder, the party who deposited the item incurs no Torah oath, even if the escrow holder himself is a relative or otherwise disqualified and therefore cannot serve as a supporting witness. He also says there is no hesset oath. See Tumim. In my view, if the escrow holder is a relative, the party must take a Torah oath against the one opposing witness. If the escrow holder is fit to testify, at minimum a hesset oath remains because we place one witness against the other. This is clear and correct. The rule merely teaches that one opposing witness does not make the status of the escrowed item itself doubtful. The escrow holder still follows the entrusted instructions in delivering it. Afterward, however, the relevant party may have to swear against the opposing witness. See there.
Even if they wrote, etc. See Tumim. If the escrow terms were written down and that writing itself was delivered to the escrow holder together with the escrowed item, the escrow holder is plainly believed even to say something that contradicts the writing, because he could have burned or concealed it. But if the escrow arrangement was written and signed and the writing was delivered to one of the litigants, then if the escrow holder says the terms were different at that very time, such as that the writing says there was no condition but he says there was, everyone agrees he is not believed. If he says a condition was added only afterward, the Shach says he is believed. See Tumim. It requires study whether he can be believed where he now lacks a migo.

Even if he contradicts him, etc. Tur writes that even if the litigant says, "I gave it to you merely as a deposit," or "You robbed it from me," or "It fell from me and you found it," the escrow holder remains believed. The Shach argues at length and says the escrow holder is believed only where the litigant admits that he appointed him as an escrow holder subject to conditions. All the more, according to the Shach, if the litigant says the item fell from him, the escrow holder is not believed, as appears from a responsum of Rosh. See Tumim. I establish that if the litigant says, "I gave it to you as a deposit," even as an unconditional deposit, everyone agrees the escrow holder is believed. Likewise, if he says, "You stole it," the escrow holder is believed, because we do not presume a person to be a thief. If he says, "It fell from me and you found it," the law depends on circumstances. If they live in different towns, or even in the same town where according to the escrow holder's account the item properly belonged in his hands rather than the litigant's, the escrow holder is believed. The argument, "If your story were true, why did you not hand it directly to me?" does not apply. But where they are in the same town and that argument does apply, the matter depends on the two opinions in Even HaEzer 141:55 whether in the analogous case the husband or the third party is believed. The same dispute governs here. If the litigant says, "I lent it to the escrow holder," then for objects not ordinarily lent or rented, he is not believed. For things ordinarily lent, if he claims an actual loan, the litigant can be believed. If he says, "I merely deposited it," however, the escrow holder remains believed even if the item is ordinarily lent, and even where the item was seen in his possession and he lacks a migo. By originally entrusting him, the litigant gave him credibility. A migo by the litigant that he could have claimed "I lent it" does not overcome that entrusted credibility; it is like a migo against witnesses.

If there is an erasure on the back of a deed and the borrower's heirs claim that perhaps a condition or repayment had been written there, while the escrow holder says that the erased writing merely contained the escrow terms, Responsa Rosh appears to say the escrow holder is believed. Sema cites this as practical law. The Shach and Taz object that the escrow holder should have no greater credibility than the litigant himself. See Tumim. Based on what I wrote at the end of siman 44, there is room under Rashba's view to validate erased writing between witnesses. Therefore, where the escrow holder is himself fit to testify, he can be believed to explain the erasure as authenticating the escrow arrangement. See there.
Provided there is no dispute between them, etc. See Tumim. Why should the escrow holder cease to be believed merely because the parties dispute the amount? He should swear that his account is correct. I therefore conclude in accordance with Bach: this limitation applies specifically where the litigant denies the entire escrow relationship and says, "I never made you an escrow holder at all." Even on an uncertain claim. Sema explains this like the rule concerning agents who may be required to swear on an uncertain claim. The Shach objects that agents may rationalize their conduct, whereas what comparable rationalization does an escrow holder have? He therefore explains the case as follows: the borrower delivered one hundred to the escrow holder, who says he gave all one hundred to the lender, while the lender says he received only eighty. The escrow holder may have to pay the missing twenty from his own pocket. Knesset HaChoshen says that in such a case an oath may be imposed, like the rule of a shopkeeper and workers, because the lender's definite statement converts the uncertainty into a meaningful claim. This explanation is correct.
