A shalish. See Shach, who asks why Rava needed the shalish's special credibility when he could simply rely on the fact that a receipt in the shalish's possession was already weakened or that the shalish had a migo. The distinction is this. If the shalish first produces a debt document and only afterward says it was paid, one might say his prior production was an admission that the document was valid. A receipt is different. Before producing the receipt, merely saying, 'I have a valid receipt against Reuven's document,' would not itself prejudice Reuven's document because the court would not rely on an unseen receipt. Therefore such a statement is not treated as an admission that later bars the shalish. When he immediately produces the receipt, his migo that he could have withheld it remains meaningful. More fundamentally, a shalish is believed not merely because of a present migo but because he once had the power to carry out the disposition he now describes. This is the principal basis of a shalish's credibility. Tosafot's alternative answer, that the receipt is not authenticated, must be understood in light of the final talmudic conclusion and does not undermine this distinction.
He is believed like two witnesses. See Shach, who rules that if one witness contradicts the shalish, the shalish need take neither a Torah oath nor hesset. Urim VeTumim objects that if the item had remained in the borrower's own hands, one witness could have generated an oath, and the shalish is believed like two witnesses only concerning disposition of the entrusted item itself. In my view Shach is correct. Where the shalish could have disposed of the instrument in a way that would have exempted the borrower even from an oath, the parties necessarily entrusted him with authority over that consequence as well. If, for example, the document remained in his hands and he said that the lender had waived the debt, the borrower would be exempt even if unaware of the waiver. Since the lender knowingly placed the document in the shalish's hands with such power, he effectively accepted the shalish's statement even for purposes of oaths, stronger than merely saying, 'My father is trustworthy to me.' The reverse is different. The borrower did not necessarily entrust the shalish with power to exempt the lender from an oath. Even if the borrower originally delivered the document to the shalish, the shalish cannot create a situation in which the lender is exempt from an oath where the lender would otherwise have to swear. For example, if the borrower says he paid part earlier, the shalish cannot turn a partially paid document into a fully valid document without the lender's knowledge. Since the shalish had no power to eliminate the lender's oath obligation, he was never entrusted with that authority, and the lender must swear wherever he would have had to swear had the document remained in his own hands.
And even if he wrote it. See Urim VeTumim. If the parties themselves delivered to the shalish a written memorandum stating the terms of the escrow, he is believed while it remains in his hands, because they entrusted him with practical control over it. But if the shalish later delivered that memorandum to one of the litigants, he cannot contradict his own writing unless he has a migo that he returned it or that it was lost through unavoidable accident. His written statement that the item was entrusted on a certain condition is comparable to deposit witnesses. Once the writing is out of his hands and no migo remains, he cannot say that afterward he conveyed it to the other party on some different condition.
He contradicted him. Shach discusses a borrower who says with certainty that the document fell from him and demands its return from the shalish. One might ask why the shalish should have to swear, since even if he wrongfully returned a paid document to the lender he may be exempt from monetary damages. The case is where the document is still in the shalish's hands. The borrower is suing for the return of his own paid document, which has evidentiary value worth a perutah. That creates an ordinary oath, just as one who sues a lender to return a paid document can require an oath. If the shalish already returned it, there may be no oath unless the paper itself was worth a perutah. Still, he can remain an interested witness because if he admits returning it improperly, beit din can place him under ban until he removes the danger he created. Shach also explains why a shalish who is personally being contradicted may no longer testify as a neutral witness. This differs from tenants who hold rent money while ownership is disputed, because the tenants admit the lease and can simply deposit the rent in beit din. Their testimony about three years of possession concerns a matter independent of any accusation against themselves. Here the claimant says directly to the shalish, 'This is my object that you are withholding or wrongfully describing.' The shalish's denial is itself the contested monetary claim. He must swear on that denial and therefore is interested in maintaining his version.
The shalish is believed. Shach writes at length about one who denies the very escrow arrangement and claims the item was merely deposited, fell from him, or was stolen. In my view the sugyot of Sanhedrin and Gittin can be reconciled without a real dispute among the authorities by distinguishing movables from debt documents. A shalish does not merely receive ordinary evidentiary credibility. In movables he acquires a genuine possessory power to hold and dispose of the item according to the agreed condition. Saying, 'I am shalish,' therefore resembles saying, 'I acquired this item for the limited purpose entrusted to me.' Just as an ordinary possessor of movables is believed against claims of loss or theft, the shalish is believed concerning that limited proprietary control. This can remain true even after the item was once shown in beit din, because his authority derives from the entrustment itself rather than merely from a present migo. A debt document is different. The paper may be in his possession, but the lien represented by it is not acquired through possession alone. Ramban explains that one who says, 'I bought this debt document,' is not believed against the named creditor's claim of loss or theft merely from possession of the paper, because the lien remains legally associated with the named creditor absent proof of writing and delivery. The same is therefore true of a shalish. Where the item is a debt document, the named creditor can in appropriate circumstances say it fell from him or was stolen and thereby challenge the claim of escrow. The shalish's physical possession proves only ownership of the paper, not of the lien. This is essentially the Shach's conclusion for documents, while Urim VeTumim is correct for ordinary movables. This framework resolves the difficulties in Sanhedrin. Rav Nachman and Rava dispute particularly the case of a document under an opinion that a debt instrument cannot be acquired by mere delivery. Rav Nachman says that because the shalish has no proprietary hold in the lien itself, he is not believed merely from possession. Rava says that since both parties admit making him shalish, they accepted his determination of the instrument, comparable to accepting an otherwise disqualified relative. In ordinary movables, even Rav Nachman can agree that the shalish's possessory control itself gives him credibility. The same explains Gittin. A receiving agent for a get stands in the woman's place and acquires the get for purposes of divorce. As to that limited acquisition, possession has full legal significance. Therefore the husband's plea that it was merely a deposit does not automatically displace the agent's claim. A delivery agent likewise can have sufficient possessory status in the get itself. Debt documents, whose underlying lien is not acquired through mere physical possession, are uniquely weaker. This also explains the authorities concerning items ordinarily lent. If the owner alleges a loan or rental, the ordinary possessory presumption is weakened because such items commonly come into another person's hands in that manner. The shalish's claim can then require a migo. But with ordinary movables, a claim of escrow is like an ordinary purchase claim and defeats claims of loss, theft, or ordinary deposit. Accordingly, if a shalish holds ordinary movables and is uncertain for whom he holds them, he is effectively a possessor on behalf of both. Where division can be true, they divide. If he later became an established liar, that does not automatically annul the original entrustment: they empowered him to carry out the arrangement even if he might later speak falsely. Likewise, the principal cannot simply retract the shalish's agreed authority after the entrustment has taken effect. Ketzot HaChoshen's suggestion that the principal can retract before the shalish speaks is incorrect. The sources he cites concern a gift not yet acquired or a condition not yet performed, not cancellation of the shalish's already vested authority. The only proper retraction is before the contemplated act has occurred and by removing the entrusted object and transferring it to another trustworthy custodian. So long as the shalish still holds it under the original arrangement, his status remains. The shalish is believed.
The shalish is believed. Sema cites Responsa Rosh that if an erasure or scraping is found on the back of the document, the shalish can say the written condition of the escrow was erased. Shach and Taz discuss this. The principle is that while the shalish still holds the entrusted item, his statement concerning the agreed terms has the special credibility of a shalish. The erasure does not by itself defeat that credibility.
They dispute the amount of money. Shach objects: why should a shalish who is interested regarding one portion cease to be believed concerning a separate portion in which he has no interest? The distinction is between his special status as shalish and ordinary witness testimony. So long as the matter remains within the entrusted transaction and he is personally financially interested in the disputed accounting, his statement is not divided into independent neutral testimony about one part and interested testimony about another. With respect to the disputed monetary amount, he is treated as a litigant and must be evaluated under the ordinary rules of possession, migo, and oath rather than as a fully independent witness.
But where he has a migo, he is believed. If the object is of a type ordinarily lent and witnesses saw it in his hands, the ordinary possessor's migo may disappear. With a debt document, the shalish needs specifically the kind of migo that could defeat the named creditor's claim, because mere physical possession does not establish the lien. With ordinary movables, by contrast, the shalish is believed more readily because his possession itself resembles a purchase claim. A migo helps only where it belongs to the shalish as the effective litigant or where he actually has the power to do what he says. One does not ordinarily grant a neutral witness extra credibility merely because he personally could have lied differently. That distinction will govern the later cases of agents and custodians as well.
A document that was in the hands of, etc. Shach explains Rav Nachman's position as a case where the shalish remained silent until the document was authenticated and only afterward sought to weaken it. That reading is difficult, for then Rava's challenge from the baraita concerning a receipt would not fit. The dispute is better understood as follows. Rav Nachman views the parties' reliance on a shalish as analogous to saying, 'My father is trustworthy to me.' Once the shalish has effectively allowed a valid document to become established, he cannot later reverse his position. Rava treats a shalish more strongly, like the very hand of the parties. Rashba indeed writes that a shalish can be like a litigant himself, so that even his uncertainty can count like the litigant's uncertainty. On that view he can sometimes explain or reverse what he previously left unstated because his authority over the entrusted object persists. This resolves Rava's proof from an unauthenticated receipt. If the shalish's authority were only an ordinary personal credibility undertaking, the creditor's migo of forgery could defeat it as a migo within the very field of the assumed credibility, as in siman 71. Rava's proof shows instead that the shalish was entrusted with substantive control: the parties accepted that the matter should be as he states even if his statement is false. Therefore an ordinary migo proving the creditor's version does not defeat the shalish. This also explains why, according to Rashba, the shalish can sometimes be believed even against conflicting evidence, because his role is not merely that of an ordinary witness.
Likewise, if the shalish died. Urim VeTumim questions Shach's statement that Tosafot likewise speaks where the shalish died. There is no difficulty. If the matter had never been established in beit din during the shalish's lifetime, his written notation made while he still had full shalish authority could itself create the defect in the document, just as though he had said it orally while alive. Tosafot therefore needed to establish that the document had first been recognized as valid while the shalish was alive. Once it was established during his lifetime and he failed to object, Rav Nachman can treat that silence as an admission that it was valid, so his later note or later claim cannot undo it.
