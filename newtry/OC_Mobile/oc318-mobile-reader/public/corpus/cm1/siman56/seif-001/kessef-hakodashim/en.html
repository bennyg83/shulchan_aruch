Seif 1. A document that was held by a third party. If a third party says that a document in his possession was merely a document of trust, but he previously gave a signed writing acknowledging that he held an enforceable obligation, he is not believed. It is as though he never had a migo, especially where his prior writing has the force of testimony investigated in beit din, and all the more where accepting his present claim would involve a prohibition. Where money was deposited with a third party under an agreement, the writing was lost, and there is doubt whether it contained an immediately effective clause or another decisive strengthening, the original owner's presumption over the money determines the case. A deposit remains in its owner's domain, and the third party's hand is legally the hand of the depositor. The same applies to comparable doubts even with movable property. If, apart from the deposit arrangement, there was a completed kinyan in a sale and afterward a doubt arose whether an unavoidable accident or similar event occurred, the matter resembles a sale made without valuation in the owner's absence: it is a case of "I do not know whether I paid," and the sale remains in its established status. If a partner deposited property with a third party concerning a transaction made by handwritten instrument, on condition that if he did not bring specified merchandise by a fixed date the deposited property would belong to the other party, and on the due date only the other partner is present, there may be room to plead that the partner who signed the handwriting already paid, since one may plead payment against an ordinary handwritten obligation. The presumption that a condition requiring an affirmative act was not fulfilled may not be decisive when the obligor is absent, especially because his partner may not know everything he did. Where the condition concerned an exchange of merchandise, the accounts were presumably balanced in value and each party merely preferred the other's goods. In such circumstances forgiveness may be as common as payment, even though ordinarily a claim of forgiveness is weak and needs a migo of payment. Forgiveness or saying "I regard myself as having received" requires little further action. If, however, the document expressly states that when the date arrives and the specified goods have not been brought, the property is to be delivered immediately, then unless the third party knows that the condition was fulfilled he should carry out the written instructions. Where two persons were in dispute and deposited money between them, and one witness supports one side, if that side also has the original ownership presumption in the deposited money, it appears that the money should be returned to him. If neither has such a presumption and, absent evidence, the money would be divided, the supporting witness exempts one party from the oath on his half and leaves the other with a Torah oath regarding the half he would take. In cases where division is impossible and the law would instead be that the property remains deposited until Eliyahu comes, or that whoever is stronger prevails, it is doubtful whether a single witness is enough to cause the money to be returned. Where money or a promissory note was deposited under a condition that constitutes asmachta and the parties later dispute what the condition was, while the third party does not know, no Torah oath and no communal oath is imposed merely because one claimant says the condition was fulfilled. The opposing party can say that according to his understanding the arrangement was an asmachta. Although the third party normally must follow the terms of the deposit, once he knows that the terms themselves are disputed, the claimant is not considered in possession.
