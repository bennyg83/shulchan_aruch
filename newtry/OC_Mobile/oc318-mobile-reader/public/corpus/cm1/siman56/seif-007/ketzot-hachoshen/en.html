A wife who conducts business. The Shach argues that a wife who conducts the household and all of her husband's financial affairs is like a shalish. Since the husband placed all his property under her control, he trusted her. Therefore even if she no longer has a present migo, she can be believed under the law of a shalish. According to what I wrote above, however, the central law of a shalish requires an arrangement in which the parties cannot retract, such as where they said, "Acquire on behalf of both of us," or where the shalish has already delivered his operative testimony. Here, although the husband trusted his wife by placing all his property in her hands, he never told her, "Acquire on behalf of both of us." During his lifetime he could have revoked her authority. Once he dies, the property lies before the heirs, and the personal arrangement of trust terminates. The heirs can stand in his place and revoke what he could have revoked. His death itself is effectively a retraction from an arrangement that had never been made irrevocable. The cases in which a shalish remains believed after the death of the person who deposited the matter with him concern an irrevocable arrangement, such as one where the parties said, "Acquire on behalf of both of us," as Rashba and Hagahot Mordechai explain. Therefore the wife does not automatically acquire the continuing credibility of a shalish merely because she had managed all household finances during the husband's life.
