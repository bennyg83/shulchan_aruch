They are believed through a migo. See Baer Heitev, citing the Shach's answer that there is no difficulty from siman 62 because here the case concerns a wife who controls all of her husband's property and therefore has the status of a third-party custodian. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 57, is dissatisfied with this answer. Had this distinction been intended, the Rosh should have stated it, and the rule would become dependent on imprecise degrees of control. He therefore explains differently. In siman 62 the woman says, or wrote, that property in her possession belongs to others because they deposited it with her or lent it to her. She is never believed in that assertion, even with a migo, because it is functionally equivalent to saying that the property is hers, a claim for which she lacks credibility. Here, however, she says that the property belongs to a particular person because that person deposited it with her husband or lent it to him. In such a case she is believed when she has a migo. Accordingly, there is no difference whether she delivered the property to beit din and at the moment of delivery said it belonged to that person, or delivered it to the heirs and told them, even outside beit din, that it belonged to that person. In either case she is believed. If she delivered it without saying anything and only afterward made the claim, she is not believed. The Rosh's statement at the end of his responsum, cited by the Beit Yosef, that if the money leaves her possession and comes into the hands of the guardian or beit din, beit din should act in accordance with whatever the widow says through her migo, must be understood as referring to what she says at the time of transfer. 'Whatever she says' is present tense, not a future statement made after the transfer. See there, and see Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat.
