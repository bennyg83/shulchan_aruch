Responsa Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 5, discusses a case where Reuven's son borrowed money from Shimon on a note while away from home. When he returned, his father Reuven was angry but promised to pay the debt, and the son thereafter ignored it. After Reuven died, Shimon sued the son orally for the balance, saying that during Reuven's lifetime he had delivered the note to Levi as a third-party custodian on condition that if Reuven paid a certain amount Levi would return the note, but Reuven failed to satisfy the condition and Levi nevertheless returned the note improperly. The son knew only that his father had originally said he would pay the entire debt and later told him that he had in fact paid it. Shav Yaakov writes that if the debt had always been oral, the borrower would have to pay because the loan is certain and repayment doubtful. Although some authorities treat 'my father told me' as a definite claim for purposes of imposing a hesset oath, that does not ordinarily free one from money certainly owed, especially where the father had some personal interest in calming his son after promising to pay. Here, however, the creditor's claim is weakened because there had been a written note and it is no longer in his possession. By his own account he compromised with the borrower's father and placed the note in escrow. His assertion that the condition was not fulfilled and that the custodian released the note improperly is itself highly questionable. Shav Yaakov therefore asks what Levi the custodian says. He rejects the assumption that once the custodian has released the escrow he is never believed. If the custodian says that the opposing party fulfilled the agreed condition and that he therefore delivered the note in accordance with the terms entrusted to him, he is believed. The rule in siman 56 that a custodian is no longer believed after releasing the escrow refers to later disputes over how to interpret ambiguous escrow terms after his role has ended. It does not mean that he cannot testify what he actually did during the escrow in accordance with the very conditions admitted by the depositor. This is also implied by seif 6, where the Rama says that once the custodian returned the item we do not suspect that he acted improperly, and the Shach explains that the recipient is exempt even from a hesset oath because the custodian effectively testifies for him. This follows the Rashba. Shav Yaakov argues at length against the Shach's attempt to distinguish a claim that the custodian erred in the law of asmachta from a claim that he deliberately released the item improperly. On the contrary, where no defect is evident, there is less reason to suspect deliberate wrongdoing than a legal mistake. The Rosh responsum cited by the Shach concerning a woman who had already released the escrow is not proof, because there the very existence or terms of the escrow were contradicted. Shav Yaakov therefore concludes that if Levi is present and says that Reuven fulfilled the condition and that he returned the note for that reason, the son is exempt on his testimony. Even if Levi is absent, there is a strong basis to exempt the son from the creditor's own admission that the note was placed in escrow subject to a condition for its return, because a third-party custodian is presumed to carry out his agency. Shav Yaakov is hesitant, however, to rely on that presumption alone in practice when the custodian cannot be questioned. If Levi is present and admits the creditor's account that he released the note improperly, then if the borrower himself claims with certainty that payment was made and that this was why Levi returned the note, Levi is no longer believed to impose liability after the escrow left his hands. In the actual case, however, the son admits that he himself did not pay and Levi admits wrongdoing. This destroys the presumption that a custodian performs his agency. Although a person ordinarily cannot make himself wicked by his own admission, his admission still weakens the presumption on which his special credibility rested. It is comparable to a creditor whose document grants him credibility but who is later found to have lied in another matter, thereby losing that special credibility. Thus the son must pay, though Shav Yaakov would require the creditor to take a hesset oath before, or at least after, payment, because the son's statement based on his father's report has strong corroborating circumstances. Regarding Baer Heitev's further citation of the Shach, that if the custodian now restores the matter to its former position his credibility returns, Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 3, siman 149, explains that he does not regain the status of two witnesses that he had while the escrow was in his hands. At most he returns to the status of one witness, as is also clear from the Shach's own later words and his summary rules of escrow. Shevut Yaakov adds that if the custodian disobeyed an express order of beit din in releasing the escrow, beit din may disqualify him entirely from testimony in that matter as a penalty. See there.
