And if he produces it, and so forth. This law requires clarification. Suppose Reuven deposited one hundred with Shimon as a third party to give to Levi, and Shimon delivered it to Levi in accordance with the entrustment. Reuven now challenges Levi. Is Shimon still believed like two witnesses in Levi's favor, or do we say that once the deposited item has left Shimon's hands, he loses all special credibility and becomes like any ordinary witness? Rashba, in his chiddushim to Gittin 64b, cites Raavad that an ordinary third party remains believed in favor of the person to whom he delivered the item even though the item is no longer in his own hands, because he could have returned it to that recipient while it was still under his control. Rashba gives another reason. Once he delivered the item, at the moment of delivery he had full third-party credibility. Therefore, even if he did not deliver it before witnesses, the recipient's present possession itself proves that the third party handed it to him in the claimed legal capacity. We effectively have testimony from the very fact of possession that the third party acted properly. Rashba then explains the contrary rule that "when the third-party matter is no longer in his possession, he is not believed." That applies where the third party now testifies against the person to whom he delivered it. For example, he gave the item to Levi and later says, "My delivery was subject to a condition," while Levi denies that condition. Since Levi is now in possession, the third party has no special power to remove it from him; at most he is like an ordinary single witness. The same is true where it is known that Levi took the item without the third party's consent. Thus, where Shimon delivered to Levi and afterward testifies in Levi's favor that the delivery was proper, his statement is treated as if the deposited item were still in his own hands. Maharam in Mordechai, Perek HaMekabel, and Responsa Maimoniyot, Sefer Mishpatim, siman 44, say similarly. A third party remains believed concerning what he did while the entrusted item was in his hands even if it is no longer there. We presume he carried out his agency properly, because an agent is presumed to perform his mission and we do not assume he changed instructions merely in order to sin without personal benefit. The Tosefta saying that a third party is not believed once the item is no longer in his possession concerns a later dispute about what the original conditions had been. After time has passed, he may not remember precise old terms. But he is believed retrospectively to say, "What I did while it was in my hands was done lawfully," because at that time the matter was immediately before him and he was careful to act correctly. Hagahot Mordechai in Perek HaMekabel asks from the baraita in Perek Zeh Borer, "If there are no witnesses on it and it comes from the hand of a third party," which sounds as though the item must still be physically in the third party's hand. It answers that the same law applies even after the item has left his hand. The baraita's formulation was needed only for the later clause teaching the additional case where the receipt appears after the signatures of the note. Agudah likewise writes explicitly that the third party remains believed after the item has left his hand. Their intent is plainly the same as Rashba's: he remains believed in favor of Levi, to whom he delivered the object. Against Levi he is no longer believed once Levi possesses it and denies the alleged limitation. Rambam, Hilchot Gerushin 12:11, is also explicit. If a get is in the woman's hand and she says, "This agent gave it to me," and the agent says, "Yes, I gave it to her, and the husband gave it to me for divorce," while the husband says, "I gave it to him merely for safekeeping," the agent is believed and the woman is divorced. Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 141, seif 56, cites this without qualification. Thus, even though the get is now in the woman's hand and no longer in the third party's hand, the agent is believed regarding the lawful character of his prior delivery. Rambam rules like R. Chisda; all the more so in monetary matters, where even R. Huna generally concedes that rights can be waived. Rif also appears to support this. He explains "the husband says deposit and the third party says divorce" as referring to a delivery agent. The get must already be in the woman's hand, because otherwise the husband can simply revoke the agency. Yet R. Chisda says the third party is believed. It would be strained to say the case is specifically one where the third party first announced before witnesses, while the get was still in his hand, that he held it for divorce and only then delivered it. The Gemara states the matter generally, without such a qualification. Maharsha explains why Tosafot preferred to interpret "third party says divorce" as an acceptance agent rather than as a delivery agent: once the get has left the third party's possession, he may no longer be believed. At first glance Maharsha's statement is difficult, because Tosafot elsewhere say that the agents who require saying "it was written and signed before me" are not troubled by the husband's later claim that the get was merely deposited, since they follow R. Chisda that the third party is believed. But there the get is obviously already in the woman's hand. The resolution is that the Gemara's unqualified statement could include cases where no witnesses heard the third party declare the nature of the entrustment before delivery. Tosafot did not want to explain the core dispute through a delivery agent because the question whether he remains believed after delivery is itself not simple according to all views. In the Diaspora agency case, however, the woman necessarily proves that the agent made the required declaration before beit din, "it was written and signed before me." That declaration itself is made before final delivery and thus records his account of the entrustment while he still functions as agent. Once the woman establishes that, the later husband's denial is weaker. Therefore Tosafot can rely on R. Chisda there even if they hesitate about the unqualified general case. Nevertheless, Maharsha's reading is not compelled. Rambam clearly holds that even after delivery the third party remains believed. He still interprets the Gemara's case as an acceptance agent, as Maggid Mishneh explains. There can be other reasons for that interpretation. Ran offers another distinction. R. Huna's "if it were so" argument depends on the assumption that an agent for receipt should have immediately completed the divorce rather than keep the get. In a delivery agency, once the agent delivered the get to the woman, he did exactly what he was supposed to do. Therefore the "if it were so" reasoning is weaker. Under this distinction, after a delivery agent completes delivery he can remain believed even though the item has left his hands. This provides another answer to Maharsha. Tur in Even HaEzer siman 141, citing Rambam, also understands the rule that even when the get is in the woman's hand the agent can be believed. There is therefore significant support for applying the same principle in monetary law. We find a possible dissent in Mordechai, Perek Sheni of Kiddushin, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Ishut chapter 3. They say that an agent for delivery in kiddushin or divorce does not need written authorization or witnesses, but if the principal later denies the agency after the kiddushin money or get has been delivered, the result is doubtful and we rule stringently, because the entrusted item is no longer in the agent's hands. Shulchan Aruch cites this in Even HaEzer siman 141, seif 11. This can be limited. It may concern a case where there are no witnesses before us showing that the agent delivered the item as the principal's agent. If witnesses had heard his declaration while the item was still in his hand, then the rule would be like a third party who stated the terms of the entrustment before delivery, and his statement would stand. In kiddushin by money, witnesses are of course needed to see the money given for kiddushin. Yet even there, the difficulty is not only whether the agent delivered but whether the principal appointed him to effect kiddushin. Mere possession of another person's money does not prove he was authorized to marry a woman on that person's behalf. This explains why the issue can be doubtful even while the money once stood in his hand. I have discussed this elsewhere in my chiddushim to Even HaEzer. One can also explain Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot like Raavad. Their reason for believing a third party after delivery is that the recipient could return the item to him, thereby restoring his control. That logic fits an acceptance agent: if the woman returns the get to the agent, his possession again carries third-party force. It is weaker for a delivery agent. If the woman returns the get to him, the husband can immediately revoke his agency; the return itself may defeat the supposed completed delivery. Thus the phrase "the third-party matter is no longer in his hand" has special significance with delivery agency. This also reconciles Shulchan Aruch's two rulings. In seif 11 he cites Hagahot Maimoniyot that the husband can deny a delivery agency after the item has left the agent's hand. In seifim 55 and 56 he follows Rambam that an acceptance agent remains believed even when the get is in the woman's hand. These need not conflict. The latter case concerns an agent for receipt, where the woman's hand can be treated as the agent's continuing legal hand and the husband has no power to revoke after receipt. The former concerns an agent for delivery, where returning the get to the agent would simply restore the husband's power of revocation. This may also be what Maharsha intended in reading Tosafot. When Tosafot say "the third party says for divorce," they choose an acceptance agent because in that case even after the get reaches the woman the third party retains legal credibility. A delivery agent is more problematic once actual delivery has ended. Rama's gloss in Even HaEzer, seifim 55 and 57, appears to understand Rosh as holding more broadly that once the third-party item has left the agent's hands he is no longer believed. Beit Shmuel, seif katan 79, correctly objects that Rama's proof from Tur is not decisive. Yet Rosh's commentary to Gittin does contain language supporting Rama. Rosh asks on the Gemara's statement "R. Huna concedes if the woman says it was given to him before me": how is that similar to the earlier dispute, where the get is in the third party's hand, while here it is in the woman's hand? From this he derives that even if the get is still in the third party's hand the woman can possess a migo. The question appears to assume that if the get were already in the woman's hand, the ordinary third-party rule would not automatically apply. Beit Shmuel tries to answer that Rosh may follow Ran that once the get is in the woman's hand the "if it were so" argument no longer applies, so even R. Huna would believe the third party. I do not find this compelling. The mishnah there concerns the period of persecution, when after the divorce the get was immediately torn to avoid danger from the government. If the reason were that an acceptance agent should have handed the get immediately to the woman, there was no time to do so: once he received it, the marriage ended and the document had to be destroyed for safety. Handing the torn pieces to the woman would provide her no meaningful proof or benefit. Rosh would have said this explicitly if it were his point. Still, Rosh's language can be understood more narrowly. Since R. Huna and R. Chisda dispute the rule generally even while the get remains in the third party's hand, the Gemara's later unqualified statement should likewise naturally concern the same factual setting unless it specifies otherwise. Rosh's question may therefore be merely textual: if the later case works only where the get is in the woman's hand, the Gemara should have said so expressly. This does not prove Rosh believes the agent is never trusted after delivery. I once suggested another approach in my chiddushim. Rosh may hold that where the agent says, "I am a delivery agent," even R. Huna concedes he is believed because the "if it were so" argument is weak: the get may have been written on an earlier date, and if directly delivered later it could be considered antedated, whereas delivery through an agent creates publicity and avoids the problem. Therefore, the agent could have a migo to say he was an acceptance agent. But this does not fit Tur's language that in one city one should not appoint a delivery agent because the husband may later say it was merely a deposit. I leave the point with the qualification noted above. Even if Rama's reading of Rosh is accepted for gittin, it need not govern monetary law. Maharam, Rosh's teacher, says explicitly in Mordechai that in monetary cases we presume an agent performed his mission and the burden of proof lies on the claimant who seeks to undo the recipient's possession. With a get, where the issue is a possible prohibition of a married woman, doubt is treated stringently. Thus Rosh can be stricter in gittin while accepting Maharam's monetary rule. The same distinction can explain Hagahot Maimoniyot. Raavyah, who in one case rules there is no kiddushin at all, may follow Raavad's different rationale; but in monetary law the weight of authority supports continued credibility. Therefore, in monetary matters I do not find a genuine dispute: once the third party lawfully delivered the item to Levi and later testifies in Levi's favor concerning what he did while the item was under his control, he is believed as though the item were still in his hand. Against Levi, however, once Levi possesses the item and contradicts him, the third party is only like an ordinary witness.
It appears further that Raavad, Rashba, and Maharam needed their special reasons only where Levi himself does not claim with certainty that the item belongs to him and relies solely on the third party's statement, as in a get where the woman does not know whether the husband delivered it for safekeeping or divorce. If Levi himself says with certainty, "The money belongs to me under the terms of the entrustment," there should be no doubt at all. The entire basis of third-party credibility is that the depositor trusted the holder. The owner knew that the third party might breach the deposit and hand the item to the other litigant. By entrusting him, the owner accepted that if the holder did so, the recipient could rely on that act. Once the third party has actually delivered the item, this is precisely the risk the depositor knowingly accepted. Why should the recipient's position become weaker merely because delivery has already taken place? The special reasons were needed only where the recipient lacks independent knowledge. One might then say that the owner trusted the third party only against the possibility that he would physically deliver the item, but did not accept that the recipient could later rely on the third party's verbal explanation after the fact. In such a case we need Raavad's power-to-return rationale, Rashba's possession-as-proof rationale, or Maharam's presumption that an agent performs his mission. The same is true of migo. If Levi has a migo, he can say, "When the third party delivered the item, he expressly told me the terms were such-and-such." If a statement by the third party made before witnesses at the time of delivery would be effective, Levi's migo gives him the same force. Therefore the uncertainty discussed by the authorities is principally a case where the recipient cannot independently assert the relevant facts. This is simple and correct.
But if there is a grievance against him, and so forth. Shach, seif katan 20, explains that "grievance" here does not mean the parties dispute how much property was deposited, as Mechaber discussed at the beginning of seif 1. Rather, following R. Yehudah Albargeloni, it means that the owner says to the third party, "You wrongfully delivered my deposit to Levi." Even if the item were still in the third party's hand and he said in beit din that according to the terms of the entrustment it belongs to Levi, beit din would direct him to give it to Levi and he would owe no oath, because he has no personal stake in whether Levi receives it. But once he has already handed it over, the owner can make him swear that he did not violate the entrustment. He then becomes a witness who himself must take an oath and can no longer testify. According to what I established above, however, once the third party delivered the item to Levi and afterward testifies in Levi's favor, the situation is legally like one where the item remains in the third party's own hand. Levi's position cannot become worse because the third party already performed the delivery. If so, the entire explanation collapses. The rule in Shulchan Aruch that, once the item has left the third party's hand, he is only like one witness must concern testimony against Levi. In that situation, why should a grievance by the owner prevent him from testifying against Levi? If anything, the alleged misconduct involved favoring Levi, not harming him. Shach writes that even Rashba agrees that if the owner has a grievance that the third party delivered improperly, the third party becomes interested and cannot testify even in Levi's favor. I do not find this correct. Rashba's reason is that the recipient's possession itself proves that the third party delivered in the claimed legal manner, as though he had done so before witnesses. If there had actually been witnesses to the delivery, would the owner's later grievance make the third party "interested" and destroy what those witnesses saw? Certainly not. The same should follow from Rashba's reasoning. One could force a different case. Shimon delivered the item to Levi but now testifies that the delivery was subject to a condition containing asmachta or some other invalid clause, such that on Shimon's present version Levi must return the item to Reuven. Reuven meanwhile accuses Shimon of having delivered the deposit wrongfully and demands an oath. Shimon now has an interest in testifying against Levi: if he does not say the condition was invalid and that Levi must return the property, Shimon may remain exposed to Reuven's oath claim. By testifying against Levi, he can cause the item to return to Reuven and thereby neutralize Reuven's grievance. But this is strained, and in any event the underlying law requiring the oath is itself doubtful. If the third party would have been believed without an oath while the item was still in his hand because we say, "Why should he sin for no benefit," why should he suddenly become obligated to swear merely because he carried out the entrusted act without first consulting beit din? If he asks beit din what to do, they tell him, "Give the item to Levi; you need no oath." If he does exactly the same thing on his own, in accordance with his duty as third party, why should he now owe an oath? What difference does it make whether he delivered in the presence of beit din or before witnesses? Shach himself recognizes that if he delivered before witnesses he is believed. But why should the presence of witnesses affect whether the owner can impose an oath? The third party's conduct was the same. Rav Hai Gaon infers from R. Nachman's statement that he "believed" the elderly woman that she was believed without an oath. Shach elsewhere accepts that many authorities, including Mordechai, Hagahot Mordechai, Responsa Maimoniyot, and Agudah, exempt a third party from an oath. He nevertheless prefers R. Yehudah Albargeloni because Baal HaTerumot, Baal HaIttur, Tur, and Mechaber cite him. But R. Yehudah Albargeloni never meant what Shach attributes to him. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 52, part 3, letter 1, cites Baal HaIttur in his name. Later, letter 7 of the same section says: "Although some authorities wrote that if the litigants have a grievance against the third party regarding the matter of his entrustment, even after it has left his hand he is considered interested and is not believed..." This is plainly the same R. Yehudah Albargeloni, since no other authority is cited with that exact "grievance" language. Baal HaTerumot immediately explains: "These words of the Geonim were said only where there is a dispute between them, he says you deposited only so much and they each say there was more in his hand." Thus Baal HaTerumot himself explains "grievance" as a dispute over the quantity or amount deposited, exactly as I have written. The words "even after it left his hand" likewise imply the same rule would apply before it left his hand if the monetary dispute existed. That makes sense if the parties dispute the amount of the entrusted property. It does not fit Shach's theory that the oath arises only because he already delivered the item. Maharit, cited by Shach, likewise interprets the phrase as a dispute over the amount of the deposited property. This is therefore the correct reading, supported by both Baal HaTerumot and Maharit. There is no reason to invent a dispute among the authorities where Mordechai, Agudah, and Responsa Maimoniyot exempt the third party from oath and only one line is forced against them. Shach's only significant proof is Responsa Rosh, klal 105, siman 3, where Dona Velidira was required to swear that she returned the note to Dona Lidisia lawfully. Rosh says she cannot be exempted merely because a third party is normally believed, because the entrusted matter is no longer in her possession. This seems to distinguish precisely between before and after delivery. Even if Rosh holds this, he stands alone against the many authorities cited above. Moreover, the case itself has an additional feature. The claimant's representative apparently knew the underlying facts independently, as can be inferred from the responsum. If so, his testimony functioned as one witness against Dona Velidira, requiring a Torah oath, not merely the rabbinic oath. This explains Rosh's phrase that she must take a Torah oath. Otherwise, why would the case involve anything more than a rabbinic oath over an alleged breach of deposit? Once there is a single opposing witness, the matter is different. The presumption that an agent performs his agency can support leniency concerning a rabbinic oath and prevent Chazal from imposing routine oaths on trusted third parties. It cannot override a Torah oath triggered by an actual witness. Likewise, the policy reason not to discourage people from serving as third parties applies to rabbinic enactments, not to a Torah oath. Rosh's case can therefore be fully reconciled with the majority view. There is a second possible distinction. Perhaps Rosh holds that while the third-party item remains intact in the holder's hand, his special credibility is as strong as possession itself. In the Dona Velidira case the original note had already been returned and could not be restored to the holder. The special possession basis was therefore gone. Yet even this does not establish Shach's broad oath rule. In any event, we should not extend Rosh beyond necessity. The proper practical rule is that an ordinary third party who acts according to his entrusted instructions is not required to take a rabbinic oath merely because the owner later complains that he delivered improperly. The authorities explicitly exempt him, and imposing such an oath would undermine the entire institution of third-party entrustment: no one would agree to serve if every later dissatisfied litigant could force him to swear. Where the litigants dispute the amount of the entrusted property, however, there is a true personal monetary controversy involving the third party. He may then be considered interested concerning that disputed portion, as R. Yehudah Albargeloni, Baal HaTerumot, and Maharit state. And where a Torah oath arises through an actual opposing witness, the third party must swear. That is a separate rule. Thus Sema's interpretation is correct, and Bach's distinction above is also correct: the principal difficulty concerns a person who denies the third-party arrangement itself. Shach's extension of the grievance rule to ordinary delivery against an owner's later complaint should not be accepted.
