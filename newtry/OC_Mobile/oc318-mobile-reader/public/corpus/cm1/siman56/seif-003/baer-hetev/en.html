A grievance. The Bach writes that although in seif 1 the third party is not believed where the parties have a grievance against him concerning the amount of money, even while the deposited item remains in his hands, there the parties deny having appointed him as a third party. Here, however, they admit it, or witnesses establish that they appointed him, and therefore while the item remains in his hands he is believed even when they dispute the amount, without any oath. The Shach finds this astonishing. Although they trusted him as a third party, they certainly did not trust him to steal and take the money for himself. Plainly he must swear if they dispute the amount, even while he holds the deposited item. The Shach therefore explains Rama's term 'grievance' differently. It means that both parties agree that he acted lawfully in releasing the deposited item, but they dispute some collateral term, for example one says there was a condition that even after delivery he could redeem it until a certain time and the other denies this. In that case the third party is believed, and the recipient may be required to take a Torah oath to contradict the third party, while if the third party supports him he is exempt even from a hesset oath because the third party functions as a supporting witness. But if the complaint is that the third party released the item unlawfully, then whether the claimant demands return of the item itself or raises some other claim, the third party cannot testify because he has a personal interest in the matter, since he may need to swear. One whose credibility depends upon taking an oath cannot serve as a witness, as stated in siman 121. It appears that if the recipient is willing to return the deposited item to the third party so that he can testify, the third party is no longer interested, as stated by the Tur in siman 140. Even according to the dissenting view cited by the Mechaber there in seif 9, one may say that here it is different because he was originally appointed as their third party and they trusted him. When the item is restored to him, his original credibility returns. The Shach brings further proofs and concludes that he later found a responsum of the Rosh ruling in practice that once the third party has released the item from his possession he is like any other person and must take an oath.
