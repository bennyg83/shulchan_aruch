He will not be believed, etc. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length and established the reason. The principal rule is like Rashba. If Reuven deposited a matter with Shimon, and Shimon later delivered it to Levi for Levi's benefit, Shimon may still testify in Levi's favor even though the deposited item is now in Levi's possession, because Levi's right is no weaker than it would have been while the item remained with Shimon. But Shimon is not believed to testify against Levi, because Levi can say, "You no longer have any authority over me, since the deposited matter is no longer in your possession at all." Likewise, where they dispute the original conditions between them and the later delivery has no bearing on those conditions, we say that because the matter was not incumbent on him to remember, he may not remember it accurately, and at most he is like one witness. If Levi has a migo, however, he too is believed that the third party delivered it under those conditions, just as if the delivery had occurred before witnesses. Since he has a migo, it is as though the delivery was witnessed. Certainly this is so where Levi himself makes a definite claim. All of the foregoing concerns a case where Levi has no definite claim of his own and relies solely on the third party, yet the third party can still testify in Levi's favor. All this is explained there at length.
And they have no grievance. See Tumim, where I established against Shach that if Shimon delivered the deposited matter to Levi, Reuven cannot impose an oath on Shimon that he did not act contrary to the terms of the deposit. There is no oath at all upon the third party, because a person is presumed not to sin for no personal gain. Only where one witness contradicts him may it be possible, according to Rosh, that he must swear. But there is no hesset oath.
But if they have a grievance. Sema explains that they dispute the amount of the deposit, one saying it was one hundred and the third party saying it was only eighty. Although this was already stated at the beginning of the siman, it is repeated there regarding the third party's credibility as equivalent to two witnesses, whereas here it concerns his status as one witness, Sema. Bach answers that above, where the deposited matter remains in his possession and he admits the fact of the deposit, even though they dispute the amount, he remains believed. Here, even if he admits the deposit, because it is no longer in his possession and they dispute the amount, his status is different. Shach objects to the suggestion that while the deposit remains with him he would be exempt from an oath despite a dispute over the amount, and Shach is correct, for he must swear. As practical law, however, I concluded that although he must swear, he remains believed with that oath as I wrote above in seif 1. By contrast, where the deposited matter is no longer in his possession and they dispute the amount, he is not believed even as one witness.
