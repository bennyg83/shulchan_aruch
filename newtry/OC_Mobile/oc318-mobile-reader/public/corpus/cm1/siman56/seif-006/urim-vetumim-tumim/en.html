That the borrower came with him, and so forth. Sema explains that the borrower must actually come together with the third party because the borrower must know the terms of the entrustment and make a definite claim that the creditor delivered the note to the third party subject to those terms. If the borrower knows nothing except what the third party tells him, his uncertain claim is insufficient. Shach agrees. In seif katan 5 Shach likewise says that if a person gives a third party an object to deliver conditionally to another person, but that recipient knows nothing independently and relies only on the third party, while the giver denies ever appointing the third party, the object returns to its original owner. With an ordinary object, Shach's rule is clearly incorrect. Shach himself concedes that where the giver admits appointing the person as third party, the recipient acquires through the third party's statement even if the recipient personally knew nothing. The classic case, "the husband says for safekeeping and the third party says for divorce," concerns a woman who does not know what the husband said to the third party. This is evident from the later statement, "R. Huna concedes if she says, he gave it to him before me," which is presented as a different case. Yet in monetary law, or in another city where the "if it were so" argument does not apply, all agree that the third party is believed. Thus the recipient can acquire even without independent knowledge. Shach reaches his result only because he distinguishes between an owner who admits appointing a third party and one who says the item fell from him or was stolen. I have shown at length above that the primary view of Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Mechaber does not generally suspect such loss or theft merely because it is asserted. Therefore, with an ordinary object, once the third party is believed, the recipient acquires through him even if the recipient knew nothing in advance. A loan document requires separate analysis. Suppose a creditor places his promissory note with a third party. Even if there were no note in existence at all, the borrower still owes the underlying money. Merely destroying or returning the note does not automatically forgive the principal debt. A creditor can say: "I waived my right to have a written loan or collateral, but I never waived the money itself." If the borrower admits he never paid, why should the third party's possession of the document extinguish the underlying oral debt? Similarly, returning collateral does not necessarily prove forgiveness of the principal. One might say the creditor surrendered the security while leaving the personal debt intact. Therefore, there is a strong reason to require the borrower to make a definite claim: "The creditor forgave the debt itself." If the borrower knows only from the third party, perhaps the third party's migo to burn the note proves only that the written lien is gone, not that the underlying money obligation was forgiven. Responsa Rosh, klal 68, indeed rules that where a third party says the creditor gave him the note to tear because he forgave the borrower, but the borrower himself says he did not pay and knows nothing of such forgiveness, the borrower must pay. Rosh adds that the third party is not believed because the creditor denies ever appointing him. I discussed that responsum above at length. Rosh's words imply, however, that if the creditor admitted making him a third party, the third party could be believed even to extinguish the principal debt. The reason is the entrustment itself. Why would the creditor hand the note to a third party with power to return it to the borrower? He knows the borrower could then boldly stand before him and say, "You were paid," relying upon possession of the cancelled note. By entrusting the paper in that way, the creditor accepted the third party's disposition and the consequences of the borrower receiving it. It is as though he accepted from the outset that the third party's statement concerning forgiveness or payment would govern. I have established above that we ordinarily do not distinguish between a creditor who admits the third-party arrangement and one who denies it through an implausible loss or theft claim, except in the special one-city situation governed by R. Huna's "if it were so" presumption. In the present case, where the document was deposited subject to a condition and there is a reason for using a third party rather than handing it directly to the borrower, that presumption does not apply. Therefore, the third party should be believed even if the borrower himself did not previously know the terms. This is particularly clear regarding collateral. Tur specifically added the case where the creditor sends collateral through a third party. If the third party is authorized to hand the collateral to another person or retain it himself, the creditor must realize that the borrower will not repay until the collateral is returned. By giving the third party that control, the creditor necessarily accepts that the debt can be treated as discharged under the stated terms. There is no meaningful distinction between collateral and another object conditionally entrusted for transfer. Thus Tur's addition of collateral reveals that he follows his father Rosh only in the special case where the creditor's denial of the third-party arrangement is supported by the "if it were so" reasoning. Outside that setting, the third party can determine even the status of the underlying debt. It therefore seems more likely that the phrase "the borrower came with him" is not a requirement that the borrower possess independent firsthand knowledge. Rather, it states an even stronger case. Even if the borrower comes together with the third party, creating an argument that "if the creditor truly intended to benefit the borrower, he should have handed it directly to the borrower who was right there," the third party is nevertheless believed. In monetary matters we do not automatically apply R. Huna's "if it were so" inference in this fashion. Sema himself in Perishah notes this possible reading, and I would add that it can even refer to a condition making it possible for the item ultimately to go to the borrower himself. One might think that because the borrower was present, the creditor's use of a third party is suspicious and supports the creditor's later denial. The ruling teaches that the third-party arrangement nevertheless stands. This likely reflects a disagreement with Rosh's stricter use of "if it were so" where the owner denies the entire entrustment. But as I explained above, many authorities, including Baal HaTerumot and Ramban, do not accept that concern broadly. Therefore, in my opinion, the borrower need not possess independent knowledge of the terms merely to rely on the third party. With an ordinary object this is clear. Even with a loan note or collateral, the better view is that where the third party's credibility is established and the factual setting does not trigger the special "if it were so" presumption, his statement can establish forgiveness or discharge of the underlying debt. The wording "the borrower came with him" should be understood as teaching the stronger case, not as a prerequisite for a definite firsthand claim.
And he should return it to the one who gave it. Shach writes that this applies where the agent has no migo. If he has a migo, he may deliver the item to the other person in accordance with the condition that was stipulated. I am puzzled by the underlying ruling of Rashba. Since an agent is believed regarding everything said to him when the matter was entrusted, as appears from the responsum attributed to Ramban, why should he not act according to the condition even if he was not formally appointed as the final third party? What difference is there between being believed because of a migo, as Shach says, and being believed because the parties trusted him? In either case his statement about the condition is accepted. Even if one argues that he was never authorized to carry out the condition personally, Shach's ruling where he has a migo is still difficult. His credibility does not itself appoint him judge or empower him to transfer another's property. At minimum, however, he should not return the item to the original giver before first stating the nature of the agency and its conditions before beit din. While he still has the item, he is believed concerning the arrangement. If he returns it first, his status may fall to that of a single witness and the other party can be harmed through his action. The correct explanation of Rashba's case is different. Reuven and Shimon had a joint debt against a gentile. Shimon handed the debt instrument to Levi to deliver it to Yehudah, who was to serve as third party, with a condition that if Reuven fulfilled certain terms the document would belong to him. Yehudah refused to accept the third-party role. Rashba therefore ruled that Levi must return the document to Shimon. The reason is not lack of credibility. Rashba holds that even where a formal third party receives a document, the person in whose favor the document is intended does not acquire it unless the giver says to the third party, "Acquire it for him." With a document whose transfer requires writing and delivery, even saying "acquire" may not suffice. Rashba discusses this at length in Responsa Torat HaBayit, siman 99. In the present case Shimon did not tell Levi to acquire the document for Reuven. He instructed Levi only to bring it to Yehudah, who would then hold it as third party. Since Yehudah refused, Reuven never acquired anything and Shimon remained free to retract. Levi therefore had to return the document. Accordingly, Shach's distinction based on migo is not correct. Even if Levi has a migo, Shimon may retract because Reuven never acquired the document. The ruling is limited to this type of case, where acquisition by the intended recipient still requires an act of acquisition. But if a creditor deposits a loan note with an intermediary on terms that if the borrower does such-and-such the debt itself is forgiven, no acquisition by the borrower is needed. The forgiveness operates automatically once the condition is fulfilled. In such a case the agent should not return the document to the creditor before stating the agency and its conditions to beit din, while his statement still has the full credibility of a third party. The same applies to any comparable case where the recipient's right arises without requiring the intermediary to acquire the item on his behalf. This is the correct practical distinction.
He should say it before witnesses. See Shach's discussion of Sema. Shach answers that if the third party goes before beit din, the judges may prevent him from handing over an item where the condition contains asmachta. Therefore he should make his declaration before ordinary witnesses instead. This is strained. If the law requires beit din to restrain him, he should go before beit din and they should act according to Torah law. It cannot be proper to avoid their supervision merely so that he can carry out the entrustment. The simpler explanation is that appearing before beit din is more burdensome than appearing before witnesses, as Shach himself notes. Here the case concerns a document whose payment obligations fall at different times. When the third party states before witnesses that the document was subject to a condition, the witnesses may write that statement without a special instruction to write, because their writing functions like a receipt. Below, siman 81, seif 29, we rule that witnesses may write a receipt without being expressly told to write. This is effectively a receipt that extends the payment time or records the condition affecting collection. By contrast, if the third party is returning money or an object and merely states before witnesses that the transfer is conditional, a written record prepared by those witnesses could later be used in beit din to extract property. That raises the problem of testimony from writing. Tosafot labored to explain why a written protest does not violate the rule against written testimony. Therefore, in that type of case one should state the matter before beit din rather than rely on ordinary witnesses to prepare a writing. This distinction resolves the language naturally.
