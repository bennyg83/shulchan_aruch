The borrower came with another person. Sema and Perishah explain that the borrower's physical presence is not essential. What matters is that the borrower now makes a definite claim. An agent can have the status of a shalish regarding what he was specifically charged to accomplish. When the claimed agency itself is denied, however, that resembles denial of the escrow arrangement. The agent therefore needs whatever migo or possessory basis is required at the time of that denial. As I explained above, with ordinary movables the shalish can remain believed even against an uncertain counterclaim, while with a debt document the named creditor can have a stronger claim of loss or theft. The same distinction applies to an agent.
Like an ordinary agent, and he returns it to the one who gave it to him. Shach writes that this is only where the agent has no migo; with a migo he could deliver according to his own version. In my view Urim VeTumim is correct that even a migo does not help here. The agent's authority was to deliver the item to a particular shalish on a stated condition. He was not authorized to deliver it directly to the ultimate recipient. Even if his factual account is true, he lacks legal power to do what he now proposes. A migo cannot create authority the principal never granted. The difference between an agent and a shalish is decisive. A shalish was entrusted from the outset as the effective litigant regarding disposition of the item. An ordinary agent who admits he received the item merely as a deposit remains a custodian for the principal. The custodian's domain is legally the depositor's domain. His migo is therefore like a migo used to extract property from another's possession and does not help. An ordinary lone witness likewise is not strengthened by a personal migo where we suspect he may be speaking from an inaccurate impression. The agent is nevertheless believed to deliver the item to the appointed shalish, because that is the very act for which he was appointed. Regarding that task he is treated like a principal and was trusted to perform it. Even if he no longer has a present migo, he can complete that mission. But if the appointed shalish refuses to accept it, the agent's mission ends and he must return the property to the sender. He then reverts to the status of an ordinary custodian and cannot redirect the item elsewhere on the strength of a migo. This also explains the case in siman 255 where a person says property is in a certain place and belongs to another. There, the owner intentionally placed it beyond ordinary control and empowered the speaker to identify the rightful recipient; the speaker therefore has shalish-like authority. Here the principal specifically retained the right to have the object returned if the appointed shalish did not accept it, so the agent cannot override that instruction.
He should say it before witnesses. Sema and Shach question this from seif 3, where the shalish must speak before beit din. The distinction is simple. Here the shalish states the very condition that determines to whom he must presently deliver the object. Regarding that matter he stands like a litigant, and even his uncertainty is treated like the litigant's uncertainty. His admission before witnesses can therefore operate like an admission of a litigant. Seif 3 concerns the shalish's report of facts or conditions that were explained to him concerning what is to occur after the entrusted item leaves his hands. Regarding such a matter he is no longer the effective litigant, because even if he is personally uncertain he must still carry out the original disposition. His credibility there rests on the special rule that matters explained to a shalish are like accepting a relative as trustworthy, and on the presumption that one charged with the matter pays close attention, like a judge or seller who is specially believed. Such testimony is no stronger than ordinary testimony and therefore has effect only when stated before beit din.
