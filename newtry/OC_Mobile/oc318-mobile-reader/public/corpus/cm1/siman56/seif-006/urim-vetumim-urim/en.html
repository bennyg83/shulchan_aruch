When the borrower came, etc. Sema understands this specifically where the borrower knew about the escrow arrangement and makes a definite claim. If he was not present and has no definite knowledge, the arrangement should not affect him. See Tumim. I conclude that where an object or pledge was placed in escrow, there is no doubt at all. Even if the borrower did not know, the escrow is effective and the escrow holder acts according to its terms. Where a debt instrument was placed in escrow and the borrower did not know whether the underlying debt was remitted, Responsa Rosh also appears to indicate that if the escrow holder says the debt was forgiven, the debt is in fact forgiven even without the borrower's knowledge. This requires some study, especially where the lender contradicts the escrow holder. The Shulchan Aruch's phrase that the borrower "came" is therefore stated for added novelty. Even where the borrower was physically present and one might argue, "If the escrow holder's account were true, why did he not simply give it to the borrower while he was there?" the escrow holder remains believed. The Gemara itself uses this reasoning.
The escrow holder should follow what the agent told him. The agent is believed because originally he had a migo and could have acted directly. Therefore he is believed concerning the instructions he transmitted to the escrow holder, and the escrow holder in turn is believed to say what the agent told him. According to the Shach, the agent specifically needs that migo, because otherwise the principal's denial of agency would prevent us from saying the principal entrusted him. According to what I established above, contradiction of the escrow relationship does not change the result in this manner. Even without a current migo, the agent had been entrusted initially. Ramban's language in Baal HaTerumot explicitly supports this.
And he returns it to the one who gave it. See Tumim. The case concerns a matter in which the principal can still retract until the escrow holder acquires it on behalf of the recipient. Therefore the agent returns it to the principal. Even though the agent is believed because the principal initially entrusted him and he may have a migo, the recipient never acquired the item and the principal can retract. But where the transfer does not require a new act of acquisition by the escrow holder, once the agent is believed he should not return it to the principal until he at least states the true circumstances before beit din, so that the recipient not lose his right. So long as the item remained within his entrusted control, the agent has credibility comparable to an escrow holder. See there.
The escrow holder should always do, etc. This appears to speak where the opposing party has not protested before beit din. If he formally protests, the escrow holder certainly should not return or deliver the item until beit din rules according to law. This resolves Responsa Rosh, rule 72:5, which says that where the escrow arrangement contains an asmachta the escrow holder should not release the property from his hands. There the opposing party had already protested in beit din.
He should say, etc. Sema asks that seif 3 says the escrow holder should go before beit din. His answer, and the Shach's, are strained, as I wrote in Tumim. I distinguish the cases. Here the escrow holder merely has to extend the time of a debt instrument. That resembles a receipt, which can be established through valid written testimony. Therefore witnesses may write the statement. In other escrow matters where the testimony itself would extract property, ordinary written testimony does not suffice; the matter must be presented before beit din. See Tumim.
