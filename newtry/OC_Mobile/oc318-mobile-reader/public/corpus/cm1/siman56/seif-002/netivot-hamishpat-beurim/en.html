Witnesses contradicted him. See Tumim, who writes that regarding a bill of divorce, even if witnesses come after she has married and contradict the third party, she does not leave her husband. By contrast, in monetary matters, since the matter remains subject to reversal, contradiction by witnesses is always effective. In my view there is proof for his position that in a bill of divorce contradiction by witnesses is ineffective after she has married. Otherwise, the discussion in Gittin 66 concerning a husband who says that the document was given for deposit is difficult. By the reasoning of, "if that were so," we would conclude that it was certainly given for deposit, except that Rabbi Chisda holds it was given for divorce because the husband trusted the third party that it should serve for divorce even if the third party were to lie. See Tosafot there. Rashba asks that witnesses to delivery are required, and if he delivered it as a deposit there were no witnesses to delivery for divorce. He answers that because the husband trusted him that it should serve for divorce through this delivery, he necessarily delivered it in the presence of witnesses. Yet if the reasoning of "if that were so" establishes that it was certainly a deposit, there were certainly witnesses when he delivered it as a deposit. We should then be concerned that those witnesses may later come and contradict the third party, just as we are concerned that the husband may later come and challenge the divorce. This is unlike witnesses who are merely rumored to be standing elsewhere, where there is only a report that witnesses exist. Here witnesses certainly exist. It follows that contradiction by witnesses is ineffective after she has married. In monetary matters, however, there is certainly no such distinction, as is implied by all the authorities, who make no distinction.
Contradicted by witnesses. See Sema, seif katan 14, who writes that although when two sets of witnesses contradict one another we treat the matter as a contradiction and the rule is that the claimant must bring proof, here the third party has an established presumption of trustworthiness, and the witnesses seek to remove him from that presumption. At first glance this is difficult, for there too the first witnesses possess a presumption of fitness, and the second witnesses seek to remove them from it. There is no difficulty. There the discussion concerns a contradiction in matters of examination, in which the witnesses are not disqualified even if we treat them as contradicted, as Taz writes there. Here, by contrast, if we treat the third party as contradicted, he will be disqualified. Even where the contradiction is merely in examination, such as one hundred against two hundred, we do not treat a third party as contradicted, because so long as the entrusted matter remains in his hands he is literally a litigant, since he is charged with carrying out the matter. He therefore examines the facts carefully. It is like witnesses who contradict a litigant as to one hundred or two hundred, where the litigant becomes disqualified. See above concerning the explanation of this expression. Since we would thereby be disqualifying him, we reconcile his statements where possible.
