The witnesses contradicted him, and so forth. Beit Yosef and Knesset HaGedolah write that Rashba disagrees. Derishah writes that Rashba concedes that where the witnesses themselves are present and contradict the third party, the third party is not believed against them. Rather, his special credibility applies where the witnesses are no longer before us, even though it is known that the matter had originally been delivered before witnesses. In such a case we do not say that the owner never trusted the third party because he relied on the witnesses. He still trusted him, because the witnesses might die or travel overseas. Tosafot in Gittin write this explicitly, and Derishah is correct. Rashba himself says so expressly in his chiddushim to Gittin. He asks: if the item was necessarily delivered before witnesses, what special trust was placed in the third party? Let us simply see what the witnesses say. Rashba answers that since the third party could have handed the get to the woman and she could have gone and remarried with it, the husband trusted him even in the presence of witnesses. The husband thereby rendered those witnesses ineffective against the third party so long as the third party said it was for divorce before the witnesses later appeared. Rashba's precise qualification is "before the witnesses came." If she married only after the witnesses arrived and contradicted the third party, it is obvious the third party would no longer be believed. The husband trusted him only to the extent that the woman might act before contradictory witnesses appeared, not so that the third party would forever override witnesses who were actually present. Accordingly, in monetary law, where matters can generally be reversed and restored, the owner never intended the third party to override two witnesses who are actually before beit din and contradict him. This also explains Ramban, whose view is the opinion cited in Shulchan Aruch. Ramban says that the third party is believed to say that a later condition was added after the original delivery. That statement does not contradict the witnesses. But if his credibility existed even against the original witnesses themselves, why frame the law specifically around a later condition? The necessary inference is that where his statement does not conflict with the witnesses he remains believed, because the owner trusted him against the possibility that the witnesses would later become unavailable. But where the witnesses stand before us and contradict him, they prevail. Tosafot in Gittin say that because the third party was trusted from the outset, he is even believed concerning items ordinarily lent or rented. At first glance the word "therefore" is difficult. In monetary law Sanhedrin already establishes that a third party is believed even without a migo. Why should Tosafot need the new principle derived from gittin? The explanation is that where the owner claims the item was lent, he has a migo that could function like witnesses. One might therefore think the third party cannot prevail against such a powerful claim. Tosafot teach that since the husband trusted the third party even in a situation where actual witnesses existed in the background, all the more so an owner's migo does not defeat the third party. This is also what Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 141 intends when he says Tosafot imply that the third party is believed even where witnesses exist.
However, I am troubled by the source of this entire principle. It is conceptually strained to say that the husband trusted the third party because perhaps the witnesses would die or travel overseas, which is an uncommon possibility. We are forced into this explanation only because of a major question. The Gemara's case "the husband says it was for safekeeping and the third party says it was for divorce" appears to involve a get delivered to the third party in the presence of witnesses. If delivery witnesses are required, why not simply ask them what happened? From this Rashba and Tosafot infer that the husband must have trusted the third party even against the later absence of witnesses. But this proof works only according to the authorities who require delivery witnesses. Rif, Rambam, and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 133 hold that a signed get can be valid even without separate delivery witnesses. According to them, the entire sugya can be discussing a get delivered privately with no witnesses present at all. Then there is no proof whatsoever that the third party was trusted despite the existence of witnesses. Ramban can still be understood because he labored to reconcile Rif with the view that delivery witnesses ordinarily create the get. He may also agree with Rosh that an agent for delivery must be appointed before witnesses, as the Yerushalmi says. If the Gemara's "third party says for divorce" refers to a delivery agent, there were witnesses to the appointment and Tosafot's reasoning remains relevant. But according to Rambam and those who hold that an agent for delivery need not be appointed before witnesses, the proof disappears. This may explain Maharsha's observation that Tosafot specifically interpret "the third party says for divorce" as an agent for receipt rather than for delivery. Tosafot may hold like Rambam that an agent for delivery can be appointed without witnesses. If the dispute concerned a delivery agent, there would be no necessary witnesses and no basis for the Gemara's later question about why acceptance witnesses are needed. By interpreting it as an acceptance agent, who requires witnesses because no matter of forbidden marital status is established with fewer than two witnesses, Tosafot force the existence of witnesses and can derive that the third party was trusted despite them. One might then ask the reverse: if R. Huna and R. Chisda disagree only regarding an acceptance agent, where there are necessarily witnesses, perhaps R. Huna agrees that a delivery agent with no witnesses is believed because the husband has no other evidentiary basis. But that cannot be. The Gemara answers that monetary matters differ because they are capable of waiver, not because one case involved witnesses. And according to those who do not accept Rabbenu Tam's requirement of delivery witnesses even for R. Meir, the distinction would collapse. Therefore R. Huna's "if so, he would have given it directly" argument must apply regardless of whether witnesses existed. Tosafot's proof that the third party was trusted even in the presence of witnesses may therefore be understood differently. The Mishnah says that a woman who appointed an acceptance agent needs two sets of witnesses, those who heard her appointment and those who saw receipt. It adds that one witness from the first set and one from the second can combine with an additional witness. If the acceptance agent himself were a qualified witness, he could potentially join each set. Yet the Mishnah's formulation suggests the agent is not necessarily counted, perhaps because he may be a relative or otherwise disqualified. The Gemara nevertheless asks why receipt witnesses are needed; let the agent be believed. That means the third-party rule is invoked even where the necessary evidence would otherwise come from independent witnesses. This supports Tosafot's broad trust principle. Still, our practical halachah follows the view that an acceptance agent can sometimes combine as a witness. Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 141, seif 9, cites this as the primary view, though he also brings an opposing opinion. If the agent joins the witness set, perhaps Tosafot's proof loses some force. One can nevertheless say the Mishnah itself discusses a case where the agent is a relative or disqualified and cannot join. Then the remaining two witnesses truly exist independently, and the Gemara still asks why the third party cannot be believed. This shows that, at least according to the sugya, third-party trust can coexist with background witnesses. Yet I remain uncertain how far this principle should be extended in practice. It may be that once actual witnesses are before us and directly contradict the third party, the special credibility ends, as Rashba's own language indicates. The trust protects against later disappearance of evidence, not against extant contradictory testimony.
In this matter, one can also understand why Rif, who rules like R. Chisda, copied the teaching that even R. Huna concedes where the woman says, "He gave it to him before me." At first glance this seems unnecessary. If we rule like R. Chisda, the third party is believed in any event. One could say Rif copied it merely to show that R. Chisda's rule applies even without a migo, but that is strained. According to what I have explained, there is a real practical difference. If the get was delivered to the agent before witnesses, the husband may ordinarily argue that he relied on those witnesses rather than granting unlimited credibility to the agent. But where the woman herself says, "He gave it to him before me," she possesses a migo or direct knowledge that can support the agent even under a stricter framework. Thus Rif records the concession because it has legal significance in a case involving background witnesses. For these reasons, the law cited here requires study. Many authorities do not have a clear Talmudic source for saying that a third party prevails against actual contradictory witnesses. Mordechai in Perek HaChovel, in the case of a third party who burned a note, also implies in the name of R. Baruch of Mainz that where witnesses contradict him, the third party is not believed even if he otherwise has a migo that he returned the document. Responsa Perach Mateh Aharon, part 1, siman 82, writes that if two witnesses contradict the third party and two other witnesses support the third party, we set the two against the two and the third party then prevails through his special credibility. I do not think so. It is true that the third party's credibility is compared to two witnesses. But suppose four ordinary witnesses supported him and two contradicted them. The law would still be that the testimony is contradicted, because two are like one hundred. The presence of more supporting witnesses does not create a numerical majority in testimonial contradiction. The same should be true here. If the third party is treated as two and two actual witnesses support him, we still have one group of testimony against another group. Once two witnesses contradict, the case has the status of conflicting testimony. Therefore this ruling too requires study, and it appears clear to me that it is a case of contradicted testimony.
