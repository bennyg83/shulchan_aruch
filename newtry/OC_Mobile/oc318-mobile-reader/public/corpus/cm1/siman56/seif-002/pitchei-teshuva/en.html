See the Shach, s.v. vehash'ta asi nami shapir, through his statement that the third party is financially interested in his testimony. Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes in his glosses that this is difficult to understand, because after the third party returns the document to the lender as required, his interest ceases and he should be fit as one witness to say that the document was waived. See further in the Shach, who disputes Baal HaTerumos's comparison to the agents in Kiddushin who, after the institution of a hesset oath, became interested witnesses. Responsa Veshav HaKohen, siman 76, explains Baal HaTerumos and resolves the issue. See Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat as well. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 15, addresses a third party who says that an item was entrusted to him to deliver to a certain person, while the owner says it was deposited with him only. The third party has a migo of complete denial, purchase, or return, but refuses to take the hesset oath that would accompany those claims. Beit Ephraim rules that his refusal to swear does not affect the beneficiary's right. His credibility does not arise from the oath, but from his control over the item and the claims available to him. Just as if he had said that the item belonged to the beneficiary and then died or went overseas we would still deliver it, so too his refusal to swear does not delay delivery. The same principle appears in simanim 47, 62, 99, and 222. Only where he has no migo and seeks to function solely as one witness does his testimony fail, even if he is willing to swear, because a witness who requires an oath is not a valid witness. He concludes that this is clear in practical law. See there at length.
