Forged or paid. At first glance this is difficult, for one should be believed against the third party when he has a migo that he could have claimed that the document was forged. Yet in Even HaEzer, siman 141, seif 58, it is ruled that if a bill of divorce has not been authenticated and the husband claims that it was deposited with the third party, he is not believed against the third party through a migo that he could have claimed forgery. It appears that the cases differ. There we say that the husband trusted the third party that the document should operate as a divorce even if the third party lies and says it was given for divorce, even where the husband has a migo of forgery. By placing the bill of divorce in the third party's hands, there is reason to fear that he may give it to the woman and she may marry on its basis even without authentication, because she may marry on the strength of an unauthenticated bill of divorce, as explained in Even HaEzer, siman 152, seif 9. Moreover, the agent can authenticate it by saying that it was written and signed in his presence. Here, however, an unauthenticated monetary document is different. We may say that the borrower did not trust the third party at all on this point, for what was there to fear? Even if the third party were to deliver it to the lender, the borrower could always assert payment through a migo of forgery. Even in the borrower's absence, beit din would raise the claims of forgery and payment on his behalf, as explained below. Ketzot HaChoshen compares a third party to an express credibility clause in siman 71, where such credibility is ineffective when the defendant has a migo of forgery, because the migo concerns the very matter of the credibility. He therefore reasons similarly that where one has a migo of forgery against a third party, he is believed. According to this, however, those authorities who hold that a third party is not believed when the very fact of the deposit is denied should also hold that when there are no witnesses to the deposit, the third party is not believed even where the depositor admits that there was a deposit. For here too the migo concerns the very basis of the third party's credibility, since the depositor could deny the deposit and say that he never trusted him. Yet the Gemara and all the authorities state the opposite, for in Gittin, where the husband says it was for deposit, the third party is believed. According to Shach, that case concerns a deposit subject to a particular condition, and necessarily there were no witnesses, for if there were witnesses we could simply hear what they say. Rather, a third party is different and stronger than an ordinary credibility clause. Wherever the matter was in his power to carry out as he says, the depositor necessarily trusted him and consented that the result should be as the third party states, even if he lies. What relevance does a migo have to this? A migo is only evidence that the party's present assertion is true, but even if it is true, that does not matter. In Gittin, where the husband says the bill was given for deposit, there is likewise evidence that the husband speaks truth from the reasoning that had he intended divorce he would have delivered it directly to her. Nevertheless we say that he trusted the third party that it should be for divorce even if the third party lies. Rashba even holds that for this reason the third party is believed against witnesses. Those who disagree do so only where witnesses contradict him, but in the face of a mere migo the third party is certainly believed, as is evident from Even HaEzer, siman 141, seif 55, and as stated above. The Mechaber holds specifically that where the document is unauthenticated the borrower is believed, because he did not entrust the third party with any effective power regarding it. What could the third party have done, when even if he delivered it to the creditor the debtor could still plead payment? Nimukei Yosef and Shach, however, hold that once the debtor admits that he wrote the document, he must at some point have been capable of authenticating it, and he therefore entrusted the third party even if the third party were to lie. Yet there is a difficulty with Nimukei Yosef and Shach. The reason we say that he trusted the third party is that by placing the matter in his hands he consented to whatever the third party could then accomplish. If so, when the document remained in the creditor's own hands and at the time of the transaction he could have authenticated it, but the witnesses later died, and the debtor claims that he paid before the witnesses died, we should similarly say that he consented that matters would be as the creditor says because the creditor once could have authenticated the document. Why should delivery into the hands of the owner himself be weaker than delivery into the hands of a third party? It is unreasonable that the owner's own hand should be weaker. It therefore appears that there is an additional basis for the special credibility of a third party. Baal HaTerumot explains that what was specified by both parties to the third party is comparable to their saying, "My father is accepted upon me as judge," and that the third party is careful and precise. It resembles the judge who is believed to say, "I ruled in favor of this one," and the seller who is believed to identify to whom he sold, in siman 222. Mordechai, in Perek HaTekabel, and Hagahot Maimoniyot to Sefer Mishpatim, siman 44, likewise ground the third party's credibility while the entrusted item remains in his hands on the fact that he is exacting in his recollection. Most authorities also explain it by the presumption that a person does not sin where he gains nothing. At first glance, if so, every single witness should be believed because of this presumption. Necessarily, we fear that an ordinary single witness may speak on the basis of an impression, as Tashbetz writes, whereas a third party is especially careful and exact. The reason may be as Penei Yehoshua writes at the beginning of Gittin regarding an agent for delivery: when a person is charged with carrying out the matter, we say that he is exacting. Alternatively, as Penei Yehoshua writes in Kiddushin regarding one who says, "I betrothed my daughter," the presumption that a person does not sin without benefit is effective only to maintain possession, not to extract property. A third party, however, has a proprietary hold over the entrusted object, and is therefore treated as maintaining possession, as explained above. Still, why were these reasons needed? Why did the authorities not rely solely on the Gemara's reason that the parties trusted him, meaning that they consented that the matter should be as he says even if he lies, as Tosafot in Gittin and Hagahot Maimoniyot, siman 44, explain? Necessarily, these additional reasons were written for the practical distinction addressed by Nimukei Yosef, where the debtor claims payment and contradicts the third party concerning an unauthenticated document. In that situation the reason of trust alone is insufficient. Even when a document is delivered to the creditor himself, whom the debtor certainly trusted in the ordinary sense, a migo can nevertheless defeat the document. Therefore they add that a third party is especially exacting and is comparable to the judge and the seller, whose credibility Sema in siman 222 explains on the ground that they are exacting. It is as though the Sages themselves gave him the force of two witnesses, even against a migo. A migo against such testimony is a migo against witnesses. Rashba writes similarly in his novellae to Kiddushin regarding the credibility of a judge, that even where the litigant has a migo that he could deny having accepted him as judge, the judge remains believed, as explained above in siman 23. For this reason a migo is ineffective. Those who disagree reject this special principle of precision, and therefore rule that a migo is effective. Accordingly, the principal ruling follows Nimukei Yosef and Shach.
Or paid. See Tumim, who writes that if the debtor claims, "I paid you after I deposited it with you," he is not believed. His only credibility to claim payment rests on a migo of forgery, and if he were to claim forgery he would have to swear against the third party, who testifies that the document is genuine. He would therefore be one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and must pay. Tumim distinguishes a claim that it was already paid when he deposited it, where even now he directly contradicts the third party and is believed through his migo. In my humble opinion he is believed even when he claims later payment. This is not within the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear merely because the third party knows that the document is not forged. Otherwise, whenever a document is authenticated by one witness, the debtor could never assert payment through a migo of forgery, because he would be unable to swear against that witness and would have to pay. Rather, whenever a document has not been authenticated by two witnesses, a claim of payment is itself a proper claim, for the reason stated by Sema in siman 46, seif katan 2, and elsewhere. Whatever the precise reason, since we rule that one may plead payment against a document authenticated by only one witness, the same applies against a third party. This is clear.
