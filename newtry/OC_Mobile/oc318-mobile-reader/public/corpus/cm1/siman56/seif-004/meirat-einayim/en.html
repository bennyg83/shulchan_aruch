He is not believed. The Tur explains that the custodian's possession gives him no greater credibility than the litigant himself. If the document were in the lender's hand and could not be authenticated, it would be worthless, because one who admits that he wrote a document must still authenticate it. The custodian cannot transform a forged or unauthenticated document into a valid one merely through his own statement. In Perishah I cited Maharshal that the custodian is not even liable to take a Torah oath against the opposing party based on his claim that the latter admitted before him that he owed the amount in the document, unless the parties originally said to him, 'You shall be a witness to this matter.' If they did so, he would have the law of one witness and an oath would be required, as explained in siman 81:10. Otherwise the opposing party can say, 'I was joking with you,' or 'I said it so as not to appear wealthy.'
