Even if one of them contradicts him and says that he did not appoint him as escrow holder. This implies that the escrow holder is believed even where the owner flatly denies ever having entrusted the item to him and says, “You stole it from me.” The Tur states this expressly, following the Baal HaTerumot. The latter explains that the rule is not limited to a case in which the owner admits having delivered the property as a deposit, so that his original confidence in the holder supports the holder’s account. Rather, even when one party says, “You robbed me,” and the holder answers, “No, you appointed me as escrow holder between yourself and the other party, upon such and such terms,” he retains the status of an escrow holder, provided the parties agree about the amount or identity of the property and dispute only whether he received it as escrow or by theft. Since he claims no personal ownership and gains nothing for himself, he is treated as a witness rather than as a litigant. The earlier authorities similarly ruled that when an owner says, “The utensil in your possession is mine and I deposited it with you,” while the holder says, “I hold it in escrow and it belongs to another person, who may come and take it,” the holder need not swear, for an escrow holder is believed against both parties and there is a presumption that a person does not sin for the benefit of another. The Baal HaTerumot and the Tur appear to apply this even when the holder has no alternative claim by which he could have prevailed. Their language is unqualified, and the presumption that one does not sin for another applies independently of such an alternative claim. They further say that the holder is not believed when the dispute concerns the amount entrusted, whereas, if he possessed a valid alternative claim, he would be believed concerning the amount as well. The Rama and the Ir Shushan also speak expressly of a case in which he has no such claim. The Bach likewise writes that if either borrower or lender says, “You seized it from me by robbery,” or, “It is merely deposited with you,” while the holder says that it was placed with him in escrow, he need not swear. Because he does not claim it as his own but for another, the presumption applies even if the matter has already appeared before beit din and he can no longer say that he could have destroyed the instrument. This is unlike the responsum of Maharit, who distinguishes between a case with an alternative claim and one without it. In truth, however, this ruling is very difficult. If accepted without qualification, anyone could steal another’s property, claim to be an escrow holder, conspire with a third person, and divide the proceeds. Nor is the proof cited from the earlier authorities conclusive, because their case begins with an admission by the owner that he deposited the item with the holder. Their statement therefore supports only the question whether a depositary may claim that the deposit was entrusted to him as escrow, not a case in which the owner says that the item was stolen. After examining the other authorities, I found many who disagree with the Baal HaTerumot, although the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe did not cite them. It therefore appears to me that the principal halachah follows those authorities rejected by the Baal HaTerumot: where the interested party denies ever appointing the holder as escrow holder, the holder is not believed. The Rosh ruled in a responsum that if Levi possesses a promissory note owed by Shimon to Reuven and says, “Reuven gave it to me upon condition that I return it to Shimon because Reuven forgave the debt,” while Reuven says, “That is false; the note fell from me and Levi found it,” Levi must return it to Reuven. This is unlike the case in Sanhedrin in which Rav Nachman believed the woman who said that the note deposited with her had been paid, because there the parties admitted that the note had been placed with her as escrow, whereas here Reuven does not admit entrusting anything to Levi. This is expressly contrary to the Baal HaTerumot. It is therefore surprising that the Tur followed the Baal HaTerumot rather than his father, the Rosh. The Bach suggests that the Tur follows his own ruling at the end of siman 41, where a depositary is believed to say that the lender entrusted the note to him, because he could have returned it privately to the lender, and similarly may say that he held it as escrow upon specified conditions. This comparison is not correct. In siman 41 the depositary is believed only by virtue of the alternative action available to him, whereas here the Tur and the Bach themselves say that an escrow holder is believed even without such an alternative claim. Moreover, siman 41 determines only that the depositary may deliver the note to the lender and that the borrower must pay. It does not establish that the depositary is a competent, disinterested witness when the borrower directly accuses him. If the borrower says with certainty, “The note fell from me and you found it,” the depositary must swear and is consequently interested in the testimony. Here, by contrast, the Tur treats the holder as a fully competent witness, with the practical consequence that his testimony may release the other party from an oath. On that point the Rosh clearly disagrees. The Gidulei Terumah questions the responsum of the Rosh on the ground that a lost note is impaired in any event. That objection results from not examining the full responsum. Shimon initially claimed that he had paid and produced the torn note. When beit din required him to swear, he admitted that he had not paid but asserted that Levi had told him in Reuven’s name to take and tear the note because Reuven had forgiven the debt. The Rosh ruled that Levi’s statement had no force, since Reuven denied appointing him, and Shimon, having admitted nonpayment, remained liable for the entire debt. There was no alternative claim available to Levi, because the note was already torn in Shimon’s hands. Thus the responsum does not prove that the Rosh rejects a valid alternative claim in every case. It does prove that, absent an admission of escrow, we do not invoke the presumption that one does not sin for another, and the purported holder has no standing as a witness. Two rules follow from the Rosh. First, if a person says, “You gave me this item to deliver to so and so,” the intended recipient knows nothing of the matter, and the alleged giver denies ever delivering it, the holder must return it to the giver. This is so even if he could previously have delivered it privately to the recipient, and even if he has already delivered it, for the recipient’s knowledge rests solely on the holder’s statement. Second, if the owner admits appointing him as escrow holder upon a condition but says that the condition was not fulfilled, the holder is believed concerning fulfillment of the condition, even where the recipient has no independent knowledge of the gift or escrow. The Rosh distinguished the Sanhedrin case solely because there the note was admittedly entrusted to the woman. He did not distinguish according to whether the debtor himself knew with certainty that payment had occurred. The Gemara in Gittin likewise shows that an acknowledged escrow holder is believed in monetary matters even where the recipient does not know the facts, and Tosafot, the Agudah, and the Mordechai show that this belief does not depend upon an alternative claim. Yet Tosafot and the Agudah limit the rule to a case in which the owner does not say that the article came to the holder as a loan. Their words demonstrate that when the owner says, “It came to you only as a borrowed article, and I never appointed you as escrow holder,” the holder is not believed. The first ruling of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, that a depositary who claims to be an escrow holder is believed without an oath, is therefore also difficult. Otherwise every depositary could conspire with another person and avoid the oath imposed upon depositaries. Even where he has an alternative claim because the item was not seen in his possession, once the owner contradicts him he must swear; otherwise every depositary could escape the rabbinic oath through a fabricated escrow claim. A responsum of Maharam of Prague records that Rabbeinu Baruch required such a holder to swear. He reasoned that when the claimant says, “I gave it to you as a deposit,” the holder cannot exempt himself merely by saying, “I am an escrow holder,” because that would destroy all protection afforded to owners. Rabbeinu Eliezer HaLevi reported that the Riva likewise required an oath due to concern for collusion, although Rabbeinu Tam exempted him on the presumption that one does not sin for another. Rabbeinu Baruch concluded that at least when the claimant expressly says that he gave it as a deposit, the holder must swear. The same conclusion follows from Tosafot and the Agudah. With articles ordinarily lent or rented, where the holder lacks an alternative claim, he must return the article when the owner denies appointing him as escrow holder. With other articles he may sometimes prevail through an alternative claim, but not by virtue of the law of escrow, and he must therefore swear. The Rosh and Nimukei Yosef also write that an escrow holder is believed without such a claim because the parties trusted him. This implies that where the owner says he did not trust or appoint him, the special credibility does not apply. This is the principal halachah, notwithstanding the Tur, Shulchan Aruch, Baal HaTerumot, and the responsum of the Rivash that followed them. It further appears that this was the view of the authorities cited by the Baal HaTerumot who said that any grievance against the escrow holder concerning his stewardship renders him interested in the testimony. The Baal HaTerumot understood them as referring only to a dispute over the amount. That is not persuasive. They did not state such a limitation, and logically a denial of the very appointment is at least as serious as a dispute over amount. Indeed, where the owner admits the appointment but disputes a separate portion of the amount, the holder’s obligation to swear concerning that portion should not disqualify him from testifying about the acknowledged escrow matter, in which he has no interest. The comparison to agents who become interested witnesses because of the rabbinic oath is inexact, for there their interest concerns the very subject of their testimony. Accordingly, where the owner denies appointing the holder as escrow holder, the holder is a litigant and must swear. If he lacks an alternative claim, he must return the property. Where the appointment itself is admitted and the dispute concerns only the escrow instructions, the holder retains the special credibility of an escrow holder. Where the parties agree about the escrow but dispute an additional amount, his oath regarding that additional amount does not necessarily disqualify his testimony concerning the separate, acknowledged escrow.
