If they divided by lot. See Radbaz, part I, siman 324. Two people jointly bought fifty cubits of cloth from a non-Jew, measured it, cut it in half, and divided the halves by lot. A defect was later found in one half that reduced its value. Radbaz rules that the division was mistaken. The lot is no stronger than a completed sale, which can be undone for a defect. Nevertheless, the lot has some effect: the partner who received the defective half may remain with that half if the other partner pays him half of the diminution in value. If the other refuses to pay the loss and instead insists on canceling the division, he may do so. They then appraise the two portions and cast lots again, with an adjustment in money so that the one who receives the defective portion is compensated. The reason the defective partner cannot insist on total cancellation where the other offers half the loss is that unlike an ordinary purchaser, who may say that he wants an entirely sound article, this partner necessarily owned half of the defect from the outset. The lot therefore can determine that he receives the entire defective portion, provided his partner equalizes the values by paying half the loss. Shaar Mishpat notes difficulties in the scope of this ruling. Radbaz's broad wording might imply that the adjustment applies whether the defect is less than one sixth or more than one sixth, which is difficult in light of siman 227:37. He therefore suggests that Radbaz may have dealt specifically with a defect amounting to exactly one sixth. Shaar Mishpat further explains that the principle is most persuasive where, before the original division, it would have been possible to divide the cloth so that each partner received half of the defect, but after the cut each resulting portion no longer admits further division. Since each was necessarily destined to bear half the defect, the lot can determine that one receives all of it with monetary equalization. If each half can still be subdivided fairly, the first division should be canceled and a proper division made. If the defect was from the outset indivisible and even with knowledge of it they would have needed a lot with monetary adjustment, Shaar Mishpat leaves open whether the original lot remains valid.
However, the Rosh wrote. See Shevut Yaakov, part III, siman 163, concerning partners who conducted a lot in the following form: whoever's name was drawn first would choose whichever share he wished, the second would then choose, and then the third. After the drawing they wished to retract. He rules that according to all opinions they may retract. Even according to Rambam and those who hold that a valid lot effects a complete acquisition, the lot must itself identify the share, like the division of Eretz Yisrael where a particular territory and a particular tribe rose together. The explanations of Rashbam, Ran, and Ri Migash likewise indicate this. A drawing that merely determines who chooses first does not itself clarify or transfer any specific share. It therefore creates no acquisition, and each party may retract.
