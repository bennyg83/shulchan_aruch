And neither has a right of passage through the other's property, and so forth. See Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 2, siman 149, concerning brothers who divided a house. One received the room containing a large baking oven, and the deed of partition stated that neither brother would have any right of access through the other's portion, except that Reuven could use the baking oven on Erev Shabbos and Erev Yom Tov. Reuven's wife later gave birth to a son, and the question was whether he may also use the oven for the meal of the circumcision and redemption of the firstborn, on the theory that this is his Yom Tov. He concludes that because deeds are interpreted according to ordinary human speech, only what was explicitly specified is included. This is especially so because brothers who divide are treated as purchasers, and neither has a right of passage through the other's property except what they expressly stipulated. The claimant therefore has the weaker position. Nor can the other be compelled on the ground of middat Sedom, because one must enter his room, and the oven is also damaged by additional heating and similar use. His established right is entirely lost. See Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 3, siman 163. He was asked about one who sold part of a house to another and stated in the deed of sale that the purchaser could use the privy, and afterward the privy collapsed and the pit was filled. Do we say that since it fell, his right is lost? He replied that at first glance this seems explicit in Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 173 concerning a spring well, where if it collapses his established right is entirely lost. Nevertheless, he thought otherwise, because this rule is not found in the Gemara or the early authorities other than the Rashba, whose proof from the end of the chapter HaShoel, concerning lending a well, may be rejected. There it is merely a loan, and since the rule is novel, it should not be extended beyond its stated case. A privy, however, is an essential household need and use, which a person is particular to have near his residence; therefore, even if it collapses, his right should not be lost. He then saw Responsa Bach, siman 7, which writes that a privy is also comparable to a well, though he did not know the source for that ruling, and he regarded his own view as primary. He further saw Responsa Maharam Galanti, siman 67, who writes that even the Rashba spoke only where one granted the use to his fellow by way of gift or loan, but in a sale the purchaser certainly acquires the substance of the land, and one who rules otherwise is simply mistaken.
