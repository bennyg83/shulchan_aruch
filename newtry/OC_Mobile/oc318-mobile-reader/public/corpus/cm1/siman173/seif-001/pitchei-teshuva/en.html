If they divided by lot. See Responsa Radbaz, part 1, siman 324, concerning two men who purchased from a non Jew a piece of cloth fifty cubits long, measured it, cut it into two, and divided the halves by lot. A defect that reduced the value was afterward discovered in one man's share. He was asked whether the division is void. He replied that it is clear that this was a mistaken division, and the one who received the defective share may recover from his fellow for his portion of the defect. Is a lottery stronger than a completed kinyan? In a sale, if a defect is found the transaction may be rescinded. Nevertheless, he writes, the lottery was effective in one respect: each one acquired the portion that fell to him. His fellow must therefore reimburse him for half of the amount by which the defect reduced the value, if the fellow wishes. If the fellow does not wish to pay the reduction but instead wishes to annul the division, we heed him. What is done? The two portions are appraised in money and they cast lots again. Whoever receives the defective portion takes from his fellow the amount by which they valued the sound portion above it. But if the fellow wishes to give him his share of the loss caused by the defect, the division is not annulled. Although ordinarily, when one sells an item and a defect is discovered, the seller cannot say, Accept the reduction in value, because the buyer may answer, I want a complete item, as stated in siman 232, seif 4, this case is different. Here, against his will, the recipient cannot receive a completely sound item, because he must bear half of the defect in any event. Since that is so, the lottery was effective to make him the recipient of the entire defective portion, because half the defect is his in any case, while his fellow reimburses him for half of the reduction in value. This is a reasonable principle and should be followed in judgment. From the unqualified wording of the Radbaz, it appears that his ruling that the fellow reimburses half of the reduction applies even if the reduction is less than one sixth of the value, and likewise even if it is more than one sixth, the recipient must keep the portion that fell to him and his fellow merely pays half the reduction. This appears difficult in light of Shulchan Aruch below, siman 227, seif 37. I found that Shaar Mishpat at the beginning of siman 175 raises this difficulty and writes that the Radbaz must be speaking where the reduction caused by the defect is exactly one sixth, and therefore the reduction is reimbursed while the division remains in force. Shaar Mishpat further writes that the essence of the Radbaz's ruling, that the lottery was effective so that this person acquired his portion, appears to concern a case in which, before the division, the cloth could have been divided so that each would receive half of the defect, but after they divided it there is no further practicable division within either portion. In such a case the reasoning is sound: because in any event he could not have received a completely sound item, the division is effective and he bears the entire defect with reimbursement from his fellow. But if, even after the lottery, each of the two portions can still be divided, there is no basis to say that because he must accept half the defect the lottery made him accept all of it. Since they can still divide fairly, the first division is void. If the circumstances are such that from the outset the defect could not have been divided, and even had they known of it they would have needed a lottery together with monetary equalization, it requires study whether the lottery is void or not.
