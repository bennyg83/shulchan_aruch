Seif 6. "And if the victim seized it, we do not remove it from his possession." Note: See the Beit Shmuel, who writes that it is obvious that seizure is effective, etc. See also Ketzot HaChoshen, who asks from the words of the Maggid Mishneh in Hilchot Gezelah concerning the special ordinance allowing a victim to recover in a case of a deposit, where the claimant relies on the testimony of the defendant's hired worker or household member. There, because the matter is doubtful within a rabbinic ordinance, we leave the matter according to ordinary law and seizure does not help. What Ketzot answers there I already alluded to in my responsum and in my marginal note. See also my responsum to the community of Kartchov in Choshen Mishpat, siman 360. At present it appears that the matter can be explained as follows. Several authorities hold in the laws of kiddushin that a rabbinic rule can acquire Torah force through the principle that property declared ownerless by beit din is ownerless. If so, one can say similarly here that although the rule began as an enactment, since beit din has power to divest ownership it takes on the force of Torah property law, and seizure should therefore be effective. Accordingly, in the present law of improvement, since the enactment clearly assigns the improvement to the purchaser, if we say that beit din's power to divest ownership applies, the purchaser's title is like a Torah title, and seizure has effect. But this is only where the enactment clearly applies. In the case of the victim's oath concerning a deposit, even under the enactment he does not recover until he actually swears, and he has not yet sworn. It therefore cannot yet be said that beit din has transferred the property to him. The case remains only a doubt within the enactment and ordinary law governs. In Even HaEzer siman 118, although the enactment there operates without an oath, it was not an enactment of the Sages of the Gemara but of later authorities, and the same principle of beit din's power cannot necessarily be invoked for a rule that is not part of Talmudic law. This also resolves an old difficulty I raised concerning a claimant who has partially admitted impairment of her document in the presence of witnesses, where there is an unresolved question whether impairment of a perutah upon a perutah requires an oath, and we rule that she may not collect without an oath. Since the oath itself is only rabbinic, why do we not leave a doubt in the enactment according to ordinary law? The borrower is in possession, and even seizure does not ordinarily help in a doubt about an enactment. Here, however, it is appropriate to invoke beit din's power: so long as she has not sworn she does not collect, and therefore the matter is not merely a doubt in the enactment. In the victim's ordinance the direction is opposite, namely that he is allowed to collect through an oath. There we can say, "Who says that he will swear?" Before the oath, beit din has not transferred anything. He therefore cannot swear under the ordinance, and if he cannot swear in the form ordained by the Sages, even an oath he volunteers is ineffective for collection, being legally as though he had not sworn. This is correct. Examine carefully.
