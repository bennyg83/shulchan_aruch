One of the residents of a blind alley, etc. Every alley contains several courtyards. In a through alley, open continuously at both ends, passersby enter from one side and leave from the other, so large numbers already pass through it. The residents therefore cannot claim that an artisan increases traffic and prevent him from working there, since crowds are present anyway. In a blind alley, however, with only one entrance through which people enter and then leave the same way, there is no public passage. Rama concludes that even there some say the claim of increased traffic applies only to residents of the same courtyard, not merely from one courtyard to another within the alley.
Who sought to establish, etc. In the Derishah I proved that 'sought' is not literal. The same rule applies even if he already established this trade and practiced it there for many years. Chazakah does not help. Rambam states this, and the Mechaber cites it below in seif 4.
A physician or artisan. The word 'artisan' means a bloodletter. Rashi explains 'physician' as a mohel, and Ir Shushan follows him here, but that cannot be the Mechaber's view, because in seif 3 he rules that one cannot protest against activities that are mitzvot. It therefore appears that 'physician' means one who treats people's bodies, and 'artisan' means bloodletter. This fits the reading 'physician or artisan.' Rambam and the Tur read 'an artisan physician,' which Beit Yosef explains as a physician who is a professional practitioner.
A scribe of documents. This excludes a scribe of Torah scrolls and sacred books, which is a mitzvah activity, unlike Ramban, who holds that neighbors can protest even there. See the Derishah.
Instruction that is not Torah. Darkei Moshe cites Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah: if one teaches children arithmetic or geometry, the neighbors can protest.
Even if all consented except one. The same applies in every case in this siman where a neighbor has a right to object. But regarding communal enactments, the Rosh writes in klal 6, ruling 4, that one individual cannot nullify them, because there we follow the majority.
He should not rent it to him, etc. The Tur concludes that this applies even if he does not evict the current tenant until the other is ready to rent it. See the Derishah, where I distinguish rental from sale.
But to sell it, some say it is permitted. The reason appears to be that a sale does not as clearly show that the purchaser intends immediately to conduct that trade there. Perhaps he bought it simply as property, to reside there through his son or rent it to someone else until he ceases that occupation. If the purchaser later begins the trade, the courtyard residents can litigate directly with him. Therefore the Sages did not prevent the sale where the damage is not certain. Rental is different. Since it is for a limited time and people generally rent a house in order to occupy it immediately, the residents may object to the landlord. In the Derishah I wrote that Mordechai cites Riva as taking the opposite view regarding sale to a non-Jew who is not an artisan; see there for his reasoning.
