The neighbors may object. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 92, discusses three brothers who inherited a house. Two received the upper area and one the lower area. One upstairs brother wished to sell liquor by the measure to customers, while the lower brother objected because customers would enter and leave through the courtyard shared by all three. The questioner thought the objection valid, comparing it to opening a shop into a partners' courtyard. Chatam Sofer agrees that, as a matter of law, increased traffic and disturbance can support an objection even when sales occur by day; the reference to inability to sleep is not confined to nighttime, for noise and traffic disturb ordinary residential use at all hours. He rejects the suggestion that prolonged exile and economic need have generally abrogated these rights. Yet he develops an important qualification from Responsa Ralbach: where an occupation cannot realistically be conducted in the marketplace and must be carried on in the home, neighbors cannot cut off a person's livelihood merely because the necessary work creates noise, just as the Mishna permits tools to be made at home and sold in the market. Thus a wine or liquor business that depends on customers sitting and drinking on the premises may sometimes be treated like necessary home production rather than ordinary retail. He nevertheless distinguishes ordinary wine from spirits, where gatherings are more likely to produce disorder and other concrete harms, and therefore concludes that an objection to a spirits business may be stronger. The ruling ultimately depends upon the particular nature of the business and the harms it predictably creates.
