Open at both ends. Every alley contains several courtyards. An alley open at both ends is one that remains open on two sides, so that passersby enter from one side and leave from the opposite side. Since the public already passes through it, the residents cannot claim against the craftsman that he increases the number of people entering, nor can they prevent him from practicing his trade there, because many people are present there even without him. In an alley that is not open at both ends, however, there is only one entrance, and those who enter must return the same way; it is not a public thoroughfare. The Rama concludes that even there some authorities hold that the residents of the alley cannot object, and only those who live with him in the same courtyard may object on the ground that he increases the traffic upon them. This is the Sema's language. See Responsa Maharashdam, simanim 421, 441, and 267, and Responsa Rashach, part 2, siman 173.

A physician. Rashi explains this as a mohel, and the Levush likewise writes so, but this is imprecise. The Mechaber cannot hold this view, because in seif 3 he writes that one cannot object to activities that are mitzvot. It therefore appears that 'physician' means one who treats people's bodies, while 'craftsman' means a bloodletter, according to the text that reads 'physician or craftsman.' The Rambam and Tur, however, read 'a physician-craftsman,' and according to that text it can mean a physician who is a craftsman. The Beit Yosef likewise explains it so. This is the Sema's language. The Taz writes that even the Rambam means 'or a craftsman,' because he concludes, 'he may not rent it to a physician nor to a craftsman.' As for the Sema's objection to explaining 'physician' as a mohel from the mitzvah cases in seif 3, the Taz asks what has been gained, for is healing lives not itself a mitzvah? Rather, the examples of mitzvah activities given there, such as distributing charity or praying with a quorum, show that the exception applies specifically to a mitzvah that depends upon gathering people together. Where one can instead go to the homes of those who need his service, the neighbors may prevent him from serving as a mohel if doing so would cause many people to come to his house.
