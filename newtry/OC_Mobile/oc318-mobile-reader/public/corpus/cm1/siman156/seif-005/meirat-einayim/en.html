The residents of an alley may compel one another, etc. The Mechaber's words are the Rambam's, and they require careful explanation. The Rambam sometimes speaks in the plural and sometimes the singular, and mixes the grounds of loss of livelihood with the burden of increased traffic. The Beit Yosef offers two explanations, but they are difficult upon close reading. I will therefore set out the baraita and Gemara. Bava Batra 21b teaches: Residents of alleys may compel one another not to establish among them a tailor, tanner, or other artisan, but one cannot compel his neighbor. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua says: It is obvious that a resident of this town may prevent a resident of another town, but if the outsider pays the town tax he cannot prevent him. Whether a resident of one alley can prevent a resident of another alley remains unresolved. Since the question was unresolved, we rule leniently for the defendant, and one resident of an alley cannot prevent a resident of another alley. The Rambam understands the baraita's first clause as based on increased traffic. It must therefore concern a closed alley, because that concern applies only there. The plural "residents of alleys" does not mean that one entire alley can prevent another alley, for increased traffic is not a basis for objection from one alley to another. Rather, it means that in each alley any one resident can compel the other residents not to establish such an artisan among them. Even if all other residents consent, the one who objects may say that the artisan will increase traffic past him. The clause, "but he cannot compel his neighbor," refers to a neighbor who already lives with him in that alley or in another alley of the same town, in accordance with the lenient resolution of Rav Huna's question. This must concern a situation where another artisan of that trade is already present and no one objected to him. Otherwise everyone agrees that any resident may object even to a person from his own alley because of increased traffic. Since an artisan is already there, the Rambam holds that this traffic argument is no longer available. The existing artisan himself likewise cannot prevent the newcomer for loss of livelihood, because the newcomer is his neighbor and fellow townsman. This explains why the Rambam says in the plural that "they cannot prevent" him, meaning neither the existing artisan nor the other alley residents. If the newcomer comes from another city, however, the existing artisan may prevent him from establishing anywhere in the town because of loss of livelihood, unless he pays the local tax. When he pays the tax, the Rambam treats him as a town resident. The other commentators understand the baraita differently. They read it as speaking of ordinary open alleys, where increased traffic is not a valid objection. It therefore must concern a place where another artisan already exists, and the objection is loss of livelihood. On their reading, the reason the baraita limits the objection to the alley rather than the whole town is that the newcomer already pays the local tax. This is the basis of Rama's second gloss, citing the many authorities who nevertheless allow the local artisan to object even in that case. The first dispute cited by Rama follows as well. According to the Rambam, because the baraita concerns a closed alley and nevertheless says one cannot compel his neighbor, once one artisan already exists the other residents cannot object to another artisan on grounds of increased traffic. According to the dissenters, the baraita concerns an open alley, where traffic is no objection, so in a closed alley, where the law resembles a courtyard, the residents may object to an additional artisan because of added traffic, even if one artisan already operates there. The existing artisan himself cannot object merely on livelihood grounds to a resident of another alley in the same town, because Rav Huna's unresolved question is decided leniently for the defendant. With this explanation the Rambam, Mechaber, and Rama are all consistent.
And the townspeople may object in every circumstance, etc. This is somewhat difficult, because it was already explained that the argument of increased traffic does not apply in an open alley according to anyone, and certainly not from one alley to another throughout the town. The meaning must be that the existing artisan cannot prevent the newcomer from entering the town on grounds of loss of livelihood except within the alley where the artisan himself lives. In another alley he cannot prevent him. The residents of the town, however, may each object with respect to their own courtyard or closed alley because of increased traffic. This remains so even if the newcomer pays local taxes, because residents of a courtyard can compel one another not to establish an artisan or weaver there. According to the dissenters, even where one artisan already exists they may still object to an additional artisan on this ground, as explained above. See the Ir Shushan, whose words on this seif are very confused and internally contradictory.
