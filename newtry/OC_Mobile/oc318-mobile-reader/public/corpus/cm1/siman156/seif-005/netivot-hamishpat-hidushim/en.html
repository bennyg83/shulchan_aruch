The residents of an alley can compel. The Sema explains the Rambam and those who disagree with him by first presenting the Gemara in Bava Batra 21: the residents of alleyways may compel one another not to establish among them another practitioner of the same trade, but one cannot compel his neighbor. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua said: it is obvious to me that a resident of one town can prevent a resident of another town; if the outsider pays the taxes of this town, he cannot prevent him. Whether a resident of one alley may prevent a resident of another alley remains unresolved. We rule leniently for the defendant in an unresolved question, so one cannot prevent even a resident of another alley. The Mechaber, following the Rambam, rules in seif 1 that in a closed alley residents may prevent establishment of a trade because of increased traffic. He interprets the first clause of the baraita, that residents may prevent establishment of another practitioner, as concerning a closed alley with no existing practitioner, and the reason is increased traffic. The latter clause, that one cannot compel his neighbor, refers to a case where another practitioner is already in the alley. Then residents cannot object because of increased traffic, since such traffic already exists, and only the existing practitioner wishes to object because his livelihood will be diminished. The baraita teaches that he cannot prevent his neighbor on that ground. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua then says that a person from another town who does not pay the local tax may certainly be prevented because of loss of livelihood, while an outsider who does pay the tax is treated like a town resident and cannot be prevented, following the lenient resolution of the unresolved question. The Rama cites authorities who understand the first clause of the baraita as referring to an open alley, where increased traffic is not a concern. It must therefore concern a place where another practitioner is already present, and the objection rests on loss of livelihood. It cannot refer to an outsider who pays no local tax, because the entire town can object to such a person. Nor can it refer to an actual resident of the same town, because under Rav Huna's unresolved question such a person is his "neighbor." It must therefore refer to an outsider who pays the local tax. This proves, according to those authorities, that even such an outsider can be prevented because of loss of livelihood. That is the view cited in the Rama's second gloss. Consequently, since they interpret the first clause as concerning an open alley, they also hold that in a closed alley the residents may object because of increased traffic even if another practitioner is already present. This is the view in the Rama's first gloss. This is the general framework of their positions; I will now explain the clauses individually. Sema:
Residents of an alley. Meaning a closed alley, and the objection is increased traffic. This is the Rambam's view in seif 1:
He cannot prevent him. Meaning the existing practitioner cannot prevent the newcomer because of loss of livelihood. The statement, "even if he is from another alley," refers back to this: even if the person who wishes to set up nearby comes from another alley, the practitioner cannot stop him merely because of competition:
They cannot. The text should read, "and they cannot," meaning that the residents of the alley likewise cannot prevent him because of increased traffic once another practitioner is already there and already draws traffic. The Rama disagrees and holds that even where another practitioner is present, residents of a closed alley may object because the newcomer adds traffic. The baraita in Bava Batra saying that one cannot compel his neighbor therefore concerns an open alley, where increased traffic is not a concern:
A shop beside his shop. This is not literal, because an outsider who pays no town tax can be prevented from operating anywhere in the town. Sema. See the Beurim. The ruling teaches that even in circumstances where the town cannot exclude him entirely, as explained in seif 7, such as on market day, the local practitioner can still prevent him from setting up directly beside him because of loss of livelihood, even in an alley that does not belong to the practitioner or in the public domain:
He pays the king's tax, he cannot demand. Meaning he cannot prevent him even from setting up nearby. On this the Rama cites the dissenting authorities who hold that even one who pays the local tax can at least be prevented from establishing himself directly beside the existing practitioner:
And the townspeople can. Meaning each town resident may object to someone operating a trade within his courtyard or within a closed alley because of increased traffic. In an open alley, and certainly from one alley to another, increased traffic is not a valid objection. Sema:
To engage in his trade and pay tax. This refers to a case where the townspeople themselves do not object. Nevertheless, the existing practitioner may still object on the ground of loss of livelihood. Sema:
