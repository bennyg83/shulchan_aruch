The residents of an alley may compel. This is explained as a case where no one of that trade is presently there. The residents may prevent the newcomer because he increases traffic, as in seif 1. Where that trade is already present and the residents themselves do not object, they cannot object to an additional person merely because of traffic, and the remaining dispute is whether the existing craftsman may object because the newcomer reduces his livelihood. The halachah follows the first Tanna, as the Rif rules. This is the meaning of, "but he may not compel his neighbor." Otherwise the opening clause would have no clear subject.
Many authorities say. Ramban and Tosafos indicate there is no such distinction. Even when that trade already exists, residents of a closed alley may object to an outsider from another alley because of increased traffic. The statement permitting competition concerns a through-alley, where increased traffic is not a valid claim. Darchei Moshe likewise writes that this supports the Rif's distinction between through and closed alleys. The unresolved question of Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua therefore concerns only whether a craftsman may object on livelihood grounds where traffic itself supplies no objection.
But one who lives... All this concerns the claim that the newcomer cuts off the existing craftsman's livelihood. See Bach.
Many authorities say. Tosafos and the commentators explain the opening clause in this manner because otherwise "they compel" has no subject.
Since. Otherwise residents of another alley could already object because of increased traffic, as above.
The residents of the courtyard. The text should read "courtyard." They may object because of people entering and leaving. This differs from the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch's distinction above. According to Tosafos, four categories emerge: courtyard residents may always object because of traffic; residents of a closed alley may object to someone from another alley but not to a resident of their own alley; residents of a through-alley cannot object even to someone from another alley on traffic grounds; and a resident of another city may be opposed even if he pays some taxes, depending on whether he is attached to the local tax burden.
If... some disagree. This is the dispute between Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam. Rabbeinu Tam understands attachment to the local tax as sufficient even if he has not previously paid, whereas Rashi requires that he already be paying it. Or Zarua rules like Rashi. See Hagahos Ashri.
Until he rents to him. The Rosh writes in a responsum that the Gemara speaks of someone who lives elsewhere and is not part of the local tax. A person may reside where he wishes. Beit Yosef says this accords with Rabbeinu Tam. Darchei Moshe adds that Rashi can also agree where the person actually moves into the town, because Rashi's case is one who remains resident elsewhere and merely works here.
Some say not... Maharik infers from Rashi that the relevant tax is not the king's fixed levy but the local head tax. The Gemara itself distinguishes the terms for royal levy and poll tax.
There is no distinction... because there is no. From the Gemara's discussion of itinerant merchants and Torah scholars. A Torah scholar is favored where his ordinary mode of earning differs, but where conduct is prohibited to everyone, scholarship gives no exemption. Compare the communal excavation levy in Bava Basra 8a.
Some say that if. Like teachers of children, regarding whom Rav Huna concedes. The same applies to teachers generally. See seif 10 in the gloss and seif 3.
Even though. The Gemara says this regarding teachers of children.
A Torah scholar who brings. This is explicit in the Gemara.
Some permit... and some prohibit. This is essentially the earlier dispute and depends on the explanation of the fishery case. One view follows Rashi: although taking ownerless property generally does not render one wicked, here he could place his net elsewhere and this is his regular livelihood, so interference can be prohibited. On that reasoning, interference with an established nonexclusive commercial relationship may likewise be prohibited. The other view follows Ri Migash, who explains that the fish send scouts and, upon seeing the first net, flee entirely, so the second fisherman destroys the first's livelihood altogether. Mere reduction of livelihood is not enough, for each may say that Heaven apportions each person's earnings.
Nevertheless, if. As with property of a non-Jew that becomes ownerless, where Rashbam still describes one who preempts another as wicked. Kiddushin likewise says that what he did is legally effective but blameworthy.
Two creditors. As in Bava Basra, where each may say, "I am dividing my own share," and Bava Metzia 60a.
