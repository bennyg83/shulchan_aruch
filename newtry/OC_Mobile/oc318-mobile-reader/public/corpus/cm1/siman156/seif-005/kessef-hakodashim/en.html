Seif 7. (At a lower price.) Regarding what is written in Choshen Mishpat 156, according to one opinion, that when an outsider sells more cheaply the local tradesmen cannot prevent him: where the objectors are numerous and presently have greater practical power, while the purchasers are unwilling to support the seller sufficiently to enable him to withstand them, the objectors may in practice prevent him under the rule of "whoever is stronger prevails," as at the end of siman 139. In our matter there is a dispute among the great authorities, with no decisive ruling in Beit Yosef, and Shulchan Aruch records only a "some say" opinion. If, however, many purchasers later sign in support of the seller so that they have sufficient strength to withstand the objectors, the objectors may no longer prevent him merely on the basis of practical force. The rule in siman 139 differs, because once one litigant has prevailed, the other may not thereafter overpower him, since the first acquired possession of the movable property or land immediately under that rule. Here there is no specific property acquired by prevailing; the practical result depends upon the relative strength at each time. If one Jewish party is stronger, the other may not go and prevail through non Jewish authorities, as in siman 4. If he nevertheless does so, his fellow may counter him through lawful strategies involving those authorities. This matter requires further study. If the outsider sells wholesale to townspeople who then sell the merchandise, there is room for greater leniency, as indicated in Beit Yosef. In currency exchange it may be that there is no distinction between wholesale and retail, because merchandise bought for use has a recognizable measure beyond normal use, whereas coins are all intended for circulation. The concern mentioned by Beit Yosef, that ordinary commerce in goods will be disrupted, likewise does not apply in precisely the same manner to currency exchange, since currencies and their movement between countries depend upon governmental arrangements and are not permanently fixed. If the outsider sells more cheaply than the townspeople and is personally powerful, while townspeople buy from him and resell to non Jews, their status depends upon the transaction. If they have not bought absolutely, with the risk upon themselves and without an obligation to return the merchandise, they are merely his agents, and the local tradesmen can prevent them because they are no better than one who derives his right from the outsider. If they buy absolutely for resale, one might compare this to siman 175, seif 28, where an evasive device does not help. Although legal devices sometimes work in rabbinic law, that rule too is rabbinic, yet an arrangement made merely to evade it is ineffective. Still, the various enactments of the Sages are not always identical, just as one does not automatically derive one rabbinic prohibition from another, and perhaps monetary enactments differ from prohibitory law. It therefore remains possible that such an arrangement works here, and the matter requires further study. A resident artisan can ordinarily prevent a visiting artisan from competing. If the visitor performs the work more cheaply, perhaps he is treated more leniently, like a merchant who sells at a lower price. The same may be true where his workmanship is materially finer or stronger. For his livelihood, however, the visitor can arrange to work for a local artisan as an employee or genuine partner. If visiting customers pay separately for their meals, that expense may itself make the visitor's service effectively cheaper, under the principle that an additional household member does not add proportionately to the household's expense.
