The residents of an alley may compel. See Sema, seif katan 16. Rambam's wording, "beside his shop," is not incidental. Even where the townspeople cannot prevent the newcomer from trading altogether, such as on market day, where he has local credit, or where he sells more cheaply, as explained later, they may still prevent him from setting up directly beside an established shop because of interference with livelihood. A resident of the same town who pays the same taxes is not treated this way, while a resident of another town who does not contribute to the local tax may be restrained because of interference with livelihood. This also resolves the difficulty raised on Rashi and Tosafot concerning the baraita that residents of an alley may prevent another from settling among them. It can refer to an outsider who does not pay the local tax, even where the whole town cannot prevent his general commerce because of one of the exceptions just mentioned. Shach, seif katan 3, asks why Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua was uncertain regarding another alley if a taxpaying outsider is clearly not worse. Beit Yosef answers that a taxpaying outsider has no fixed alley of his own, whereas a local tradesman has another alley in which to operate. In my view Rambam means more: a nonresident may be prevented from opening beside the established local merchant even in another alley or in the public domain, because the local resident has already established his livelihood there and supports the town through taxes. This is why Rambam says "they may prevent him," rather than that the individual may prevent him from his own premises. The town may act to protect the local taxpayer from one who cuts off his livelihood. The remaining question is why the unresolved problem is ruled leniently merely because the entrant acts in his own property, when in other unresolved neighbor-damage cases one may object initially. This requires study.
