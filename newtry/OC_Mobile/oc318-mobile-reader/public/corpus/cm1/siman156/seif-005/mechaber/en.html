The residents of an alleyway may compel one another not to admit among them a tailor, tanner, or other craftsman. If a resident craftsman was already present and they did not object, or there was already a bathhouse, shop, or mill there, and another resident opened a competing bathhouse or business opposite it, the first cannot prevent him by claiming, 'You are cutting off my livelihood.' Even if the newcomer is from another alleyway, they cannot prevent him, since that trade is already present among them. Rama: Many authorities say that the residents of a courtyard or closed alleyway may object even in such a case; only the existing tradesman himself cannot object on the ground that the newcomer is cutting off his livelihood. A person from another city who comes to open a shop beside this person's shop, or a bathhouse beside his bathhouse, may be prevented. If he pays the royal tax together with the townspeople, they cannot prevent him. Rama: Many authorities say that even then he has no right to enter an alleyway in which members of his trade are established. The existing tradesman may prevent an outsider from entering his alleyway because he is from another city, although he cannot prevent him from operating in another alleyway if he pays the tax. The townspeople may object in every case and say that they cannot sleep because of the noise of those entering, even if that trade already exists there. If he had not previously paid tax and now comes to engage in his trade and pay tax, some say a member of that trade may prevent him until he rents a house and becomes one of the townspeople. Some say payment of the royal levy is not considered payment of the local tax unless he pays his own tax to the ruler of that city, thereby becoming subject to their jurisdiction. In all these matters there is no distinction between a Torah scholar and another person; the distinction applies only to peddlers, as explained below. Some say that if the townspeople need his Torah learning they cannot prevent him, even if another Torah scholar is already in the city, because rivalry among scholars increases wisdom. When a Torah scholar brings merchandise to a city, the residents must prevent everyone else from selling until he has sold his merchandise. If non-Jews are selling so that this preference yields him no benefit, anyone may sell. See Yoreh Deah, siman 243. Where a person has an established non-Jewish customer, some places rule that others may not encroach upon his livelihood by dealing with that customer, while other places do not so rule. Some permit another Jew to approach that non-Jew, lend him money, transact with him, induce him with a gift, and draw him away, because a non-Jew's property is treated as ownerless in this respect and whoever acts first acquires the opportunity; others prohibit this. Even when a Jew regularly performs work for a non-Jew, another Jew may not enter and underbid him. If he seeks to do so, he is rebuked. If he nevertheless did so, the proceeds are not taken from him. See below, siman 227. If two people live together and one wishes to offer non-Jews loans at a lower interest rate, the other cannot prevent him. The same applies in comparable cases. See below, siman 228, seif 18.
