He cannot prevent him and say to him, etc. Beit Yosef cites Aviassaf concerning a three-sided closed alley. Reuven lived at the closed end, and Shimon sought to establish himself at the entrance, so that every customer reaching Reuven would first pass Shimon. Aviassaf allowed Reuven to object, because this arrangement effectively cuts off his livelihood. Although Beit Yosef understood Aviassaf as inconsistent with the accepted ruling of Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua, Darkei Moshe and later authorities distinguish ordinary competition from a case of certain and total displacement. Where the second entrant merely competes, the first cannot object; where the second's position necessarily diverts all customers and eliminates the first trade entirely, even a local resident may be restrained. Mashat Binyamin and She'erit Yosef make the same distinction where a concession or lease can be held by only one person. Chatam Sofer applies this principle to communal kashrut income and to a butcher whose established occupation would be wholly displaced. He emphasizes that the prohibition is especially strong when the newcomer does not merely weaken the first person's income but destroys that particular livelihood altogether. At the same time, later responsa distinguish cases in which new shops are merely better situated and customers may still choose among competitors. Beit Shmuel Acharon and Beit Ephraim therefore permit new shops on more favorable routes when they do not eliminate the old merchants' trade entirely. The governing distinction is between lawful competition and conduct that predictably cuts off another's livelihood altogether.
