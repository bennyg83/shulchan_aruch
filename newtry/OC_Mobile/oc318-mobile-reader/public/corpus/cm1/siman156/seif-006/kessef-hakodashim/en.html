(Who fled to an inhabited place because of danger.) Shulchan Aruch rules that people who flee because of danger may work in the place to which they fled despite the local artisans. It appears that this license is clearly necessary when the townspeople do not provide them with food. If the town fully supports the refugee, however, he should not ordinarily encroach upon the livelihood of the local artisans by practicing their trade. His argument that his wife and household elsewhere need support does not by itself resolve the matter, for the local artisans likewise need to support their wives and households. Where the town supports the refugees, it is therefore proper to arrange a compromise with the local artisans through the communal leaders. There is nevertheless a substantial argument that one who has fled danger for an indefinite period, without knowing when he can return, is treated like a resident of the place to which he fled. The town's support is then merely a gift and does not diminish his independent right to earn a livelihood there, particularly because he still needs to support his household. If it were certain that his earnings were sufficient for his support, the town might cease supporting him, but that is often impossible to know with certainty. If the support turns out not to have been necessary, it was simply a gift. Thus, if the Sages established a general status for refugees as temporary residents, the permission to work may apply even where the town also gives them support. On the other hand, it remains proper to avoid needless encroachment upon local artisans, and for that reason compromise is best. If the danger is known to be only for a few days, and it is certain that after seven or ten days they will safely return home, it is more likely that the Sages did not treat them as residents. In such a case the permission to work was granted only so they can live from their own labor rather than depend upon others. If the town is already providing their food, their need to support their families must be weighed against the identical needs of the local artisans. One might argue that the Sages did not distinguish among individual financial circumstances. Sometimes a wealthy refugee arrives while the local artisans are poor, yet the general permission still applies. Perhaps, therefore, the rule treats all refugees as residents whether the danger has a foreseeable end or not. In my view, however, the more plausible general rule is that they are treated as residents only when there is no certain near end to the danger. Where return is certainly imminent, their license extends only to what is needed for sustenance. If the town supplies that sustenance, they should not compete further. The matter is not fully decided, but the local townspeople may also have some standing comparable to a prior possessor. It requires careful study.
