From the sound of the hammer. See Sema, seif katan 10, who explains that the hammering sound is generated inside the artisan's own house, and therefore the neighbor cannot object, whereas the noise of people entering and leaving occurs in the jointly owned courtyard and can therefore be objected to. This explanation can fit the authorities cited by Rama who hold that even initially one cannot object to the sound of hammering. But according to Mechaber, who holds that even hammering can be objected to initially, there is no substantive distinction between hammering and the sound of people entering and leaving except concerning whether chazakah is effective. A different reason is therefore necessary. With customers entering and leaving, new people arrive every day. The disturbance is continually renewed by different persons. Chazakah therefore does not help against it, like smoke or a privy, because the neighbor can say, "I thought I could tolerate it, but now I can no longer tolerate it." The sound of a particular hammering activity, by contrast, is one fixed recurring disturbance. Chazakah can therefore be effective after the fact even though one could have objected initially. Perishah gives this rationale. This is also how Maggid Mishneh, cited by Beit Yosef, must be understood. He writes that Rambam learned that one can initially object to hammering because hammering is certainly no better than the sound of people entering. At first glance, if so, chazakah should also fail for hammering just as it fails for incoming customers. Necessarily the distinction is the one I have given. Initial objection depends on the fact of disturbance, while the effectiveness of later chazakah depends on whether the disturbance is the same fixed activity or is continually renewed by new entrants. Sema himself alludes to this rationale in seif katan 15.
