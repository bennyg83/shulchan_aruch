Provided that they sell in the marketplace. In the Perishah I explained the Tur to hold that on market day outsiders may sell in the market even to local residents, without distinction, provided they do not go door to door. Only lending is prohibited even in the market to local people, because a loan produces profit after market day as interest or gain continues from week to week. A sale, by contrast, is completed at the time of sale. See what I wrote there further.
They may sell enough for their livelihood. The Tur says they may sell enough to earn a livelihood from the profit until they collect their outstanding debts. In the Perishah I wrote that Rambam deliberately changes the Gemara's expression. The Gemara says 'enough for their life,' from which some infer that they may preserve the principal and sustain themselves from profit, because otherwise once principal is consumed they will have nothing. Rambam instead says 'enough for their support,' which does not imply indefinite subsistence from profit, and appears to limit sales to the amount needed for their present support even from principal. Perhaps this is why the Mechaber does not say 'from the profit.' Rama, however, later writes like the Tur regarding a lender, allowing him to remain until he earns enough, and there is little basis to distinguish loans from merchandise sales.
Until they pay their debt. This wording implies they themselves owe others and remain until they pay. Otherwise it should say 'until their debts are paid to them.' When the text continues, 'if they have a loan,' it means a loan owed by them. The Tur says instead, 'until they collect their debt.' Both are correct in law, for the reason applies either way.
Or if it is of better quality, etc. Beit Yosef writes that it is reasonable that the same applies if their goods are worse, because then they are effectively a different type. He leaves the matter requiring study.
Therefore they can prevent them, etc. Even one who does not himself engage in commerce may object and say, 'You are cutting off my livelihood with opportunities that would otherwise come to me.' Hagahot Maimoniyot and Mahari Weil, responsum 106, state this.
The ban of residential rights. Meaning, the townspeople impose a communal ban that no one may house a newcomer without permission of the entire community. Even if the newcomer wishes to live with a non-Jew or build his own house, they may enact a ban that no member of the community conduct business with him. The ban does not intrinsically fall upon the newcomer, because under strict law they may not be able to exclude him. A rabbi's ban upon his student, however, does take effect. This is the meaning of the continuation, 'if there is a rabbi in the city, etc.'
The residential ban does not apply to a Torah scholar. Darkei Moshe cites Or Zarua to Berachot that Rabbeinu Tam declined to rule in support of such residential restrictions, saying the early authorities instituted them only because of violent men and informers unwilling to pay taxes. He criticizes customs that exclude a person needed by the community who does not interfere with others' livelihoods, calling this a serious wrong and the trait of Sodom. See Maharik and the numerous responsa cited by Darkei Moshe concerning residential rights and communal agreements.
And they assert a chazakah that exists, etc. See above siman 37, where Rama writes that matters of residential rights and similar public needs may be adjudicated even on hearsay and with otherwise disqualified witnesses, because in any event all local residents are interested parties. This follows Terumat HaDeshen, siman 342.
And they can prevent, etc. Mordechai rules that if the ruler on his own says, 'I do not want any Jew to live here except with Reuven's permission,' Reuven may prevent others from settling. This implies that Reuven may not solicit such an order from the ruler. Maharik similarly writes that if the ruler's law was enacted merely for his own benefit, not to benefit Reuven by granting him an exclusive right to exclude others, then Reuven cannot prevent another from settling even if he suffers loss from the newcomer.
