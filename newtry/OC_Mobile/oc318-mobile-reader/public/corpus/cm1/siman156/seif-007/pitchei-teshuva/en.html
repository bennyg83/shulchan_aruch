A person who has a regular non-Jewish customer, etc. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 42, discusses Reuven, who had sold horses for a nobleman and held the proceeds with instructions to purchase superior replacement horses. Before Reuven succeeded in doing so, Shimon sold suitable horses to the nobleman, who directed Reuven to pay Shimon from the funds in his hands. Reuven claimed that Shimon had invaded his established commercial relationship. Chavot Yair ruled that Shimon was free of liability and was not even deemed wicked under the rule of one who interferes with another's pending acquisition. The anticipated transaction was neither certain nor yet in existence, and even if Reuven had already possessed horses and negotiated with the nobleman, Shimon's completing a sale before the price was finalized would not necessarily create that status. Chavot Yair also explains that the law of an established customer generally supports communal restraint or sanction, not extraction of money by beit din after the fact. Chatam Sofer accepts this criticism of Maharshal, though he disputes Chavot Yair's broader suggestion that entering another's occupation is generally permitted. The term 'regular' in this context means a continuing source of livelihood, not merely two prior dealings. Chatam Sofer further rejects an agreement among merchants to mutually waive claims for prohibited commercial encroachment. Such an arrangement would increase strife and desecration of God's name, and halachically it cannot validate conduct forbidden by Torah law, such as theft, overreaching, or wrongful encroachment. Even a formal acquisition would not cure the problem, both because the undertaking is indeterminate and because it concerns future matters not yet in existence.
