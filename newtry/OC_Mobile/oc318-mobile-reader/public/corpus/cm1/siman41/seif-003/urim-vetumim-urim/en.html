The heirs may say, “We paid.” Sema asks from siman 255:6, where Mechaber follows Rosh that one cannot claim payment against a written will. He answers that if the deceased instructed his heirs to give a gift, they may claim that they already gave it, while there the deceased acknowledged that he owed someone money and the will remained in the claimant's hands, making payment implausible. Taz distinguishes between a general will distributing amounts to many people, against which heirs may claim payment, and a will directed to one specific person, against which they may not because they should have taken back and torn the document. There is no concern that he will find it. The person who lost the document does not know the place of the loss. Beit din must therefore notify the person in whose custody the document was lost that a replacement has been issued, for otherwise that custodian, who may know where it was lost, might innocently find it and return it to the creditor. Once informed, he is not suspected of wrongdoing. It must also be clear that the creditor had entrusted the document to him and that it was lost from him accidentally. And every document for which copies are made, etc. Sema emends the wording and explains that the earlier prohibition on issuing a copy of a sale deed with responsibility concerns a copy carrying full operative force. But one may make a literal transcript from the original and expressly state that it is only a copy. Because the copy itself shows that the original deed exists, there is no concern of double collection. Sema's language is somewhat obscure. Such a copy certainly helps establish title to the property given or sold, and that is precisely why it is useful. But regarding collection from encumbered property based on responsibility, it cannot be used without production of the original deed. Therefore, in a sale there is no concern that he will collect twice; foreclosure requires the original. Likewise, in a loan one may issue such a copy. Its utility is that if the debtor claims total denial, the copy refutes him. But if he claims payment and says he returned the original deed, he is believed, because otherwise there would be double-collection concern. Thus Rama writes that every document may be copied in this limited form. Still, although Rama's language sounds broad enough to include loan documents, Rivash appears to permit this only for sale and gift deeds, where the copy serves to maintain title to the property, and not for loan documents, because the borrower does not want additional instruments proliferating against him. This requires study.
