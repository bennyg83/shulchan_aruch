And even if it was lost before witnesses, etc. Rosh, responsum klal 94, derives this law from Ketubot, where a woman who says her ketubah was lost is treated as though she may have hidden it, because we fear she will later find the first ketubah and collect once through it and a second time through proof of the wedding procession. Rashbam in Perek Get Pashut implies that if there are witnesses to the loss, replacement can help. Rosh explains that Rashbam must mean a loss by fire or another destruction witnessed in a manner proving the original instrument cannot reappear. At first glance, the Ketubot proof applies according to the opinion that receipts are not written. According to the accepted view that receipts may be written, once the woman collects the first time the husband can obtain a receipt and prevent a second collection. Likewise, in ordinary loan documents, the concern of a receipt against purchasers does not operate in the same way. Beit Yosef answers that we still fear the creditor will later find the complete original document and collect with it. When the debtor sees the original document, he may not suspect any danger and may simply take the returned paper after payment rather than demand a separate receipt. The creditor can then use the replacement document later. Shach asks from Ketubot itself: if that is the concern, why does the Gemara answer by saying receipts are written? The woman could first collect with the original ketubah and later collect through witnesses to the wedding procession. Shach therefore suggests that when a woman is paid on the basis of her ketubah and delivers the ketubah to the husband, that surrendered document itself functions like a receipt. He then tries to extend the same logic to loan documents and distinguishes a case where the debtor was never informed that another document had been prepared. His discussion is not persuasive. Beit Yosef's answer is correct. According to Tosafot, where a woman claims a ketubah after divorce or widowhood, the husband often cannot simply say "I paid" even if she lacks the physical ketubah, because the obligation arises from an act of beit din. He therefore must protect himself with a receipt or by keeping the surrendered ketubah. He has strong reason to do so. In our ordinary loan case, by contrast, if the debtor is handed back his apparently original loan document after payment, why should he preserve it forever? He naturally assumes the debt is extinguished and may discard the torn or returned paper. Even according to Rif and Rosh, who allow a repayment claim where the ketubah is ordinarily written and she no longer possesses it, the Ketubot passage remains coherent. If the woman first appears only with witnesses to the wedding procession and the husband admits the debt, he pays and receives a receipt. If she later comes with the ketubah, the receipt protects him. If she first comes with the ketubah, however, and he pays against the actual instrument, then later she comes only with witnesses to the wedding procession and no ketubah, he can say "I paid," and according to that approach he is believed. Thus there is no parallel double-collection concern. The ordinary loan case is different because possession of the actual document is precisely what ordinarily defeats the repayment claim. This explains why Beit Yosef's concern survives. Shach also suggests that if the debtor is informed that a replacement was written, he will know to preserve the original as a receipt when it is later returned. But Rashbam's language indicates otherwise. He says we do not write another document lest the creditor find the first and collect again. If simply informing the debtor solved the problem, Rashbam's concern would disappear. Moreover, the Gemara indicates that the restriction applies even where the borrower is present. The baraita speaks generally about loan documents and does not distinguish whether the debtor was told. Human memory, death, travel, and future collection in the debtor's absence make mere notice insufficient. There is, however, a more serious question. Since we rule that receipts may be written, why can beit din not solve the matter immediately by giving the debtor a receipt that says: "Every document that may appear from this creditor against this debtor before today is void, except the following replacement"? Then there would be no danger from rediscovery of the original. Tosafot themselves ask this and give a very strained answer. One response is that the principle compelling a creditor to accept a receipt applies when he presently seeks payment. Otherwise, refusing to accept a receipt would allow him to hold the debtor's money indefinitely merely because his original document was lost. But where the creditor is not yet demanding payment and only wants a new document for safekeeping, the debtor can say: wait until the debt comes due. At that time, demand your money, and if your document is missing I will give you a receipt. The debtor is not required now to burden himself with both preserving a receipt and facing multiple creditor documents merely to make the creditor more comfortable. This is especially so because increasing the number of outstanding documents is itself a recognized detriment. Therefore the rule, "a lost document of this one should not make the other lose his money," does not justify issuing a replacement before payment is due. This distinction is reasonable. There is a second and, in my view, stronger answer. The immediate receipt cannot simply extinguish the underlying debt, because then the newly issued document could not properly be dated from the original transaction date. The receipt would need to invalidate only the original paper while leaving the underlying obligation intact. That creates a new danger if the creditor had previously sold the original document. Suppose the creditor sold the original note to Purchaser A in Nisan. Later he claims it was lost and receives a replacement, which he sells to Purchaser B in Iyar. The debtor may receive a receipt from the original creditor stating that the first paper is void. Purchaser B can collect on the replacement. When Purchaser A later appears with the earlier purchased original, the debtor's receipt from the original creditor may not defeat him. A receipt issued after the creditor had already sold the debt cannot impair the first purchaser's acquired right. In the Gemara of Bava Metzia, a receipt issued after transfer does not simply erase the earlier purchaser's right, except through separate principles of forgiveness that depend upon who still owns the underlying claim. Here the creditor is not forgiving the debt itself but only purporting to invalidate one paper. He cannot necessarily destroy the earlier purchaser's acquired right through a later receipt. The debtor could therefore be forced to pay twice. This is a real reason why receipts do not solve the duplicate-document problem. In a sale document, the Gemara's receipt solution is stronger. A no-warranty sale document may be only evidentiary and may not be freely transferable in the same way as a debt instrument. Also, where two sale documents concern the same field and same day, only one title can prevail. The structure of duplicate collection differs. This resolves the Beit Yosef-Shach difficulty without relying on the theory that the creditor will first use whichever document is convenient. It also explains why the Ketubot case is safer. If the woman herself later sues on witnesses to the wedding procession, she must personally appear and can be bound by the receipt or waiver she gave. If the ketubah was sold to another person, the analysis changes, but the husband's defenses against a transferred ketubah and the woman's own later claim do not line up like two transferred loan instruments. This also answers Rosh's challenge to Rashbam. Rosh infers from the baraita's phrase, "one who comes and says, my document was lost," that perhaps the rule differs where witnesses establish the loss. But one must distinguish a witnessed destruction from an ordinary witnessed disappearance. If witnesses saw the document burn, there is no concern it was previously sold and remains in another person's hands. If they merely saw it fall or disappear, there can still be transfer concerns, especially according to authorities who allow acquisition of a document through methods not requiring physical possession at every moment. Indeed, according to Rosh, a document can be transferred together with land through agav. The fact that the creditor still physically held the paper at one moment does not always prove he had never sold the underlying right. Thus even witnessed loss may not eliminate the concern unless the witnesses establish circumstances proving no prior transfer and no possibility of recovery. This helps explain Rambam's precise wording. Rambam speaks of one for whom a document was written and who immediately returned saying it was lost. The word "immediately" is important. After even one day, concern for transfer is obvious. Rambam nevertheless says even immediate claimed loss is insufficient because perhaps payment already occurred or another defect exists. He is teaching that beit din does not create a new operative instrument merely because the creditor reports immediate loss. One cannot argue that since the loss is so recent no transfer could have happened. Other concerns remain. This also explains why the law is not redundant with the rule that a found loan document is not returned. The present case is one where the court knows the creditor previously possessed the original and that it has now been lost or destroyed. The question is whether the court may actively create a new paper. That is different from whether an existing found paper should be returned. Even where a found document would be returned upon adequate identification and proof of loss, issuing a fresh document creates additional risk and requires greater certainty. Accordingly, Rambam's law teaches something independent. The same structure appears in Rashba's responsum concerning women exiled from their homes whose ketubot were destroyed. Rashba permits new ketubot because the catastrophe made it certain the old instruments would not reappear. If the only concern were that the husband needed a receipt whenever he paid, exile would add nothing. He already needs a receipt in many ketubah cases. The special significance of the disaster is that it eliminates the possibility of duplicate instruments or prior transfer. This supports the analysis above. In practical halachah, however, the matter remains difficult. Most authorities do not appear to accept Rosh's broad prohibition where there are reliable witnesses that the original document was actually destroyed and the debtor is prepared to receive a proper receipt. Tosafot themselves indicate that receipt can sometimes solve the problem. Therefore one should distinguish carefully. If the creditor merely says the document was lost, no replacement should be written. If witnesses establish complete destruction in circumstances excluding prior transfer and the debtor receives a comprehensive receipt protecting against the original document, there is substantial basis to issue a replacement. Rosh's responsum may have involved a debtor unwilling to accept the risks or to maintain such a receipt. Where the debtor willingly accepts the protective arrangement, Tosafot's reasoning has greater force. The law therefore requires study in each factual setting and should not be reduced to an absolute rule merely because the original paper is no longer present. There is another way to formulate the practical concern that strengthens this conclusion. A replacement document is not merely a new sheet of paper proving the same debt. It can become a separate transferable asset. That is why the possibility of selling the old and new documents to different purchasers is more serious than the ordinary possibility that the creditor himself will try twice. A receipt given by the creditor or by one later purchaser does not necessarily destroy a right that had already been transferred earlier to someone else. Accordingly, even where the debtor is perfectly diligent about receipts, duplicate transferable instruments can create a genuine third-party problem. This also explains why a witnessed burning is categorically different from an ordinary witnessed loss. If witnesses saw the paper consumed by fire while still in the creditor's hands, there is no longer a physical original that can later emerge in the hands of an earlier purchaser, unless there had already been a transfer of the underlying debt by another mechanism. The court can investigate that possibility separately. If witnesses merely saw the document fall, disappear, or leave the creditor's possession, the possibility of prior transfer is harder to exclude. The same distinction answers why the baraita's phrase "one who comes and says, my document was lost" matters. A mere claimant's report creates no reliable basis for the court to know whether the original remains outstanding somewhere else. Once independent witnesses establish destruction, the legal posture changes. Still, even witnessed destruction does not automatically solve every concern where the debt itself may have been transferred previously through assignment or another recognized method. That is why the court must consider the actual facts rather than applying a simplistic rule that "witnessed loss always permits replacement." The safest practical structure is therefore: where destruction is certain and there is no evidence of prior transfer, a carefully drafted replacement can be written; where the original may still physically exist or may have been transferred, a replacement should not be created merely because the creditor offers a receipt; and where the debtor voluntarily agrees to a comprehensive protective arrangement and the court can exclude third-party rights, there is greater room to rely on the authorities who permit replacement. This preserves both the concern of Rosh and the more permissive logic of Tosafot and the receipt rule.
Unless they testify that it was burned. Shach proposes a novel rule. Even where witnesses saw the original document burn and a replacement may be written, Shach says the replacement should ordinarily be dated from the later date, unless there was only one group of witnesses to the burning. His concern is that if many people witnessed the fire, the creditor might take one group to one beit din and another group to another and obtain two replacements dated from the original date. Even a receipt may not solve the problem because the creditor could collude to harm purchasers or conceal the receipt. This has no foundation. If we accepted such a concern, the Gemara's distinction between loan documents and sale documents based on the efficacy of receipts would collapse. One could always say the creditor will hide the receipt and collude against purchasers. More decisively, Bava Kamma asks concerning one who burned another's document: if there are witnesses, let them write another document. That question makes sense only if the replacement preserves the original priority date. If the replacement were dated only from the new date, the creditor could lose to intervening earlier creditors and the burner would have caused real damage. The Gemara could not say, "write another document," as though the loss were cured. Tosafot in Bava Batra 170a point to this very proof and conclude that the replacement is written from the original date. There is another simple reductio. When a debtor pays and returns the original document, we do not normally refuse to hand the paper back unless it is torn on the theory that the debtor might secretly return it to the creditor for collusive collection from purchasers. The law does not build endless speculative conspiracies upon every ordinary procedural act. Likewise, where reliable witnesses establish a genuine burning, beit din can write the replacement from the original date according to the usual rules. Shach's concern that multiple witness groups will be used to obtain multiple replacements is too remote to uproot the clear Talmudic structure.
That it was burned, etc. See Urim for the statement of Rabbenu Yerucham and Shach's comments. At first glance there is a question from Bava Kamma. The Gemara says: if there are witnesses to the burned document, let them write another. If there are no witnesses, how do we know what was lost? Perhaps the case is this: The original document was authenticated. The same people who later witnessed its burning were also the original signatories, but they no longer remember the underlying loan. Had the document survived, the creditor could still collect because the signatures were authenticated from another source. Once it burned, those witnesses cannot recreate the loan from memory. The burner therefore caused a real loss. Why does the Gemara say they can simply write another document? The answer is based on the principle explained above in siman 46 and siman 28. If we know with certainty that the original signatories no longer remember the underlying testimony, the document was in truth procedurally defective according to the stricter view. They themselves may not be believed to invalidate an authenticated document merely by saying, "We no longer remember," because once their signatures are independently authenticated they cannot destroy the document through their own later statement. But after the document burns, if they are the very witnesses proving the destruction, they have a migo: they could remain silent. Their statement that they no longer remember can now be believed. It is thereby revealed that the document was substantively defective from the outset according to that view. The burner can then say: what did I really destroy? An instrument that should not have produced collection once the truth was known. The fact that beit din would previously have collected upon it only because the witnesses were not believed to invalidate their own authenticated signatures does not create liability for burning a document that was in truth defective. The same reasoning applies where the original witnesses now say they had signed under life-threatening compulsion, were relatives, or suffered another defect. While the authenticated document existed, they could not necessarily destroy it through their own statement. After the document burns and their testimony becomes necessary to reconstruct it, their migo can make them credible. Then it is revealed that the original instrument was never valid. The burner did not cause a legally cognizable loss. This explains the Gemara even according to the stricter authorities. Still, another difficult case remains. Suppose Reuven had an authenticated document against Shimon. Yaakov burned it. The witnesses to the burning are relatives of Shimon. They are invalid to testify against Shimon and therefore cannot help Reuven reconstruct the debt. But they are unrelated to Yaakov, the burner. Against Yaakov they can testify that Reuven possessed an authenticated document against Shimon and that Yaakov destroyed it. Why should Yaakov not be liable under dina d'garmei for the value of the enforceable paper he destroyed? The Gemara's statement, "if there are witnesses, write another," does not answer this case because these witnesses cannot recreate a claim against Shimon. One cannot simply dismiss the question by saying the Gemara has already given the alternative case of a trusted claim. Later authorities derive many practical rules from the Gemara's very formulation concerning burned documents. This scenario therefore remains difficult and requires study.
And this is known through witnesses, etc. Why does the creditor not simply take the original document with him? If it is lost and witnesses establish the loss, beit din could issue another copy. Alternatively, beit din could retain the original and the creditor could travel with an authenticated copy. Sema answers that if a judicial copy is lost, beit din can issue another even without witnesses to the loss of the first copy. There is less danger from holding two judicial copies because when the creditor collects on one, the debtor can obtain a receipt. But if the original document is lost without witnesses, beit din will not issue a copy, because perhaps the creditor first collected on the original and the debtor, seeing the original returned, did not protect himself with a receipt. The creditor could later collect again on the copy. Mahari ibn Lev answers differently. If beit din merely keeps the original and writes elsewhere that the creditor holds a copy, another court may not know the arrangement and the original may remain indefinitely exposed to loss. This answer is incomplete if one proposes that beit din write directly on the original document that an authenticated copy exists. Then any later court would see the notation. Kenesset HaGedolah makes this point. Perhaps Baal HaTerumot follows Rashba below in siman 44, that an erasure or scraping on the back of a document does not necessarily invalidate it if the text itself remains intact. Then writing a warning on the back of the original would not solve the problem. The holder could erase or scrape off the notation and still present the document as valid. If so, Mahari ibn Lev's concern can be understood. But there is another difficulty with Sema's solution. Why is it safe to allow two judicial copies merely because receipts may be written? Perhaps the creditor sells one copy to Purchaser A in Nisan and the other to Purchaser B in Iyar. Purchaser B collects first. The debtor receives a receipt. Later Purchaser A appears with the earlier purchased copy. The debtor's receipt from the later purchaser does not necessarily defeat the earlier purchaser's prior acquired right. The later purchaser cannot waive or forgive a debt he did not own at the time of the earlier transfer. Thus two transferable copies can create double liability despite receipts. Bach may have had this in mind when he says there is concern that the creditor will collect and then collect again. It is unlikely that Bach simply overlooked the accepted rule that receipts may be written. Perhaps he means precisely collection through sales of separate copies to different people. One possible defense of Sema is that a judicial copy does not have the same transferability as the original debt instrument. The sale of documents is rabbinic, and perhaps Chazal did not extend the special rules of transfer to a mere judicial copy. If so, possession of two copies would not allow the creditor to create two independent purchasers. But that is a significant premise and not clearly established. I therefore prefer a stricter reading. A new judicial copy should be issued only when the prior copy was itself lost before witnesses. Otherwise we should fear that the creditor retains both and can create conflicting claims. Likewise, the original document should not ordinarily be released while another judicial copy remains outstanding. One might ask: why not simply take the original, and if it is later lost before witnesses then apply for a replacement? The answer is practical and strong. As explained above in seif 1, reconstructing an original loan document can require many conditions. According to numerous authorities, the original transaction witnesses must still remember and testify to the loan. Even according to the broader view, there may be requirements of careful examination concerning the exact wording, date, amount, conditions, and signatures. A traveling creditor should not deliberately place himself in a situation where loss of the original will force him to meet all those demanding reconstruction requirements. It is far safer to obtain an authenticated judicial copy in advance, where witnesses to the loss of that copy can later suffice more readily. This simple practical reason answers the original question better than the complicated concern about writing on the original. I am therefore surprised Sema and Bach did not emphasize it. The many safeguards required to reconstruct an original document are themselves enough reason for a creditor to travel with a judicial copy instead.
