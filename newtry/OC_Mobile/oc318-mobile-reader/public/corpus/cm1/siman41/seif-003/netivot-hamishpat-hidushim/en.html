There is no concern that he will bring one group of witnesses to the burning before one beit din and another group before a second beit din and collect twice, because a receipt can be written. The Shach holds that the replacement document should be dated from the later date, because we are concerned about collusion in which the borrower himself might conceal the receipt. See Beurim. If he seeks to collect from encumbered property based on actual witnesses that the creditor previously collected from it, he may collect and we do not fear a scheme to collect twice. They may say, "We paid." The Sema asks from siman 255, seif 6, where the Mechaber rules that such a claim is not believed. He answers that if a dying person instructed that a gift be given, the heirs may say they already fulfilled the instruction. There, however, the dying person admitted that he owed a specific person one hundred, and in that case they are not believed to claim payment. The Taz distinguishes differently: if the dying person wrote generally, "Give such and such to this person and such and such to that person," the heirs may claim payment, whereas if he wrote an instrument specifically for one beneficiary they are not believed because they should have taken the document back. The conclusion is that whether one may claim payment against such a testamentary instruction is a matter of unresolved law. The primary distinction is that stated by the Shach at the end of siman 253: if the dying person told the witnesses, "Write and give," the instruction has the status of a document. If he did not say, "Write and give," it does not. This is clear:
There is no concern that he may find it. Since the place of the loss was unknown, the creditor cannot simply search for it. Beit din also informs the person for whom a replacement document is written that another was issued, because otherwise there would be concern that this other person, who may know the place of the loss, will find the original. The case assumes two witnesses to the loss and that it was known to have remained in the lender's possession as a deposit, because otherwise the borrower could claim payment:
And this is known by witnesses. If he has no witnesses, the original document itself is not returned to him, because perhaps he still possesses a copy and will first collect with the original and later with the copy. Even without witnesses, however, they may give him a second copy, because we do not fear that he will collect twice with two copies. Whenever he collects using a copy, he must give the debtor a receipt:
And they testify before them that it was lost. We do not fear that it will later be found, because when an item is lost while traveling one does not ordinarily continue searching for it indefinitely:
They make a copy. The Sema emends the wording and explains that although one does not normally make a replacement copy of a deed of sale containing responsibility, that restriction applies to a copy given the full force of the original. A document that expressly states on its face that it is merely a copy may be made, because there is no concern that the holder will collect and then collect again when the writing itself shows that the original still exists. The practical benefit is that the opposing party cannot deny that the transaction ever occurred, though in a loan document he may still claim payment:
