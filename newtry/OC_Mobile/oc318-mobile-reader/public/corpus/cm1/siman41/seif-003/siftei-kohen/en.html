Even if it was lost in the presence of witnesses, they do not write another for him, lest he find it. The Rashbam explains in the final chapter of Bava Batra that when the claimant has witnesses that the note was lost, a replacement may be written, and we do not fear that he will find the first and collect twice. The Rosh objects from the second chapter of Ketubot, where even a ketubah lost before witnesses cannot be collected on evidence of the bridal procession unless it was burned, because perhaps she will find it and collect twice. The Rosh suggests that the baraita's word "lost" can mean destroyed by fire. In my view the Rashbam is correct. The sugya in Ketubot follows the opinion that a receipt is not written, whereas we rule that a receipt is written. The Rashbam expressly discusses the law according to the rule that receipts are written. Ketubot itself implies that according to that rule there is no concern even where the document was lost. The Beit Yosef says that one may not make a copy of a loan document because even though receipts are written, the creditor might first present the original, and the court would not require a receipt when he surrenders the note; he could later present the copy in another court. This is incorrect. Ketubot itself asks why we do not fear that the woman will first collect on evidence of the bridal procession in one court and then present the ketubah elsewhere, and answers that receipts are written. Rashi explains that when she is paid she gives the husband a receipt. The same concern that she may first present the ketubah necessarily exists, yet once the husband later possesses the original ketubah she cannot collect on the secondary evidence, because his possession proves payment. Likewise here, once the borrower later possesses the original note, the creditor cannot collect on the copy. This assumes the borrower knows a copy was issued and therefore safeguards the original after paying. The Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, Semag, and Raavan likewise imply the Rashbam's view. They say that one who merely claims his note was lost or burned receives no replacement, lest it was paid or waived. This implies that if witnesses establish an unavoidable loss and nonpayment, a replacement is written. Gidulei Terumah asks that even a loss before witnesses does not remove the possibility of payment, for even the Raavad only disputes this within the due date. The answer is that the witnesses establish an unavoidable loss in circumstances showing nonpayment, such as a city captured by troops. He also asks why we do not fear that the creditor later found the note. Once the debt is certain, the borrower will take a receipt upon payment, so finding it causes no harm. Rashbam's concern applies where the claimant merely says it was lost, for perhaps it was never truly lost or he may find it and force an unnecessary second payment. Gidulei Terumah also misunderstands the Rosh's phrase that "lost" may mean lost in fire, taking it as the Rosh's interpretation of Rashbam. The Rosh means only that even if Rashbam's grammatical inference from the baraita is accepted, the baraita may refer to destruction by fire. Rashbam himself plainly discusses ordinary loss, because he says perhaps it will be found. The Bach's attempt to reinterpret Rashbam as a loss from which recovery is impossible is therefore untenable. Maharik likewise implies that where loss is certain, a replacement is written. It appears that the Rosh and Beit Yosef prohibit writing another document only when the borrower is not informed. Then the creditor may first sue on the original, the borrower may not preserve it after payment because he does not know of the replacement, and the creditor may later sue on the replacement. This resolves Gidulei Terumah's objection from the Rosh's responsum that where witnesses know the document was lost, another may be written. That responsum concerns a replacement made with notice to the borrower. Alternatively, it concerns total destruction like burning. Accordingly, there is no practical dispute. The Rosh and Beit Yosef discuss writing without informing the borrower. The Rashbam, Rambam, and other authorities discuss a loss before witnesses where a replacement is written with notice to the borrower. This is the correct and straightforward ruling.

One might object that the creditor could burn the document before several separate pairs of witnesses, collect on one replacement, and then collect again on another. The answer is that since the law is that a receipt is written, there is no concern. The Bach gives a similar answer, although his phrase that a receipt is written only where no other solution is possible is inaccurate, for receipts are always written. Accordingly, the replacement must be dated from the second date. If it were dated from the first date, the borrower could conspire with the lender, suppress the receipt, and enable collection from purchasers through several notes. This explains why the Ramah, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch above require tearing the first note. If receipts are written, why is that necessary? Because the beit din writes the replacement from the original date, and a receipt does not protect purchasers where the borrower himself may hide it and collude with the lender. The Bach says that whenever a direct correction is possible we do not initially rely on a receipt, lest the borrower lose it. This is not persuasive, because the earlier law declares the second document invalid even after the fact. If the only concern were loss of the receipt, the court could simply require one. The reason must be the danger of collusion against purchasers. The Baal HaTerumot cites the Raavad regarding the case in Bava Kamma of one who burns another's note, stating that witnesses testify before a beit din and the court writes a replacement dated from the first date. This must concern a case where the witnesses establish that no other witnesses were present at the burning, as the Rosh, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Tur, and Mechaber state in a similar case below in siman 116 in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. The Rosh and Tur here may likewise be understood this way. But regarding an erased document such a limitation cannot work, because no one knows how many witnesses the creditor arranged to see the document before it was erased. The Bach's assertion that the Rosh disagrees with Tosafot in the second chapter of Ketubot is also incorrect. Properly understood, Tosafot necessarily agree with the Rosh as a matter of law.
They testify that it was burned, etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that witnesses who testify concerning a burned document, who were not themselves witnesses to the original but saw it and recognized the original witnesses' signatures, provide sufficient testimony. This is not testimony from writing rather than from their mouths. It is as though they testify that they saw testimony already investigated by a beit din, since witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony was investigated in court. Tosafot explains this in the second chapter of Ketubot. If the testifying witnesses were themselves the original witnesses, they must remember the substance of the document, because otherwise their testimony would be from their writing rather than their mouths. Darkei Moshe and the Beit Yosef cite this. This requirement follows the Rambam's view, cited by Rabbeinu Yerucham and by the Mechaber in siman 46, that original document witnesses who do not remember its contents are like deaf persons. According to the dissenting view there, the problem exists only when neither original witness recognizes the other's signature and no additional witness joins them to authenticate both signatures. If either condition is met, they too may testify even without remembering the contents. See siman 46, seif 10.

The Sema says he therefore added the words "even if they are relatives." This is inaccurate.
