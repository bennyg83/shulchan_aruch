Nor can one solve the problem by writing on the copy, "This document is not for collection," because if so the copy serves no purpose. Thus the Sema.
And they gave it. Even where there is no concern that the borrower merely had the document written in anticipation of borrowing but never actually borrowed, the court still does not create a replacement. Sema.
With witnesses. Because perhaps the original document will later be found. Rashbam explains that if witnesses attest that it was lost, a replacement may be written and we do not fear that it will later be found and used for double collection. Rambam, Baal HaTerumot, and Raavan appear to agree. The Shach concludes that in practical halacha there may be no dispute: the Mechaber concerns a case where the debtor is not informed, while Rashbam and the others concern a case where the debtor is informed, or where a replacement is written because the loss was witnessed. This is a sound approach and is primary.
That was burned. One might ask why we are not concerned that the creditor will burn the same document before several different groups of witnesses, obtain several replacements, and collect repeatedly. Since we rule that a debtor may demand a receipt, however, this concern is removed. According to this, the replacement must bear a new date, because if it bore the original date there would still be concern that debtor and creditor might collude to collect repeatedly from purchasers under multiple documents while the debtor conceals the receipts. This also explains why the Mechaber in seif 1 requires the first document to be torn up. Why is a receipt not enough? Because there the replacement carries the original date, and the problem cannot be fully corrected through a receipt. Alternatively, the present case may concern witnesses who attest that no one else was present when the document burned except themselves. That explanation cannot apply to an erased document, because no one can know how many groups may have seen the document before it was erased. Thus the Shach.
Warranty. With a sale document it was necessary to specify that it lacked warranty, meaning the parties expressly stipulated so. Otherwise warranty is presumed as a scribal omission. With a gift document there is no such presumption. Sema.
That was lost. Meaning that the owner says it was lost, even if there are no witnesses to the loss. With documents of sale and gift there is no concern of improper double collection. Shach.

The Sema finds difficulty because the Tur and Mechaber in siman 255, seif 6, rule that heirs are not believed to say they paid when a written will remains in the claimant's possession. One must perhaps distinguish that there the dying person admitted that a maneh belonging to the claimant was held by him, which is considered a full legal document. The Sema says this distinction is somewhat strained and the matter requires study; see his longer discussion there. The Taz writes that one can distinguish between a will written generally and one written in formal documentary language. This distinction is mentioned in siman 250, seif 21. For practical halacha, however, money should not be extracted merely on the strength of a will's documentary form, as the Taz demonstrates in siman 43, seif 9.
To be concerned. Such concern applies particularly where the document was lost from his possession and he knows the places where he was when it disappeared, because then one may fear that a later search will recover it. Sema.
With witnesses. If he lacks witnesses, the original document itself is not returned to him, because perhaps he still possesses the copy and will first collect with the original and afterward with the copy. We need not fear that he will collect twice using two copies, because whenever one collects using a copy he must issue a receipt, and afterward he cannot collect again. Nor did Chazal fear that he would later find the copy, first collect with the original, and then collect a second time with the copy, because once an item was lost on the road people generally stop searching for it. Thus the Sema.
For which copies are made. The Sema discusses this gloss at length and proposes emending the text to read, "Every document may be copied," etc. The Taz suggests the reading, "Every document that may be copied is transcribed," meaning that whenever a copy is permitted because there is a need, it is copied in the stated manner. The Sema's objection that a beit din is required to copy a document, as with an erased document, is answered because there the beit din is needed not merely for the copying but for the formal declaration that the court examined and validated the matter.
Beit din. The Rivash writes that even if two of the persons signing a transcript are related to one another, the transcript may be valid because no collection is made from it until the original document is produced; the transcript is merely evidence of what the original contained. The Sema says this is why he added the word "even." The Shach responds that the Sema was imprecise. One who studies the Rivash will see that he validates the transcript only when three persons signed and two of them are related to one another. If only two related persons signed, the transcript has only the force of one witness. See below, siman 45, seif 12. The Taz likewise criticizes the Sema and writes that this statement should be removed from his gloss so judges are not led into practical error.
