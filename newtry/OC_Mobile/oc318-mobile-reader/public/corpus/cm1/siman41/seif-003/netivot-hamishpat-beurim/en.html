If they did not write in it the reason why they made another document. This is difficult. We ordinarily do not suspect beit din of a substantive legal error, only of forgetfulness. Here there is no natural place for mere forgetfulness, so why should failure to record the reason invalidate the second instrument? The ruling therefore requires clarification.
Even if it was lost before witnesses. The dispute concerns whether beit din may write a replacement deed after a debt instrument was lost before witnesses. Those who permit it reason that the borrower will be informed and will therefore insist on a receipt when paying, preventing double collection. Those who prohibit it distinguish between requiring a borrower to pay after a lost document, where the Sages may rely on a receipt so the creditor does not consume another's money unjustly, and actually creating a new deed. They hold that the enactment of receipts was not extended so far as to authorize a second instrument. The permissive authorities extend the same principle because without a replacement document the creditor may lose effective collection. Shach therefore rejects proofs based on passages following the opinion that receipts are not written. Even if one accepts receipts for payment, it can be argued that they also support a replacement deed. Beit Yosef asks that the creditor might first collect with the original document and later with the replacement. Shach answers that because the borrower was notified of the replacement, he would insist on a receipt even when the original later reappears. Yet a torn original document in the borrower's hands is not necessarily itself sufficient proof of payment, because debt documents can be written for the borrower even without the lender present and may have remained with him from the outset. Nor does the ketubah case prove otherwise, because the whole point there is that when direct return of the original is impossible or unreliable, a receipt is required. The better understanding is that if a replacement is written after a witnessed loss, the borrower must be notified. We need not await his response, but the notification alerts him to demand a receipt. Still, another concern remains: perhaps the lost document was later found and used for payment before the borrower learned of the replacement. Therefore the witnesses' knowledge must extend far enough to establish that no such payment occurred in the interim. If a deed was burned before witnesses, there is no possibility of later finding that same instrument, so the problem is much smaller. Where it was merely lost, if witnesses testify only months later, one may need the replacement to record precisely what occurred, and the debtor can still raise payment arguments arising after the loss. The source's discussion of Rashbam is best understood accordingly: the novelty is that even a replacement document explicitly stating that the creditor claimed his original was lost can mislead a later court into thinking the loss itself was independently established and that the original, if found, may safely be returned to the creditor. The underlying danger is therefore not simply fabrication of the loss, but the interaction between mistaken judicial assumptions, later finding of the original, and double collection.
Unless witnesses testify that it was burned. Shach says the recreated deed should be dated from the second writing, out of concern that the borrower might conceal a receipt and conspire to harm purchasers. Nevertheless, once the replacement exists, collection can reach back to the original lien date. The concern for conspiracy is a reason not to create the new document initially in a form that facilitates abuse, not a reason to deny the creditor his original priority once the court properly adjudicates collection. Otherwise the Talmudic rule that money is presumed still owed and that a creditor can be restored after a destroyed deed would make no sense. We ordinarily do not suspect collusion unless the document has fallen and thereby suffered a specific defect. It is enough to use the second date in the physical replacement while preserving the original lien rights through judicial process. Shach's proof from an erased document is also understandable: once a replacement is written, the debtor knows he must demand a receipt when paying.
And this is known by witnesses. Urim VeTumim asks that if two certified copies exist, the creditor may sell one in Nisan and the other in Iyar, and a receipt from the later purchaser would not appear to extinguish the earlier purchaser's independent right. In my view it does. Just as payment made to the original creditor in accordance with his authorization discharges the debtor, payment to a person whom the creditor has authorized through delivery of a certified copy to collect is legally payment to the creditor. By handing the copy to that purchaser for collection, the creditor effectively tells the debtor, 'Pay this person.' Once the debtor pays someone authorized by the creditor, the underlying personal obligation is discharged. The earlier purchaser's lien is no stronger than Rabbi Natan's lien, where payment made before notice can extinguish the later claimant's right.
And beit din is not required for copying a document. Urim VeTumim writes that the value of the copy is at least that it defeats a plea of 'it never happened.' That must be so, otherwise the copying accomplishes nothing. At first glance there is a difficulty: witnesses who independently write that they saw a document in the creditor's hand are giving testimony from writing without the borrower's authorization. The answer follows Rashba's general rule. Witnesses may not create a new writing on their own where it increases the obligor's liability, such as converting an oral loan into a written loan or creating a new encumbrance. But where they merely copy an existing valid written loan and explicitly state that it is a copy, no additional obligation or proliferation of effective deeds is created. Ramban similarly explains that once the first deed was written under the borrower's authorization, a faithful copy that adds no new liability is treated as derivative of that original authorization rather than as forbidden testimony from writing. This is analogous to the rule that a deed of gift without responsibility may be rewritten where the second writing does not enlarge the underlying obligation.
