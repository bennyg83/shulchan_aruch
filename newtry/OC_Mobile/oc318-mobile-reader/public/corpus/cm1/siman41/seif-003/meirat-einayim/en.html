He could first collect on the original and then again on the copy. Nevertheless, they give him a second copy of the first document. There is no concern that he will collect twice with two copies, because anyone collecting on a copy must give the debtor a receipt. Once the debtor holds that receipt, further collection is prevented. For this reason the original debt document is left with beit din. If he later loses the copy and has no witnesses to the loss, he may return and receive a second copy. This would not be possible if he walked around with the original and lost it without witnesses. And every document that is copied and transcribed, etc. The printed wording cannot be understood without emendation. It begins, "and every document that they copy and transcribe," but never states what follows from that. Some delete the vav before "and transcribe" and explain that every document they copy must be transcribed letter for letter. That is difficult, for if this is required for every copy, why does the text then say that no beit din is required? That would contradict the Mechaber's earlier requirement that beit din prepare the copy, like the case of an erased document. From the Ir Shushan it appears he deletes the vav before "and they do not require beit din," yielding: every other document copied in this manner, letter for letter, does not require beit din. That too is unsatisfactory. In my view the correct emendation is to delete the shin in the word "that they copy." The meaning is: every document, even a sale or gift document containing responsibility, which the preceding rule says ordinarily is not duplicated because of concern for double collection, may nevertheless be transcribed letter for letter if the copy explicitly states that it is merely a copy of the original. Then it is evident from the document itself that the original remains in existence, and no beit din will allow collection on the copy until the original is produced. There is therefore no concern for double collection. The gloss then adds a further novelty: such a transcription does not require beit din. This is proved from Rivash, siman 413, which is the source of Rama's gloss. Rivash explains that the prohibition against issuing another responsibility-bearing document applies only when one makes a new formal instrument like the first. But a copy that expressly identifies itself as a transcription may reproduce it letter for letter because the nature of the copy is apparent and beit din will not allow collection on its responsibility clause. Darkei Moshe likewise explains that a complete new instrument is forbidden, but a copy expressly marked as such may be made where there is some need. Rama omits the words "where there is some need" here, apparently following Rivash's simpler wording. In any event, ordinarily a person does not spend money and effort to obtain a copy without some purpose.
And no beit din is required, etc. Rivash further writes that even if the two persons who sign the copied document are related to one another, the copy is valid. Since nothing can be collected on it until the original document is produced, it serves only as disclosure of information.
