Even if it was completely erased. Many authorities imply that Rabbi Yitzchak disagrees and holds that if the document is completely erased, outside witnesses cannot recreate it. Urim VeTumim found this difficult because even a completely burned document can sometimes be restored. His proposal that complete burning requires only the original deed witnesses is itself difficult. The better distinction is whether the outside witnesses saw the document while it was already in the process of being erased and therefore specifically intended to preserve all its language, or merely happened to see it earlier while it was intact. Ramban asks how outside witnesses can accurately reproduce a deed when there may have been legally significant formulations they did not understand or remember. If the witnesses saw the writing while its traces were still visible and understood that it was about to be lost, they would deliberately examine every relevant phrase. If they merely saw an ordinary intact document long before any danger, they would not necessarily pay close enough attention, and their testimony cannot safely recreate all of it. A burned deed can be treated similarly where they deliberately observed it as it was being destroyed, or where the applicable opinion requires testimony concerning the underlying loan itself.
They may testify outside the presence of the litigant. Shach questions Rama's ruling. The case can be explained according to Mechaber's reliance on Rambam, who requires witnesses to the underlying loan in addition to witnesses identifying the erased document. Where there are witnesses to the actual loan and the separate 'erasure witnesses' are needed only to identify that this was the document bearing those signatures, their testimony is largely a matter of clarifying an already established fact. Sema likewise writes that ordinary intensive examination is unnecessary for these secondary identifying witnesses. If so, it is understandable that their testimony may also be received outside the borrower's presence. A further possibility is that testimony about a document the borrower authorized to be written is categorically different, because the original witnesses could in principle have written the deed outside his presence, and a beit din recreating that authorized instrument may have similar latitude. Nevertheless, Shach's stricter ruling appears primary.

And not from a creditor. Sema explains the concern in a sale document by saying a receipt in the seller's hands does not necessarily protect purchasers. The more basic issue is the danger of double collection. If the creditor first collects using the original document from unencumbered property, he can return the original and avoid issuing a receipt. He may then later collect again using the certified copy. In a sale deed one can sometimes write a universal receipt declaring all deeds concerning that particular field void except the known one. That device is unavailable in the same way for an ordinary loan. Therefore the Talmud's additional concern about purchasers is needed particularly in the sale context: the creditor may first collect using the copy and afterward use the original against purchasers, who may not know of the seller's receipt. If the seller presently owns unencumbered property, ordinary foreclosure rules reduce the immediate purchaser concern, but if no such property exists the danger is direct.
