And some say even if it was completely erased, etc. Sema writes in Perishah that there is no real dispute here and that all authorities agree. I do not understand this. Rama's wording and Tur's wording plainly present two views. Tur first cites Ri, who requires that some trace of the original writing still remain, and then cites Rashbam that even if the writing was completely erased a replacement can be made, adding that Rashbam's view is more reasonable. If there were no substantive disagreement, Tur would not frame it this way. Moreover, Bava Kamma 98b seems explicit. The Gemara says that if a person burned another's document and there are witnesses who know what was written in it, they can write him another valid document. That appears to permit replacement even where the original document is completely destroyed. I searched the Rishonim and found the underlying dispute clearly in Rashba's novellae to Bava Kamma. Rashba first corrects the text and then explains one view: if witnesses saw the document and its contents and also saw that it had been authenticated, beit din can write another document based on them. Since the original document had already been judicially authenticated, those witnesses are effectively testifying about an act of beit din. Another view says even prior judicial authentication is unnecessary. If the witnesses recognize the signatures of the original signatories, beit din can authenticate the former document through their testimony and issue a replacement. The first view is essentially Baal HaMaor's position, cited above at the end of siman 30. He says witnesses who merely saw a document cannot testify to its debt contents because that would be testimony from the mouth of other witnesses. I previously asked how Baal HaMaor could reconcile the Bava Kamma passage. The answer is now clear. He establishes it where the original document had been judicially authenticated. The later witnesses testify to that judicial status, not merely to the hearsay contents of a paper. For the same reason, according to this view the witnesses' testimony is insufficient even against the person who burned the document. If their testimony cannot establish the debt against the borrower, it cannot establish with certainty that the burner destroyed a valuable enforceable debt instrument. Thus Ri can require that some trace of the original writing remain. Where the original is still faintly visible, the witnesses' testimony serves only to clarify and identify a thing that beit din itself can still inspect. It becomes a form of revealing evidence rather than testimony from other witnesses. But if the document has completely disappeared and was never authenticated, their report of what other witnesses wrote can be treated as inadmissible secondhand testimony. Rashbam disagrees. Either he follows Rashba's final view that the signatures themselves, once recognized, are treated as though the testimony had been examined in beit din, or he follows the structure explained above that the original signatories also testify to the underlying loan and the replacement witnesses merely establish that the creditor did not receive the old document back. According to the latter approach, the replacement witnesses do not prove the loan by hearsay. The original loan witnesses prove the debt. The additional witnesses only establish that the original creditor still held the document until it was destroyed, thereby blocking a repayment claim. In any event, Tur's two views reflect a genuine old dispute. This also explains the baraita cited by Rashbam: "One who says, I lost my document." Rashbam explains the case as one where there are no witnesses to the loss. The implication is that if witnesses do know the document was lost, a replacement can be made even though the original no longer exists at all. Tur's attribution of the broader rule to Rashbam is therefore well grounded and not based merely on a weak inference from Rashbam's commentary on the Mishnah. The practical difference is significant. According to Ri and Baal HaMaor, if the original paper was completely destroyed before authentication and only outside witnesses remember its contents and signatures, they may be unable to create a full substitute document. According to Rashbam, Rashba's final view, and Tur's preferred conclusion, they can. Tur later rules in siman 386, concerning one who burned another's document, that if witnesses know the document they can create a replacement, without distinguishing whether the original had been authenticated. That confirms Tur rules like the broader view. Therefore Rama correctly writes "some say," because there is a real dispute, and Sema's attempt to erase the disagreement is not correct.
They may testify even outside the presence of the litigant. Shach is surprised. If the original document was completely erased, how can witnesses obligate a person by saying that a valid debt document once existed, when their testimony is accepted outside his presence? We do not ordinarily impose liability upon a person through testimony received in his absence. Because of this, Shach presses Tosafot and Rashba into narrow readings. Even if one could reinterpret Tosafot, Rosh cannot be avoided. Rosh, klal 94, responsum 5, discusses one witness who says a creditor's document was lost. Rosh says one witness is not enough because perhaps the borrower will later say he paid. He advises that before public knowledge of the loss spreads, beit din should send to the borrower and ask that he satisfy the debt, so his response will reveal the truth. The borrower was plainly in the same city. If testimony concerning the lost document could not be received outside his presence, Rosh's entire advice would be unnecessary. Beit din would simply summon him before receiving the witness. Then they would immediately hear whether he claimed payment. Rosh instead says to approach him strategically before he learns of the loss, so he will not fabricate a repayment defense. This is explicit that the testimony about the lost document can be received outside his presence. Shach himself, in seif katan 11, effectively assumes the same thing when he tries to explain Rosh. He says perhaps Rosh's concern is that a replacement document may be written without the borrower knowing, and the creditor may later use it against him while the borrower did not know to protect himself. But if the underlying testimony could never be received outside the borrower's presence, it would be impossible for the borrower not to know. Thus Shach's own explanation presupposes the law he questions. The reason for the exception is that most cases of erased, burned, or lost documents arise from emergency, deterioration, travel, war, or other circumstances in which delay threatens irreversible loss of evidence. Chazal therefore allowed testimony to be preserved outside the litigant's presence in this narrow context, just as they allow testimony where witnesses are about to depart overseas and just as they created special evidentiary rules for protest in order to preserve a person's property rights. This is a protective rule designed to prevent loss of evidence. The baraita, "One who comes and says, I lost my document," does not contradict this. One might ask why it says merely that the creditor claims loss rather than saying witnesses testify to the loss. The answer is that the baraita is teaching whether a replacement document is written. If the legal concern were only the absence of the borrower, the testimony itself would be worthless and the whole discussion would be pointless. Rosh himself infers from the baraita's wording that where actual witnesses establish the loss, the case is different. That is another proof against Shach. There is some support from Bava Kamma as well. The Gemara asks concerning one who burned another's document: if witnesses know what was written, let them write another document; if not, how do we know what loss occurred? Imagine that the burner was present before beit din but the borrower was absent. The witnesses' testimony could be received against the burner in his presence, yet, according to Shach, would be unusable against the borrower. If they later died or became disqualified before the borrower returned, the creditor could recover damages from the burner because the witnesses had established the document's value, but could not collect from the borrower because the testimony was never received in his presence. The Gemara gives no indication of such a distinction. The simpler implication is that in this preservation setting their testimony is accepted even though the borrower is absent. Accordingly, Rama's law is correct. This is not a general rule permitting testimony against a litigant outside his presence. It is a special necessity-based exception for preserving proof of a document whose physical evidence has been lost or destroyed.
But the original witnesses to the document themselves, etc. This is Rambam's wording. Tur, however, writes more generally, "but witnesses shall not make him a new document," which sounds like even other witnesses. The difference reflects Rosh's position in responsum klal 68, siman 31. Rosh says that where the original witnesses made an error in the first document, such as writing the wrong borrower's name, they certainly may write a corrected document because they never properly completed their agency. He goes further. Even if the document was correctly written and delivered to the creditor, but it later became known that it was torn or burned without payment, the witnesses may write another document. Rosh proves this from the baraita that says one who merely claims, "I lost my document," does not receive a replacement. The restriction is because the loss is not known to the witnesses. Where the witnesses themselves know with certainty that the original was destroyed without payment, they can replace it. This shows that according to Rosh the original document witnesses are not automatically barred by the principle that they already completed their agency. Tur therefore deliberately does not copy Rambam's phrase, "the original witnesses themselves." According to his father, those original witnesses can sometimes write again. Many earlier authorities attacked Rosh from Bava Batra 171a. Where a debtor paid part of a debt, the Mishnah says a new document may be substituted. The Gemara asks: a beit din has power to do this, but how can witnesses who already performed their agency perform it again? It then challenges from the rule that ten documents may be written over one field and answers that this applies to a gift or a sale without warranty. The implication seems explicit: for a loan document, witnesses may not create a second document after their first agency was completed. Mahari Sasson, responsum 66, leaves Rosh difficult. The answer emerges from Tosafot there. Tosafot ask that where the witnesses never completed their first agency at all, it should be even more obvious they cannot now write a document dated from the earlier time, because that would be antedated. Until the document is written, the lien does not arise. They answer that the Gemara's novelty is even where the witnesses previously wrote a valid document and the lien already existed, they may still be unable to write again because doing so resembles creating an antedated document and may lead to error. Thus, according to Tosafot, "they performed their agency" is not the true substantive problem in loan documents. The concern is antedating. Witnesses who see themselves permitted to write a replacement dated from the original loan date may later mistakenly write an ordinary loan document after delay from the original date, even though no earlier valid lien existed. This concern does not operate the same way in a gift without warranty. If the gift itself occurred in Nisan and the witnesses write the deed later, dating it from Nisan does not falsely create an earlier debt lien against purchasers in the same manner. They are recording the true date of the gift. They will not infer from this that they may write a document dated before the actual gift, because that would be an obvious falsehood. So the distinction is not simply "agency completed" versus "agency not completed." It is the risk of an improper earlier lien date. Once this is understood, Rosh is coherent. Rosh holds that where a loan was accompanied by a kinyan, the property lien arises from the kinyan date even if the paper is written much later. Therefore, if the original lost document involved a kinyan, the witnesses can safely write a replacement from the kinyan date. There is no antedating. They are not likely to confuse that with an ordinary loan without kinyan, because the distinction between a loan with kinyan and one without kinyan is well established. If there was no kinyan, they can still write a replacement dated from the new date rather than the original date. Again there is no antedating. Thus Rosh's responsum must concern either a kinyan document or writing a second document from the later date. That is why the question there is whether the witnesses had exhausted their agency, not whether the replacement would be antedated. According to Rosh, once antedating is removed, the fact that they previously wrote a document does not by itself bar them from writing again. This also explains the Gemara's answer about ten sale or gift documents. The Gemara prefers that establishment because Rav's statement says "ten documents over one field." If the rule were being taught merely for a loan with kinyan, Rav would not naturally have spoken specifically of a field. Sale and gift fit the wording directly. There is another question from the baraita about a lost sale document. According to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, perhaps the seller still possesses another copy. Tosafot explain that the case involves a kinyan. If so, why can the original witnesses not simply write again according to Rosh? The answer is that the baraita may concern writing by beit din rather than the original witnesses, or the dispute may turn on the danger of duplicate operative documents in the purchaser's hands, not merely the witnesses' authority. Where beit din writes a replacement, it can record that the original was lost and thereby prevent misuse. Ordinary witnesses, by contrast, generally reproduce the original text and are not authorized to add a narrative of why they are writing again unless their mandate permits it. This distinction also explains Tosafot's two interpretations. Under one interpretation the entire baraita concerns witnesses, and the special concern is that another unrecovered original may still exist. Under the other, beit din's broader power to record the circumstances becomes central. Rosh's actual responsum concerned a document containing a mistaken name. If the first writing was legally worthless, there was no earlier lien unless a kinyan independently created one. Therefore, if the question was whether to rewrite from the original date, the case must indeed have involved a kinyan. Otherwise the new document would be plainly antedated and the discussion about exhausted agency would be beside the point. This confirms the reading above. Thus Tur's wording is intentional. He does not say, like Rambam, that "the original document witnesses" may not write. According to Rosh, where there is no antedating concern, those very witnesses can write another document. Sema's explanation that Tur and Rosh prohibit because the witnesses already completed their agency does not fit Rosh's own theory. The practical difference is real. If the first document was destroyed but the loan had a kinyan, or if the replacement will be dated from the new writing date, Rosh allows the original witnesses themselves to write again. Rambam is stricter. Tur follows Rosh. That is why his wording departs from Rambam's. The antedating analysis also explains why the Gemara treats gifts and sales differently from loans. A gift without warranty does not create a creditor's right to collect from third-party purchasers in the same way. If the gift truly occurred in Nisan, writing a replacement in Iyar and dating it Nisan records the date on which the ownership transfer actually happened. The witnesses are not creating a new retroactive lien. They would still never write Adar if the gift occurred only in Nisan, because that would be an outright factual falsehood. Thus allowing a replacement does not train witnesses to write genuinely false earlier dates. A loan document is different. If there was no kinyan and the original paper was never effective, writing later with the earlier loan date can create a lien against purchasers from a time when no written lien existed. That is the precise danger Tosafot address. This distinction also clarifies Rav's statement that one may write ten documents over one field. The Gemara prefers to establish it in gift or sale because the wording naturally concerns a field and because multiple replacement instruments do not create the same creditor-lien danger. Were Rav speaking merely of a loan with kinyan, the reference specifically to a field would be less natural, since the same rule would apply to many kinyan debts. Rosh's responsum therefore fits the Gemara once its factual setting is identified correctly. Either there was a kinyan, in which case the lien already arose from the kinyan date and a replacement document dated from that date is accurate, or the witnesses were asked to write a replacement from the later date. In neither case do they create an improper earlier lien. The apparent contradiction with "witnesses who completed their agency may not repeat it" disappears because, for Rosh, that phrase in the loan context is not a freestanding law like agency in a get. It is a shorthand for the antedating and document-duplication concern. Tosafot themselves say the agency formulation is not literal in the same manner as get agency. That is why once the danger is removed, Rosh can permit a second writing. There is also a practical distinction between witnesses and beit din. Beit din has broader authority to write the history of what happened. A judicial replacement document can state explicitly that the original was lost or destroyed and that this document is issued only as a substitute. That notation itself prevents some forms of double collection and explains the existence of the replacement. Ordinary witnesses, however, were originally appointed to write the transaction itself. They may not have authority to add a new judicial narrative about later events. They may therefore be limited to reproducing the original formula. This is one reason the Gemara can permit beit din to act where ordinary witnesses may not. It is not merely that judges are "stronger" in an undefined sense. Their institutional authority includes recording procedural history and issuing a replacement instrument whose character is evident on its face. The same distinction appears in sale documents. Where beit din issues the replacement, it can write that the first document was lost. Where witnesses simply reproduce the first document, the seller may later claim that the purchaser possesses two original instruments and can manipulate priority or title. This resolves Tosafot's difficulty according to the second interpretation of Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel. The first interpretation can remain entirely within witness writing. The second naturally invokes beit din's broader ability to record the loss. Maharam Sasson's objection to Rosh is therefore serious only if one assumes the Talmudic "agency" bar is literal and universal. Once Tosafot reveal that antedating is the operative concern, Rosh becomes coherent. Tur's wording then becomes exact. He says merely "witnesses shall not make him a document" in the general problematic case. He does not copy Rambam's categorical phrase "the original document witnesses themselves," because according to Rosh those original witnesses are not permanently disabled. If the original transaction had a kinyan or the replacement is dated properly from the new date, they can write again. This is the practical difference between Tur and Rambam, and Sema's explanation misses it.
And if they wrote, "We investigated," etc. Sema, seif katan 10, writes as follows. If the witnesses who originally signed the document themselves testify, beit din must examine each one separately and verify that their accounts agree, as explained in siman 28. If the replacement record says, "We investigated," we presume the court already performed the necessary examination. If it does not say so, a new examination is required, and only then may the creditor collect from encumbered property. Likewise, if other witnesses testify concerning the writing and signatures of the original witnesses, Sema says they require full examination regarding everything written in the document because the original document is no longer before us. He says this is the view of Tur and Baal HaTerumot. In my view this requires clarification. If the original document witnesses themselves testify, what special interrogation is at issue? According to Rambam they must remember the substance of the original testimony when testifying about their handwriting. Obviously they must testify to all the details. That is not the special procedural interrogation discussed in siman 28. If, however, other witnesses merely identify the signatures, Sema says they require investigation concerning all the document's contents. I believe that according to Tosafot, Rashbam, and Rambam, such outside signature witnesses alone are not sufficient once the document has been erased. The original document witnesses must also testify to the loan itself. Tosafot say that the benefit of the replacement procedure is that the debt remains a written loan; without the original signatories who later testify, it would be only an oral loan. If outside witnesses who recognized the signatures alone sufficed to establish the erased document fully, Tosafot's discussion would be unnecessary. Even if the original signatories died, traveled overseas, or forgot, the outside witnesses could establish the document and the creditor would collect as from a written loan. Therefore Tosafot must hold, like Rashbam, that the phrase "they wrote and investigated" means beit din summoned the original witnesses who signed the document, questioned them, and found their testimony consistent. Rashbam says similarly that if this was not done, the creditor must bring proof regarding the date, amount, and all other contents. He does not discuss a case in which outside witnesses merely identify the signatures. The reason is clear. If those outside witnesses alone were enough, what would the phrase "we investigated" add? The court already records what they say the document contained. Ordinary judicial procedure can be assumed. There would be no independent need for special interrogation. And if the witnesses did not identify the signatures, the replacement record would be useless forever. Once the original document is erased, no later authentication could occur. Even if the original signatories appeared and said, "This is what we wrote," their testimony would at most establish an oral debt unless the erased document itself had been authenticated. Thus Rashbam's view must be that outside witnesses who saw and recognized the document can support the case, but the original transaction witnesses must still testify to the underlying loan. Their outside testimony then serves an important subsidiary function. The original transaction witnesses establish the debt itself. The witnesses who saw the document before it was erased establish that the lender still possessed the original document and had not returned it to the borrower. That supports the presumption, "If you paid, why is your document still in his possession?" It prevents the borrower from saying, "I paid and he returned the document to me." This is not testimony from the mouth of other witnesses, because they are not proving the loan. They are proving that the actual document remained in the lender's possession until it was erased. That is a direct fact they observed. This reading explains Tosafot and Rashbam precisely. Maggid Mishneh says the same regarding Rambam. He first discusses witnesses who saw the erased document and then writes that if beit din records that the original document witnesses came before them, were examined, and their testimony was consistent, the creditor need bring no further proof. If not, he must bring proof, because otherwise the replacement stands like an unauthenticated original document and the borrower could deny the transaction. Thus even after outside witnesses identify the erased document, Rambam still requires testimony from the original transaction witnesses. The reason is as stated. There are, however, dissenting authorities. Ritva, as cited by Nimukei Yosef, holds that if witnesses recognize the original signatures, that is sufficient. It is not testimony from other witnesses, because they testify to what was established for them through the document itself. This appears also to be Rashba's view in Bava Kamma, cited above, where he writes that after a document is burned there is no need for the original signatories so long as witnesses can establish the destroyed document. Tosafot in Bava Kamma and Ketubot likewise support that broader position. According to this second group, what then does "we investigated" mean? Ramban's explanation in his novellae to Bava Batra answers this. The replacement witnesses do not necessarily reproduce the original document letter for letter. They may tell beit din, "We saw an erased document whose meaning was that Reuven owed Shimon such-and-such, under these conditions." Ramban asks: perhaps the document contained technical or ambiguous language that the witnesses misunderstood. Therefore beit din must examine them carefully concerning the wording, conditions, amount, date, and every other element that could affect the document's meaning. If the replacement record states, "We investigated," no further concern remains, because the court already tested whether the witnesses properly understood the original document. If that phrase is absent, further proof may be needed because the first court may have merely recorded their general recollection without fully resolving ambiguities. Accordingly, under this approach, "we investigated" does not mean that the original signatories were examined. It means that beit din examined the witnesses who saw the erased document to ensure they understood it correctly. If the original document was copied letter for letter before being erased, this concern disappears. The court can read the actual wording itself. This harmonizes Ramban's explanation with Ritva and Rashba. Taz says Tur requires the original document witnesses in addition to the witnesses who saw the erased paper, while Rambam does not. That is incorrect. As shown, Maggid Mishneh's explanation of Rambam is the same as the stricter Tosafot-Rashbam position. The true dispute is between that stricter group and Ritva, Rashba, and the Tosafot passages that treat recognition of the original document itself as sufficient. This also clarifies the practical disagreement among the authorities. According to the stricter approach, the replacement record cannot rest merely on people saying, "We saw such a document and recognize the signatures." The original debt must still be established by direct testimony from those who witnessed the transaction or by the original signatories who remember it. The erased-document witnesses then support the written-loan status and defeat the repayment claim. According to Ritva and Rashba, by contrast, recognition of the original document can itself carry the debt forward because the witnesses are testifying to a legal instrument they personally saw and identified. Under that view, no separate testimony from the original loan witnesses is needed. The phrase "we investigated" must therefore be understood according to the underlying theory being followed. It cannot be given one mechanical meaning for all authorities. Where the replacement is a literal copy of the old document, the danger of misunderstanding is reduced greatly. Where it is only a narrative summary of what the witnesses remember the document to have meant, careful judicial examination is indispensable.
That it must say that they tore it, etc. Shach, citing Maharshal and Mahari ibn Lev, explains that if the original document was not actually torn, the replacement can create a risk of double collection. Therefore the replacement should record the fact that the original was destroyed. They add, however, that even if the replacement does not expressly say so, it should remain valid because we presume beit din acted properly and do not suspect judicial error. This is difficult. The Gemara states explicitly that any writ of collection that does not say, "We tore the original document," is not a valid writ. Likewise, an enforcement document that does not say, "We tore the prior writ," is invalid. Ramah derives the present law directly from those rules, as Baal HaTerumot explains in Shaar 54. The correct explanation is Sema's. We do not suspect judges of misunderstanding the law, but we do fear ordinary human forgetfulness. Even great and wise judges can forget to perform a required physical step. Therefore, if the replacement does not record that the original was torn, we cannot assume from silence that it occurred. The record is invalid until the required safeguard is established. Bach asks: since we rule that a receipt may be written, why is actual tearing necessary? When the creditor later collects, the debtor can obtain a receipt. In loan cases there is no concern of a receipt against purchasers in the same way. Bach answers that where a problem can be prevented in advance, we do not rely upon forcing the debtor to preserve a receipt forever. If the court can eliminate the risk by destroying the first instrument, that is preferable. Shach rejects this, asking why the replacement should be invalid even after the fact when the debtor could still be protected by a receipt. Bach is correct. The rule from the outset is that the court issuing the replacement must destroy the original. Otherwise the court itself creates a situation in which the creditor can hold two apparently independent instruments. Suppose the first court improperly issues a replacement without tearing the original. The creditor can then go to a second court with the original document and witnesses to its deterioration or loss. The second court, following the law, tears the original and issues another replacement explicitly stating that the first was destroyed. When the creditor later presents that second replacement, the debtor sees that it records proper destruction of the original. He has no reason to suspect another valid replacement exists, because a court issuing one is presumed to follow the law. He therefore pays without demanding a receipt. The creditor can then present the first improperly issued replacement and collect again. At that point a later receipt does not help. Double payment has already occurred. This is why a replacement that does not record destruction of the first document is invalid even after the fact. The requirement is not merely procedural etiquette. It is the mechanism that allows the debtor safely to rely upon the uniqueness of the replacement instrument. A further issue depends upon which authorities one follows concerning proof of an erased document. According to the broader view, different groups of witnesses could each testify that they saw and recognized the original document before it disappeared. The creditor could potentially go to several courts with different witness groups and obtain multiple replacements. The requirement that each court destroy the original and record that fact is therefore essential. According to the stricter Rashbam-Rambam-Tosafot view explained above, outside witnesses are not enough. The original transaction witnesses, or other witnesses to the actual loan and writing, must also participate. If there were only one such group, the risk of multiple replacements would be much smaller, because those witnesses could tell the second court they had already participated in the first replacement. Still, there may have been many people present at the original loan and writing, and different groups could support different applications. Thus the safeguard remains relevant even under the stricter view. This may explain why some authorities do not emphasize Ramah's rule. Its practical necessity varies according to their theory of how an erased document is reconstructed. But where the risk exists, the explicit statement that the original was torn is indispensable.
But a gift document. As explained in Urim, according to Ramban we must say that when the donor originally instructed the witnesses to write, he ordinarily intended to authorize whatever replacement writing would later be necessary, because no financial loss results to him from a second evidentiary document. Accordingly, if the donor now expressly protests, the witnesses themselves should not write another document without his consent. By protesting, he revokes their original agency. According to Ramban, once the agency to write has been revoked, a new writing by the witnesses is no longer direct testimony authorized by the party and is subject to the problem of written testimony. Ramban says this explicitly in Perek HaSefinah: the grantor can retract from the document because it is no longer the "book of transfer" authorized by him. Maharik, responsum 115, writes that even if the borrower or grantor stands and protests, a replacement can still be written. His proofs, however, do not establish that ordinary witnesses themselves may write against an express protest. He cites the erased-document case, but according to Tosafot and Rashbam the obligor is not standing before beit din there. When he is absent, we can assume he remains content with the original instruction. That is very different from explicit revocation. He also cites the case of one who burns another's document, where the Gemara says that if witnesses know the document they can write another. But even if the obligor protests, the underlying debt remains enforceable. The burner therefore did not necessarily cause a complete loss, because beit din can still establish the obligation through testimony and judicial process. Thus that source also does not prove that ordinary witnesses may write a fresh document over the obligor's protest. Nevertheless, Maharik is correct regarding a judicial replacement. Where beit din itself issues the replacement, the obligor's protest does not control. A judicial act is not merely the witnesses' private written testimony, and the rule of unauthorized written testimony does not apply in the same way. Therefore the distinction is clear. If beit din issues the replacement after proper proof, the obligor's protest does not prevent it. If the original witnesses themselves seek to write a new gift or sale document after the grantor expressly revoked their authority, they should not do so according to Ramban. Ritva, cited by Sema at the end of this siman, supports this. Where a gift document was torn and the donor claims the gift was waived, the witnesses should go to beit din, and beit din may issue the replacement. Ritva does not authorize the witnesses themselves to rewrite it over the donor's objection. This is the correct distinction.
A gift document, etc. The same applies to a sale document without warranty; the Gemara states this explicitly. There is, however, a difficulty according to Tosafot's explanation that witnesses who completed their agency ordinarily may not write again because repeated writing can lead them to create an antedated document. Why, then, may they rewrite a sale or gift document without warranty? Suppose the sale or gift occurred in Nisan, no document was written then, and the witnesses later write in Tishrei but date the paper from Nisan. Even without warranty, that earlier date can cause real legal harm. For example, Bava Batra teaches that possession by three successive purchasers can combine only where the intermediate transfers were by written document, because a written sale has publicity. Suppose Reuven possessed the field for two and a half years, then in Nisan sold it orally to Shimon, who possessed for another half year. The original claimant may argue that Reuven never completed three continuous years and that he had no reason to know Shimon was possessing under Reuven, because the sale was unwritten and therefore lacked publicity. If the witnesses now write a document in Tishrei dated back to Nisan, Shimon can present it and claim that from Nisan onward his possession was publicly linked to Reuven. The earlier date can therefore convert what was not a valid continuous chazakah into one that appears valid, harming the claimant. The opposite can also occur. Where a possessor held for two and a half years before a new purchaser bought from the former owner in Nisan without a document, the possessor may have had no reason to treat that sale as a protest because an unwritten sale lacks publicity. If witnesses later create a Nisan-dated document, the sale retroactively appears to have been a public protest during the three-year period, and the possessor loses his chazakah unjustly. Thus an antedated sale or gift document can cause harm even without warranty. The difficulty therefore remains: if Tosafot prohibit repeat writing because witnesses may come to antedate documents, why do they permit it for no-warranty sales and gifts? Perhaps the answer is that the rule allowing ten documents means they are all written on the same day as the original transaction and delivered together or successively so the purchaser has duplicates for proof if one is later lost. Then no antedating exists. The Gemara's challenge, "Witnesses who performed their agency may not perform it again," and Tosafot's concern of antedating arise only under the initial assumption that the case involved a warranty document that was lost or burned and then recreated later from the original date. Once the Gemara answers that the documents have no warranty, the case can be limited to multiple documents written that same day. This is possible, though it would be useful to see it stated by the poskim. Another possibility is that only a warranty document creates the type of publicity relevant to purchasers and third-party claims. A no-warranty writing may not generate the same legal public notice. If so, backdating a no-warranty document would not affect chazakah and protest in the way described. That distinction also requires study. The issue remains difficult and should not be treated as obvious.
