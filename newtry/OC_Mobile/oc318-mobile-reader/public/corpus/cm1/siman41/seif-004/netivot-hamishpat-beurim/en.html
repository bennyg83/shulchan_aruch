See Shach, who concludes that if the witnesses are still available and testify that the debt is still within its term, the borrower is not believed to say he paid, because even if we disregard the document there remain witnesses that the debt is within its term. Urim VeTumim asks from a ketubah, which is not returned even while she is still married although we know the debt has not yet matured, and notes that the Gemara does not distinguish found documents according to whether there are witnesses that the debt is still within its term. In my view the Shach is correct. The fall of a document does not create a presumption that it was paid merely because the lender failed to guard it; perhaps it was lost through accident or because of some other defect. The fall only weakens the usual presumption arising from the document, especially the argument, 'If it was paid, why is your document in my hand?' because perhaps it fell from the borrower. But where there is an independent presumption against payment, such as a debt within its term, we rely on that presumption and say the document was accidentally lost. As for the ketubah, the found-document rule there concerns return of the physical document, not whether she can eventually collect. A ketubah is collectible by virtue of the judicial enactment even if the writing is lost. We therefore need not strengthen her hand by returning the document when there is a competing claim that it fell from the husband. The Tosafot likewise indicate that their concern is loss of future documentary advantage, not present inability to collect the money. If there are no living witnesses to the original loan and the only indication that the term has not expired appears in the found document itself, the borrower can say that he prepared this document but ultimately did not borrow on those terms and later borrowed under a different document whose term already expired. If, however, witnesses recognize that this very instrument was the one under which the loan was made, that is equivalent to living witnesses that the debt remains within its term. Ketzot HaChoshen's answer based on a special concern for bundles of money in ketubah cases is not persuasive, because that concern ordinarily affects an oath, not the substantive presumption of nonpayment. The principal rule is as stated.
