One who had a debt, etc. These are the Rambam's words at the end of chapter 14 and in chapter 23 of Hilchot Malveh. The Raavad objects in chapter 23: since the debt is still within its term, why should the lender not collect and write a receipt? In chapter 14 he likewise says the ruling is not clear or well founded, though the lender should at least collect upon oath. Some printed texts insert into the Raavad's gloss words beginning "if it is so even when lost," but those are a copyist's error taken from the Maggid Mishneh. The Raavad's gloss is only as quoted here, as appears in reliable editions and in the Baal HaTerumot. I mention this because Maharam Padua saw a difficulty in an early printed Mishneh Torah, thinking the alleged words of the Raavad were actually the Rambam's, and the Kesef Mishneh cited the confusion. It is an error. The Maggid Mishneh extensively questions the Raavad. The entire first chapter of Bava Metzia and its Mishnayot show that even within the due date a fallen note is returned neither to lender nor borrower. The Ramban, Rashba, and other commentators say the same. Here the borrower actually claims payment and the note is entirely lost, so the lender should certainly not be believed. The Maggid Mishneh finds the law so obvious that he wonders why the Rambam attributes it to his teachers. He rejects the suggestion that the law of a fallen note concerns only debts after maturity, since the Gemara proves otherwise. Because of this, the Kesef Mishneh says the Rambam's special attribution to his teachers concerns a note found in another person's possession where that person does not remember how it came to him, whereas a note known to have fallen would be ordinary law and would not have drawn the Raavad's objection. The Bach follows this. This distinction is untenable. The first chapter of Bava Metzia gives no basis to distinguish a fallen note from one found in another's hands. Moreover, the Rambam expressly invokes his teachers regarding a note wholly lost and no longer before us, which is even worse than a fallen note, and the Raavad expressly objects there as well. Gidulei Terumah suggests that the teachers' ruling concerned a debt still within its term because the Gemara does not establish that a fallen note within its term is withheld. This is forced and contrary to the Maggid Mishneh. The Mishnah says without qualification that found debt instruments are not returned and never distinguishes before and after maturity. Rav Amram and Rabbi Yirmiyah also cite documents of beit din without answering that those cases concern debts not yet due. The opening sugya of Bava Metzia is even clearer. Regarding two people holding a note, the Gemara challenges Rabbi Yose's lack of concern for payment from a lost ketubah. The Rabbis' view is understandable only if loss creates concern for payment even during the husband's lifetime, when the ketubah is not yet due. The case where the husband admits and the ketubah is returned must be one without concern for collusion, such as where there is no property warranty. At minimum, the sugya shows that loss creates concern for payment before maturity. The dispute is limited to a ketubah, which is not ordinarily payable during marriage; a regular debt note within its term is certainly impaired by loss. The Ritva states expressly that although Rabbi Yose is concerned for payment regarding ordinary notes even within their term, he is not concerned for a ketubah because it was not intended for collection during the marriage. The Nimukei Yosef similarly writes that a ketubah is not impaired by loss during the marriage, whereas a debt note is impaired even within its term because it is certainly intended for payment and borrowers sometimes pay early. This is clear. Nevertheless, it appears to me that the Maggid Mishneh and later authorities did not fully understand the Raavad or Rambam. The Raavad certainly agrees that a fallen note is impaired even within its term, that payment is suspected, and that even the borrower's admission may involve collusion. This is why he did not object when the Rambam ruled that a found ketubah is not returned even if both spouses admit. His objection concerns the Rambam's additional words in chapters 14 and 23: "the witnesses are still present," yet the lender collects nothing on their testimony, even where they testify that the debt was for a fixed term. The Raavad's point is that when the original witnesses stand before us and testify that the loan was made and is still within its term, we may disregard the lost document. This is no worse than an oral loan before witnesses, where the borrower is not believed to say he paid before maturity under siman 78. The Bava Metzia sugyot address the impairment of the document itself, especially collection from purchasers, or a case where the lender relies only on the document and the original witnesses are not presently testifying to the loan and its term. Here the witnesses personally establish the loan and that it is not due. Why should the lender not collect? This also explains why the Rambam attributes the ruling to his teachers. Their innovation is that even where the witnesses are present and testify to a term, the lender collects nothing on their testimony after the note is lost. There is no need for the forced distinctions of the Kesef Mishneh, Bach, or Gidulei Terumah. The Tur may also agree with the Raavad, for when citing the Rambam he omits the phrase that the witnesses remain present. The Raavad's objection appears strong. Migdal Oz's claim that the Rambam's ruling is simply the Gemara in Bava Metzia and Get Pashut reflects insufficient analysis. The Baal HaTerumot reports an objection to the Raavad's statement that the lender should collect upon oath: if fixing a due date gives him the ordinary presumption, he should collect without oath; if it gives no protection, why should an oath help? This is not difficult. Some authorities in siman 78 require an oath even for collection within the term. The Raavad therefore means that even according to those opinions, the lender should at least collect upon oath. Gidulei Terumah's different answer is not correct. For practical law, the Baal HaTerumot appears to follow the Rambam. The Semag and Rabbeinu Yerucham also cite the Rambam without dissent. They must hold that although in an ordinary oral loan the borrower is not believed to claim payment within the term, this case is worse because the parties deliberately created a document and the loan was fundamentally made upon that document. Once the document is lost, the loan itself is impaired, and the borrower may claim payment even against the original witnesses. The Rashba says this expressly in responsum 1073, cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 39: even if witnesses testify that the debt is still within its term, the borrower may claim payment because the lender's failure to produce the document weakens his claim. The Mechaber rules accordingly in siman 39. This is his position here as well. Still, the ruling is not compelled by the Gemara and all decisors. Practical law requires study, for the possessor may perhaps say that the law follows the Raavad.
