The debt has a fixed term. Rambam rules that where a debt instrument is lost, the creditor cannot collect merely because the debt is still within its term. Raavad objects that before the due date a debtor is presumed not to have paid, so the creditor should at least collect with an oath and write a receipt. Maggid Mishneh responds that the sugyot concerning lost documents show that even before the due date a fallen instrument is not returned. The Shach argues that the sugyot indeed include debts within term. He nevertheless interprets Raavad as agreeing that a lost instrument is weakened even within term, but as distinguishing where the original witnesses are present and testify orally to the loan and its term. In that case, says the Shach, even if one disregards the lost document, an oral loan established by witnesses within term should be collectible. I do not find this distinction convincing. If the defect of loss is strong enough to create a concern of payment despite the document's proof of the loan and due date, why should the same witnesses repeating those very facts orally restore the presumption? The ketubah passage is even stronger. While the wife is still married, the existence and term of the ketubah are judicially known through the enactment of beit din even without any document at all, yet the Sages still fear payment or deposited funds when a ketubah is found fallen. It follows that the fall can undermine even a strong independent basis. In my view, however, the ketubah case does not prove that an ordinary within term loan loses its presumption. A ketubah is unique because the presumption against payment within term does not operate normally. Even during the husband's lifetime there is concern that he deposited bundles of money with her, just as a widow collecting from orphans must swear because of the concern for deposited funds. The Rosh and Tosafot explain that ketubah rights arise by beit din condition and are therefore subject to this special concern. Thus a fallen ketubah can be weakened even while she remains married because the ordinary within term presumption is absent. In an ordinary loan, where no concern of deposited funds exists and the presumption against early payment is strong, it is possible that the fall of the document does not defeat that presumption. This gives substantial force to Raavad. Maharshal argues that a handwritten ketubah signed by the husband can still collect from heirs based on the within term presumption, subject to an oath. Mishneh LaMelech disputes this and says the special ketubah concern defeats the presumption entirely. The issue depends upon the competing explanations of the Rishonim. The Gemara itself supports Raavad's conceptual distinction: Rabbi Yose says that while she remains under her husband the ketubah should be returned because she is not yet subject to payment. The Sages respond specifically, "Perhaps he deposited bundles with her." They do not say that the mere fall defeats every presumption. This implies that an ordinary debt without such a concern could indeed retain its within term presumption despite the fallen document. Rambam nevertheless records the contrary practical ruling in the name of his teachers, and his language may reflect precisely that this is a special additional law not obvious from the general sugyot.
If the borrower claimed, "I paid you." The Tur writes that if a creditor's document was lost and he sued the borrower, who first denied the entire matter, while witnesses established that the loan and document existed, the borrower becomes established as a false denier and cannot afterward say, "I paid." The source is Bava Metzia concerning Shabtai son of Rabbi Marinus, who gave a cloak to his daughter in law as part of her ketubah and later denied it after the ketubah was lost. Witnesses proved the writing, and Rabbi Chiya treated him as a false denier. Beit Yosef says the law of ketubah and other documents is the same. According to the novellae of Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi that I cited in siman 65, however, the proof is not conclusive. There are two forms of being established as a false denier. In one, the person's statement can later be supported by witnesses, as explained in siman 79. Where the document has fallen and is independently weakened, such a person may be believed on his own later statement even without witnesses. In the stronger form, where even witnesses supporting his later claim would not help, such as one who first said, "I never borrowed," and later says, "I repaid," because his original denial itself admitted nonpayment, he remains barred even though the document fell. The Shulchan Aruch's omission of the Tur's explicit rule is therefore difficult, but the distinction explains which type of false denial should remain operative after the document is weakened.
