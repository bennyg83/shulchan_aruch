One who had, etc. Rambam gives two rules in Hilchot Malveh 14. First, where the lender's document is lost even during the loan term, the borrower can claim payment. Second, where the document is found in another person's possession during the term, the borrower can likewise claim that it fell from him after he paid. Rambam attributes both rulings to his teachers. Raavad objects to both: how can the borrower be believed to say he paid within the term? Maggid Mishneh is puzzled in the opposite direction. Why does Rambam attribute this to his teachers? The Gemara says even a document lost on the very day it was written is not returned unless the debtor admits the debt. There is no stronger case of being within the term than that. Shach adds that Rambam rules similarly concerning a found ketubah, which is not returned to the woman because of concern for payment and collusion. Raavad does not object there, although a ketubah during marriage is surely not yet payable. Shach therefore distinguishes. If the lender has no independent loan witnesses and relies only on the lost document, everyone agrees repayment can be feared even within the term. But if the original loan witnesses are present, the lender can say, "Ignore the document. I sue you on the basis of the witnesses, and the debt is still within its term." Rambam's teachers rule that even then the borrower may claim payment; Raavad disagrees. This is difficult. A married woman herself supplies proof stronger than ordinary loan witnesses that a ketubah obligation exists. If the document is found during marriage and within the ordinary nonpayable period, why should she not recover, at least against the husband? And if the ketubah's public status is sufficient, why not against purchasers as well? One cannot simply say concern for payment applies there but not in an ordinary loan. If the presumption that a person does not pay within the term is strong enough to collect even from minor heirs, it should also have force in a ketubah setting where the obligation is publicly known. It is possible to distinguish a ketubah because no specific payment term was fixed by agreement; its maturity depends on divorce or death. That may be weaker than an expressly fixed loan term. Likewise, a document written and authenticated on the same day may not always imply an explicit term lasting that entire day. Still, it is strained to explain every Talmudic case of an authenticated document as either lacking a term or already being after the term. And when someone goes to the effort of executing and authenticating a document today, it is reasonable to infer he did not expect immediate repayment the same moment. Thus Shach's distinction remains difficult. The better explanation appears from Rambam's exact wording. Rambam's first case, where the lender says, "My document was lost," does not merely involve a borrower saying "I paid." The very loss of the document creates a defect in the lender's position. Rambam had already addressed found documents in Hilchot Gezelah and Aveidah. Why repeat the rule here? Because the present chapter concerns the legal consequence of the lender's own document no longer being in his possession. The first case says only that the borrower claims payment. The second says more specifically: the document is found in another person's possession, and the borrower says, "It fell from me after I paid." This distinction is deliberate. Where the borrower affirmatively claims that he paid and the lender returned the document to him, everyone, including Raavad, can agree that the discovered defect may overcome the ordinary within-term presumption. The Gemara's rule that a found document is not returned, even a ketubah, fits this. The concern is not merely abstract early payment. It is that possession of the document has been disrupted in a way consistent with return after payment. Consider Raavad's position. If a debtor actually did pay within the term, would he be required to insist upon a receipt rather than accept return of his original document? Of course not. Returning the instrument is the ordinary proof of discharge. Therefore, where the borrower plausibly says, "I paid early and he returned the paper to me," the presumption against early payment is countered by the very fact that the paper is no longer with the creditor. Rambam's more difficult first case is where the document is known to have fallen from the lender's possession, not from the borrower. There the lender still has one strong presumption: "If you paid, why was the document still in my possession?" And within the term there is a second presumption: people ordinarily do not pay early. These two presumptions together are powerful. Raavad therefore says the lender should prevail. Rambam's teachers nonetheless say the loss itself weakens the lender enough that the borrower can claim payment. This is precisely why Rambam attributes the rule to them. It is not obvious from the ordinary found-document sugya. This reading also explains Rambam's later law in Hilchot Malveh 23, where a creditor says his document was lost and beit din refuses to write another because perhaps the debt was paid, even during the term. As I explained above, that too should be read where it is known the document was lost from the creditor, though the circumstances of the loss are uncertain. Rambam says the very failure to safeguard the document creates a concern that perhaps it was already discharged and therefore treated casually. Raavad again objects within the term because the two presumptions favor the creditor. Thus their disagreement is consistent across the chapters. The second case, where the document is in another person's hand, is different. If the third person remembers how the paper came to him, the case may be easy. The difficult case is where he does not remember. Rambam says the borrower can claim, "It fell from me after payment." Kesef Mishneh and Gidulei Terumah explain this. If the borrower admitted the document never came back to him and instead fell directly from the lender, there would be no significant defect. The third person's forgetfulness would not weaken the lender's rights. The borrower must specifically claim that he recovered the instrument upon payment and later lost it. Because the third party cannot contradict him, the paper's present location supports that possibility. Rambam therefore again treats the instrument as impaired despite the unexpired term. Raavad disagrees. Since the third person does not know that the document fell from the borrower, and since the debt is within its term, we should presume it somehow passed from the lender to the third person without payment. The lender's presumptions remain stronger. This gives a coherent explanation of both disputes. It also explains why Rambam separates the cases rather than treating them as one rule. In the first, the defect is known loss from the lender. In the second, the document is in another person's possession and the borrower affirmatively claims return after payment. The evidentiary structures are different. Accordingly, Shach's explanation through the presence or absence of loan witnesses is unnecessary and creates more problems than it solves. The dispute is about the weight of the document's impaired possession against the ordinary within-term presumptions. Rambam's teachers give greater weight to the impairment. Raavad gives greater weight to the unexpired term and the principle, "If you paid, why is the document still effectively traceable to the lender?" This is the correct reading.
