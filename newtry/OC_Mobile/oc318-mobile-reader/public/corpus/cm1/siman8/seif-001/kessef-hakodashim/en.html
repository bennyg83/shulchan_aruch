Seif 2. As though a sword were resting. The Sages instructed a judge to view himself as though a sword were resting upon him and Gehinnom were open beneath him. Maharam Schiff explains this in connection with the two crowns of freedom, freedom from the angel of death and from subjugation to the kingdoms, and with the choice made by Avraham Avinu between exile and Gehinnom. The meaning may be that one who corrupts justice is called a perpetrator of injustice and causes the Shechinah to depart. It is therefore as though those two crowns have been removed from him, so that both subjugation and Gehinnom become conceivable consequences. Avraham chose subjugation in place of Gehinnom for ordinary sinners, but perhaps this choice does not protect a judge who deliberately corrupts justice, which Scripture calls an abomination. Maharam Schiff's language also suggests that the sword itself represents subjugation to the kingdoms. This too may mean that the judge must fear lest both subjugation and Gehinnom apply. His precise intent requires further study. Alternatively, the sword may refer directly to the statement in Avot that the sword comes upon the world for delay and corruption of judgment and for those who rule in Torah not according to halachah. The judge must therefore feel the fear of a sword lest he distort the law. Tosafot explain that "a true judgment in its truth" means both a judgment according to halachah and one that penetrates a deceptive case, so that the ruling matches the actual facts as closely as possible. To discover the truth from the litigants' arguments, a judge must avoid anger, which silences a party's arguments, and flattery or favoritism toward one party, which can likewise silence the other. The Sages said that one who becomes angry is subject to the powers of Gehinnom, and flattery too leads to Gehinnom and to Divine anger and suffering. By keeping the fear of Gehinnom before him, the judge will guard himself from anger and flattery and judge properly.
