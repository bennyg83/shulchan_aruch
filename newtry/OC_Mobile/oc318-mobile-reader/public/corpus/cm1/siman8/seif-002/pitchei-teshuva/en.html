One appointed because of money. Beer HaGolah cites the Bach that the prohibition concerns one who is not learned in Torah wisdom. If he is learned in Torah, even though there are greater scholars than he in the city, there is no prohibition. The Bach himself concludes, however, that this is only where he did not pay money in order to be appointed, but the community appointed him because he was also wealthy. If he paid money in order to obtain the position, then even if he is greater in wisdom and stature than the other scholars it is forbidden to stand before him. Knesset HaGedolah likewise cites the Bach. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 160, writes that Tosafot Yeshanim to Yoma concerning Yehoshua ben Gamla indicates that only where there is another scholar superior to him is the appointee called wicked; the Bach's statement that he is called wicked even where no one is superior to him may reflect that the Tosafot Yeshanim escaped his attention. Chatam Sofer further discusses a city that needed to appoint a rabbi. They selected four candidates to place in a lot, with the understanding that the first name drawn would be put to a vote; if a majority accepted him he would become rabbi, and if not they would proceed to the second, and so forth. The majority rejected the first two candidates, and the third was accepted by a majority. Several days later it became known that many who had voted for him had received bribes from the rabbi's relatives, and a letter was found indicating this. Chatam Sofer rules that if two valid witnesses, who are neither members of the community nor relatives of its members or of the rabbi, testify that bribes were received, the entire selection made through that vote is void from the outset. The voters were required to state their opinion for the sake of Heaven, as the Rama writes at the beginning of siman 163, yet they acted because of bribes. This invalidates not only the acceptance of the third rabbi but even their rejection of the first candidates, because that too was not for the sake of Heaven. Even if the bribed voters were a minority and an unbribed majority would still remain for the third candidate, the others may argue that only because the first candidates had already been rejected were they compelled to choose the third, so the entire procedure must be repeated. If valid witnesses testify that the rabbi himself instructed that bribes be given, he is disqualified from the rabbinate altogether until he repents, even if otherwise fully qualified. If there is no testimony that the rabbi himself knew of the bribes and they were given only by relatives or associates, he retains his presumption of innocence and loses no right because of their actions. The community must vote anew among the three remaining candidates, and whichever receives more approvals than objections should be appointed. Those who accepted bribes may not participate in the assembly at all. Even if they return the bribe and accept upon themselves under solemn oath never again to receive such a bribe, they may not participate in this appointment; possibly they are disqualified from communal appointment even permanently until they repent. Even if they constitute the majority, they cannot necessarily argue that the minority cannot impose a rabbi and decisor upon them whom they do not want, because all had already agreed to choose one of the four names placed in the lot, so the harm is limited. In any event, there is no alternative, and this is how the matter should be adjudicated in similar cases. All of this applies where there are valid witnesses. Without such witnesses, the rabbi does not lose his appointment under any circumstances. Even if the recipients of the bribes and the relatives who gave them admit what occurred, they are not empowered through their testimony to cause the rabbi to lose his position. The recipients must nevertheless return the money, because they admit receiving it. Although a person cannot render himself wicked by his own admission, we divide his statement and accept that he received the funds as a deposit or loan, requiring repayment. If the recipients deny receiving anything, the givers may impose a heset oath upon them based on their definite claim. None of this causes the rabbi to lose his appointment. Even the secret letter discovered stating that bribes were received is insufficient to remove him. The members of the community may, however, impose a communal ban upon anyone who knows that he was appointed through bribery and nevertheless exercises authority over them. Chatam Sofer concludes that all this appears very clear.
