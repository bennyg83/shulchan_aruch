One who causes him pain, beit din has authority, etc. Meaning, where witnesses establish that he caused pain to the court officer, beit din may administer disciplinary lashes. Without witnesses, the officer is not believed to have him beaten merely on his own statement, although he is believed for purposes of placing the offender under ban. According to this, the conjunction in the text should be removed so that it reads 'to testify.' The Tur's wording supports this. Although excommunication is regarded by most people as more severe than physical beating and under Talmudic law it is also weighty, regarding credibility the officer was not believed to authorize bodily force without witnesses. He was believed only for a ban, which remains in the hands of beit din and does not itself lay hands upon the person's body. This resembles the rule that a court officer is believed to say that a litigant refused to appear so as to place him under ban, but not to issue a written notice carrying monetary expense. Causing pain and humiliation are essentially one issue; the sources use different terms because the statements appear in different tractates. I explained and proved this in the Perishah.
There is no prohibition of lashon hara in this. The Mechaber himself writes this in siman 11. The Gemara learns it from the incident of Moshe, where the court messenger reported the litigants' insolent words. Had this constituted forbidden speech, Moshe would not have recorded the report in the Torah.
