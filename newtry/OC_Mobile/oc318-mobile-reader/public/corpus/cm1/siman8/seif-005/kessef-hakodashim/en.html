Since siman 8 states explicitly that the concern regarding eating and drinking before the public applies to a social feast, it appears that on Shabbat, even at seudah shelishit, there is no concern, because it is a mitzvah meal. Even where one fulfills some aspect through translated study, the meal itself remains a mitzvah. Just as one may invite more guests than strictly required to any mitzvah meal, there is no objection on that account. Perhaps there is likewise no concern where the judge eats his own private meal while many ordinary people merely see him. The concern may be specifically the social association of eating together with a group. Since the restriction is not explicit in the Gemara and was derived by analogy, the derivation may extend only to joining a social feast. Possibly the references to gatherings of unlearned people and to a social meal describe the same concern: joining them socially, not eating a mitzvah meal in their presence. At a mitzvah meal there is no prohibition merely because an unlearned person is present, since the Sages faulted one who refuses a mitzvah meal after being invited. The statement that one who eats with an am haaretz is compared to an earthenware vessel may likewise refer specifically to reclining with him as part of a social companionship, perhaps especially where the am haaretz is the host. By contrast, where one invites an ordinary person to his own table, the same concern may not exist. The hospitality of Avraham Avinu presumably involved eating with guests, and hospitality itself may be included in a mitzvah meal. Similarly, a meal of a mitzvah association, such as a siyum of Mishnah or Gemara, is plainly a mitzvah meal. Associations formed for a sandak, visiting the sick, acts of kindness, clothing the poor, or supporting Torah study may also qualify because they directly facilitate mitzvot. Other associations whose meal is not itself connected to improving performance of the mitzvah may require distinction and further study. Although the wife of a Torah scholar is treated like the scholar in many laws, these restrictions do not appear to become a literal prohibition for her. The comparison is analogical rather than complete identity, and in these matters, which are themselves rabbinic, the analogy should not create a new prohibition. Still, it is proper, where feasible, to conduct the household in a manner that supports the honor of Torah and proper respect among people.
