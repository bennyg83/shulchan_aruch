And the court messenger is believed like two witnesses, etc. This means that where there are witnesses, the offender may receive corporal lashes. Without witnesses, the court messenger alone is not believed to justify lashes, though he is believed for excommunication. Sema explains that corporal punishment controls the litigant's body and therefore requires two witnesses. This is strained. Knesset HaGedolah's explanation is more convincing. For excommunication the messenger has a migo: he could have said the person refused to come to beit din, which itself warrants excommunication. For lashes he has no such migo, and therefore cannot establish the case alone. Bach asks from the incident of Rav Yehudah in Kiddushin, where a man was lashed based on the court messenger's report. There is no difficulty. The offender insulted Rav Yehudah in the presence of both the court messenger and the butcher, providing two witnesses. Maharit further asks that in that incident the offender did not physically distress the rabbinic messenger. Rashi already answers: he spoke insulting words about the sender in the messenger's presence, forcing the messenger to hear his master's humiliation silently. That itself is considered distress caused to the court messenger.
Who insulted him so as to require excommunication. Beit Yosef, Maharit, and Sema note that the section first uses the language of distress for lashes and then humiliation for excommunication, though they seem closely related. The better distinction is that one who humiliates a court messenger thereby raises his hand against beit din itself and therefore deserves excommunication. One who merely causes the messenger pain, or even strikes him in order to escape, without intending contempt for the court, has not openly repudiated beit din. His misconduct warrants lashes but not necessarily excommunication. This is a sound distinction.
