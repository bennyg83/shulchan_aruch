And nevertheless they would not sit in judgment, etc. This refers specifically to judicial office. The Sages praised one who avoids judgment, for he avoids hatred and related burdens. Other communal appointments are different. If a person believes himself fit for a public role, he need not refuse it merely because people have not pressed him. This resolves Taz's difficulty from Kiddushin 76b, where Rabbi Bibi contended with a convert and sought municipal authority even without repeated urging. That office was not judicial but concerned other communal matters, as Rashi explains. Sema asks that here even a uniquely great scholar seems to be advised not to accept judicial office without urging, while siman 10 teaches that a scholar qualified to rule who refuses to do so is included among those called "many are her slain." Sema answers that if there is no comparable scholar anywhere, he must judge, whereas if comparable scholars exist elsewhere he may refuse unless urged. That distinction is strained. Why should a judge one hundred parasangs away count as available? Rather, if competent judges are present locally, even if they are less learned than he, he may refuse because justice will still be competently administered. The condemnation in siman 10 applies where no qualified person is present and his refusal causes ignorant people to adjudicate and corrupt justice. The wording there is precise: if he knows that there is another person "fit to rule," his own refusal is praiseworthy. It does not require one equal to him, only someone adequately qualified.
