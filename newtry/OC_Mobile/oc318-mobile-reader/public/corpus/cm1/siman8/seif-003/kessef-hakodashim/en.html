I further found. I wrote elsewhere that the rule forbidding a judge appointed over his city to perform work before three people does not apply to occasional labor. The universal practice is that judges write and sign gittin publicly, although writing is skilled labor and can involve effort. Labor that directly facilitates a mitzvah likewise appears unobjectionable, because activity that increases the honor of Heaven is not degrading. The Rosh personally participated in grinding flour for Pesach even though he held communal authority across many communities and could have delegated it. Work done for a mitzvah does not diminish honor, particularly when it is not a fixed occupation. It also appears that all the restrictions stated in the Shulchan Aruch apply specifically to judges permanently appointed as the exclusive judicial authority of the city. Where a scholar is not uniquely appointed, and cases are sometimes heard before him and sometimes before others, he is not in the same category. This explains the public labor of Karna and similar incidents in the Gemara, and the fact that many Tannaim and Amoraim earned their livelihood through trades. It would have been impossible for a permanently practicing craftsman to ensure that no three people ever saw him at work. Accordingly, unless a scholar is the city's principal av beit din before whom matters must be brought, he is not considered "a judge appointed over the public" for this purpose. Just as the craftsmen of a city could be gathered for communal needs, so the sages of a city could be gathered for judgment, without any one of them having exclusive judicial appointment. Even among regularly appointed judges, the strongest application of the restriction is to the av beit din who can adjudicate as an expert individual. In large cities that require several permanent judges who devote themselves entirely to adjudication, all those fixed judges before whom cases exclusively come should observe the restrictions where the community can support them sufficiently, including not performing labor before three and maintaining the degree of dignified separation described by the authorities. This is the preferable arrangement.
