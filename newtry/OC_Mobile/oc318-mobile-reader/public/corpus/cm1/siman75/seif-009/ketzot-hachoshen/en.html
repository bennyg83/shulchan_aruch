If he wishes to fulfill his obligation before Heaven. Rivash rules that where the claimant has no enforceable right in human court and the defendant is obligated only to satisfy Heaven, seizure by the claimant is ineffective and the property is removed from him. A question arises where the defendant, after saying, "I do not know whether you lent me," pays without qualification and later says he paid only because he mistakenly thought he was legally obligated. Beit Yaakov treats this as mistaken waiver. In my view, where he truly had a moral obligation to satisfy Heaven and paid without qualification, we do not ordinarily treat the payment as mistaken. The case of heirs who voluntarily pay a father's debt from movables illustrates this: when they pay without saying that the money is specifically for release of the land, we say they fulfilled the mitzvah and do not call the payment a mistake merely because human court could not have compelled it. Similarly, where a transaction is not legally binding but a person performs because he wishes to stand by his word, the payment is not automatically treated as a mistake. Rabbeinu Tam explains that where one could retract but ought not do so because of trustworthiness, we assume that even had he known he could retract he might still have honored his word. All the more so where he owes the matter before Heaven. Rivash also distinguishes a factual mistake from a mistake of law. In some monetary settings a payment made because of a legal misunderstanding is still treated as effective waiver. Therefore, if a person who is obligated before Heaven pays without qualification, the stronger rule is that the payment remains effective. The episode where Rava refused to accept stolen sheep returned by thieves can be understood as one where they expressly returned only because they believed themselves legally liable, not because they wished to satisfy Heaven. That does not establish a general rule of mistaken payment in our case.

The claimant need not swear. Taz discusses a borrower who repaid, and afterward the lender found a counterfeit coin and sought to return it, while the borrower said he did not know whether that coin came from him. Taz treats this as doubt whether a new obligation arose, because the debt had already been definitely paid and the parties had separated. Maharshal's responsum treats it instead as "I do not know whether I repaid you," because perhaps the original payment was defective. Rashba's discussion of a three party transfer supports Taz where there was a completed, legally effective payment and only afterward a new asserted defect arose. If the transferee later admits that the calculation was mistaken, the debtor can say, "I certainly paid. Your later claim of error is uncertain as to me." There the original payment remains definite against the debtor because the transferor is not ordinarily believed to say he miscalculated. The counterfeit coin case can nevertheless differ because the later discovery directly creates uncertainty about the quality of the original payment itself. The coin may never have constituted valid payment. Thus the matter did not necessarily reach a state of certain repayment. This explains why the authorities can disagree. The case in Gittin where money was thrown and it is uncertain whether it landed closer to lender or borrower is clearly "I do not know whether I repaid," because the doubt arose at the very moment of payment. The debt was never definitely discharged first. Rashba expressly distinguishes such a case. Thus Taz's principle applies where the payment was certainly complete before the later doubt arose, not where the doubt inheres in the original payment event.
And if he later retracts. Maharshal argues that the rule allowing one who said, "I do not know," later to say, "I remembered," should not apply where the first statement already changed him from liability to exemption under our law. In my view Ramah's ruling in siman 59 gives strong proof that later recollection can be accepted. A creditor who first says concerning his document, "I do not know whether it was paid," does not collect, yet if he later says, "I remembered that it was not paid," he is believed. There too his first uncertainty had already prevented collection. All the more should a defendant be able to say he remembered. The remaining difficulty is from witnesses. If witnesses say simply, "We do not remember," they cannot ordinarily return and say, "We remembered," unless they initially qualified their statement by saying, "We do not remember now." Since a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses, why can he retract from "I do not know" to "I remember"? This requires distinction between evidentiary testimony and a party's pleadings. The prohibition against returning and testifying again is unique to witness testimony once completed. A litigant can amend an uncertain plea before it has been fixed as a contradictory factual admission. In any event, if he said explicitly, "I do not remember now," everyone should agree that he may later say he remembered, because that is not a contradiction at all.

In the ordinary case of "I do not know whether I repaid," his liability comes from his own unresolved doubt about exemption. When he later asserts a certain defense, he removes that doubt. But in the fifty and fifty case the law itself already imposed payment through the rule that one who must swear and cannot swear pays. Perhaps a later certain claim is therefore an impermissible new plea. Ramah's rule in siman 59, that a creditor who first says he is unsure whether his document was paid may later say he remembered it was unpaid, does not conclusively resolve this. As I explained there, the creditor's inability to collect is not primarily the rule of inability to swear. It is the strength of the borrower's certain claim against the creditor's uncertainty together with possession. Once the creditor remembers, the case becomes certain against certain. That is different. Knesset HaGedolah suggests that where one witness supports the claimant and the defendant first says, "I do not know," he cannot later say, "I remembered," because at the moment of uncertainty the one witness acquired the force of two against him. This rationale is stronger. Once Torah law trusted the one witness so long as the defendant did not contradict him by oath, the defendant's failure to swear allowed the witness's testimony to become fully operative. A later contradictory oath cannot undo that. In an ordinary fifty and fifty case without a witness, there is more room to permit a later claim of recollection.
