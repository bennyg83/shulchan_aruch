And I do not know whether I returned it to you. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 23, discusses Rabbi Hirsch, who demanded from Rabbi Ber five hundred gold coins of principal borrowed years earlier at the Frankfurt fair, together with two hundred as profits under a heter iska. Rabbi Ber answered that he believed he had repaid, pointing to the many years in which no demand had been made. Noda BiYehudah rules that the principal must be paid under the rule of “I do not know whether I repaid you,” and the claimant need not swear even a heset oath. As to the claimed profits, Rabbi Ber is exempt without an oath. The claimant cannot assert with certainty that profits were earned, because the arrangement was an iska and the claim is fundamentally one that would call for a partner’s oath. Even if Rabbi Hirsch claimed with certainty that the money remained invested and produced profit, Rabbi Ber could answer, “I do not know whether I ever became liable for that profit,” because if he had repaid the principal immediately after the fair, no later profit obligation arose. Ordinarily such uncertainty as to whether liability arose would require an oath of nonknowledge, but here the claimant cannot assert certain profit, so at most the claim is one for a partners’ oath. Just as a definite assertion that the principal was repaid and the partnership ended would exempt the defendant without an oath under siman 93, seif 9, so too uncertainty whether the partnership continued does not generate payment or oath. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that this conclusion appears difficult according to the Shach here and in siman 87, seif katan 13. If part of a claim is answered by “I do not know whether I repaid” and the balance by “I do not know whether I ever borrowed,” the defendant may be regarded as one who partially admits and is unable to swear on the remainder, which can generate payment. In the present case the principal is admitted as an obligation subject only to uncertainty of repayment, while the profits are denied on the ground of uncertainty whether liability arose. One could therefore argue that the rule of one who must swear but cannot applies to the profits. Perhaps Noda BiYehudah follows his view elsewhere that a defendant may rely on the Sema’s position that an amount established by beit din is not treated as a partial admission, and that one need not combine several stringent positions to extract money. This still requires study.
