See Shach regarding a case in which the uncertainty about repayment arose before the loan, where he writes that because the lender makes a definite claim, he collects, but if the lender is likewise uncertain he does not collect. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that because each party has an independent right of collection, the lender is treated as saying, "I do not know whether I became obligated to you," in response to the borrower's counterclaim. In my view this is incorrect. When the borrower says, "You have money of mine in your possession and I apply it as payment," the case is not governed by the doctrine that each side collects separately. See what I wrote above in siman 72 on Shach, seif katan 7. Where the debtor was not expected to know, Mishneh LaMelech holds that one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," is exempt. Tumim sought to prove the contrary from Tosafot's question in Ketubot, that perhaps a husband should not have to pay even one hundred because the wife may have committed adultery willingly. Tumim argues that the husband is saying, "I do not know whether I paid you." This proof has no substance. Even if the husband made a definite claim that she acted willingly, he would not be believed, because such a claim resembles a claim of payment against a written obligation. The case there has a different reason: the doubt arises before beit din independently of the parties' claims, because the husband's report that he found an open entrance is believed, and it is therefore treated as known that adultery occurred with uncertainty whether it was forced or willing. This is like any doubt arising in a document and prevents collection. Moreover, Tosafot in Bava Metzia 100 write that where there is a derara demamona, seizure helps even for one who claims uncertainty if he originally held the property. Here too the husband was initially in possession of his own money. Tumim's proof from Mordechai regarding one who hired a donkey for his agent and the donkey died on the way is also no proof. Mordechai exempts the hirer because it is a case of "I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you," implying that if it were uncertainty about repayment he would be liable even though he was not expected to know. But Mordechai holds that where both parties are uncertain, one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid," is liable, as Mishneh LaMelech records in chapter 4 of the laws of borrowing and deposits. On his reasoning there is no need to distinguish whether the defendant was expected to know. The rule that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one requires a strong definite claim against a weak uncertainty; where the defendant was not expected to know, his uncertainty is strong and the claimant's definite assertion correspondingly weak. Nor can one challenge this from the law of the shopkeeper and workers in siman 91, where the employer is liable to the workers because he says in effect, "I do not know whether I paid you," even though he did not personally know whether the shopkeeper paid them. There the employer was negligent. He should not have entrusted their payment to the shopkeeper in a way that left them without direct recourse, as the workers can say, "What claim do we have against the shopkeeper?" The liability arises from that negligence. Therefore, where one says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," and he was not expected to know, he is exempt, unless his lack of knowledge resulted from his own negligence, as in the case of the shopkeeper and workers. Likewise, where the doubt arises before beit din independently of the parties' claims, such as uncertainty whether adultery was willing, the defendant may be exempt even against a written obligation. See Taz's responsum regarding one who paid and later found a counterfeit coin. That is treated as uncertainty whether a new obligation arose after a completed payment. Tumim brings a clear proof from the case of gardeners who later made an accounting. Ketzot HaChoshen attempts to distinguish it on the ground that a person normally checks his accounts carefully, but that is not persuasive, because the same assumption would apply if he claimed that the error resulted from a counterfeit coin. There is also a textual error in Shach, seif katan 26; the correct reading is, "but in seif 2," and the additional word there is erroneous.
