Ketzot writes regarding the Shach's discussion that even if the lender says, "I do not know how much the counterclaim was worth," the borrower must still pay, because the defense "I have an amount of yours in my hands against it" ordinarily needs a migo, and where the defendant does not know the amount there is no usable migo. In my view this requires great study. When the lender himself says only, "perhaps you have a counterclaim against me," perhaps the borrower is believed even without a migo. A practical difference would arise where the borrower admitted in beit din that he still owed the debt and only after the time needed for speech claimed a counterclaim. He then has no migo because it would be a retroactive migo, yet he may still be believed so long as the lender does not assert with certainty that no such counterclaim exists. Similarly, if a person formally borrowed money but says that his sole purpose in receiving it was to seize funds against an existing claim he had against the lender, one might regard the resulting uncertainty as "I do not know whether I ever became obligated," rather than as uncertain repayment preceding the loan. He did not receive the funds with the intent of becoming a debtor and later setting off a separate obligation, but only as collection of his own claim. On the other hand, unexpressed intention is ordinarily ineffective. If the parties explicitly described the transaction as a loan, the later defense may instead be treated as uncertainty whether the loan was repaid. If before receiving the money he disclosed to witnesses that the transaction was only outwardly a loan and that he intended merely to secure his preexisting claim, perhaps that prior declaration helps. Yet this may resemble a notice given before a sale, which is ineffective unless coercion is independently established. Where the lender holds a promissory note, prior private designation of witnesses should certainly not defeat it unless the borrower has some independent migo, such as the ability to plead payment. With such a migo, the prior declaration may have force. This requires further study. Regarding the Sages' expression "perhaps it is an old loan," one may ask why they did not say simply that perhaps the person is uncertain whether he already repaid. Urim VeTumim derives from the expression that uncertain repayment preceding a loan is treated as "I do not know whether I repaid." The point is familiar, although there is still room to analyze the passage differently. The phrase "old loan" in the oath discussion may refer to a situation where the present oath arises without any independent monetary doubt at the start of the obligation. Otherwise, where the loan itself is proved by witnesses, he could simply claim uncertainty about repayment rather than take a definite false position and be expected to withdraw because of the oath. This may also bear on a case where the creditor claims that the debtor made a kinyan sudar stipulating that repayment would be valid only in the presence of specified persons, while the debtor says he does not know whether such a condition was made and nevertheless claims that he paid. His uncertainty concerns whether he ever became subject to the special condition, which suggests "I do not know whether I became obligated" regarding the condition. This helps explain why the Gemara could not simply say that a person is uncertain whether he repaid. If he definitely says there was no such condition, an oath may apply; if he is uncertain whether it existed, he may be exempt, producing a dilemma in which no oath should be imposed. Even though such a condition would make payment outside the specified witnesses a gift rather than repayment, one might still say that because he certainly handed over the money, the issue is whether he ever became obligated to repay again under the condition, which is an uncertainty about initial liability. By contrast, where an independent payment preceded a later loan, our rule that each debt is collected separately makes the later uncertainty one of repayment. The matter requires careful study.
