And because he cannot swear, he pays. Tosafot in Shevuot explain the classic seized-ingot case as follows. The possessor cannot say, "I seized what was mine," merely on his own word. He needs a migo. But the obvious alternative is to deny the seizure, and that would require him to contradict the witness under oath. People do not willingly choose such an alternative, so the migo is weak. Tosafot in Bava Batra add that if he directly contradicts the witness, the witness and claimant may later combine to disqualify him. A difficulty remains. Rav and Shmuel, who do not apply the rule that one obligated to swear and unable to swear must pay, also reject the ingot result. But if the possessor's present claim is intrinsically weak and he lacks a good migo, why should rejecting the unable-to-swear doctrine rescue him? The answer is that the possessor has another migo. He could say, "I do not know whether I seized it," or "I do not know whether it was given to me voluntarily." Such an uncertain claim avoids direct contradiction of the witness. Ordinarily, "I do not know" is a weak claim. But his present claim, "I seized it and it was mine," is itself extremely weak because the very act of violent seizure contradicts the ordinary chazakah that property in another's hand belongs to that person. According to Rav and Shmuel, an uncertain answer to one witness does not produce payment. The alternate claim therefore remains viable enough to support the possessor. According to the view that inability to swear causes payment, the uncertain alternative would itself require payment. The migo disappears, and the possessor loses. This explains the link between the ingot case and the unable-to-swear rule. It also resolves a separate difficulty regarding Abaye, who can hold that a definite claim defeats a doubtful one while still accepting the ingot ruling. For Abaye, the uncertain alternative is unavailable for an independent reason: the definite claimant would defeat it. There is also a second, perhaps stronger, explanation. Where the defendant says "I do not know" in response to one witness, he may himself owe an oath that he does not know. If so, he does not have a migo to a claim free of oath at all. The two Tosafot passages can therefore correspond to the two explanations found in Ran. The main point remains that once no viable alternative claim exists against the witness, the possessor is obligated to swear and cannot take the necessary oath, and therefore pays.
