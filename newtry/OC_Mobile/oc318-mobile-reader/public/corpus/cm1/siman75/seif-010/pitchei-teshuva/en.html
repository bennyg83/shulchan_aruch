See Responsa Brit Avraham, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, at length. Baer Heitev gives the example of a deposit previously held by the creditor and states that where one party is certain and the other uncertain, the certain claim prevails. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that even if the creditor is also uncertain, the borrower must pay. Netivot HaMishpat disagrees, reasoning that where the borrower asserts an offsetting debt, he treats it as payment and the case is not one of two independent collections. Mishovev Netivot responds that Ketzot HaChoshen had already explained that the plea “you owe me an offsetting amount” is believed only through a migo of payment. Where the defendant himself is uncertain, he has no such migo and therefore no effective plea; it is merely as though he says, “Perhaps you became indebted to me,” while the creditor answers, “I do not know whether I became indebted to you.” The creditor is exempt from that uncertain counterclaim, while the borrower’s certain debt remains payable. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 67, agrees with Ketzot HaChoshen and goes further: even if the borrower certainly asserts an offsetting debt and the creditor says that he does not know, the borrower must still pay his admitted debt, while the creditor swears that he does not know whether he previously became indebted to the borrower. The responsum applies this to a case where Shimon admitted a loan from Reuven but asserted that earlier business accounts may have left Reuven owing him an equivalent amount. It distinguishes between a claim that the very documented debt was paid, where the creditor’s uncertainty prevents collection, and an independent counterclaim from another transaction, where the admitted debt remains collectible and the creditor need only address the counterclaim according to the rules of uncertainty. Pitchei Teshuva questions the extension of this reasoning to an oral debt where the borrower asserts the counterclaim with certainty, because Shulchan Aruch above, seif 7, states that an oral debtor who certainly claims an offsetting debt is exempt from paying upon taking a heset oath, even where the creditor denies the counterclaim with certainty. The difference is that with a document there is no migo of payment, whereas with an oral debt there is. Accordingly, the responsum’s conclusion is persuasive only where the borrower himself is uncertain about the counterclaim. Tumim cites Radbaz that even in a case of “I do not know whether I repaid,” the defendant may be exempt where he had no reason to know, and Mishneh LaMelech appears to support that view. Tumim brings proofs for liability and concludes that the matter is disputed among earlier authorities, allowing a party to plead kim li. Netivot HaMishpat rejects Tumim’s proofs and rules that where the defendant had no reason to know, uncertainty whether he repaid exempts him, unless his ignorance resulted from his own negligence, as in the case of a shopkeeper and workers in siman 91. See also Beit Meir, Even HaEzer, siman 178, seif 15.
