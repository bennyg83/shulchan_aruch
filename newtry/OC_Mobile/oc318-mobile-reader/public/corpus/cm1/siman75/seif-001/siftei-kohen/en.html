One who claims against another, etc. It is forbidden to plead falsely in order to distort or delay judgment, even when the pleader is substantively entitled to the money. Thus, one owed one hundred may not claim two hundred so that the defendant will admit one hundred, become liable to an oath, and thereby permit additional matters to be included through gilgul shevuah. If one is owed one hundred and is falsely sued for two hundred, he may not deny everything in beit din and later admit one hundred privately merely to avoid a Torah oath. If three people are each owed one hundred by one person who denies all three debts, one may not sue while the other two testify and then divide the recovery. If one claims one hundred supported by a single witness and the defendant denies it, he may not tell another person, “Come, you and I will testify against him and remove stolen money from his possession.” All these rules are explained in Shevuot 31a, Tosafot in the fifth chapter of Shevuot, Rambam at the end of Hilchot Toen VeNitan, and Semag, positive commandment 107. I do not know why the Tur and Mechaber omitted them entirely.
They say to him, “Clarify your words,” etc. If he clarifies his claim and says that he has witnesses, see siman 70, seif 2. The Darkei Moshe cites Mahardakh, responsa, bayit 2, cheder 1: where one has litigation against another and the defendant is in possession, beit din does not first remove the property and place it with a third party before adjudicating. Beit din does not burden itself regarding a matter that will become clear, since the defendant may prevail and then prove to have held lawfully. The Darkei Moshe comments that siman 72, concerning seizure, appears otherwise. I do not know how siman 72 implies otherwise. Mahardakh’s view is primary, and this is the custom.
Or he damaged your property. The Rambam explains: tell the claimant how the defendant became liable, because a person may mistakenly imagine liability, for example where he merely suspects the defendant of theft or where the defendant said, “I will give you one hundred,” and similar cases. The Sema, seif katan 1, writes that the Tur and Mechaber abbreviated the Rambam’s words by saying, “Did you lend to him, deposit with him, or did he damage your property?” Since those legal categories are equivalent for this purpose, the Sema understands the instruction to mean: clarify whether you lent, deposited, suffered damage, or none of these occurred and you merely suspect him of theft. In my humble opinion they omitted nothing. The Rambam means that beit din is concerned that the claimant may mistakenly believe the defendant liable, such as because he suspects theft. He does not mean that beit din must suggest, “Perhaps you only suspect him.” Rather, they tell him to clarify whether he lent, deposited, or was damaged. If he answers that he lent, that suffices. If his claim is merely suspicion of theft, he must answer truthfully that he suspects him. The Sema further writes that the Perisha proves the Tur requires clarification not only to prevent mistake but also because beit din suspects that the claimant is demanding property not held by the defendant. I do not see any such proof in the Tur.
He must clarify his words. Beit din tells the defendant to answer the claim and explain his answer just as the claimant explained his claim. He must state whether he borrowed or did not borrow, whether property was deposited with him or was not, whether he robbed or did not rob, whether he hired the claimant or did not, and similarly in every type of claim. Why is the bare answer, “I owe him nothing,” not accepted? Perhaps the defendant is mistaken and will come to swear falsely. The claimant may indeed have lent him the money, but the defendant returned it to the claimant’s son or wife, or gave the claimant a gift corresponding to the debt, and therefore mistakenly believes himself discharged. Beit din consequently says: “How can you simply say that you owe nothing? Perhaps under the law you remain obligated and do not know it. Explain the facts to the judges, and they will tell you whether you are liable.” These are the Rambam’s words. The Bach understands from this that if both claimant and defendant speak only in general terms, beit din does not press one more than the other. It tells both to explain their claims. If neither does so, the case reverts to the status it would have had had both explained, and beit din rules accordingly. If the claimant says generally, “So-and-so owes me one hundred,” while the defendant responds, “Let him clarify how I owe him, for I know of no debt,” beit din tells the claimant to state whether the obligation arose from a loan, deposit, or another cause.
Lest he be mistaken, etc. The Darkei Moshe and Sema, seif katan 2, cite the Nimukei Yosef in HaMocher Et HaBayit, page 193a, that a person’s unnecessary words are not interpreted against him unless he stated them explicitly and precisely; ordinarily we say he spoke foolishly and without need. I searched for this passage and did not find it.
If it appears to the judge, etc. The Rosh, responsa, klal 71, siman 5, rules that where the defendant asks the claimant to clarify his words and the claimant refuses, the defendant is not administered an oath, because a heset oath is a rabbinic enactment and is imposed only upon a clarified claim. The Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema, seif katan 5, cite this. It appears that the Rosh speaks where beit din is uncertain and cannot determine whether the claimant’s conduct is deceptive. If beit din sees deception, it does not administer even a Torah oath, notwithstanding that such an oath is not merely a rabbinic enactment. If beit din sees no deception, it administers even a heset oath. The case must therefore be one of unresolved doubt, as stated.
