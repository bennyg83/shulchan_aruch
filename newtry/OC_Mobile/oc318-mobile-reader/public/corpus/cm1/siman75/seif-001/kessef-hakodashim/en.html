See Shach, seif katan 2. The Shach at the beginning of siman 75, and Urim VeTumim there following Mahardach, rule that a litigant cannot ordinarily compel his opponent to deposit disputed money before adjudication. That was certainly true when beit din rulings were reliably enforced under Torah law. In our present circumstances, however, there are cases where even after the parties sign what is called a compromise or arbitration undertaking, one party later obstructs enforcement through influence with governmental authorities. If the judges see no deceitful tactic in the request for security and believe that the parties' signatures may not in practice ensure compliance, the principle that beit din should not labor in vain supports requiring the disputed funds to be deposited before proceeding. On the other hand, where one party asks that both sign a binding arbitration undertaking and the other refuses, beit din may permit resort to secular authorities solely to compel execution of the arbitration instrument appointing the Torah judges. In most cases, a properly executed written undertaking is the safer course.
