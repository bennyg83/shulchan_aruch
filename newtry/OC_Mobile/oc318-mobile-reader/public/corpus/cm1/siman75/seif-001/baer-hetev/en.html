Clarify. The Shach writes that it is forbidden to make a false claim in order to distort or delay justice even when one is substantively entitled to the money. One who is owed one maneh may not claim two hundred merely to induce an admission of one hundred and thereby create an oath with possible gilgul. One who is truly owed one hundred but is sued for two hundred may not deny everything in beit din while privately admitting one hundred merely to avoid a Torah oath. If three creditors are owed by one debtor who denies all three, one creditor may not sue while the other two testify and then divide the recovered money. Nor may one who has a claim supported by one witness ask another person to join him as a false second witness in order to recover the money. These rules emerge from Shevuot, chapter Shevuat HaEdut. The Shach notes that he does not know why the Tur and Shulchan Aruch omitted them.
Thinks. Darkei Moshe cites Mahardach that when a plaintiff has a claim against a defendant who presently possesses the disputed property, beit din does not first remove it to a third party and only then adjudicate. Matters that will become clear need not burden beit din, because the defendant may ultimately prevail and be shown to have been properly in possession. Darkei Moshe questions this from siman 72, but the Shach sees no contradiction and regards Mahardach's view as correct and customary.
Mistaken. The Sema writes that in his Derishah he proved that the Tur understood the Rambam to require clarification not merely because the litigant may misunderstand the law, but also because beit din has some suspicion that he may not be stating the facts accurately. For that reason a lender may be required to clarify whether the transaction was a loan or deposit.
Loss. Even if the litigant is a scholar who certainly would not err in the law, there is no loss to him in clarifying the facts and thereby removing suspicion. Moreover, a person is not objective regarding his own disadvantage. Sema.
Wants. The Bach understands the Rambam as follows. If the claimant states only generally, "You owe me one maneh," and the defendant also answers generally, beit din does not favor either side but tells both to explain their positions. If neither explains, beit din treats the case as though both had stated their positions and rules accordingly. But if the claimant merely says, "You owe me one maneh," and the defendant responds, "Let him specify how I supposedly owe it, because I know of no debt," beit din tells the claimant to explain whether the money was a loan, deposit, or otherwise. Shach.
