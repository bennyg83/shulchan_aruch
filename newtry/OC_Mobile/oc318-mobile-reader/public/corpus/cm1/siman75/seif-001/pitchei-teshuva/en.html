Clarify your words. See Baer Heitev, which states that if one is owed one hundred but is falsely sued for two hundred, he should not deny everything before beit din. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 136, asks why the defendant should not answer a dishonest claimant in kind, deny the entire claim in court, and then privately pay the one hundred he truly owes. Would this be worse than admitting one hundred in court and thereby becoming liable for a Torah oath as one who admits part of the claim, even though in truth that oath is unnecessary? He answers that once the judge rules according to Torah law upon the pleadings before him, the judgment is not false, and the resulting oath is not an oath in vain. The Torah is more particular that the defendant not utter a false denial in beit din, even if he intends privately to pay what he owes and thereby avoid an oath generated by the claimant’s misconduct. One may not respond to improper conduct with similar misconduct. Birkei Yosef, siman 18, section 3, cites this and derives all the more so that a judge may not falsely say, “I do not know,” merely in order to add additional judges. See what I wrote above, siman 18, seif 1, seif katan 4. As to Baer Heitev’s further discussion of one sued for one hundred on the basis of a single witness, and the Shach’s question why the Tur and Shulchan Aruch did not mention the rule, Mishnat Rabbi Eliezer notes that this is surprising, because the rule is explicit in the Gemara and was indeed brought by the Tur above, siman 28, and by Rama there, seif 1. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 60, makes the same observation.
