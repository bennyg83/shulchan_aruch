And the other says, “I am certain,” etc. The Sema writes that if he did not use the language of certainty, the rule does not apply, for the reason explained there. In my opinion, the word “certain” in the Tur and Mechaber is not exact. In Maharam's proof, cited by the Rosh and Mordechai in Yesh Nochalin and in a responsum of the Ramban, the language need not mean that he literally said “certainly.” It may mean only that beit din knows that he told him he owed nothing. The Mordechai later proves the rule from one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley, where Rashi explains that the claimant waived the barley by claiming wheat alone. Maharam's concluding words likewise state simply that when he said, “You do not owe me,” he waived the debt wholeheartedly, without requiring the word “certainly.” The same appears from the Rosh and from the Rama's responsum, siman 33.
Although he knows with certainty that he owes him, etc. This means that he is exempt even from the obligation to satisfy Heaven. This is also implied by the wording and is stated by the Bach and Maharshal.
For it is as though he waived it. The Tur concludes that this is not a mistaken waiver. Once the debtor told him, “I owe you,” the claimant should have reflected and examined the matter carefully. Instead he said with certainty, “You owe me nothing,” and therefore waived the debt wholeheartedly. The Sema derives that even if the claimant later says, “I originally erred in my accounting, and after reviewing it I found that you do owe me,” he is not believed, because this is like an express waiver which cannot be withdrawn. The Bach disagrees. Maharam's responsum as cited by the Tur, Rosh, and Mordechai appears at first glance to support the Sema. See also the Ramban's responsum, siman 117, and the cited responsa of Maharit and Maharashdam.
