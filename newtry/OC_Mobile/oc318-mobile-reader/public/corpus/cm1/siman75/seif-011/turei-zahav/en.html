(Seif 25) “All witnesses you possess.” If a creditor produces an unauthenticated document and the debtor claims payment, the debtor may be required to swear even though the creditor refuses to cancel or tear the document. The creditor says he currently knows of no authenticating witnesses but may find them later. Just as a litigant who does not presently know whether he has witnesses need not renounce them, the creditor need not destroy a potentially valid document. For now the claim proceeds orally; if authenticating witnesses are later found, he may use them. This differs from the creditor’s oath in siman 72 designed to prevent later production of a pledge, because there an immediate oath supplies a remedy; here there is no present remedy except preserving possible future proof. I was also asked about a debtor who paid and afterward the creditor produced one counterfeit coin, saying it was among the payment. The debtor said he did not know. This is “I do not know whether I owe,” not “I do not know whether I paid.” The Mishna allows return of a deficient coin after the ordinary inspection period only if the recipient recognizes it, implying that mere uncertainty does not obligate the payer. Likewise, where a gold ring was later found to contain tin, the seller who does not know may swear ignorance and be exempt. The reason is that once payment was accepted, the debt’s prior presumption ended. A new doubt then arises whether one coin was counterfeit. The law leaves matters in the status produced by the completed payment, reinforced by the majority of coins being valid. The creditor now presents a new claim, and the debtor swears heset that he does not know and is exempt.
