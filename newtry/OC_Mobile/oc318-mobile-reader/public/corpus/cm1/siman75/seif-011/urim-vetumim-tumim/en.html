Tur cites Ramah that the unable-to-swear rule also applies in a one-witness oath only where two witnesses on the same matter would themselves create a direct monetary obligation rather than merely require the claimant to swear. For example, in the hired-worker case, even if two witnesses support the worker, the worker ordinarily still collects through the special claimant's oath. If only one witness supports him, the employer's inability to contradict that witness does not automatically create the classic unable-to-swear payment rule. Sema and Bach explain this correctly, and Mechaber rules similarly below in siman 89. Shach adds the storekeeper-on-his-ledger case. If the employer does not know whether he instructed the storekeeper to pay the workers and one witness says he did, Shach says no unable-to-swear rule applies because even if the employer admitted the instruction, the storekeeper would still have to swear that he actually paid. This depends on the dispute about the nature of the storekeeper's claim. According to the view that by Torah law the storekeeper would collect without oath and Chazal imposed an oath only to reassure the employer, the underlying claim is a full Torah monetary obligation. In that system, one witness plus the employer's inability to swear can indeed trigger payment. That is even stronger than the seized-ingot case, because there the possessor would have been believed absent the witness, whereas here the employer's own admission would already create the Torah debt. According to Ran and those who hold that the storekeeper has no Torah collection right and Chazal created the entire sworn-collection framework, Shach is correct: the unable-to-swear doctrine should not be extended to a monetary right that is itself only rabbinic. Shach later favors Rambam's view that the storekeeper's claim is Torah-based. He therefore should not state the present exemption without qualification. Taz raises an objection from the collateral rule above, where one witness proves delivery of the pledge and sighting witnesses exist. The creditor can then prevail because the borrower cannot simply contradict the witness, even though with two witnesses the borrower would still take an oath concerning the amount. This is no contradiction. There the collateral remains in the creditor's possession. The witness destroys the creditor's need for a migo regarding possession of the pledge. Any oath the borrower later takes is an ordinary defensive oath on the separate oral monetary claim. A claimant's oath that is itself necessary before he can take money is fundamentally different from a defendant's oath that merely responds to an independent claim. Thus Ramah's rule concerns the former, not the latter.
