Even his relative. A responsum attributed to Rabbeinu Tam says that a son can litigate his father's claim, because a person's agent is like himself. If someone disputes this, the son can write an authorization to the father, and the father can swear. Maharshal derives that where the father of a groom is already holding the defendant's property, he can take the required oath and retain it once the son assigns the claim to him. The Shach extends this to a lender who forgot the amount of a loan secured by collateral, suggesting that he assign the claim to a relative who remembers the amount. In my view this extension is incorrect where the collateral remains in the forgetful lender's hands. The lender's credibility regarding the amount depends upon his migo arising from possession. The relative who remembers has no migo because the collateral is not in his possession. An authorization does not create the missing evidentiary posture. Rosh rules similarly concerning partners where one partner held another person's collateral and wished the other partner, who knew the facts, to swear. Because the knowledgeable partner did not actually hold the collateral, his oath did not help. If the forgetful possessor hands the collateral to the knowledgeable relative so that the latter can acquire possession and thereby gain the migo, the original possessor may be liable for negligence in handing another person's property to someone else in order to create evidentiary advantage. Therefore that solution is also problematic. Maharshal's case is different. The father himself was already holding the defendant's property. The son then assigned the claim to him. Since the father had both certain knowledge and possession, he could swear and retain. Tumim objects that an oral debt cannot ordinarily be transferred except through a three party arrangement, and therefore an authorization is only agency. A mere agent should not be able to swear for the principal. But where the authorized person is already in possession of the debtor's property with the transferor's consent, the transfer can become a real acquisition. Wherever ordinary acquisition of an intangible claim is defective but the transferee has already seized the property with the seller's consent, the property is not removed from him. Thus Maharshal's case can involve true acquisition, not mere agency. Likewise, if the subject transferred is an actual pledge, it can be acquired incidentally with land, and the standard authorization includes such language. But in the Shach's case, the knowledgeable relative still lacks actual possession and therefore lacks the migo. Consequently the authorization does not solve the problem. This is the correct distinction.
