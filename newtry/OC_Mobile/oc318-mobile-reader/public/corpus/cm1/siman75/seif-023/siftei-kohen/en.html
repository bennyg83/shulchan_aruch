And some say that whenever he says he was told, etc. Since the Rama’s words here and in Darkei Moshe are unclear to me, I must elaborate. His language implies that according to this view, whenever a claimant says that a trustworthy person told him, the defendant takes a heset oath even if the informant is not before us. Otherwise the Rama should have written that whenever one witness testifies before us, even though the claimant himself does not know with certainty, the defendant takes an oath. The phrase that follows, “provided that the person from whose mouth he says it,” also implies that the informant is absent. Moreover, if the witness were present, this view would not disagree with the first view, for even the first view imposes an oath on the basis of testimony before the court. Darkei Moshe likewise appears to rule in accordance with Rabbeinu Baruch, who discussed a claimant saying that trustworthy relatives had clearly told him that his stolen property came into the defendant’s hands while he was pursuing it, although those relatives were not present. Yet it is difficult to understand how any authority could impose heset merely because a claimant says, “I was told.” Every person could make such an assertion in order to impose an oath and prevent the defendant from reversing it onto him. A careful reading of the Mordechai shows that Rabbeinu Baruch spoke only where there were objective grounds for suspicion. Rabbeinu Simchah initially held that in that incident the relatives must appear before the court. He distinguished the rule of “my father told me” and the oath that an entrusted article is not in the custodian’s possession, because in those cases it is certain that an obligation or deposit once existed. Rabbeinu Baruch answered that in a matter naturally giving rise to suspicion, heset may be imposed even without a definite claim. This also explains “my father told me,” where the claim concerns a matter reasonably open to doubt. In my opinion, Raavad, the Mechaber, and the other authorities may agree with Rabbeinu Baruch on this limited point. Their rule that no oath is imposed when the witness is absent concerns an ordinary report, not a claim supported by objective circumstances. Darkei Moshe’s reliance on Terumat HaDeshen, siman 308, is less clear. That responsum concerns money certainly delivered to an agent for transfer to Levi, where Levi denies receipt. Since it is certain that the principal’s money entered the agent’s hands, the agent must swear that he delivered it, just as a custodian swears that the entrusted object is no longer in his possession. Rabbeinu Simchah also agrees in such a case. It may be that the Rama’s phrase “told him by a trustworthy person” means a person established as truthful, so that the claim itself supplies grounds for suspicion. Yet his subsequent requirement that the informant not be interested in the matter is difficult, because Terumat HaDeshen does not discuss objective grounds in that formulation. The principal ruling appears to me to be that no oath, even heset, is imposed solely because a claimant says that another person told him, unless that person testifies before the court. If the witness appears, a valid witness can generate a Torah oath, and even a relative can generate heset according to Rabbeinu Simchah and Terumat HaDeshen, though others disagree as explained below in seif katan 83. An interested person cannot generate even heset according to all opinions. Accordingly, where one says, “My father or predecessor told me that you owe him one hundred,” even if the defendant admits part, he is exempt even from heset, and certainly so where he says, “Another person told me,” unless objective grounds support the claim. If the witness is present, a qualified witness obligates a Torah oath; a relative may obligate heset; but an interested witness has no effect.
Even his relative, etc. This rule requires study even where the relative testifies before us, because I have not found it stated by the other authorities. Moreover, the responsa of Rabbeinu Tam and Rivam, cited in Teshuvot Maimoniyot to Sefer Kinyan, siman 21, imply the opposite. In a dispute over terms allegedly promised at a marriage arrangement, the groom said, “My father told me that you promised so much.” The respondent answered that the father was disqualified from testifying for his son and that the groom himself lacked a definite claim, so even a heset oath could not be imposed. Rabbeinu Tam ruled that the groom should grant his father authorization to litigate, because an authorized agent is a litigant rather than a witness. The father could then swear as a party. Rabbeinu Simchah in the Mordechai may also have allowed heset through a relative only in the particular case supported by circumstances, where the stolen property was allegedly seen in the defendant’s hands during pursuit. Terumat HaDeshen, however, appears to understand the Mordechai as allowing a relative to generate heset even without such circumstances, and the matter requires study. Maharshal also cites Rabbeinu Tam’s ruling without qualification and adds that if the groom’s father is in possession and the other party seeks to recover from him, the son may authorize the father, who then swears regarding the terms promised and is exempt. From this one may derive that if a lender on a pledge forgot the amount of the loan but his relative knows it, he may authorize the relative, who swears and recovers. This may perhaps be distinguished because the lender’s right in the pledge is only a lien, as explained in siman 72, seif 14. In any event, the principle applies to other forms of possession, such as possession of money. Authorization is essential, for without it the relative is not a litigant, the claimant’s assertion remains uncertain, and money cannot be extracted on the basis of uncertainty.
