That it was told to him by a trustworthy person, and so forth. Shach strongly objects to imposing an oath merely because a claimant says a reliable person informed him. Otherwise no one could live: any litigant could invent a report and force another person to swear. Shach attempts to limit the rule to a case with surrounding indications of truth, such as where the informant is exceptionally reliable. But if beit din need not independently recognize those indications and the claimant's own assertion suffices, the problem remains. He can simply say, "A person so trustworthy that he would never lie told me this." Therefore the practical rule must depend on judicial evaluation, not the claimant's adjective. In any event, many authorities reject imposing an oath on a claim based only on hearsay. Although Rama rules leniently toward imposing the oath, that may have fit the circumstances of his generation. In later generations, where dishonest litigants may use an oath threat merely to force compromise and extract money unlawfully, beit din should be especially cautious. An oath should be imposed on this basis only where the judges themselves see real grounds for believing the report, or where the claimant identifies a specific informant who is presently absent but expected to return soon, so that the matter is likely to be revealed and fabrication is unlikely. Absent such concrete circumstances, beit din should not rush to impose an oath. Here the principle "a judge has only what his eyes see" applies in its fullest sense. The judges must assess the actual circumstances for the sake of Heaven.
