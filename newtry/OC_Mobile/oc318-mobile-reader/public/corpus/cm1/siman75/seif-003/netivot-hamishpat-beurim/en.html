Your father had one hundred in my possession. See Shach, seif katan 9. His words are obscure, and his continuation is likewise difficult to understand. It appears that this seif necessarily discusses a case where the claimant later makes a definite claim that his father told him the defendant owed him. Otherwise the defendant is plainly exempt, because why should he swear when there is no claim at all? Even on an uncertain claim one generally does not swear. The Sema therefore writes that the Mechaber could not have formulated the case as one speaking to another person directly, because with an ordinary claimant the defendant would be liable if the claimant later said, "I knew of this matter." With a son claiming through his father, however, saying later "I knew" does not help if it appears contrived, because ordinarily a son would not know all of his father's affairs, and there is no reason to assume he knew this one. The Sema nevertheless asks that the Mechaber could still have stated a parallel case involving an ordinary claimant and thereby taught the distinction between one who appears to be contriving his later assertion and one who genuinely acknowledges prior knowledge. The Shach calls the Sema's words obscure, because the distinction between a contrived assertion and a genuine acknowledgment was already taught by the Mechaber in siman 88. The novelty here is that with a claim inherited from one's father there is no distinction between a later assertion that appears contrived and one that appears genuine. The Shach therefore explains that the Sema's intent was that the Mechaber should have taught this rule here in order to distinguish it from what is stated in siman 68 seif 32 concerning the claim "You reminded me," where no such distinction is made. Here the Mechaber should have taught that when one later says, "I knew of this," there is a distinction. The Shach found the same explanation explicitly in the Sema's errata. As to the Shach's objection to the Sema from seif 10, the Shach is correct. Here the rule depends specifically on the claimant later saying, "I knew of this," so it is natural to speak of a lender who actually demands his loan, since the case assumes that he knows about it. In seif 10, however, the claimant remains uncertain even afterward. If he later made a definite claim, the defendant would certainly be liable under the rule of "I do not know whether I repaid you." Since the claimant remains uncertain, he cannot demand definite payment. One cannot say that it is unusual for a claimant to make an uncertain claim, because many laws in Choshen Mishpat concern a lender asserting an uncertain claim, in siman 59, siman 76, siman 300, and numerous other places.
