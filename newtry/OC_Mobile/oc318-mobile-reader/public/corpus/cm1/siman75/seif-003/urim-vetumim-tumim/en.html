He has been established as a denier. Rosh, responsum at the end of klal 94, discusses Reuven who claims he gave Shimon two hundred gold coins to invest and increase for him. Shimon denies receiving them, but testimony together with his own admissions establishes at least fifty. Rosh rules that Shimon is established as a false denier. Ordinarily, one who denies a deposit is not treated as a false denier unless witnesses prove that the specific object remained in his possession at that time, because he may merely be delaying until he searches and finds it. Sema asks why the same excuse cannot apply here, especially because the funds were given for use. Shach correctly finds Sema's answer difficult. The key lies in Rosh's emphasis that this was not a specifically identifiable deposited object but money or equivalent value given for investment. Bava Metzia explains why denial of an ordinary deposit may still be temporary. The custodian thinks, "I will search for the thief," or "I will find the animal in the marsh," and therefore denies until he can recover the exact property. Why does he not simply claim unavoidable accident instead of brazenly denying receipt? Because if the object is uniquely identifiable and he later finds it with the thief, his prior admission that it was burned or irretrievably lost could prevent him from reclaiming it. He therefore avoids making a false descriptive admission about the specific object. That logic does not apply to fungible money or unmarked property given for trade. Even if Shimon had said, "Those particular coins were lost by accident," he could later recover other coins from a thief or finder without contradiction. Money is not uniquely identified as the very same deposit. Therefore, if his purpose were merely temporary delay while searching, he could have used the less brazen accident claim. His choice to deny receipt shows genuine false denial. That is the meaning of Rosh's exact wording that no specific, identifiable deposited object was involved, only money or value entrusted for investment. This analysis follows Rashi and the authorities who do not allow a migo to exempt from every oath. Tosafot in Bava Kamma may understand the deposit-denial sugya differently, especially where the disputed object itself is before us or witnesses establish negligent loss. Their approach may weaken Rosh's inference. Thus the conceptual issue is not entirely undisputed, but Rosh's ruling is well explained on his own premises.
