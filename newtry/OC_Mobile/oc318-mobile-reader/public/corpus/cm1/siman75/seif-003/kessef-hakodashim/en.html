See Shach, seif katan 12. In siman 75:4, regarding the Rama's statement that a written note is treated as "here it is," I wish first to examine the rule of Rabbi Chiyya. Suppose witnesses testify that the defendant borrowed fifty in Nisan, but he denies it, while the claimant himself does not know whether he lent that fifty in Nisan and claims with certainty only that he lent another fifty in Iyar, which the defendant also denies. Is the defendant liable to an oath on the remaining fifty? At first glance he should be exempt. Rabbi Chiyya's rule that testimony to part of the claim can impose the oath of one who admits part is learned from the proposition that testimony of witnesses should not be less effective than the defendant's own admission. Yet a partial admission itself creates no such oath without a definite claim, and even with a definite claim the admission ordinarily must follow the claim. If witnesses are only equivalent to his admission, why should they do more? In truth, however, the Gemara's conclusion also derives the rule from the rolling oath generated by one witness. We rule like Rif that an oath produced by one witness can arise even where the claimant's assertion is uncertain. Accordingly there is room to impose Rabbi Chiyya's oath even where the claimant is uncertain concerning the fifty proved by the witnesses. This also explains the proof cited by the Shach from Rabbeinu Yerucham that where a rolling oath applies, a person who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. If that principle did not apply to a rolling oath, it could not apply to Rabbi Chiyya's rule derived from it. Nevertheless, I ultimately incline that in the precise case above the defendant is exempt for another reason. The Gemara indicates that, in principle, one who admits part might have been exempt because a person does not ordinarily brazenly deny his creditor. The partial admission removes that presumption because it shows that he is merely delaying payment. In the ordinary Rabbi Chiyya case, where he denies the fifty established by witnesses, his conduct destroys the presumption of non-brazenness in this very dispute. Here, however, the claimant himself does not know about the fifty proved by witnesses. The defendant's denial of that sum is therefore not brazenness directly against the claimant, and the ordinary presumption remains intact. He should consequently be exempt. This approach also resolves a difficulty concerning the authorities, accepted by the Shach, who hold that where a defendant denies the portion evidenced by a note or kinyan, that portion can generate a partial-admission oath under Rabbi Chiyya and is not treated as "here it is." One might ask that the note's testimony stands in place of his own admission, while an admission made before any claim does not generate the oath. Signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din, which is why documents can be collected even from minor heirs. It would therefore seem that the evidentiary "admission" preceded the claim. According to the analysis above, the difficulty disappears. In principle Rabbi Chiyya's rule could apply even on an uncertain claim because it derives from the oath of one witness. The exemption in my initial hypothetical results only from the presumption that a person does not brazenly deny another to his face. Where the creditor makes a definite claim, that presumption no longer protects the defendant once the note or witnesses establish that his total denial is false. The fact that the evidentiary proof existed before the verbal claim does not matter. This also helps explain Beit Yosef's understanding of Rambam that a note is treated as "here it is" because the debtor cannot successfully deny it only when he admits the note, but not when he denies it. When an acknowledged amount is effectively already removed from the dispute, there is nothing to adjudicate on the basis of his admission, and he resembles one who denies everything else. The same logic applies to ordinary "here it is": although the familiar reasons for a partial-admission oath might otherwise exist, the tendered portion has already left the dispute. Ketzot HaChoshen at the beginning of siman 87 asks why we do not disregard the defendant's own admission and impose a partial-admission oath on the basis of the witnesses or note regarding the portion not secured by land. I initially thought the answer might be that the evidentiary admission preceded the claim. Beit Yosef's discussion shows that this is not sufficient, because when the defendant actually denies the note he can indeed become liable to the oath. The better answer is the analysis above: once the portion is legally treated as already paid or otherwise removed, his own admission is irrelevant and the remaining dispute is a total denial; where he denies the evidenced portion, however, Rabbi Chiyya's rule can operate because the definite claim destroys the presumption protecting him.
