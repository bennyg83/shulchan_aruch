“I held one hundred belonging to your father,” etc. The Sema writes that the reference to “your father” cannot be explained by saying that if the admission were made to the creditor himself, the creditor could answer, “I already knew of the debt and intended to claim it all.” In siman 87, seif 7, the Tur, and the Mechaber in seif 14, distinguish whether the defendant preemptively admitted as a stratagem, and the same distinction should then have appeared here. Rather, “your father” is the ordinary case, because a living lender or depositor generally claims his money, whereas after his death the heir may not know of it. Tosafot likewise explain the text according to their approach. The Sema adds that on this basis the later seif 6 should also have said, “Your father lent me,” whereas the Tur says, “You lent me.” His words are obscure, and I do not understand this final objection. The correct analysis is as follows. In siman 88, seif 32, the Tur and Mechaber rule that if one says, “I owe you or your father one hundred and repaid half,” and the other replies, “I did not remember, but you reminded me; I now know that you paid nothing,” the admitting party is exempt even from a heset oath, because he is like one returning a lost object. There they do not distinguish whether his admission was strategic, because the claimant himself concedes that he had forgotten. Here, by contrast, the claimant says, “I knew of it and intended to claim the entire amount even had you not reminded me.” The older editions of the Sema’s errata expressly make this distinction. Even so, it is not certain that the present case turns on whether the admission was strategic. In siman 88, seif 14, that distinction applies because the claimant had initially claimed wheat. Here there was no prior claim at all. This appears from the Baal HaTerumot, shaar 7, part 2, citing the Ramban. The question was whether a borrower who voluntarily says, “I owe you one hundred and repaid half,” followed by the lender’s reply, “I did not remember, but you reminded me and I now recall that you lent one hundred and repaid nothing,” is deemed one who admits part of a claim and must swear. The Ramban answered that partial admission creates liability only when a claim preceded the admission. Even if the claimant later remembers independently, where the defendant first admitted fifty and only afterward the claimant asserted one hundred, the defendant is exempt. The Gemara’s case of a claim for wheat followed by a preemptive admission of barley demonstrates that although the claimant later completes his words and the final record contains a claim for wheat and barley and an admission to one, the defendant is exempt because the complete claim did not precede the partial admission. The Sages’ phrase is “one who admits part of the claim.” The Ramban writes similarly in Milchamot Hashem to HaNizakin. This indicates that even where the claimant says he was reminded, the Ramban answers more broadly that even independent recollection does not create a Torah oath when no claim preceded the admission. He does not distinguish whether the admission was strategic or candid. Only where some claim had already been made, as in wheat followed by barley, is that distinction relevant. Where the defendant volunteered fifty before any claim, he is exempt in all circumstances. Even should one distinguish here between strategic and candid admission, as Rav Hai Gaon does even regarding an heir, one conclusion follows. Where the judge cannot determine which type of admission occurred and the claimant says, “I knew you owed me and intended to claim one hundred,” the defendant is exempt from a Torah oath. He does appear liable for a heset oath. As I write in siman 88, seif 32, a prior claim is unnecessary for heset. The Tur and Mechaber exempt there only where the claimant says, “I did not remember.” Here he claims definite prior knowledge and therefore is entitled to heset. Perhaps the Sema’s words can be interpreted in this manner.
