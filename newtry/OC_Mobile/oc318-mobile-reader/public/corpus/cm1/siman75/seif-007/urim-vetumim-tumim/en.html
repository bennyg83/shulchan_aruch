Maharshal says this rule comes from the Shaarim of Rav Alfasi, which holds that even one who says, "I know I owed you but do not know whether I repaid," permits the claimant to collect only with an oath. Maharshal therefore argues that as long as the claimant has not yet sworn, liability is not fully fixed, so the defendant can still say, "I remembered that I repaid." According to our ruling that the claimant collects without an oath, the defendant's liability is complete and he should no longer be able to retract. Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Ketubot proves otherwise. He rules that one who says, "I certainly became obligated but do not know whether I paid," allows the claimant to collect without an oath. Yet he also cites the Shaarim that if the defendant later says, "I remembered that I paid," he is believed with an oath. Thus the two rules are independent. Rama accordingly follows the correct reading. There is a remaining question on the Shaarim itself. If in an ordinary uncertain-payment case the claimant must swear and collect, why does the Gemara in the hired-worker case ask that the worker should collect without an oath because the employer is preoccupied with his workers? Even if the employer expressly said, "I do not know whether I paid," the Shaarim would require the worker to swear. One can answer that the Gemara's question concerns a case of partial admission, where the employer's uncertainty could create the unable-to-swear rule. Then the claimant would ordinarily collect without an oath, and the Gemara asks why the hired worker should be worse. In any event, where the defendant currently has a genuine migo such as "I paid you now," even Maharshal should agree that he may say, "I remembered that I paid earlier," through that present migo.
