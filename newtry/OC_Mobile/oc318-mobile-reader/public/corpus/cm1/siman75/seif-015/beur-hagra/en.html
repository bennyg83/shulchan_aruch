Whether he did not have... See the Shach. This is what the Rama means by "rather, he shall swear," meaning a shevuas hesses, or the claimant may reverse it, etc., because a Torah oath cannot be reversed, as stated above in siman 87. The phrase "he shall swear..." is as the Sema and Shach explain: if the defendant makes a definite claim regarding the gilgul, he swears concerning that definite claim; if he says that he does not know, he swears that he does not know. See below, siman 94, seif 7: "He claimed..." There is an opinion that says..., and there is an opinion that says... It implies that according to the first opinion he takes a Torah oath, even though that opinion likewise holds that we do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay in such a case, as stated there in the Shulchan Aruch: "although the principal oath..." The second opinion there, that he swears regarding the gilgul, uses the very language the Rama writes here. The Sema there, seif katan 13, already questioned this and wrote that the second opinion does not disagree there, as the Shach writes here. It also appears from the Shulchan Aruch there that according to the opinion that the rule does apply even in such a case, he would have to pay for the gilgul even though he makes a definite claim, contrary to the Shach's explanation here that "but" means that we do not adjudicate in that manner, etc. So too it appears from the Shulchan Aruch in siman 72, seif 30, that he must swear even concerning a definite claim. See the Shach there, seif katan 129. Below, siman 364, seif 6, however, implies like the Shach. See there.

This is the view of Tosafos in the chapter cited above, who hold like the second opinion, "and some say that since he became obligated..." They explain the Mishnah by saying that we do not impose gilgul, etc.
And some say that since... They prove this from the statement there on 100a: "The purchaser says, I bought the larger one, and the other..." The Gemara explains it as a case where he claimed a slave together with its garment, etc., and through gilgul he also acquired the slave. The first opinion holds that when the Gemara says there that he acquired the larger one, it refers only to the garment. See the Sema and Shach, who explain that the latter opinion does not disagree with the first view except concerning the second clause. See the end of siman 94, which appears to follow the first opinion.

(Collected note) Some say... and some say... The proof for the first opinion is from Bava Metzia 97a, as Tosafos there, s.v. "on the day..." write, and it is a strong proof. This is also Rambam's view, except that he holds that one swears on an uncertain claim, as stated in siman 344. Nimukei Yosef's answer to Tosafos' question is exceedingly forced and difficult to sustain. See there. Nimukei Yosef, author of the latter opinion, proves his view from the Mishnah there on 101a, which says, "I do not know, he acquires the larger one," and the Gemara did not also ask that this should refute Rav Nachman, but only explains it by gilgul, as the Gemara says, "the field together with its sheaves..." This proof is not valid. If so, before the Gemara asks on the latter clause, "Why must he swear? He did not admit what was claimed..." and then adds another question, it should also have challenged the first clause, which says, "I do not know, he acquires the larger one." Rather, since the first clause of the Mishnah was established there according to Sumchus, and likewise the latter clause, there is no difficulty at all. Sumchus certainly holds that with a definite claim against an uncertain response there is liability, for he does not accept the general rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. He does not require the qualification of "great certainty in the law," etc., even in the case of the injured party, etc. Although Bava Kamma 35b says, "No, the latter clause..." there his definite claim is weak, as Tosafos explain there, s.v. "from the latter clause..." (End of note.)
