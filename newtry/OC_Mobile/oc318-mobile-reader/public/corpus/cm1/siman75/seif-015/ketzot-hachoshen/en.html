We do not say this regarding an oath arising through oath extension. The early authorities derive this from the sugya of a cow borrowed for half a day and rented for half a day. Even without the specific oath discussed there, the custodian must swear that the animal died naturally, and by oath extension he would also have to swear whether it died during the borrowing or rental period. If inability to swear on an oath extension produced payment, the Gemara could have resolved the case through that mechanism. Since it instead requires an independent principal oath, Rosh, Ran, and other authorities infer that the rule, "since he cannot swear, he pays," does not apply to a matter that enters only through oath extension. See my discussion in siman 344 for another resolution of the underlying sugya.
We apply the verse properly. The Sema, Bach, and Shach all explain that where the defendant cannot swear the principal Torah oath, he pays that principal amount, but this payment does not itself obligate him to pay an uncertain matter that would have entered only by oath extension. He may still take hesset regarding the extended matter and be exempt. Pnei Yehoshua suggested the opposite from the sugya of exchanging a cow for a donkey: if the defendant cannot swear even the principal oath, perhaps payment extends to the additional claim as well. Ritva, however, notes that such a reading is strained and offers another explanation. The straightforward rule therefore remains like the Sema and Shach. Oath extension has no independent "unable to swear, therefore pay" mechanism. If the principal oath disappears through payment, the extension likewise loses its Torah basis.
