Shach says that a clever heir should answer, "I do not know," and thereby avoid the oath, citing Raavad's advice that a defendant can answer "I do not know" regarding matters added by gilgul. That follows Shach's reading that the claimant does not assert the heir personally knows. According to the explanation above, the claimant does say, "You know what your father instructed you." Then the cases differ. Raavad's gilgul case involves an uncertain collateral matter where the claimant himself cannot say the defendant knows. Here a definite assertion of knowledge makes "I do not know" look like a poor and possibly dishonest response. I am also uncertain whether an heir who truly should remember a direct instruction from his father is entirely free of the moral duty to satisfy the claim if he says he does not know. The Torah exemption from the formal unable-to-swear rule does not necessarily eliminate the obligation to act beyond strict judgment where one's own uncertainty is blameworthy. Rav and Shmuel reject the unable-to-swear doctrine generally yet can still recognize a duty to satisfy doubtful obligations toward Heaven. Therefore advising the heir simply to say "I do not know" is not always proper. If he genuinely does not know, he should say so. But if the only purpose is to evade an oath despite a matter he should remember, that is not sound counsel.
