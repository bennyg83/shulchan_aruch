Whether he was not, etc. For example, the claimant demands one hundred; the defendant admits fifty, says he does not know about fifty, and the claimant then adds other matters by gilgul. On the gilgul the defendant answers that he owes nothing. Although he cannot swear on the principal claim and therefore pays under the rule that one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay, we do not automatically apply that same Torah oath status to the gilgul. Thus if he definitely denies the gilgul, he does not incur a Torah oath through the gilgul, and if he says he does not know on the gilgul, we likewise do not say that inability to take a Torah oath requires payment. Rather, he takes a heset oath regarding the gilgul, whether his response there is definite or uncertain.
Whether he can swear on the principal oath, etc. For example, the claimant demands one hundred, the defendant admits fifty and definitely denies fifty, and when he comes to swear the claimant adds other claims by gilgul. If the defendant says he does not know regarding those additional claims, according to the Raavad, Baal HaTerumot, Baal HaMaor, and Rosh he takes the Torah oath on the fifty he denied and only a heset oath on the gilgul matters about which he is uncertain.
And some say that since, etc. The Nimukei Yosef reports that later authorities agree that once the Torah oath exists, the gilgul shares its status, and therefore inability to swear on the gilgul can create liability to pay. This also appears to be the view of Mar Yehudai Gaon, Ramban, and Maggid Mishneh. The Maggid Mishneh limits the rule where the principal claim itself is uncertain, but the other authorities appear to apply the rule even there. See simanim 94 and 344. Rabbeinu Yeruham’s treatment is difficult, because in one place he says the rule does not apply to gilgul, while elsewhere he derives from a case of two witnesses that it does.
Since, etc. The Sema and Bach correctly explain that this second opinion disputes only the latter case. Where the defendant already must pay the principal claim because he cannot swear, there is no surviving Torah oath on which to base gilgul, so on the added claim he takes only a rabbinic oath. This also explains the Rosh’s responsum cited in siman 94, that there is no gilgul where the defendant was never required to swear but instead must pay by Torah law.
We properly apply the rule that one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay. This means that liability to pay applies only to the gilgul claims about which he says he does not know. As to the portion he definitely denies, he takes the Torah oath and is exempt. Even when gilgul is treated like the principal oath, the same division applies as in a single principal claim where one portion is admitted, one portion definitely denied, and one portion uncertain.
