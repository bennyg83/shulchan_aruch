We do not apply the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear through an oath imposed by gilgul. This implies that the underlying oath that he does not know is imposed through gilgul, only that we do not apply the consequence that one who cannot swear must pay. The Taz writes likewise. Yet in siman 94 the Mechaber writes that when the defendant's claim is uncertain, no oath is imposed through gilgul at all, and the Tur likewise writes, as cited by the Sema, that he takes only a hesset oath regarding the matter brought in through gilgul. In siman 344, however, the Mechaber writes that claims of "I do not know" are imposed through gilgul. This can be reconciled as follows. The source of these laws is Bava Metzia 96. Tosafot ask why the Gemara must establish the first clause as involving two animals and the latter clause as involving three in order to create an oath obligation, since in any event there is an oath through gilgul from the claim that the animal died by unavoidable accident. They answer that an uncertain claim of the defendant cannot be imposed through gilgul because it is unlike Sotah. They then ask regarding the middle clause, where the lender claims uncertainty and the defendant claims certainty and is exempt, why he should not swear through gilgul. They answer that the case is where the lender trusts him, or where he admits that the animal died by unavoidable accident. Tosafot further answer that an uncertain claim of the claimant likewise cannot be imposed through gilgul unless there is a basis for suspicion analogous to Sotah. The reason a definite claim can be imposed through gilgul even without such a basis is that it resembles the principal oath, which is also based on a definite claim. Maharsha asks that here the principal oath concerning unavoidable accident itself rests on an uncertain claim by the claimant. If so, one should be able to impose through gilgul even an uncertain claim of the defendant, since that would resemble the principal oath. To resolve Maharsha's question, it appears that Tosafot understood the principal oath of bailees concerning unavoidable accident to be an oath that the item is not in his possession, as the Sema writes in siman 294. There is a basis for suspicion, so it is treated like a definite claim and the proposed gilgul does not resemble the principal oath. Tumim writes similarly. According to our ruling, however, that the principal oath of bailees is that he was not negligent, we must use Tosafot's other answer, that the case is one where the lender trusts him or he admits that the animal died by unavoidable accident. Nevertheless, it remains established that an uncertain claim of the claimant is not imposed through gilgul when the principal claim is definite, from the Gemara's conclusion establishing the case as one involving partial admission. For according to Rami bar Chama, who establishes the first clause with three animals and the latter clause with four, and according to Rabbeinu Tam in Tosafot here and in Bava Kamma, a deposit creates an oath of partial admission even without a claim of unavoidable accident. If so, the middle clause should still create the consequence that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay by means of gilgul. We must therefore say that an uncertain claim of the claimant is not imposed through gilgul. The same is also established regarding an uncertain claim of the defendant, even where the case is one in which the lender trusts him or he admits the unavoidable accident. Otherwise, why did the Gemara have to establish the case as one involving an oath obligation? The lender should be believed through a migo that he could have refrained from admitting that the accident occurred, for a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one and a migo may sometimes extract money, as Tumim writes. It follows that an uncertain claim of the defendant is not imposed through gilgul. We can now understand the Taz. He writes that the oath itself, namely that the defendant does not know, is imposed through gilgul, but the consequence of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear does not follow. Tosafot appear to say that no gilgul applies at all because it is unlike Sotah. Their intent must therefore be that we do not say that, through gilgul, he became obligated to swear a definite denial of liability and, since he cannot do so, must pay. Tosafot rule that this form of gilgul does not apply because it is unlike Sotah, where one imposes only an oath that the woman is capable of taking. But the actual oath that he does not know can be imposed through gilgul, because that does resemble Sotah, where every oath she can take may be imposed through gilgul. This must be the Taz's intent. This applies, however, when the creditor definitely asserts that the borrower knows, along the lines of the Shach in seif katan 56 and seif katan 74, and the borrower seeks to exempt himself by saying "I do not know." In that case one may impose upon him through gilgul an oath that he truly does not know. But where the creditor himself says only, "Perhaps you know," no such oath is imposed through gilgul, because an uncertain claim of the claimant cannot be imposed through gilgul. The oath that the defendant does not know would arise solely from the claimant's uncertain assertion that perhaps he knows. This resolves the authorities. In siman 94, where the Mechaber rules that no oath at all is imposed through gilgul, even an oath that he does not know, the case is one in which the claimant does not definitely assert that the defendant knows, and the principal oath rests on a definite claim. Thus an uncertain claim of the claimant cannot be imposed through gilgul. In siman 344, by contrast, the case concerns the oath of bailees, whose principal oath itself arises from an uncertain claim, and therefore even an uncertain claim of the claimant may be imposed through gilgul, as Maharsha's question indicates. Here the rule teaches that even in a case where the oath that he does not know may be imposed through gilgul, either because the claimant definitely says that the defendant knows or because the principal oath itself concerns an uncertain claim, we still do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. The later authorities further ask from Bava Metzia 100, where one party says the large animal and the other says, "I do not know," and the latter is liable. This is difficult according to Rav Nachman and Rabbi Yochanan, who hold that a definite claim does not prevail merely because the other party's claim is uncertain. One must seemingly establish the case as involving an oath obligation, such as where the claimant also asserts a claim concerning a garment, as the Gemara there does. But slaves are not subject to a Torah oath, so the liability regarding the animal would arise only through gilgul from the garment. Yet through gilgul we do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. This can be resolved as follows. In siman 312, seif 19, concerning two upper stories where the lower one collapsed, the matter remains unresolved and the ruling is that if the occupant is already dwelling there he is not removed. This seems difficult from Bava Metzia 103 regarding a bathhouse, where even if the renter already occupied it, he is removed because land is presumed to remain in the possession of its owner. We must say that the presumption that land remains in the owner's possession applies where both parties dispute the same parcel of land, each claiming it as his own, so the land remains with the original owner. But where there are two parcels and one certainly left the owner's possession while it is unknown which one, the original presumption cannot determine the matter. Tumim writes similarly. Accordingly, the case of one saying "the large one" and the other "the small one" is not difficult at all, because the Mishnah is established where the animals are standing in a marsh. There is no current possession, only an original presumption, and where one says the large one and the other the small one that original presumption does not apply. Therefore even Rav Nachman and Rabbi Yochanan agree that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one because there is no opposing presumption.
