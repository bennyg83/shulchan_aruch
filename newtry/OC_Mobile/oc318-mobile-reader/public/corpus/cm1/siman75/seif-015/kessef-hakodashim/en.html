Seif 17 in the gloss. (He may impose a shevuat heset upon him.) At the end of siman 75, the Urim VeTumim disagrees with the Taz concerning a shevuat heset where the claim runs contrary to the evident circumstances. It appears that even according to what the Tumim writes there, there is no decisive basis for a definite obligation. The matter remains doubtful, and the Shach writes in siman 87, seif katan 11, that where there is doubt one is exempt from an oath. The Urim VeTumim likewise rules there, and its proof concerns a shevuat heset. Since a shevuat heset is not imposed because of a doubt, it follows that one should not impose it on a claim that contradicts the evident circumstances. Moreover, just as circumstances that lend support to a claim can generate a shevuat heset, certainly circumstances can also exempt from a shevuat heset, as I wrote elsewhere. There I did not write it as a practical ruling, but today such a case arose in practice and was decided by agreement of three judges. They stated that many people who heard the facts regarded the circumstances as clearly showing that the defendant was exempt. In siman 93, seif katan 3, the Urim VeTumim writes that one should not impose a shevuat heset where there is a doubt whether a claim of two silver maot is required. There it appears that a sufficiently strong circumstantial inference can even create liability for a more severe oath, such as a Mishnaic oath, since the proof regarding an open chest is drawn from the oath of partners, which itself rests on circumstantial concern. Nevertheless, there is room to distinguish. In all monetary matters we accept that there are very clear circumstantial inferences that can even support extracting money. Yet matters of circumstantial inference are governed by the principles explained in siman 15 regarding fraud, and we are not expert in determining which inferences are sufficiently clear. As to an open chest, although the Gemara does not expressly state that its significance is an evidentiary presumption for purposes of an oath, distinguished judges in every generation have accepted that it can generate a shevuat heset. This is no weaker than the authority of a singularly great judge of a generation to decide by circumstantial inference. For other circumstantial matters relating to shevuat heset, the agreement of many knowledgeable people who have seen the particular incident is effective, just as appraisals are decided on the assessment of three experts. Elsewhere I wrote similarly regarding action taken for the benefit of orphans, which is determined by three experts familiar with what is needed at that time. Certainly this can apply to imposing or exempting from a shevuat heset. But to exempt from a Mishnaic oath, or from an even more stringent obligation, it is difficult for us to evaluate human conduct by presumptions that the poskim did not state expressly. In the case mentioned above, there was room to regard the defendant as saying, "I do not know whether I ever became obligated." Everyone knew that he truly did not know, were it not that a letter from a government minister constituted circumstantial evidence, because such an official would not accept a bribe to write a false statement of that nature. Since the entire oath obligation arose only through circumstantial inference, another sound circumstantial inference can exempt him. Not every inference is equal, however. The principal basis was the assessment of many people regarding the case before them, which is at least effective to exempt from a shevuat heset. This is especially so where the very liability itself rests on an inference. In any event, a doubt suffices for exemption. The Urim VeTumim there brings a proof from siman 87 concerning a claim of payment for immoral relations. It appears that the authorities there did not consider explaining that the case concerns only a person already regarded as suspect. We find similarly regarding the prohibition of yichud that there is a difference between immoral persons and those not so regarded, and the Sages said concerning one whose occupation is with women, and Tanna DeBei Eliyahu likewise writes regarding yichud. See there. In siman 87, however, the point is only whether a person is believed through an oath even though, according to the claimant's account, he is suspect. At least where a person has an established presumption of uprightness and the circumstances indicate that the claimant is lying, one may say that according to all opinions he is exempt from a shevuat heset. Where there are substantial grounds that make the doubt akin to certainty, it appears that no formal acceptance of a ban is required, because the doubt itself arises before the beit din. It resembles a direct monetary concern in which the beit din itself has a doubt even without a litigant's claim. Nevertheless, the other party can respond that in his eyes the person is suspect of precisely this conduct. The weight of such grounds is determined according to the assessment of genuinely knowledgeable experts. If a piece of merchandise bears a notation by non-Jewish craftsmen stating the number of cubits, perhaps that does not create a definite claim sufficient for a partial admission. Even for shevuat heset the notation may be viewed like an open record. Perhaps a partial admission is not created by majority or presumption, because in monetary law we do not follow a majority, even where deviation is very uncommon, where a craftsman has a presumption not to damage his trade, fears a severe penalty, and most craftsmen do not lie. Even so, money cannot be extracted on that basis, and similarly an oath should not necessarily be imposed, because there is still a minority that lies, and the craftsmen are fixed persons who may be recognized by others. Since we rule that one witness, where no oath is taken against him, is equivalent to two witnesses, and especially because one witness is regarded as two with respect to matters of oath, consider a person whose accounting shows that money is missing and who is uncertain which of two people received it. Ordinarily this supports no shevuat heset, only an acceptance of a ban. If, however, one of the two has a single witness establishing that the missing money did not reach him, one may say that a shevuat heset can then be imposed on the other. As for imposing an acceptance of a ban merely because one says, without any supporting grounds, "Perhaps you stole from me," it appears that this should not be done. In the case of a custodian, however, where one says, "Perhaps it was lost while with you," there is some resemblance to the oath of custodians, and the beit din may impose an appropriate form of acceptance according to what the judges deem proper.
