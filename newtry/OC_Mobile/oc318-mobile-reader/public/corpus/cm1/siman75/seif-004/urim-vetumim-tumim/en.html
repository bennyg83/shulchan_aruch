And specifically where beit din recognized, and so forth. Sema below in siman 87 rules that if a defendant says, "I owe you nothing because I guaranteed fifty for you and paid on your behalf," and beit din determines that the guarantee payment does not legally discharge him, this is not a partial admission for a Torah oath. This follows Rashba's responsum concerning one who says, "I paid interest for you." Even if the legal claim fails, the defendant did not admit owing the money. Shach argues that this is exactly R. Chiyya's first rule: what difference is there whether witnesses establish the fifty or beit din establishes it? I think there is a distinction. If the defendant makes a factual statement that beit din rejects as false, the case resembles R. Chiyya. The defendant has in fact brazenly denied a debt that objective evidence proves. But if his facts are true and he is merely mistaken in law, he has not admitted owing anything and has not brazenly denied a known obligation. R. Chiyya's kal vachomer depends partly on the logic of partial admission. A person who admits part is less brazen, whereas one who denies everything against established fact has already demonstrated brazenness. Where the defendant says truthfully, "I paid on your behalf as guarantor and believed that discharged me," his denial of liability is not brazenness. He is simply legally mistaken. Had he known the halachah, he might have admitted. Therefore the factual basis that removes the ordinary presumption against brazenness is absent. This is consistent with Baal HaTerumot where beit din recognizes that the defendant actually knows of the liability and still denies it. There the denial is truly brazen. Accordingly, Rashba and Sema are correct: a legal error does not by itself create the R. Chiyya partial-admission oath.
