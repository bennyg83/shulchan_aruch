Who disagrees. This is the opinion of the Ran at the beginning of Shevuot, chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. There he exempts only because the debt is secured by land, implying that without that factor it would not count as 'here it is.' A practical difference arises where the debtor owns no land. Although we hold that even where he owns no land a written instrument is treated as 'here it is,' as stated in siman 88, seif 28, that is specifically when he admits the debt. If he does not admit it, the concept of 'here it is' does not apply unless he owns land, in which case he is exempt because the claim is secured by land, or because he cannot rid himself of the land and therefore the land itself is treated as 'here it is,' as Rashi explains at the beginning of Bava Metzia. This appears to be the correct practical ruling. From the Sema's words it appears that he understood the Rama to be discussing a document secured by land: according to the first view it counts as 'here it is' because the document is secured by land, while the latter view disagrees. It also appears that the Sema sees no distinction with a document between whether the debtor admits or denies the debt. This is incorrect. Moreover, if so, why does the Rama cite two views here, while at the beginning of siman 87 he writes, 'Some say that a pledge,' and so forth, 'for even a document counts as here it is,' implying there is no dispute regarding a document? Rather, the explanation is as I have written. When the Sema further writes at the beginning of siman 87 that the law follows 'those who disagree,' meaning the first dissenters who hold that a document counts as 'here it is,' according to my explanation, where our case is one of denial, the law follows the latter dissenters. Shach.
Articles. The Sema writes that the same applies to deposited money. The wording merely reflects the usual case, since money is ordinarily entrusted to a reliable person to invest and increase, in which case a different law applies, as stated immediately below. The Shach replies that had the Sema seen the Mechaber's words in Bedek HaBayit he would not have written this. There the Mechaber says that the Tur's phrase, 'But if he claimed one hundred of deposited money,' is imprecise, for how would anyone know that those particular coins were the deposit? One must therefore understand the claim as concerning articles. Alternatively, where it says witnesses saw the money in his possession, it means that he admitted before them that he held fifty as a deposit. Thus the Mechaber intentionally changed the wording to 'articles.' I further find difficulty with the Sema because even if witnesses testify that these are the identical deposited coins, he should not thereby be established as a denier. Regarding money too, one can say he was merely delaying, as with a loan, since siman 292, seif 7, states in the name of Tosafot that even while the coins remain intact he may repay with other coins. If the money was deposited bundled and sealed so that he may not use it, then it is exactly like other articles. Perhaps there is a textual error in the Sema. See there.
When the defendant. The Sema writes that if the item was not seen in his possession at the time of his denial, even if it had been seen earlier, he does not become disqualified as a false denier, even concerning a deposit. We say that he did not intend to deny the deposit, but that it had temporarily been lost from his possession and he did not wish to tell the depositor, lest the depositor force him into a Torah oath. He therefore stalled until he could search for it and return it. This rationale applies specifically where money was deposited without authority to invest it. But if he claims that the money was given to him to invest for the owner's benefit, he cannot justify denying everything by saying he had spent it, since from the outset it was entrusted to him precisely so that he could spend and invest it. He should have disclosed that fact, and by denying the entire matter he shows an intent to steal and becomes disqualified. The Rosh writes similarly in a responsum. See above, siman 30, seif 5. The Shach writes that even where the money was entrusted for investment, whenever it is possible that the money was temporarily lost he should not be disqualified, since he may have thought, 'I will delay until I search for it.' It is unreasonable to disqualify him merely because he failed to say he had spent it when that was not true. Rather, the Rosh's case is one where witnesses knew that the money was in his possession at the very time he denied it. See there.
He swears. The Sema explains that this is because he was established as a false denier only regarding part of the money. Therefore he must still swear regarding the remainder, for he is established as a denier only concerning that money and not other money, as stated in siman 79, seif 5. By contrast, where he was established as a false denier regarding the entire amount, the opposing party collects without an oath, as stated there in seif 8. The same must be understood above in siman 30, seif 5, where it says generally that he is established as a false denier regarding that money and must pay two hundred. The intent, according to the Sema, is that he pays without an oath because he was established as a denier regarding the whole amount. The Shach writes that these words are astonishing. It is obvious that in siman 30, seif 5, too, he was established as a false denier only regarding part, and the claimant collects only with an oath. This is explicit in the newly printed responsa of the Rosh. The concise wording there is because that discussion concerns only the combination of testimony, not the law of oaths. I later found that Gidulei Terumah discusses this at length and leaves the Rosh's responsum requiring study. In my opinion the matter is simple as I have explained.
