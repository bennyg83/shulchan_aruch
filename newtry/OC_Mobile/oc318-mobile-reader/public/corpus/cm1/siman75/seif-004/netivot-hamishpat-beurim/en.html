He claimed movable objects. See Shach, seif katan 17, through his statement that the fifty gold coins are in Reuven's hands, etc. At first glance his words are difficult. Even where deposited money remains in the bailee's own house, the Shach above concludes that temporary evasion is possible because the bailee is permitted to spend it. If the money is in Reuven's hands, temporary evasion should likewise be possible because the defendant may need to collect it from Reuven in order to spend it. Perhaps the distinction is that only unrestricted deposited money may be used, for the reason given by Sema in siman 232, seif katan 15, since the depositor did not bind it up. But if he handed the money to another person specifically to spend it for the owner's benefit, that rationale does not apply and the holder may not use it. Alternatively, when the money remained in the bailee's own house, since he was permitted to spend it he acquired it and it became like his own money. But once he gave it to Reuven while the risk remained upon the original owner, it is as though he returned it to the owner. If the owner retracts before Reuven collects it, Reuven may no longer collect it for his own needs. Regarding the Shach's question on the Sema, where he writes that even where the money was to be improved or invested, so long as it is possible that the money was temporarily lost he is not disqualified, one can answer as follows. Bava Metzia 5 asks concerning Rav Chiya's teaching that the four bailees require partial denial and partial admission: why is such a person not disqualified from swearing as one suspected regarding money? The Gemara answers that there too he may merely be delaying, thinking that he will find the thief and seize him, or for another similar reason. Rashi explains why the Gemara does not ask this concerning the claim of unavoidable loss: the Torah imposed an oath on a bailee who claims unavoidable loss because perhaps he was negligent and is liable to pay, but is only postponing until he obtains money and can repay. The Gemara's question concerns the portion he outright denies, because that is a definite denial of the deposit. Ritva, cited by Shitah Mekubetzet, asks why even that denial cannot be explained as temporary evasion, since perhaps the bailee was actually negligent and liable and therefore says, "It never happened." He answers that if so, why deny the deposit entirely and make an audacious claim? He could say, "It was lost through unavoidable circumstances," which is not as audacious, as Rashi explains concerning disqualification from testimony. Since he did not do so, it appears that he intends outright denial, and he should be suspected regarding money. One must then understand the Gemara's answer, "Perhaps I will find the thief, etc." Why should he not simply claim unavoidable loss? Tumim answers that he is afraid to claim that the identifiable object was destroyed, because if he later finds it in the thief's hands he will be unable to recover it, as the thief can say, "You already admitted in beit din that it was destroyed." This is difficult. He could instead claim that it was stolen by armed robbers and later sue the thief. Moreover, where it is apparent to beit din that his earlier statement was made only to put off the claimant, it is not treated as an admission against a third party, as explained in siman 47. It therefore appears that Shitah Mekubetzet's wording resolves the matter. It says: "Perhaps I will find the thief, and even in the thief's hands it will no longer be intact; therefore he claims that it never happened." The meaning is as follows. Siman 294 seif 6 explains that if a bailee claims that an object was forcibly stolen and the thief is later found, the bailee must pay the owner and pursue the thief himself. The bailee is therefore afraid to claim unavoidable loss, because perhaps the thief will be found and witnesses will establish that the identifiable object was stolen from his house, while the object itself is no longer intact in the thief's hands. He would then have to pay the owner and pursue the thief himself. He therefore says, "It never happened," meaning that he never received such an object as a deposit. Then, even if the thief is later found, he will not have to pay the claimant on the basis of that deposit. The Gemara's phrase "perhaps I will find it in the marsh" is likewise explained in Shitah Mekubetzet in this manner. This also clarifies the responsum of the Rosh cited by Sema in seif katan 18, that this reasoning applies specifically to an identifiable object. Where the object has identifying features, one can fear that the thief who stole that very object will be found and the bailee will have to pay. Money generally lacks such identifying features, so the concern that the thief will be found does not apply, as Tumim writes. When Sema says that the bailee should have disclosed the matter, he means that he could have said he already spent the deposited money and later received other money, which was then lost through unavoidable circumstances. Sema did not simply say that he should have claimed unavoidable loss because sometimes even money can be identified by distinctive coins or by visual recognition. In that case it is possible that the money really was stolen and he fears saying so, lest the thief be found while the actual money is no longer intact and he be required to pay. He therefore says, "It never happened," as a temporary evasion. But where money was given to him with permission to spend it, that concern does not apply. Even if witnesses can identify the deposited coins, he can say that he spent those coins, later acquired other money through commerce, and that his own money was then lost through unavoidable circumstances. This differs from an ordinary deposit that he was forbidden to spend.
