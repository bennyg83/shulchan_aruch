(Sema, seif katan 6) For this presumption applies to ordinary people. Note: This is Rashi's view. Tosafot, however, hold that one who denies the entire claim is exempt from an oath even where there is no element of brazenness. See Tosafot, Bava Metzia 3, Bava Kamma 107, Ketubot 18a, and Gittin 51a. According to Rashi, study is required where one claims both movable property and land, the defendant denies everything, and witnesses establish the claim to the land. Although there is no Torah oath by reason of the principle that the defendant's own admission cannot be stronger than witness testimony, because even an admission regarding the land would not impose an oath on the denial of the movables, nevertheless we now see from the witnesses that he brazenly denied the land. Why then should he be exempt from an oath regarding the movables? The exemption for total denial rests on the presumption that a person does not act brazenly, yet here we see that he did. Nimukei Yosef in the sugya of Rabbi Chiyya's first rule likewise indicates that once a person is contradicted in part by witnesses, the presumption that a person does not act brazenly no longer applies.
