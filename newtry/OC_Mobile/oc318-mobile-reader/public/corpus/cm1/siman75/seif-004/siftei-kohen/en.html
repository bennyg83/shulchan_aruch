But if they wrote a document, etc. This means that the borrower does not admit the obligation recorded in the document or kinyan. The Beit Yosef objects that although a document is considered “here it is,” that is only where the borrower admits what the document records. In such a case the Beit Yosef agrees that a document is “here it is,” as I write below in siman 88, seif 28. The Beit Yosef points to the Hagahot Mordechai’s statement that the rule applies specifically to oral testimony. That passage expressly concerns a borrower who does not admit, contrary to the Bach, who thought the Beit Yosef referred to the earlier case where he does admit. Examination of the Beit Yosef and Hagahot Mordechai confirms this. The “authority who disagrees” cited by the Rama is the Beit Yosef, as the Darkei Moshe records. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe refer to the Ran at the beginning of Shevuat HaDayanim, meaning that the Ran exempts there only because the debt is secured by land. This seemingly implies that absent a lien on land the document is not “here it is.” The practical difference is where the borrower owns no land. Although we rule that a document is “here it is” even when he owns no land, as stated below in siman 88, seif 28, that is only where he admits the debt. If he does not admit, “here it is” does not apply unless land is actually subject to the lien. Then he is exempt either because the claim concerns land or because an obligation he cannot deny is treated as land that is already presented, as Rashi explains at the beginning of Bava Metzia. This is the primary ruling. The Sema, seifim ketanim 10 and 11, appears to understand the Rama as discussing a document secured by land. On that reading, the first view treats it as “here it is” because of the land lien, and the later view disagrees. He also appears to make no distinction between admission and denial. This is incorrect. Otherwise a contradiction arises: here the Rama presents two views, while at the beginning of siman 87 he writes that according to some a pledge is “here it is,” since even a document is considered “here it is,” implying that no one disputes the rule concerning a document. The correct distinction is therefore as I have explained.
And there is an authority who disagrees. The Sema writes that, in light of his discussion at the beginning of siman 87, the law follows those who disagree. It appears that he means the first dissenters, who regard the document as “here it is,” as he writes below in siman 87, seif katan 10. According to my explanation that the present case concerns a borrower who does not admit the document, the law follows the latter dissenters.

But if he claimed movable objects, etc. The same applies to deposited money. The Sema explains that the Mechaber merely stated the usual case, since money is ordinarily entrusted to a fiduciary for investment, in which event a different rule applies, as stated below. Had the Sema seen the Mechaber's words in Bedek HaBayit, he would not have written this. There the Mechaber comments on the Tur: “Our master's wording is not precise, for he wrote, ‘But if he claimed one hundred of deposited funds, etc.’ How can it be known that those particular coins were deposited funds? One must say that he claimed objects worth one hundred and witnesses testify that they presently saw in his possession some of those objects worth fifty. Alternatively, where he claimed money and they saw it in his possession, this means that he admitted before them that he held fifty as a deposit, similar to the responsum of the Rosh cited below.” Thus the Mechaber deliberately changed the Tur's wording and wrote “objects,” because with money there is no way to identify the coins as those of the deposit. The Sema's further statement, that money is ordinarily given to the fiduciary for investment and then follows a different rule, is also unclear to me, as I explain in seif katan 17. There is an additional difficulty. Even if witnesses testify that deposited coins are the identical coins, why should the custodian be established as a denier? He may merely be delaying, as with a loan. As Tosafot state at the beginning of Eilu Metziot, even when the coins remain intact he may repay with other money, and this is also indicated by the Gemara in HaMafkid. If the money was deposited tied and sealed, so that he was forbidden to use it, as explained in siman 292, it is exactly like other movable objects. Perhaps there is a scribal error in the Sema, and this comment belongs on the Mechaber's opening phrase, “When does this apply? When he claimed a loan,” or it refers to the end of the final clause. Its meaning would then be that deposited money which is tied and sealed also requires proof that it was presently in his possession. On that reading, his legal conclusion agrees with what I have written. One may also explain that the Tur's case of one hundred deposited coins concerns money tied and sealed, without resorting to the forced explanation of Bedek HaBayit. Even otherwise, Bedek HaBayit's objection can be answered by saying that witnesses saw fifty deposited with him and never lost sight of the coins, or immediately sealed them in a manner that left no doubt that fifty deposited coins remained with him. Yet if he was permitted to use the money, he could still have been delaying and would not thereby become an established denier. Therefore the Tur's “one hundred” must be understood as tied and sealed money, or as a case like the Rosh's responsum discussed in seif katan 17.

When the defendant, etc. If the object was not seen in his possession at the time he denied the claim before beit din, then even if it had been seen with him earlier he is not established as a denier. Perhaps it was temporarily lost and he delayed his answer until he could find it. He therefore takes a Torah oath and is exempt, as with a loan. According to the Sema, present sighting is required only where objects or money not intended for investment were deposited. If money was given to him for business and investment, he becomes an established denier even if it was not presently seen in his possession. This follows the responsum of the Rosh and the Mechaber in siman 30, seif 5. Since no specific object was deposited that he could say he did not know where it was, but rather money was entrusted to him, he should have disclosed that he spent it. Although it was given to him for investment and he was authorized to spend it, he was not required to repay immediately. This differs from a borrower, who must repay at once and may therefore delay until he has money. In my opinion, however, even money given for investment does not disqualify him whenever it could have been temporarily lost. He may have intended merely to delay until he found it. One cannot say that he is liable for accidental loss, because with an investment arrangement he is exempt, as stated below in siman 108, seif 4. Nor can one say that he accepted full responsibility, because then he would all the more resemble a borrower and the defense of temporary delay would apply. Nor should he be disqualified because he failed to say that he spent the money. Perhaps he did not wish to make that claim and thereby obligate himself without cause when the money was actually lost and he was exempt. It is also unreasonable to disqualify him for failing to state something untrue. Rather, the responsum of the Rosh and the Mechaber in siman 30 concern a case where the fifty gold coins were known to be in Reuven's hands and were from the money Shimon had given the defendant for investment. The defendant later denied having received anything from Shimon, and witnesses testified to his admission. It was known that the fifty coins were then in Reuven's possession, for example because the defendant held Reuven's note for them, or had lent them to Reuven before witnesses for a fixed term which had not yet expired. The Rosh explains only that with a specific object the witnesses must testify that it was literally in his house, whereas with investment money such exact testimony is unnecessary. Perhaps this was also the Sema's intent. In any event, the distinction is not simply between money given for investment and money not given for investment, because investment money itself requires the distinction I have stated. This appears clear as a matter of law.
And the claimant swears and collects. This is because the defendant was established as a denier only regarding part of the claim. The claimant must therefore swear concerning the remainder, for the defendant is discredited only with respect to that money and not other money, as stated in siman 79, seif 5. If he was established as a denier concerning the entire sum, the opposing party collects without an oath, as stated there in seif 8. The Sema writes that the same applies in siman 30, seif 5, where the ruling merely states that he is established as a denier regarding that money and must pay two hundred; he understands this to mean payment without an oath because the denial concerned the entire sum. The beginning of his explanation is straightforward, but its conclusion is astonishing. In siman 30, too, he was established as a denier only as to part, and the claimant collects only upon oath. This is explicit in the newer responsa of the Rosh printed in 5367. The ruling there is stated without qualification only because that passage concerns the joinder of testimony and does not address the law of the oath. I later found that Gedolei Terumah discusses this at length and leaves the Rosh's responsum requiring study, but in my opinion it is simple as I have explained.
