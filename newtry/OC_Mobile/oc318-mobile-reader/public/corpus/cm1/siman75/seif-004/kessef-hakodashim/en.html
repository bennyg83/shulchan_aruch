See Shach, seif katan 17. The Shach challenges the Sema concerning money given for improvement on two grounds: first, the person may have avoided a false claim; second, he may have wished to avoid becoming personally liable by the claim he would make. A practical difference arises with a custodian who stipulated exemption from the ordinary oaths of custodians. According to the second rationale, a false denial may render him suspect. According to the first, one might instead say that he should have searched for the item and that he could not safely plead, "I do not know where it is," where such ignorance itself would constitute negligence under siman 291; he also did not wish falsely to claim definite loss. Where the custodian would be exempt even for negligence, however, it seems that a knowingly false claim should render him suspect according to all opinions, because he could simply have told the truth that he did not know where the property was and would then have been exempt without even a hesset oath. Still, this requires further study. Perhaps the disqualification of one who falsely denies a deposit is specifically because he violates the prohibition "you shall not deny" by denying another's money. In the present case, if his true ignorance would itself have exempted him, his false statement did not actually deny money that he owed; it was merely a lie. A person is not necessarily disqualified as a witness for every lie. The distinction requires attention.
