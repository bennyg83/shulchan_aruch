There are grounds for suspicion. See Baer Heitev, through the words, “It appears that these matters depend upon the discretion of the judge. When he sees that there are grounds for suspicion, he may impose an oath,” etc. This implies that it is not sufficient for the litigant merely to assert that there are grounds for suspicion. Rather, the beit din itself must perceive those grounds. This is stated explicitly by the Shach immediately afterward: “Perhaps even the Rambam and the authorities cited above said this only when the claimant himself alleges that he has grounds for suspicion. But where the beit din sees that there are grounds for suspicion, they agree. The case of the broken-open chest means that the beit din sees that his chest was broken open,” etc. However, Responsa Radbaz, part I, siman 437, reconciles the Rambam differently. He writes that the Rambam did not say, “I am certain that you stole it,” but rather, “It is highly probable in my eyes that you stole it.” The reason is precisely because he says “highly probable.” If, however, he claims, “I am certain that you stole it,” an oath is imposed upon the defendant. Even though the claimant does not say that he actually saw him steal it, since he makes a definite claim, the defendant must swear. According to this view, even where the beit din itself does not know the grounds for suspicion, if the claimant says that on the strength of those grounds he is certain, an oath may be imposed.
