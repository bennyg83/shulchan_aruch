For example, he claimed, “I lent you,” etc. The same law applies where the claimant says, “I do not know whether you repaid me,” and the other party also does not know. Rabbi Shmuel de Medina, responsum 1, cites a scholar who ruled so, but disagrees. He rules that where there is a promissory note and both parties are uncertain, the borrower must pay, citing siman 59. In an oral loan he need not pay, though one who seeks to discharge his obligation toward Heaven must do so. He seeks a proof from seif 17, which speaks of paying to fulfill one’s duty toward Heaven where the defendant does not admit. His ruling concerning a written note is correct, though the proof from siman 59 is unnecessary, for the matter is explicit below in siman 82, at the end of seif 2. His proof that an oral borrower must pay to fulfill his duty toward Heaven is not compelling. The opening case in seif 17 concerns a defendant who makes a definite denial and is plainly exempt even toward Heaven according to all opinions. The present seif then explains that where both parties are uncertain, the matter is disputed.

And some say, etc. In my humble opinion, the first view should be accepted, namely that he is exempt even from the duty to satisfy Heaven. This follows from the Yerushalmi at the end of HaGozel Batra. There, one says, “You robbed me,” and the other says, “I do not know.” Rabbi Yirmiyah thought that the defendant must legally restore the money, whereas Rabbi Yose maintained that his obligation is only to satisfy Heaven. The Yerushalmi then states: “I took money from my purse and gave it to you,” while the other says, “I do not know.” Rav Huna said that we tell the claimant, “You do not know, but he is expected to know?” The meaning appears to be that the claimant says, “I took money from my purse without counting it and gave it to you; I do not know how much,” and the recipient likewise does not know. Rav Huna says rhetorically: “You do not know, yet he should know?” Since the Yerushalmi first cites the dispute of Rabbi Yirmiyah and Rabbi Yose regarding the duty toward Heaven, and immediately beforehand explains the Mishnah’s word “liable” as referring to that duty, while afterward it states without qualification, “We tell him, ‘You do not know,’” it follows that in the case of money taken uncounted from the purse the defendant is exempt even toward Heaven.
