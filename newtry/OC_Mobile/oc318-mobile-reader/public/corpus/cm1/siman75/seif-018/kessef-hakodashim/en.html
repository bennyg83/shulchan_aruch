Regarding the Taz's discussion where a borrower is uncertain whether a counterfeit coin had been included in his payment, perhaps an error in arithmetic is more stringent where the lender says that he never took his attention from the payment and now the total is short, while the borrower does not know. Even though the parties separated assuming that no obligation remained and had no intention to recount, one might say that there is a presumption analogous to the prior status: just as the money had not been paid before the act of payment, so too the amount now in doubt remains unpaid, because completing the payment requires an act. This creates some basis for a presumption. All the more so, we should not unnecessarily increase disputes by saying that the Shach necessarily disagrees with the Taz on this point. On the other hand, one may invoke a presumption that people do not ordinarily err in their calculations. This is why the claim "I was mistaken" is a weak claim, and even a party in possession can rely upon it only where he has a migo. If the lender has some independent migo, for example the borrower later lent money to the lender privately, nevertheless one may say that using that migo with respect to the present disputed item is a migo to extract. Although the lender is in possession of other money, that other possession is not the subject of the present doubt, and with respect to the matter now being adjudicated the migo would be used to extract. If a person sends merchandise through an agent, and the sender and purchaser initially had a clear agreement, but the sender claims that he later instructed the agent to alter that agreement, the purchaser who received the merchandise is in the position of saying, "I do not know whether I became obligated according to what you say you told the agent." Even if he admits part of the claim and has not yet paid the amount that would be due according to the original agreement with the seller, he still was not expected to know whether the seller gave the agent different instructions from those originally agreed. Even if the merchandise is readily marketable at the price allegedly stated to the agent, this is not properly categorized as "I do not know whether I paid" for merchandise already received, because in any event the purchaser was not expected to know of the changed instruction. Nor is it the same form of "I do not know whether I paid" discussed elsewhere regarding the Taz's case of a counterfeit coin, where the parties had not yet fully disengaged from one another. I have acknowledged what the Shach writes in siman 232, and the matter still requires careful study. However, because there is a presumption that an agent performs his agency, we ordinarily assume that the agent said what he was told to say. This can create a definite claim that the purchaser heard from the agent that the sale was at the higher price. If the agent says that he did tell the purchaser, the purchaser must take a Torah oath to contradict that single witness. If the agent is a non-Jew, he can at least generate a shevuat heset according to the Shach in siman 149. The agent is not interested in the litigation, especially if he received no fee. Even if he received payment for the agency, perhaps, where ordinary appraisal shows that not even a perutah of that payment was for merely conveying the statement and the fee was only for transporting the merchandise, he is still not considered interested by reason of that payment. Even if he became a paid custodian through a gift, and even if no perutah was allocated for guarding but only for transport, the matter requires further study. Perhaps the agency itself still makes him interested, because if he were to say that he never conveyed the instruction, the sender could claim the merchandise from him on the ground that he had no authority to deliver it without informing the purchaser that the sale was at the higher price. There may also be distinctions depending on whether the agent knew that the statement affected a monetary obligation, or whether his omission would be considered unavoidable. This requires study. If the agent is unavailable, perhaps the presumption that an agent performs his agency itself gives sufficient definiteness to impose a shevuat heset upon the purchaser. At least where the agent told the seller that he had conveyed the statement, there may be grounds for a shevuat heset. This presently requires further study.
