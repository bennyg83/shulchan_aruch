Mordechai in Perek Shevuat HaDayanim cites Maharam concerning a seller who conveyed land by deed and, within three years, later claimed, "I bought it back from you." Maharam ruled that he cannot impose hesset on the purchaser, because R. Nachman instituted hesset principally for common money claims such as loans, not for an unusual claim that a recorded land sale was secretly reversed. Taz generalizes from this that hesset is not imposed for exceptionally uncommon claims. I do not think we follow Maharam on this. Maharam's ruling is built on his explanation of the Rabbah bar Sharshom case in Bava Batra, where he says hesset was not relevant because the claim of reacquiring sold land was unusual. Rosh explains that sugya differently. Either no hesset was available because the heirs could not make the necessary claim, or Rabbah bar Sharshom was in fact prepared to take hesset. Shulchan Aruch below in siman 150 cites both approaches. Once the source itself is disputed, Maharam's broad exclusion cannot be treated as established law. There is an even stronger indication. Below in siman 87 Mechaber allows hesset on a claim involving an alleged payment promised for forbidden relations. There can hardly be a more unusual claim among ordinary Jews. Yet the rarity of the allegation does not by itself eliminate hesset. Therefore the accepted rule is not that uncommon claims are categorically exempt from hesset. Maharam's ruling should not be generalized.
