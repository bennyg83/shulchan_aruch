Shach cites Maharshal that if both sides are uncertain whether repayment occurred, there may still be an obligation to satisfy the claim toward Heaven, although strict judicial collection does not follow. Shach is uncertain about this. The Yerushalmi at the end of Bava Kamma supports Maharshal. It discusses one who says, in effect, "I robbed you, borrowed from you, or received your deposit, but I do not know whether I returned it." R. Yirmiyah initially says he must pay as a matter of law; R. Yose says the obligation is only to fulfill one's duty toward Heaven. The wording must be read as a question about whether return occurred, not as a definite assertion that it did. Otherwise there would be no basis for liability at all. The dispute therefore concerns precisely a case of uncertainty over repayment where the claimant too lacks definite knowledge. R. Yose limits the result to a heavenly obligation. This is an important novelty and is not simply repetition of the Mishnah. It clarifies whether the Mishnah's "liable" means strict judicial liability or only the duty toward Heaven, and whether a claimant's definite demand is necessary. Thus Maharshal's rule has direct Yerushalmi support. Rashba in Gittin at first glance sounds stricter, but his discussion can be read as a special case where two sets of witnesses create a genuine evidentiary conflict and the creditor's own uncertainty is not blameworthy in the ordinary sense. The simpler general rule is: Where both parties are uncertain whether a definite debt was repaid, the possessor is not judicially forced to pay, but the debtor should satisfy the obligation toward Heaven. This fits the Yerushalmi and the general burden-of-proof principles.
