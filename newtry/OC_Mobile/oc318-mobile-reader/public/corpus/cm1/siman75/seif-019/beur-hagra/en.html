He claimed... beit din should investigate... As stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of chapter HaDayanim: "I lent you a sela, and it was worth two..." and the other says, "I do not know..." "Do you not know? This one knows..." Meaning, this is a case of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and therefore must pay. Sefer HaTerumos, section 38, and the Rosh in chapter HaDayanim cite it. Ramban writes that although he does not initially admit an item defined by measure or weight, and does not even admit a perutah, beit din asks him: although you do not know, etc. Even though he admits only as a result of beit din's inquiry, he is considered one who admits part of the claim, and the admission concerns an item defined by measure. This accords with Abaye in Shevuos 43a: there is no difference... but if he says, "this house," his claim is defined, meaning we can go and see whether it is up to the projection, etc. Rava disagrees and requires him to state the measurable amount himself. Thus whenever he does state it himself, even though he initially admitted only generally and then, through beit din's inquiry, admitted and specified how much he owes, he is considered one who admits part of the claim.
Two silver ma'os and a perutah. Below this amount there is no Torah oath, as stated at the beginning of chapter HaDayanim, following Rav, whose view is accepted as halachah, and as stated above at the beginning of siman 88. Where there is no Torah obligation to swear, we do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay, as stated in seif 14.
And specifically where he claimed. As stated in Shevuos 46b and at the end of HaKones: "he claimed a silver goblet..." and Kesubos 85b, "one thing I know..." Although the Yerushalmi at the end of HaKones asks Rabbi Yochanan whether one may claim against him items unsuitable for him, and seeks to resolve it from the incident of the sharecropper of Bar Ziza, "Can a person not conceal his means?" as cited by the Rif and Rosh there, that passage disagrees with our Bavli.
And he is exempt under human law. See the Shach. This is because the claimant asserts certainty, as above.
And some say. So writes the Rosh in chapter HaDayanim, and he likewise brings proof from the Yerushalmi cited above at the end of HaDayanim.
