But her nichsei melog and nichsei tzon barzel, etc. The ruling that she takes the property if it still exists is straightforward. Even a woman who committed adultery does not lose existing remnants of her property, Ketubot 101b. Likewise, the ruling concerning nichsei melog, that the husband is exempt for theft but liable for unauthorized use, is understandable. But the ruling concerning nichsei tzon barzel, that if he himself caused their loss he need not pay while if they were stolen he must pay, is astonishing. One who studies Maharam's responsum in Mordechai at the end of Ketubot will see that he compares a woman who refuses to ascend to Eretz Yisrael to a rebellious wife. Under the Geonic rules for a rebellious wife, as Rif writes in chapter Af Al Pi, property that is entirely consumed and no longer fit for its original use, and certainly property stolen or lost through accident, is repaid by the husband from his own assets, because with nichsei tzon barzel we learned, "if they died, the loss is his." With nichsei melog, if they were entirely lost we do not extract anything from the husband. How then can the ruling here reverse the logic and the established law of a rebellious wife? According to Maharam, the Geonic rule that for nichsei melog entirely lost we extract nothing from the husband means only where he did not make unauthorized use of them. If he did make unauthorized use, there is even more reason to require payment for nichsei melog than for nichsei tzon barzel, as appears from Ketubot 71a, where existing nichsei melog are hers, and Tosafot there explain similarly.
