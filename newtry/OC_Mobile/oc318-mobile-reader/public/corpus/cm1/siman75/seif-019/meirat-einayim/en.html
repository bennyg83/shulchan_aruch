And if he admits, this is an admission of part of the claim, etc. This applies specifically here, where the claimant demands one hundred, or two silver ma'ah and a perutah, and the defendant admits part of the claim. This differs from the preceding case, where the claimant does not claim two silver ma'ah. Even if the defendant admits a perutah, it may be that he owes no more than that and is willing to give that perutah because he says, "It is true that you lent me." Even if he owes slightly more, he is still not within the rule that one who is obligated to swear and unable to do so must pay, because for that rule the amount he denies must be at least two silver ma'ah. One might ask: Here too, why do we say that his admission is only a perutah, leaving two silver ma'ah denied? Perhaps his admission was more than a perutah, leaving less than two silver ma'ah in dispute. The answer is that the defendant did not initially seek to give anything. Rather, through questioning he was forced to admit that he owes at least a perutah, because below that amount it would not be termed a loan. Since we derive his liability only by necessity, we may impose only the minimum amount that is necessarily established. This is clear. See the Perishah.
