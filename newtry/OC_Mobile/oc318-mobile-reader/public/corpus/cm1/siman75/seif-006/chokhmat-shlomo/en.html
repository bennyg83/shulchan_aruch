Seif 16: One who claims an oral loan from the borrower’s heir, etc. Note: I saw that Netivot HaMishpat, siman 72, seif katan 25, writes that if a person became obligated to swear and then became mentally incapacitated, his inability to take the oath does not invoke the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay. He cites as proof the Shach’s explanation here that with heirs we do not say that one who cannot swear must pay, because perhaps had their father been alive he would have asserted a definite claim. Yet death is certainly an unavoidable circumstance. In my humble opinion this is not correct. Where the litigant himself becomes mentally incapacitated, although he cannot swear because of circumstances beyond his control, we say that his own field has been stricken, meaning that his own fortune caused the impediment. This resembles the rule in the first chapter of Ketubot where, if he became ill or she became ill, he must still provide her sustenance because his field has been stricken. So too here, his own circumstance caused his inability to swear, and he must therefore pay. There is thus no proof from heirs. Before their father died he still could have sworn. The unavoidable circumstance begins only at his death, and at that point the assets are no longer his but belong to the heirs. No unavoidable circumstance happened to the heirs themselves, so the principle that “his field has been stricken” does not apply to them. By contrast, while the original litigant is alive and the assets are his, the misfortune occurred to him and he must pay. In my opinion this is correct as practical halacha. There is also a clear proof. In Yoreh Deah siman 232 there is a dispute whether forgetting is considered an unavoidable circumstance. According to the view that it is, why is one who says, “Fifty I know, and fifty I do not know,” obligated to pay? His lack of knowledge may be due to forgetfulness, which is an unavoidable circumstance. Even if one follows those who hold there that forgetfulness is not an excuse because he should have acted before the final day arrived, without that consideration they agree that forgetfulness itself can be unavoidable. Why, then, does one who does not know still have to pay? It follows that an unavoidable circumstance does not help in this context. Admittedly, according to what I wrote in my Sefer HaChaim, siman 108, this particular proof may be rejected. Nevertheless, the ruling itself is correct for the reasons stated. Further in the same seif: See Shach, who writes that even with heirs there is an element of brazenness, because when they assert a definite claim they contradict the claimant. This is difficult for me in light of the Ran in the chapter Kol HaNishba’in. Regarding the Gemara’s question how the Torah’s oath of bailees can ever apply, since the bailee should be believed through a migo, the Ran asks why that is a migo at all, since it would be a migo involving a more brazen claim. He answers that the Gemara’s question is based on the statement that it is an excellent ruling that an employer is believed to say, “I paid you,” through a migo that he could have said, “I never hired you.” Although saying “I paid you” is not particularly brazen because an employer is preoccupied with his workers, saying “I never hired you” would be a brazen denial. The Gemara therefore assumes that even such a migo can operate, and on that basis asks about the oath of bailees. According to the Shach, however, the Ran’s question remains. Tosafot explain that the employer is believed through the migo when he says with certainty that he paid within the proper time. Where there are witnesses that he hired the worker, we say he is preoccupied with his workers and does not truly know, so his claim of definite knowledge is false. Where there are no witnesses, however, he is believed when he says with certainty, “I paid you,” through the migo that he could have said, “I never hired you.” According to the Shach, even when he now says with certainty, “I paid you,” he is being brazen toward the worker, because he contradicts the worker’s claim that his asserted certainty is false. The employer would then resemble heirs in every respect, so this is not a migo of brazenness. What proof, then, is there from this to the bailee’s claim of an unavoidable accident, where saying “an unavoidable accident occurred” is not brazen? From where would we know that a migo involving greater brazenness operates? The Ran’s difficulty therefore remains according to the Shach. This requires great study.
