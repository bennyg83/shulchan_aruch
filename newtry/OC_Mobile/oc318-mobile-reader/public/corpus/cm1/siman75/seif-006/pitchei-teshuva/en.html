Or he claimed, “I have an offsetting claim against you.” If the defendant claims that the offset is money previously paid as fixed interest, does the rule still apply that the borrower swears and is exempt? See Rama, Yoreh Deah 169:25, concerning a creditor who comes to collect from a borrower, and the Shach there. Responsa Chavot Yair, simanim 99 and 115 to 116, cites a responsum of Rabbi Tevlin regarding Reuven, who lent Shimon twenty Reichsthaler against pledges which appeared worth more, and wrote a sale document so that Shimon would redeem the pledges by a fixed date. They were later found to be worth less than the loan. Reuven demanded the deficiency, while Shimon answered that Reuven had previously taken interest from him for years. After analyzing the language of the handwritten agreement, the responsum concludes that it was not written as evidence by which to collect the loan balance and that its clause of credibility related only to disputes about the pledges. Any claim by the creditor beyond the value of the pledges was therefore an oral claim, against which the borrower could swear and be exempt. He then adds that there is nevertheless a strong argument for the creditor: since the borrower admits receiving the loan and merely seeks to retain other money because the creditor took interest in the past, he cannot make himself wicked by his own claim in that manner. This differs from Rama’s case in Yoreh Deah, where the borrower claims that the very money now demanded constitutes the prohibited interest and he refuses to perform an act of paying interest. Here he seeks to retain unrelated money on account of interest already paid, although according to his own account he himself participated in the prohibition, and it is unreasonable to permit an oath on that basis. Chavot Yair agrees that this is a sound distinction. He notes, however, that another authority, Rabbi Yeshayah, av beit din of Frankfurt, disagreed and understood Rama to apply even where the borrower claims that the interest had already been paid earlier. He answers the Shach’s difficulty accordingly. In the case at hand he nevertheless concludes that a compromise is preferable if one can be arranged. See there.
