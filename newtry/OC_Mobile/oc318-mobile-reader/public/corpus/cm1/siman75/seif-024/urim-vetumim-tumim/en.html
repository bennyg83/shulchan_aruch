Even a relative, and so forth. Shach asks from Rabbenu Tam's case in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Sefer Kinyan, siman 21. A son-in-law claimed that his father told him the father-in-law had promised a certain dowry, and that he entered the marriage on that basis. The father-in-law answered that the son-in-law had no definite claim against him, not even one sufficient for hesset. Rabbenu Tam advised that the son-in-law give his father a power of attorney, so the father himself could make a definite claim and impose the proper oath. Apparently, merely being told by his father, a close relative, was not enough. The wording there is important. The father-in-law says, "You have no definite claim against me, and not even hesset." Why say "even hesset"? Rabbenu Tam holds according to one version of R. Nachman that hesset was instituted only where there is already some monetary basis, such as a dispute about payment, not where the defendant denies the transaction entirely. The dowry case likely involved at least a partial admission that some dowry had been promised. The attempted oath would therefore have been a Torah partial-admission oath, which unquestionably requires a fully definite claim based on admissible evidence, not merely a relative's report. Rabbenu Tam's advice was to make the father an authorized claimant so he could personally assert the definite facts. According to our accepted rule that hesset can be imposed even without the same prior monetary basis, one can distinguish. Hesset may sometimes be triggered by a reliable report even when the report would not qualify as formal testimony. Thus Rabbenu Tam does not conclusively refute Rama. Still, Rabbenu Tam's case teaches that where another person actually knows the facts, an authorization can allow that person to litigate directly. Maharshal goes further and says that if the authorized representative already holds money of the defendant, he may retain it and swear hesset just as the principal could. Shach agrees. I have serious doubts about both propositions as applied under our law. Rabbenu Tam held that authorization over disputed movables worked by the law of the Gemara itself, making the representative closely resemble the principal. Rambam and many authorities hold that authorization of denied movables works only through a later Geonic enactment designed to prevent oppressive litigation expense. Such an enactment need not give the representative every procedural power of the true litigant, especially the power to create new hesset obligations. We do not automatically make an enactment upon an enactment. The second innovation, allowing the representative to seize and swear for the principal, is even harder. A representative is fundamentally an agent. How can an agent seize another person's money for someone else merely because the agent has a migo? If a relative of the claimant happened to hold the defendant's money without witnesses, no one ordinarily says he can keep it by swearing that the defendant owes his relative. Maharshal may follow his own view that an authorized representative is treated as an actual litigant and therefore cannot testify. Under that theory, seizure is more understandable. But we rule below that a representative can sometimes testify, showing he remains an agent rather than the substantive owner. On that approach, retaining the defendant's money through a personal oath for another's claim is difficult. There is also an oath-quality problem. The true defendant's false hesset can involve a false oath concerning money in his own hands. The representative, who owns no part of the claim, would be swearing only that Reuven owes Shimon. The halachic character and severity are not identical. Therefore the seizure rule requires study. One important consequence does follow if a real transferee, rather than a mere agent, purchases the claim or note and already holds the debtor's money. Such a person truly owns the claim and can invoke kim li like the relevant authorities while in possession. Chavot Yair ruled otherwise in one case, while Shevut Yaakov disagreed. The present analysis strongly supports Shevut Yaakov in the true-assignment case. Even if an ordinary authorized representative cannot seize, an actual purchaser of the debt is a substantive claimant and can defend existing possession according to the applicable safek din.
