With an oath that arises through gilgul, and so forth. This rule is learned from Bava Metzia 97. The Mishnah says one party claims the borrowed animal died and the other says he does not know. The Gemara asks that this contradicts R. Nachman, who holds a definite claim does not defeat a doubtful defense, and answers that there is another Torah oath between them. The disputed matter is then included through gilgul. The commentators ask why the Gemara needed a separate Torah oath. The custodian already swears that he was not negligent; the borrowed-versus-rented issue could be rolled into that oath. If inability to swear on a gilgul produced payment, the Mishnah would be explained without adding more animals. From this they infer that the unable-to-swear doctrine does not apply merely to a matter introduced through gilgul. There are alternate readings of the sugya, especially according to Rabbenu Tam and authorities who give the custodian oath different status. Some can explain the Gemara without deriving this limitation. The more difficult challenge comes from the later Mishnah: one says, "I bought the larger slave," and the other says, "I do not know," yet the claimant can receive the larger one. Slaves and land do not themselves carry the ordinary oath, so the result appears to depend on gilgul through the slave's garment or produce. That would suggest inability to swear through gilgul can indeed cause payment. Mordechai answers that in the large-versus-small slave case everyone, even R. Nachman, agrees the definite claim is stronger because the defendant's professed ignorance looks evasive. A person should know whether he sold a large or small slave. That answer has logic but is not clearly codified. A better solution is that the slave Mishnah follows Sumchus. Sumchus gives greater force to monetary doubt and can allow the definite claimant to prevail or divide without relying on the unable-to-swear doctrine. In the borrowed-animal Mishnah, by contrast, there is a bodily chazakah favoring the possessor, and Tosafot say even Sumchus can agree that the ordinary possessor rule is stronger. The Gemara's challenge to R. Nachman therefore comes specifically from that clause and requires a genuine Torah oath. This also helps explain Rambam's rulings concerning an exchanged cow and donkey. Where both parties are uncertain and the object stands in a neutral place, Rambam can order division even though we do not generally rule like Sumchus, because the normal possessory chazakah is weakened. Accordingly, the slave case does not overturn the core rule. A matter that arises only by gilgul does not generally invoke "unable to swear, therefore pay" in the same automatic manner as the principal Torah oath.
However, because the sugyot admit several readings, some authorities disagree. The practical rule should follow the major poskim who distinguish the principal oath from gilgul.
