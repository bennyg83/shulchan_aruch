But I repaid you, etc. The Sema and Bach rightly object that if he claims actual repayment, then even two witnesses to the loan do not defeat him, because one who borrows before witnesses need not repay before witnesses. With one witness who testifies both that the loan occurred and that it was not repaid, the defendant may swear to contradict the witness. The Mechaber therefore does not mean actual repayment. He refers to a defense that is believed only through a migo, such as “you waived the debt,” “you gave it to me as a gift,” or the like. Since the witness testifies that he did not repay, the defendant is obligated to swear and cannot do so, and therefore must pay. This is a correct rule.
Likewise, if he initially claimed, “There was never any debt,” etc. A responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe appears to disagree. The Rashba holds that even if two witnesses would render him liable only because they establish him as a denier, one witness does not create the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. See there.
In all these cases he is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore pays. Gedolei Terumah writes that whenever this rule applies, the defendant may take a heset oath after payment, as stated below in siman 82, seif 10. This is straightforward and is explicit in the Ramban's responsum, siman 84, and in the Mechaber above, siman 72, seif 23.
But where, etc., he is believed through a migo. This means that he is believed upon a heset oath through the migo. The Sema explains that if the witness does not testify that he never lost sight of the object, the defendant could claim repayment and is therefore also believed to say, “I have a corresponding claim against him.” The Sema says this to reject the view of Ribam, cited by Tosafot in Bava Batra 34a, that one is not believed to assert an offset through a migo of repayment. According to the Sema's understanding, Ribam regards the migo as standing against testimony: the single witness testifies to the loan, the defendant does not contradict him but raises a different defense, and the Torah empowered the defendant only to swear directly against the witness. Since he admits the witness's account and cannot swear against it, he pays. In my opinion this is an error. Ribam certainly agrees that where the defendant could have claimed repayment or return, he is believed. A single witness cannot create greater liability than two witnesses. Ribam's point is only that a migo based on a possible denial of the witness does not suffice by itself. The Torah requires him actually to swear against the witness, and if he does not, the witness stands as two. This is necessarily subject to the same conditions as two witnesses, including testimony that the object never left his sight. The Ramban in Bava Batra and the Ran in Kol HaNishba'in state Ribam's answer and immediately explain that it applies only where the witness never lost sight of the object. Otherwise, even against two witnesses the defendant would be believed to say, “I seized my own,” through a migo of return. This is clear.

He is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore pays. Although the witness does not testify that the object seized was not the defendant's, the defendant himself admits the seizure. The Torah gives one witness the force of two unless the defendant swears against him. Here he accepts the testimony and cannot swear, so he pays. He is not believed through a migo that he could have denied the seizure, because that alternative would require him to contradict the witness under oath. Tosafot explain this in the name of Ri. Although Tosafot elsewhere challenge Ri from the views of Rav and Shmuel, they cite his explanation without objection in Shevuat HaDayanim. The Ran and Ramban also give this explanation. One may answer Tosafot's question by saying that Rav and Shmuel hold that even without a migo, since the witness does not directly contradict the defendant's claim of ownership, the Torah did not require payment merely because he cannot swear. We rule like Rabbi Abba. Tosafot also conclude in the name of Ribam that even a valid migo does not excuse him from the Torah's requirement to swear against the witness or pay. The Ramban and Ran adopt this answer as well. The Sema writes that he cannot say, “It was mine,” through a migo of return, because the Maggid Mishneh holds that a robber is not believed to say that he returned the object except with witnesses. Alternatively, the object was seen in his possession at the time of the claim, or the witness says that he never lost sight of it after the seizure and knows it was not returned. The Maggid Mishneh, however, gives the rule requiring witnesses of return only according to his understanding of the Rambam. As I explain at the end of siman 361, the Rambam does not necessarily hold that way, and the accepted law is that even a robber need not return before witnesses. We must therefore rely on the other explanations. The Ran says so, and Rabbeinu Yerucham cites Ramah, Ramban, and Rashba that the case is where the witness remained with him from the seizure until now, or where the object is presently in his hand before us. Otherwise he is believed to say, “I seized my own,” through a migo that he returned it, since even a robber need not return before witnesses.
