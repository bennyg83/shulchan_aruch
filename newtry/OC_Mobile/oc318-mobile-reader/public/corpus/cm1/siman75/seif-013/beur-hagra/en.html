One hundred, etc.; if this witness, etc. This refers to the second clause, where the witness says, 'You owe him.' As Maggid Mishneh writes in Laws of Claims 4:8, a statement that 'you owe me' against the defendant is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed payment, as explained above in seif 7. Here he has no such migo because the witness contradicts him, so he cannot claim payment, as Tosafot write in Bava Batra 34a, s.v. 'it would be.'
Or he is, etc. This refers to both clauses. Whenever the defendant does not contradict the witness's testimony, although had he contradicted it he could have sworn, as Tosafot write there, he is one who cannot swear and therefore pays. Here too, had he denied the fixed due date he could have sworn. Since he does not deny it, we treat the due date as established by two witnesses. See siman 78. The same applies in every case of one who cannot swear and must pay. This is the meaning of, 'Likewise, if he claims,' etc.
But where he could, etc. Meaning, where he could advance that defense without contradicting the witness, since the witness is no stronger than two witnesses, as Tosafot write in Bava Batra there. This differs from a case where the defense itself contradicts the witness. Even according to Ribam's resolution there, that a migo does not help him, the point is likewise that a migo cannot be used to contradict the witness, who is treated like two witnesses. See Shach.
Some say specifically, etc. This is why the Gemara says, 'Let him swear,' etc.; he is like a robber specifically because of the seizure. Although he would ordinarily be believed to contradict the witness, as stated above in seif 8, here he is not believed. By contrast, where the object came to him lawfully, although he contradicts the witness concerning the loan, he is not deemed a robber, as stated in Bava Metzia 48a concerning one who designated a utensil, etc. Proof comes from the incident of Avimi in Ketubot 85a, although there was one witness, since an agent can become a witness, as stated at the beginning of chapter 2 of Kiddushin.
(Anthology) Some say specifically, etc., but if, etc. If two witnesses testify concerning the collateral, the lender is not believed to claim a loan up to its value, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of chapter HaDayyanim. The Yerushalmi recounts a case where one person claimed two dinars and held collateral worth two dinars; the other admitted only one dinar. The judges of Nehardea ruled that since everyone agreed the collateral was worth two dinars, the claimant must bring witnesses for the other dinar. They had not heard Rabbi Yochanan's rule that a lender is believed up to the value of his collateral. Another incident concerned one who seized another's sheet in the marketplace and said it would not leave his hand until the debt was paid. Shmuel told him to return the sheet and litigate. The Yerushalmi distinguishes: there the pledge was established in his hand, whereas here it was not established in his hand. 'Established' is used as Tosafot explain in Sanhedrin 31b.
And some disagree. The incident of Avimi is no proof, as Tosafot explain in Ketubot and Bava Batra. It occurred after enactment of the hesset oath, when an agent cannot become a witness, as stated in Kiddushin there. The phrase 'he is like a robber' is not literal; it means that two witnesses saw him seize it. Since he does not contradict them, he is not believed to say, 'I seized what was mine,' under the law of one who seizes in the presence of witnesses. According to Rambam in Laws of Robbery 4, one who robs in the presence of witnesses must return the object in the presence of witnesses. The reason the Gemara specifically says he is like a robber is that the witness does not know whether he returned it, as Maggid Mishneh writes in Laws of Claims 4:8 and as explained above in the first gloss, 'But where he could,' etc. Therefore the phrase 'he is like a robber' is necessary.

And the claimant need not swear. Shevuot states that all agree regarding the opposing party, etc., and asks, 'Who says,' etc. It then repeats that all agree, demonstrating that whenever one cannot swear and must pay, the claimant need not swear.
But if he answered him, etc. This follows Bava Metzia 95a, where the case is one involving several animals, in accordance with Rava, etc. The latter clause involves three cows, where he says, 'three cows,' etc. But with two cows, where he returns one and says he does not know about the second, he is exempt.
