But. The Sema and Bach have already written at length in astonishment at the Mechaber's ruling here. Certainly, if the defendant claims, 'I repaid you,' then even where there are two witnesses to the loan he is believed upon taking a hesset oath, because one who borrows before witnesses need not repay before witnesses. Therefore, where one witness testifies that he borrowed and did not repay, he may swear to contradict the witness. One must say that the Mechaber is not speaking of actual repayment, but of a defense that is credible only through a migo, such as, 'You waived the debt,' 'You gave it to me as a gift,' and the like. In such a case he is believed only through a migo that he could have claimed repayment. Therefore, where the witness testifies that he did not repay, he becomes one who is unable to take the oath and must pay. This is a correct ruling. Still, the Shach questions the further statement that if the defendant says, 'You owe me an offsetting amount,' he is likewise not believed, for with an oral loan he would be believed in this claim even against two witnesses. One must therefore say that the witness testifies that the object came into his possession as a loan. He cannot then say that the creditor owes him an offsetting amount, because if so he should have taken it in payment, and he is not claiming that the original loan was still within its term. Presumably there was not a long interval between the loan and the present claim, since the witness says that he never left them unattended. Baer HaGolah resolves this differently. See there.
And I repaid. Responsa Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, and also by the Sema in siman 79, seif 9, appears to disagree with this ruling. According to that view, since even two witnesses would not directly create monetary liability and the liability would arise only because the defendant was established as a false denier, one witness does not create the status of one who is unable to swear and must pay. See there. Shach.
Through a migo. The Sema writes that this means that if the witness does not testify that he never left the parties unattended, and the defendant could therefore say, 'I repaid,' he is likewise believed to say, 'I have property of yours corresponding to the debt,' through the migo of repayment. This is unlike Ribam, cited by Tosafot in Bava Batra, chapter Chezkas HaBatim. See there. The Shach challenges this and writes that Ribam too agrees that he is believed through a migo. See there. See also siman 51, seif 2, and the Sema and Shach there.
To swear. The reason is that although the witness does not testify that the item he seized was not his, the defendant himself admits that he seized it. The Torah gives one witness the force of two unless the opposing party swears against him. Here the defendant admits the witness's testimony and therefore cannot take the required oath, so he must pay. He is not believed through a migo that he could have said, 'I did not seize it,' because that alternative would have required him to swear against the witness. Tosafot write this in Bava Batra, chapter Chezkas HaBatim. Although they raise a difficulty there, in Shevuot, chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, they state the rule without qualification. The Sema writes that he is also not believed to say, 'It is mine,' through a migo that he could have said, 'I returned it,' despite the Rama's statement that a migo is effective, because, as Maggid Mishneh writes, one who steals from another is not believed to say he returned the object except with witnesses. Alternatively, the case is where the object was seen in his possession at the time of the claim, or the witness says he never left him unattended. In truth, Maggid Mishneh did not write that a robber must return before witnesses. According to what I wrote at the end of siman 361, nothing of the sort is proved from Rambam, and we rule that a robber too need not return before witnesses. We must therefore rely on the other answers. Ran and Rabbeinu Yerucham write likewise in the names of Ramban and Rashba. See also siman 90, seif 12, siman 146, seif 2, siman 364, and Responsa Maharit, siman 9. The Shach further writes in the name of Gidulei Terumah that whenever the rule of 'unable to swear and therefore must pay' applies, the defendant may after payment require the claimant to take a hesset oath. This is obvious and is explicit in Responsa Ramban, siman 84, and implied by the Mechaber in siman 72, seif 23. It appears that with our promissory notes, if one witness verifies the signature, since the instrument contains a clause of credibility concerning repayment and the debtor's only defense is 'I repaid' through a migo that he could have claimed forgery, once one witness testifies that the signature is genuine there is no migo. He is therefore one who cannot swear and must pay.
Believed. Although the Sages believed a creditor regarding a pledge up to its value only through a migo that he could have claimed purchase or return, and here he cannot claim return, while it is also uncomfortable for him to claim purchase because the witness knows it came to him as a pledge and he does not wish to brazenly contradict the witness, nevertheless, since he does not deny the witness by saying that no loan was made on the pledge, the creditor is believed upon oath to state how much is owed to him against the pledge. The item came into his possession lawfully and he is able to swear. Sema. The Taz challenges this. See there.
