He is one who is obligated to swear and, being unable to swear, must pay. Shach, seif katan 42, writes that from here one can infer a rule regarding our mamramot when one witness verifies the signature. This is difficult. If so, with an ordinary note signed by two witnesses, even without a special credibility clause, since the debtor is not believed to say "paid" except through a migo that the signatures are forged, why should one witness verifying the signatures not suffice to defeat that migo?
It came into his possession as collateral. See Sema, seif katan 38, who distinguishes this from one who claims payment while one witness testifies that he borrowed and did not pay. At first glance his explanation is difficult. Sema says that here the lender can swear, but why should that matter? He has no migo allowing him to contradict the witness, and a claim regarding the amount secured by collateral ordinarily requires a migo. His comparison to one who claims payment while a witness says the loan was not paid is likewise difficult, because above, seif katan 31, Shach concludes that when a witness testifies to nonpayment, the debtor can swear against him. Sema's intent can be explained as follows. The earlier case is one where the witness testifies that the debt is still within its term. Since the debtor cannot be believed through a migo, he becomes one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. In a collateral dispute, by contrast, if the witness agrees that the object is collateral and merely does not know the amount advanced, the lender's claim as to the amount does not contradict the witness at all. The rule of inability to swear arises in Sema's view only where the defendant presently contradicts the witness at least in part. Thus, if the witness testifies to the precise amount lent and the lender claims a larger amount, the lender is contradicting the witness and the inability rule would apply. Shach, however, explains this opinion differently. Where even one witness testifies that the object is collateral and the borrower does not contradict him, that witness is believed like two concerning the fact that the lender holds it as collateral. The lender is therefore treated as a muchzak, just as if two witnesses testified that it was collateral, and he can then swear against the witness concerning the amount advanced. If there are no witnesses that it is collateral and only the borrower admits that fact while the parties dispute the amount, the lender is not independently a muchzak. He is deemed a muchzak only to the extent that the borrower's own admission makes him so. The borrower's statement is a case of the mouth that prohibited being the mouth that permitted, which is stronger than an ordinary migo. Thus the lender's credibility concerning the amount depends on the scope of the borrower's admission, as explained in seifim 17 and 18.
