The one witness supports the creditor as to the term, so the borrower cannot rely on a migo. Yet if the borrower denies the witness only regarding the date and says the term has already expired, it is difficult to identify the Torah oath. The witness does not then contradict the existence of the debt, only an evidentiary fact from which nonpayment would be inferred. Denial of a proof is not necessarily denial of money. The Torah oath of one witness ordinarily requires testimony regarding the monetary obligation itself. The case of a one witness document is different because denying the document can mean denying the obligation created by it. Here, however, disputing the date alone appears to be only disputing evidence. Therefore the Sema's formulation requires study.
In all these cases he is one obligated to swear but unable to swear. Shevuot derives the rule from the verse, "The oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them," excluding the heirs. This indicates a Torah law. Maharshal's responsum seems to describe the "therefore he pays" rule as rabbinic. The distinction appears to be this: where the defendant says, "Fifty I know and fifty I do not know," he is personally unable to take the Torah oath that would discharge him. Payment there follows by Torah law. A suspected swearer is different. He is physically capable of swearing, but the Sages refuse to administer an oath to him. His payment therefore does not arise from the Torah rule of inability to swear but from the rabbinic enactment that the opposing party swears and collects. This is supported by Bava Metzia. The Gemara rejects creating one enactment upon another in the case of a suspected defendant. If his payment were already Torah law and the only enactment were that the claimant takes an oath before collecting, there would not be two layers of rabbinic enactment in the same way. One could argue from Tosafot's first explanation that the claimant's oath was instituted for the suspected defendant's benefit, but the stronger conclusion follows what I wrote in siman 92: by Torah law even a person suspected concerning oaths can technically be given the oath, and his disqualification is itself rabbinic. Thus his payment is plainly rabbinic. The Torah rule of inability to swear applies where the person truly cannot supply the required oath, particularly because he says he does not know.
He claimed, "You snatched an object." Tosafot asks why the defendant who admits snatching but says, "I snatched my own," is not believed with an oath through a migo that he could have denied snatching. The answer can be sharpened. The migo exists only from the perspective of people who did not see the snatching. For the witness who actually saw it, there is no migo. To that witness the object came into the defendant's hand by seizure from another person's possession, and the ordinary presumption is that what a person holds belongs to him. Therefore, from the witness's perspective, the snatcher is like a robber unless he supplies an independent lawful explanation. If the witness himself later bought the seized object from the snatcher, he would have to return it to the original possessor because he knows directly how it entered the snatcher's hand. This resembles Tosafot's rule concerning a purchaser who knows that his seller occupied another's land for only one day. Even though the seller might have a migo in court, the purchaser who personally knows the adverse fact cannot rely on that migo. Here, because the defendant does not contradict the one witness, the witness is accepted as fully reliable against him until he takes the required oath. The court therefore accepts the witness's perspective that the object entered the defendant's hand through wrongful seizure. This explains the Gemara's phrase that he is "like a robber." His migo cannot restore him because the very witness whose testimony creates the oath saw circumstances in which no such migo exists.
