Whether he said, etc. If an heir says, “I do not know,” he is exempt. Only a litigant who should have known must pay when he cannot answer. An heir had no duty to know. This is the Tur's ruling, following Tosafot and the Rosh at the end of HaKones. See my comments in siman 72, seif 12, where I give another reason to distinguish an heir from other successors.
Even without the heirs' oath. This means without the oath that their father did not instruct them otherwise. When heirs make no definite claim and merely seek exemption, they are exempt without any oath, as explained at the end of siman 69 and in siman 71, seif 22.
He is like any other person who admits part, etc. The Gemara says, “What difference is there between him and his father?” The Ran explains that the claimant asserts certainty against the heir, for example, “Your father admitted the debt in your presence shortly before his death.” It cannot mean that the claimant does not allege that the heir knows, because then the heir would plainly be exempt as one returning a lost object, and no verse would be needed. The ordinary reason for requiring one who admits part to swear is that he cannot brazenly deny a creditor who knows the truth. The Ran similarly understands the Gemara's later case, where the heir says, “I know about fifty and do not know about fifty,” as one where the claimant alleges that the heir was present at the father's admission. The heir is exempt because heirs are not expected to retain such matters with precision. The Ran asks why heirs who say, “You are owed fifty and no more,” are not believed through a migo that they could have said, “We do not know.” He answers either that “we do not know” would still require an heirs' oath, or that a migo based on an assertion of ignorance is not a valid migo. The Ran's statement that where the claimant does not assert the heir's knowledge, an heir who admits fifty and denies fifty is plainly exempt as one returning a lost object, does not appear consistent with the Tur, Mechaber, and other authorities. They rule without qualification that an heir who says, “You are owed fifty and no more,” is like any other partial admitter. They do not require the remote circumstance that the creditor says the heir personally witnessed the father's admission. Moreover, in the opening clause they exempt the heir without an oath, imposing only a general ban. If the creditor asserted, “You personally knew because the matter occurred in your presence,” the heir should at least take a heset oath. The ordinary exemptions for heirs without an oath apply only where the creditor does not claim definite knowledge on the heir's part. It therefore appears that the Gemara asks in all cases, “What difference is there between him and his father?” Even without an allegation that the heir personally knew, once the heir definitively says, “You are owed fifty and no more,” he contradicts the claimant's definite assertion regarding the balance. He is not considered one returning a lost object. The reason an ordinary partial admitter is not so regarded is that his partial admission shows that he hesitates to deny everything. The same reasoning applies to the heir. One might have believed him through a migo that he could have said, “I do not know,” but a migo based on ignorance is not valid, as the Ran himself concludes. As to the case where the heir says, “I know about fifty and do not know about fifty,” I would have said, were I not hesitant, that the statutory exemption applies only where the creditor does not claim that the heir personally knows. If the creditor says, “This occurred in your presence,” the heir should be obligated to pay because he cannot swear. The Gemara's phrase “What difference is there between him and his father?” concerns a case where the heir makes a definite denial and is contradicted. In the case of uncertainty, the claimant is not saying that the heir's statement is false, and the Torah exempted heirs. Rashi, however, explains that the distinction is simply that the heir makes a definite claim yet cannot swear, and the authorities write without qualification that an heir who knows fifty and does not know fifty is never required to pay. They therefore follow the Ran. Even so, it appears clear that where the creditor asserts that the heir himself knows, the heir must at least take a heset oath. The verse exempts heirs from the Torah oath, but Rav Nachman's institution of heset applies when one person says with certainty, “You know that my money is in your possession.” This is explicit in siman 88, seif 20, where the Tur and Mechaber rule that an heir takes a heset oath concerning his father's debt when the claimant asserts with certainty that the heir knows of it.
Like any other person who admits part. As explained, because the heir makes a definite statement, the claimant necessarily tells him, “Your statement is false.” If the heir is astute, he should answer that he is uncertain, in which case he incurs no oath and only accepts a general ban. This does not violate the prohibition against distancing oneself from falsehood discussed at the beginning of this siman. The Baal HaTerumot cites Raavad that definite ancillary claims may be added through extension, but uncertain claims may not. Therefore, if one who is obligated to swear sees that ancillary matters are being added, he may prudently say, “I do not know,” regarding them and thereby avoid the stringent oath, accepting only a general ban. Raavad would not have advised this if it were forbidden.
