The heirs. Meaning the heirs' formula that their father did not instruct them otherwise. Where heirs do not make a definite claim and merely seek exemption, they are exempt without any oath, as stated at the end of siman 69 and in siman 71, seif 22. See also siman 72, seif 12, where another reason is given for distinguishing heirs. Shach.
Like other. The Shach cites the Ran that this refers to a plaintiff who definitely claims that the heir knows his father admitted the debt in his presence. Even then, if the heir says, "Regarding fifty I remember, and regarding the rest I do not know," he is exempt because he was not expected to commit every detail to memory. One might ask why, if "I do not know" exempts him, he should not be believed when he says, "You are owed fifty and no more," through a migo that he could have said he did not know. The Ran answers that if he had said he did not know he would still need to swear that he does not know, whereas in the father's own case such a response could lead to payment, while the heir is exempt with the oath. Alternatively, a migo of "I do not know" is not a valid migo. The Shach questions the Ran's suggestion that if the plaintiff does not definitely claim the father admitted before the heir, then an heir who responds definitively that only fifty is owed is plainly exempt as one returning a lost item. The Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and other authorities state without distinction that an heir who definitely says only fifty is owed is treated like any partial admitter. The Shach therefore concludes that at least a shevuat heset is required. Although a Torah oath can be excluded by the verse "between the two of them," understood as excluding heirs, Rav Nachman's institution of heset applies where the claimant says, "You yourself certainly know that my money is in your possession." The Shach later found this explicit in siman 88, seif 20, and in the Sema, Bach, and other later authorities.
Torah law. The Shach advises that a prudent heir may say he is uncertain and thereby avoid an oath, subject only to accepting a cherem. This does not violate the prohibition against falsehood. He proves it from Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Raavad concerning gilgul: one who gives definite responses may have claims rolled onto his oath, but if he says he does not know, the gilgul cannot be imposed. Therefore a person facing an oath may answer uncertainly regarding additional rolled claims and accept a cherem instead. Beit Yosef cites this at the end of siman 94, and the Shach reasons that the Raavad would not have advised conduct that was prohibited.
