There is no distinction. Responsa Mahara ibn Chayim proves from here that the same applies personally where one had no reason to know: if he says, 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' he is exempt. He applies this to cases of 'kim li' where the doubt is a dispute among authorities regarding whether a transaction can be retracted, arguing that it is not treated as an ordinary 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' because the litigant had no way to know. His words are correct according to Tosafot and Rosh, who derive in the law of one obligated to swear who cannot swear that where he had no reason to know, exemption is learned from the heirs' case of 'I do not know about fifty.' The reason is the same for heirs and for the litigant himself when knowledge was unavailable. According to Rambam and those who follow him, however, even one who had no reason to know may be obligated under 'one who must swear and cannot swear.' Their reason for exempting heirs must be that beit din advances on behalf of heirs every claim their father could have made. If so, the heirs' exemption teaches nothing about an ordinary litigant who says, 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' The heirs are exempt because beit din itself advances a definite defense on their behalf. See what I wrote in siman 108, seif katan 5, and siman 291.
