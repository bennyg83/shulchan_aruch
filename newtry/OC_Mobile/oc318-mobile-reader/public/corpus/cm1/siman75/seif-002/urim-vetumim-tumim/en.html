And some disagree. Shach correctly explains that this dissent applies only where the debtor has no land. If he owns land, everyone agrees the admitted written or kinyan-backed amount is treated as heilech or as secured by land. Still, the first view itself is difficult where the debtor owns no land. How can a denied debt be called heilech merely because there is a written instrument or kinyan? If there is no land from which collection is fixed and the debtor denies the obligation, movable property can be hidden or disposed of. Beit Yosef at the end of siman 88 says that even without land, existing movables can sometimes create heilech, but that case concerns an amount the debtor actually admits under the document. A denied amount is different. Rashi in Bava Metzia says even a written amount can be heilech, but Rashi's explanation there is that the debtor's fields are already liened. That rationale is absent when he owns no land. The first opinion comes from Mordechai, who in turn abbreviates the Shaarim of Rav Alfasi. Examining the Shaarim, I found that he repeatedly explains the rule through heilech and not through land liens. This may reflect the older Geonic position that land outside Eretz Yisrael is treated like movable property for certain oath laws. Even if such land does not receive the Torah exemption from oaths applicable to Eretz Yisrael land, it still cannot be hidden or moved. For heilech purposes it functions like fixed property. That would explain why the Shaarim consistently uses heilech language. For practical halachah, however, where there truly is no land or fixed property and the amount itself is denied, the dissenting view cited by Shach is stronger.
