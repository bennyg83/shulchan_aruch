He is obligated to take a Torah oath, etc. This is the case of one who admits part of a claim, regarding whom we say throughout the law that he must take a Torah oath. We derive this from the verse in the section concerning custodians, "that this is it," which is understood to mean that the custodian says: This item, meaning part of your claim, you gave into my hands to guard. The passage then concludes, "the oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them." The same rule applies when one admits part of a monetary loan claim. One who denies the entire claim, by contrast, was not required by the Torah to swear, because there is a presumption regarding ordinary people that a person will not brazenly deny everything in the claimant's presence. When he admits part, however, his partial admission leads us to say that he would really like to admit the entire claim, but he lacks the money to pay and thinks, "I will delay the lender until I have money, and then I will repay him." The Torah therefore imposes an oath upon him, because he will not swear falsely where he truly owes the money, since a false oath taken now cannot later be repaired. This is clear. See what I write further at the beginning of siman 87.
Some say that it exempts him from the oath, etc. The Tur states the reasons for the two opinions at the end of siman 46 concerning whether one witness can exempt a person from a Torah oath, and again at the end of siman 84 concerning whether one witness can exempt a defendant from an oath of hesset when he denies the entire claim. In both places the Tur concludes that the witness can exempt him, and the Rosh rules likewise. He derives it by an a fortiori argument: If one witness brought by the claimant can impose an oath upon the defendant who is in possession, then all the more so one witness brought by the defendant who is in possession can exempt him from the oath. Rama writes the same in Darkei Moshe to this siman and in Shulchan Aruch below, siman 87:6. See there.
