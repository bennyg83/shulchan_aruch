Specifically when beit din recognized it. Rambam cites Geonim who hold that if a defendant says, regarding part of the claim, "I seized it, but it was mine," and one witness says he seized it, while regarding the rest he denies seizure, the court's ruling obligating the first portion can create a Torah oath on the remainder. Rambam disagrees and requires only hesset. Ran, citing Ramban, appears closer to the Geonic view in cases where judicial determination establishes part of the obligation. Rambam's rule can be explained from the opening sugya of Bava Metzia. Rabbi Chiya's oath from partial testimony works where the claimant has witnesses to part and the defendant has no matching evidentiary basis. A judicial ruling that obligates one part because of uncertainty or legal rules is different. The same court may simultaneously be ruling that the defendant is exempt on the other part. The judgment itself therefore cannot be treated as independent witness testimony against him. Only actual witnesses, or evidence equivalent to witnesses in the substantive fact, create the Torah oath. This also explains why measuring later does not convert an initially unmeasured admission into testimony by beit din. An admission that emerged exempt remains exempt even if the judges later learn its amount. Thus Rambam's view is coherent: liability imposed by legal ruling is not itself "testimony of witnesses" for partial admission oath purposes.
Because he is not believed by his statement. Rashba rules that the Torah oath of partial admission applies only where the defendant explicitly admits that he presently owes part of the money. If he denies everything but becomes liable for part only through the legal consequences of his own claim, that is not the Torah's "he shall say, this is it." Thus if he says he paid part through a transaction that the law does not recognize as payment, or says he discharged part by paying prohibited fixed interest on behalf of the claimant, and beit din rules that this was not payment, the resulting liability does not turn him into one who admitted part. The Shach argues that where beit din knows with certainty that the alleged payment was legally ineffective, their ruling should be no weaker than witnesses who establish part of the debt. According to him, the judges effectively become witnesses through hearing the defendant admit the facts and determining that those facts do not constitute payment. In my view the Sema and Rashba are correct. Beit din knows the underlying facts only through the defendant's own mouth. One cannot combine his statement with the court's legal conclusion and transform the whole into "witness testimony" against him once his own admission, as framed, did not itself create a Torah partial admission. Ran proves a comparable principle in the law of an admission not stated in a measured amount. Even if beit din later measures and now knows precisely what he admitted, the initial admission already emerged exempt and is not retroactively converted into a Torah oath through the court's later knowledge. This also distinguishes cases where an independently trusted claimant, such as an injured person who has taken the required oath, establishes fifty of a larger claim. There the evidence supporting the first fifty is external to the defendant's own statement, and can resemble witness testimony. But where the entire foundation rests upon the defendant admitting all the facts and merely making a legal mistake, there is neither an explicit partial admission nor independent testimony. Therefore he does not incur the Torah oath on the remainder. This is the proper reading of Rashba and the Sema.
