Specifically when they recognized, etc.; but if he became liable, etc. The Sema in siman 87, seif katan 16, appears to distinguish the two clauses as follows. In the first, the defendant's own words show that according to the accounting he still owes a stated amount. In the second, he becomes liable only because he is not believed or because beit din imposes liability, such as where he says, “I repaid you through interest that I paid on your behalf,” and beit din rules that the lender need not credit that payment because it was fixed interest. In my opinion, if beit din establishes liability with certainty, even though the defendant admitted nothing expressly, he must take a Torah oath on the balance. It is inconceivable that a conclusive ruling of beit din should be weaker than testimony, for throughout the law beit din has greater force than witnesses, and the arguments by which the Gemara derives liability through witnesses from one's own admission apply all the more to a judicial determination. This is also indicated by the Rambam at the end of chapter 4 of Hilchot Gezelah. He cites Geonim who rule that if the defendant says concerning part of the property, “Yes, I seized it, but I seized my own,” and one witness testifies to the seizure, while concerning the remainder he says, “I did not seize it,” he must take a Torah oath, since beit din has required him to pay part. This ruling is cited by the Tur in siman 364, the Semag, Mordechai, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. Although the Rambam disagrees and requires only a heset oath, and the Mechaber rules accordingly in siman 364, seif 6, that is because beit din does not know with certainty that he owes the money. He still denies it, and liability follows only from the single witness and the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay. Where beit din knows with certainty that he remains liable because the alleged payment is legally ineffective, the Rambam would certainly agree that a judicial determination cannot be weaker than testimony. The Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia likewise treats the legal presumption created by possession as equivalent to witness testimony in Rabbi Chiyya's first ruling. The attempted proof from the Mishna fails only because the Gemara asks that the same presumption supports both holders. But absent that objection, the determination of beit din would stand like testimony because “we are witnesses” to the legal fact. I agree that where liability arises only because the defendant is not believed, it is not deemed a partial admission. Examples include cases listed by the Rambam: he claims one hundred, the defendant says regarding fifty, “You required payment before witnesses and I paid you”; or regarding fifty he says, “There was never a loan,” and regarding fifty, “I do not know whether you lent me,” while one witness testifies that fifty was lent; or he denies the entire hundred and one witness testifies to fifty, after which he says, “I borrowed and repaid.” In each such case he pays fifty because he cannot take the required oath, but he does not thereby incur a Torah oath on the remainder. Beit din does not know with certainty that he owes the money and merely rules against him because he cannot swear. It is therefore still possible that his statement is true. But where beit din knows with complete certainty that he owes fifty, for example because the borrower erred in law, a Torah oath is required on the balance. Thus, if Reuven claims one hundred from Shimon and Shimon says, “I paid fifty directly, and for the other fifty I paid interest on your behalf,” while beit din rules that Reuven may not accept that interest because it is fixed interest, Shimon must swear a Torah oath. Through his own statement beit din knows that the loan was one hundred and that only fifty was legally repaid. The judges are therefore witnesses to the remaining liability. The same applies where he says, “I paid fifty, and you owe me fifty because you became guarantor for another,” but beit din determines that the purported guarantee is ineffective, such as a guarantee undertaken after the money was given. Likewise, if he says, “I paid fifty by assigning you, in the presence of all three parties, to collect from another,” but beit din determines that the assignment was ineffective because he had no funds in the third party's hands at that time. In all such cases he must swear a Torah oath, because the legal ruling establishes the remaining debt with certainty. This is the plain meaning of the Baal HaTerumot, Mechaber, and Rama, who write that from the parties' arguments beit din recognized that twenty dinars remained owing from the two loans, and that this is like two witnesses. Had they meant that the defendant expressly admitted the balance, they could simply have said so. There would be no need to compare the judges to two witnesses, since an admission is effective whether made at the beginning or end of one's pleading. Their language, “the judges are witnesses to what he admitted,” means that he admitted the factual basis but mistakenly regarded an ineffective act as payment. Beit din, applying the law, knows that the debt remains. The responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 87, concerning a claim that the defendant paid interest on the claimant's behalf, can be explained differently. Reuven claimed one hundred from Shimon. Shimon denied owing anything and additionally claimed that Reuven owed him fifty for interest paid on Reuven's behalf. One witness testified that Shimon borrowed fifty, and Shimon answered that he had repaid that fifty, creating liability through the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Reuven admitted that Shimon had paid fifty of interest for him. Had Shimon's defense of repayment been accepted, he would have owed nothing. He became liable for fifty only through his inability to swear. The Rashba ruled that although the interest was fixed interest and therefore could not be deducted, this did not render Shimon one who admits part of the claim, since his liability did not arise from an admitted debt but from his denial combined with the single witness. Even if one understands the Rashba otherwise, the primary law follows the Baal HaTerumot. The Mechaber and Rama adopted his formulation here and did not cite the Rashba's contrary implication. This is also supported by the Ran in Shevuat HaDayanim, as I explain in siman 87, seif 5.
