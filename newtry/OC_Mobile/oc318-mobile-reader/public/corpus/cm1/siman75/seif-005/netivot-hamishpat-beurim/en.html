And specifically when beit din recognized it. See Shach, seif katan 19, who distinguishes as follows. Where the defendant claims, "I paid interest on your behalf," so that beit din determines with certainty that he became liable for part of the claim, he must swear. But where beit din imposes liability only because his own explanation is not believed, he is exempt from the Torah oath. Tumim adopts the opposite view based on Nimukei Yosef in Bava Metzia 3, who explains the Gemara's question why admission by one's own mouth should be stronger than testimony of witnesses. One might have thought that only partial admission requires an oath because the presumption that a person does not act brazenly does not apply, since he may merely be delaying payment. With testimony of witnesses, however, where he denied everything, one might have thought that he is exempt because of the presumption that a person does not act brazenly. The Torah therefore teaches that he nevertheless must swear, since the witnesses demonstrate that with respect to part of the claim he did in fact overcome that presumption and deny falsely. Accordingly, Tumim argues, where beit din recognizes that the defendant's partial admission results from having paid interest for the claimant, he has not been shown to depart from the presumption that a person does not act brazenly. In my humble opinion we do not rule in accordance with Nimukei Yosef on this point. We see that one who partially denies a deposit must swear even when the very item he denied is still before us, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 107a, s.v. Eruv Parshiyot. In such a case there is no possibility of temporary evasion, because the denied object is before us, yet he must swear. This demonstrates that a Torah oath can apply even where temporary evasion does not. There is, however, an apparent difficulty from Tosafot in Bava Metzia 3, s.v. BeKulei Ba'ei DeLodi. Tosafot explain that because a person ordinarily would not act brazenly, his partial denial should be accepted as true, and he should be believed by a migo that he could have denied everything. The Gemara answers that partial denial is not so brazen because he may merely be postponing payment. Why then, in the case of partial denial of a deposit where the denied item is before us and temporary evasion seemingly does not apply, must he swear? One must answer that with a deposit, when the item itself is intact he has no monetary liability because returning the item is heilach. Monetary liability arises only when he admits that it was lost through his negligence. Even where the very item he denied is before us, temporary evasion can still apply, because he may need to deny that item so that he will have funds available to pay the amount he admits. If one asks why he does not admit the item that is before us and deny the other item instead, the answer is that if he admitted the item before us, his denial of everything else would become a complete denial and would be brazen. According to this, that formulation indeed accords with Nimukei Yosef. But according to the poskim whose unqualified language indicates that even if the denied item is before us and he presently wishes to pay the amount he admits, he nevertheless must swear, it follows that a Torah oath can apply even where the presumption against brazenness remains fully relevant. They explain the Gemara's phrase "he would prefer to admit all of it" in accordance with Rashi. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who seeks to reject the Shach's proof from the Gemara's phrase "we are witnesses." Ketzot writes that according to the Gemara's conclusion, since we are witnesses for this party just as we are witnesses for the other, so too a judgment of beit din is equivalent to witnesses for both sides. This is incorrect. There are two types of chazakah. One type clarifies the factual truth, like the bodily chazakah of a betulah and other evidentiary presumptions derived from Scripture. A chazakah of monetary possession, however, does not clarify the truth and is not derived from Scripture, but rests on logic, as Tosafot explain in the chapter HaParah and as Terumat HaDeshen discusses, and as Ketzot himself writes at the end of his work, rule 1. The cases are therefore not comparable. With respect to the portion he must pay, the Torah believed one witness like two where the defendant cannot swear, and it is therefore as though we have witnesses. The same is true where there is an evidentiary chazakah that clarifies the truth. But regarding the remainder from which he is exempt only because of monetary possession, it is not as though we have witnesses, since that chazakah does not clarify factual truth. In the Gemara, by contrast, the two presumptions are of equal character. Ketzot further writes, in attempting to answer Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia 3, that according to this the Gemara did not apply the oath to the first portion because what he physically holds is considered an admission. His words there have no substance. In my humble opinion Tosafot's difficulty can be answered from the Shach in siman 87, who cites Ran that if one claims fifty for a loan and fifty for an injury, then if the injured party has already sworn he is treated as having partially admitted the claim, while if he has not yet sworn there is no partial admission. Tosafot hold that with respect to the portion of a garment reached by his hand he takes without an oath, while for the remainder he takes only with an oath. Accordingly, the portion within his grasp is treated as an admission because the other party takes it without an oath, and this is analogous to a Torah case, as the Gemara concludes. The remaining portion, however, is not a partial admission because the other party still requires an oath and has not yet sworn; it therefore lacks a Torah analogue. At the Gemara's initial stage, when it sought to place the oath on the first portion and assumed that heilach is exempt, it could not classify the matter as partial admission because of the piece he was holding. It therefore had to rely on the remainder that he was not holding, which was established not by his admission but by testimony. One could then have thought that this was not heilach because he denied it, just as even a denial of an amount stated in a note is not treated as heilach according to the Shach. The Gemara therefore asks, "Just as we are witnesses for this one, etc." But according to the conclusion that the Torah oath applies and that heilach itself obligates an oath, he is considered to have partially admitted by virtue of the portion actually held by the other party, for which that other party needs no oath. Although the Gemara sets up the case where each holds only the tassels, it could equally have set it up where they hold equal portions, as Torat Chaim writes. Ketzot further seeks to reject the Shach's view that when the defendant says, "I paid interest for you," this does not create partial admission. He cites the mishnah's rule, "What you left, you take," where even if witnesses later testify to the measure, their testimony does not create partial admission because their testimony derives from his own initial admission, and that initial admission was made in a context of exemption. He argues that our case should be the same. The comparison is wholly inapt. Here, at the moment of denial, beit din already knows both his admission and his explanation. If one is mistaken in arithmetic or in law, it is as though he said, "I gave you these coins, which equal the amount you claim," while beit din sees that they are worth less. So too here, beit din sees that what he calls payment was not legally payment. Ketzot's explanation of "What you left, you take," that the testimony derives from his own admission, is likewise incorrect. Even if witnesses came after his admission and testified to a measurement they had independently known beforehand, he would still be exempt. The reason is that witness testimony in this context is learned from admission by one's own mouth. Since if he himself later admitted the true measurement he would remain exempt, because his first admission had already placed him in a posture of exemption, the later testimony of witnesses cannot impose an oath more strongly than his own admission could. But where from the outset he denied everything, and a later admission by his own mouth would obligate him to swear, later testimony of witnesses likewise obligates him.
