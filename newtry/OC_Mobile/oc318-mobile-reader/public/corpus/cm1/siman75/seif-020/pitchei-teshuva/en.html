In Atarat Tzvi, in the responsa printed at the end of the work, an incident is recorded in the community of Olyka. Reuven and Shimon had adjoining shops separated by a plank wall. Money and merchandise were stolen from Reuven. He examined the wall and found one plank displaced, and he also found identifying signs in merchandise associated with Shimon. Several days later Reuven seized from Shimon property equal to his loss. The author ruled in accordance with the Sema regarding a broken-open chest, that if the owner of the chest seized property from Shimon, his seizure stands. He reasoned that this case was even stronger, because there were identifying signs in Reuven’s merchandise and other compelling circumstances showing that no other thief had entered the shop, since the doors were found locked. Scholars later objected that the Shach disagrees with the Sema. Initially he answered that since Reuven had already seized, he could say, “I rely upon the Sema.” He then reexamined the matter and wrote that the Rosh himself appears to support the Sema, and afterward found a similar ruling in Knesset HaGedolah, citing Responsa Pleitat Beit Yosef, likewise in accordance with the Sema. In my view, his citation of the Rosh adds little, because the Sema himself already cited those words of the Rosh, and nevertheless the Shach and Taz disagreed and explained the Rosh differently. His initial statement that Reuven could rely upon the Sema because he had seized is also difficult, since the very question whether seizure helps is itself disputed. The Mishneh LaMelech writes that where the effectiveness of the seizure itself is the subject of dispute, one cannot say “I rely upon” one side. I cited this above in siman 28. Moreover, Responsa Chavot Yair, cited above in siman 25, states that where the Sema and Shach disagree, one generally rules like the Shach, even to extract money, unless the judges discern a sound reason why the Shach departed from the correct path. Netivot HaMishpat likewise states this principle. On further consideration, however, the practical conclusion of Atarat Tzvi is correct, because the Mishneh LaMelech’s rule is not universally accepted. The Shach in Tekfo Kohen cites Maharit to that effect and disagrees with him, bringing many proofs that seizure can be effective even where the very right of seizure is disputed. Tumim agrees, and Netivot HaMishpat also appears to adopt this view. In addition, Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part II, siman 168, disagrees with the Shach in our specific question and rules like the Sema. He explains that the Sema cannot mean a merely doubtful claim based upon suspicious circumstances, because the Sema had just written that Shimon cannot reverse the oath onto Reuven since Reuven did not actually see the theft. If the claimant only has doubt, how could seizure help when he himself would then need to swear? Rather, the Sema distinguishes between a claimant who says “perhaps” on the basis of suspicious circumstances, in which case the defendant swears and is exempt, and a claimant who says, “I am certain, on the basis of these circumstances,” and is prepared to swear to that certainty. In the latter case he may seize. This, he says, is the intent of the Rosh in Elu Metziot when he says, “if it is clear to him,” and the same distinction is found in Tosafot. He therefore ruled in practice that where the claimant is genuinely certain, on the basis of clear circumstances, even though he did not see the act, he may seize and retain what he seized through an oath. The court should, however, warn him severely not to swear unless the matter is absolutely clear to him, without any residual doubt. Tumim also agrees with the Sema that seizure can help in such a case and brings proof from the Rosh in the chapter HaMafkid. He distinguishes, however, between one who seizes and does not disclose to beit din why he seized, merely claiming that he has a counterclaim, and one who tells beit din that he seized because it is highly probable that the other stole from him. In the first case, since in his own mind the debt is certain, he may retain possession. In the second, once he explains the basis to beit din, the court must order him to return the property, because a court rules only upon a legally established certainty. He himself may be compelled by his own conviction, but the court cannot issue judgment on that basis. Responsa Brit Avraham questions this distinction. The defendant is generally required to clarify his claim. Further, if the possessor merely says, “I have a counterclaim against you,” the court will impose an oath upon him, and how can he swear? According to the Sema, seizure can help where there are grounds for suspicion, and the oath would concern those factual grounds. According to Tumim, however, if he explains that he seized because of a broken-open chest and suspicious circumstances, the court will rule that seizure is ineffective, while if he gives only a general claim he cannot honestly swear that he knows with certainty that the other owes him. I believe Tumim can perhaps be defended in light of the law cited above in siman 72 seif 17. Brit Avraham further discusses a case where an object was stolen from a house and only a male servant and a female servant had access. The male servant had no reputation as a thief, while the female servant had such a reputation. The householder wished to wait until the end of her term and withhold her wages. The questioner thought he could rely upon the Sema concerning a broken-open chest, especially since Tumim agreed that seizure helps. Brit Avraham responded that this is not the law. The Shach indicates that even for imposing an oath, the case of a broken-open chest applies only where there was no other possible person. If another person had access, it is not the same case, even where the owner personally does not suspect that other person. Rashi in Bava Metzia 26b likewise indicates that where another partner could have taken the item, one does not have a definite claim against the particular person suspected. Tosafot does not contradict this. Thus here, the fact that the owner does not suspect the male servant does not make his accusation against the female servant legally definite. Indeed, the fact that she has a prior reputation as a thief adds no legal weight. A responsum of a Gaon cited by the Tur in siman 348 states that even one proven to have committed other thefts acquires no special monetary disability in a new case merely by reason of that reputation. The court may impose extraordinary measures as it deems appropriate, but ordinary seizure or oath does not follow automatically. He therefore concludes that seizure does not help in this case, though whether an oath may be imposed depends upon the court’s assessment.
