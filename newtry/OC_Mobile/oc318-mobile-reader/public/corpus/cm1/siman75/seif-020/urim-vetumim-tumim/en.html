He reads the phrase "You do not know and he knows" as meaning that where both parties are uncertain, the defendant is exempt even toward Heaven. I do not think that is the correct reading. The straightforward meaning is the opposite: "You do not know, but he knows." The claimant's definite knowledge can prevail over the defendant's doubt. This accords with Rav Huna's view that a definite claim is stronger than a doubtful one. A parallel Yerushalmi in Shevuot confirms this. It discusses the collateral Mishnah where the borrower says the pledge was worth more and the lender says he does not know. Rav Huna says, "You do not know; he knows," meaning the definite claimant is credited. The continuation distinguishes where an actual oath obligation exists, which is exactly the framework of the Bavli's unable-to-swear rule. This cannot be reconciled with Shach's reading. Thus the Yerushalmi is not evidence that two doubtful parties are completely exempt. It is discussing the force of a definite claim against doubt. The earlier rule concerning both sides being uncertain therefore remains as above: no judicial extraction, but a possible duty toward Heaven where a definite debt once existed and repayment is doubtful.
