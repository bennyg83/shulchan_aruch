Worth a perutah. Taz was asked about a debtor who paid a sum of money, and only afterward the creditor returned one coin claiming, "This coin was among your payment and is counterfeit." The debtor answered, "I do not believe it came from my payment; our account was already settled." Taz rules that because the payment was completed and the parties separated, the debtor is now like one who says, "I do not know whether I owe you," and is exempt. Shach elsewhere cites Maharshal and Maharam di Lonzano in a similar sale case and treats it as "I do not know whether I repaid," which would create liability. The issue requires careful distinction. Rambam's rule about returning a deficient coin gives substantial support to Taz. If the recipient recognizes the very coin, he can return it even after a long time. If he does not recognize it, he cannot simply demand a replacement. The Mishnah itself concerns only cases where the deficient coin can still circulate with difficulty and therefore imposes only a measure of piety after the ordinary time. But Rambam's formulation extends the recognition requirement even where the coin is entirely unusable. This shows that once an exchange or payment has been completed, the recipient cannot reopen the transaction merely by producing an unrecognized bad coin. Still, one must distinguish sale from repayment of an antecedent debt. In a sale, the transfer of goods and money occurs as one exchange. No prior monetary debt necessarily existed. If afterward one side says a coin was counterfeit, the issue can be framed as uncertainty whether a new liability ever arose. A preexisting loan is different. The debt was certainly owed, and the only question is whether the payment fully discharged it. That looks like classic uncertainty about repayment.
It appears, however, that the deeper question is whether the rule "I do not know whether I repaid" applies even where the debtor was not expected to know of the defect. Radbaz and Mishneh LaMelech indicate that where the debtor could not reasonably know, the strict repayment-uncertainty rule may not apply. Baal HaTerumot likewise exempts an heir who says he does not know whether his father repaid, explaining that the heir was not expected to know. Other authorities, including Ramban, treat definite claim versus doubt more broadly and do not tie the rule entirely to negligence or failure to remember. There are proofs in both directions from the sugyot of hired workers, ketubah, collateral, and cases of uncertain accident. The practical distinction should therefore be as follows. If the payer handled the money carelessly, failed to inspect it when he reasonably could, or simply passed on coins received from someone else without checking, then he should have known. If the creditor later identifies a counterfeit coin and the payer cannot contradict him, the case is treated as doubtful repayment and the payer must make good the loss. If, however, the payer examined the coins reasonably and the counterfeit was skillfully disguised in a way an ordinary person could not detect, he was not expected to know. In that case the possessor can rely on the authorities who exempt even in repayment uncertainty where knowledge was impossible.
I later found clear support for exemption in Rashba at the end of the first chapter of Gittin. Workers made an accounting with a landowner and concluded that five istira remained due to him. Later the landowner admitted there had been an accounting mistake. Rashba says the workers are not thereby forced to repay merely because he now says the prior settlement was wrong. Even if they themselves no longer know whether the calculation was mistaken, they are exempt. The prior account was treated as settled, and the present uncertainty is like "I do not know whether I now owe you," not "I do not know whether I repaid." Once a payment or settlement has been completed and the parties separated, a later allegation that the payment was defective can therefore be treated as a new doubtful claim rather than reopening the old certain debt. This closely resembles the counterfeit-coin case. Accordingly, where the payer made a reasonable inspection and the alleged defect was not discoverable, he is exempt. Where he failed to inspect when he should have, liability remains stronger. This is a major general principle in monetary law.
