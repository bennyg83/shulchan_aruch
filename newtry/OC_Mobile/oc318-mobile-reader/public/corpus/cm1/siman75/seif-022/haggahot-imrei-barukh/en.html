(Shach, seif katan 58) However, one can question him, for he should be believed by a migo. Note: One can reject this. It is not a valid migo because he fears that the claimant may concentrate and remember, then sue him with certainty, and he would have to take a shevuat heset. If we were to exempt the defendant from a shevuat heset by his claim of payment, that exemption would remain even after the claimant later remembered. Indeed, through the defendant's answer that he repaid, the claimant may remember the loan and his claim may become certain. If the defendant had instead answered that he never borrowed, and afterward the claimant said that he now remembered with certainty that he had lent him the money, the defendant would surely have to swear a shevuat heset. Nor can we say that the defendant should be exempt by a migo only where the claimant does not say that he remembered, for the claimant himself has a migo that he could say, "I remembered." The Rif, at the end of chapter Kol HaNishba'in, discusses a person who said to another: "I have one witness that you came to my house in my absence, opened it, and took a load of cotton." The defendant answered: "Yes, I opened it and took it, but I took what was mine, and you told me to take it on account of what you owed me." The Rif rules that he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot, and therefore must pay. The Rosh, Ran, Rashba, and Ritva infer from this that the Rif holds that one may impose an oath on a doubtful claim supported by one witness. At first glance this is difficult according to the Mabit, for here the claimant should have a certain claim by virtue of the defendant's own admission that he took the cotton, while the defendant's assertion that it was with permission is certainly denied by the claimant. One cannot answer that if the law were that no oath is imposed on a doubtful claim supported by one witness, the defendant would be exempt by a migo that he could have denied taking the cotton, for according to those who do not accept a migo to exempt from an oath, how would this prove the Rif's position? Moreover, according to Tosafot in the sugya of the seized ingot, where a migo is not accepted if it would force the defendant to swear concerning a matter the witness knows to be false, here too there may be no migo at all. Even on a certain claim supported by one witness presently before us there is at least a shevuat heset. Thus the defendant may have admitted taking the cotton so as not to be forced to swear a shevuat heset in contradiction of the witness. Once there is no migo, even if its absence results only from a rabbinic oath, the law returns to the ordinary Torah obligation generated by the witness and the certain element of the claim created by the defendant's admission, and we can apply the rule that one obligated to swear but unable must pay. See Ramban's novellae to Bava Batra 33, who writes that according to the students of our teacher, a one-witness oath applies only where the claimant asserts certainty. This wording suggests that he was not certain that the Rif disagrees with those students. This accords with the foregoing, because the inference from the Rif is not decisive. Particularly, Ri Migash, a student of the Rif, rules that an oath is not imposed on a doubtful claim even with one witness, and he also holds that a migo does not exempt from an oath, as I wrote in my novellae on the sugya of wage fixing. The Rashba's novellae there report that Ri Migash brought proof from the case of one who is obligated to swear against one witness and cannot be exempted even where he has a migo, and the Rashba rejects that proof based on the special force of one witness, like the Rivam in Tosafot to Bava Batra.
