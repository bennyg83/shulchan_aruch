He is like any other partial admitter. Ran infers from the verse excluding heirs from the unable-to-swear rule that the claimant must be making a definite claim against the heirs. Otherwise no verse would be needed. If the claimant himself did not know whether the heirs knew of the debt, their statement, "We know about fifty and do not know about fifty," would not create an ordinary partial-admission oath at all. They could have simply said they knew nothing. Shach objects that the poskim do not state this limitation and that they exempt the heirs even from hesset. If the claimant makes a definite claim that they know, why no hesset? The proper case is more precise. The claimant says definitely: "Your father instructed you to pay me the full hundred." The heirs admit he instructed them concerning fifty but say they do not know whether he instructed them about the other fifty. If they denied the second fifty definitely, this would be an ordinary partial admission. If they say they do not know, one might have applied the unable-to-swear rule. The Torah therefore excludes heirs. Why is there no hesset on their uncertainty? Because the claimant does not say, "I personally know you heard a separate instruction about this exact fifty." He knows the father acknowledged the hundred and believes the father instructed them accordingly, but the precise extent of the instruction remains uncertain. The heirs are not expected to know all their father's affairs, and Chazal did not impose hesset on such inherited uncertainty. This also avoids Shach's concern that "I do not know" is generally a weak migo. For ordinary parties, ignorance about one's own debt looks suspicious. For heirs, Chazal themselves say that even an adult is like a minor regarding his father's affairs. Their ignorance is natural. Some uncertainty remains where the claimant truly says, "I was present and heard your father instruct you personally about all one hundred." In that case the heirs look much more like direct litigants. Yet even there, the poskim do not clearly impose the unable-to-swear doctrine on heirs. The verse "between the two of them, but not between heirs" may exempt them broadly. For practical halachah, therefore, one should not extract from heirs through the unable-to-swear rule merely because the claimant asserts they should know.
