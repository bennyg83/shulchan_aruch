Only with a Torah oath. Ramah says that the rule of inability to swear and therefore pay applies only where the one witness would itself create a Torah oath. If two witnesses would obligate the defendant only after the claimant first swore, one witness does not create the ordinary Torah oath. This explains the difference between an employee and a shopkeeper. If an employer says, "I never hired you," and one witness says he did hire him, the employer must swear against the witness. Had two witnesses proved the hiring, the employee could collect without the employee oath once the employer's total denial also implied nonpayment, under the principle that one who says he never borrowed is as one who says he never repaid. Therefore the one witness directly creates a Torah oath. The shopkeeper's case is different. Even if two witnesses prove that the employer instructed the shopkeeper to pay the workers, the shopkeeper still must swear that he actually paid them before collecting. The witnesses do not by themselves create an immediately collectible monetary obligation. Consequently one witness concerning the employer's instruction does not fall within "where two obligate money, one obligates an oath." The same principle applies to collateral claims. One must examine the alternative claim by which the defendant could have contradicted the witness. If that alternative itself would require a Torah oath, then a migo cannot free him from the witness. If even two witnesses would leave the claimant needing an oath or some further proof, the one witness may not generate the full rule of inability to swear. Several sugyot turn on whether we follow the original alternative claim or the defendant's present claim. Tosafot gives two explanations in the piece of metal case. According to the first, the migo fails because it would require the audacity of swearing against the witness. According to the second, a migo can exist but the Torah nonetheless says that one who cannot swear against the witness pays. Under the first explanation we look strongly to the alternative plea; under the second, the present legal posture is decisive. This distinction has practical consequences in one witness documents and collateral cases. The governing rule is that inability to swear produces payment only when the one witness by itself creates the Torah oath, not when additional claimant proof or claimant oath would still be necessary.
