Only with a Torah oath. The rule that one who cannot take an obligatory oath must pay does not apply to rabbinic oaths. Examples include an admission of part where the denial is less than two silver ma'ot, a claim not involving a defined measure or weight, an admission not of the same kind as the claim, or a case of “here it is.” The same applies to matters concerning which no Torah oath is taken, such as servants, documents, and land. The Tur, citing Ramah, adds that regarding the oath imposed by one witness, if two witnesses would not by themselves impose monetary liability without the claimant's oath, or if there is one witness, the defendant is not considered obligated to swear. The wording indicates that this final rule is broader than merely saying that the defendant does not pay when unable to swear. It teaches that no oath based on one witness arises at all where, even if two testified, the claimant could collect only after swearing. An oath is imposed only where, had the defendant admitted the fact, he would owe money outright. If even an admission would leave the claimant required to swear, denial of that fact is not a denial of money. The Ran states this in the name of Raavad concerning an employer who told a shopkeeper to give a sela to his workers. The shopkeeper says he gave it, and the workers say they received nothing. If the employer denies having appointed the shopkeeper, the employer takes no oath, not even heset, because there is no monetary denial. Even if he admitted appointing him, the shopkeeper could collect only upon oath. Such a denial is not a denial of money. The same applies if one witness says the employer instructed the shopkeeper to pay. If the employer says, “I do not know,” he is not one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear. This does not conflict with the Tur's ruling in siman 89 that when one witness testifies that an employee was hired and the employer claims payment, the employer takes a stringent oath. There the employer denies money, because if he admitted nonpayment he would owe without the employee's oath. Even without the witness he would at least take a heset oath. The witness elevates that oath to a stringent oath resembling a Torah oath. It is not literally a Torah oath, because one witness never imposes a Torah oath where the claimant himself could collect only upon oath. The Maggid Mishneh explains in the name of Ramah that the employer's oath is elevated because his usual migo, that he could have denied hiring, is defeated by the witness. Thus when he says, “I hired you and paid,” he takes a stringent rabbinic oath. But no oath arises where an admission would still not create monetary liability without the claimant's oath.
But if under Torah law, etc. The Sema cites the Tur in the name of Ramah and reads the final phrase as, “Regarding an oath imposed by one witness, where two witnesses would obligate him only through the claimant's oath, if there is one witness he is not obligated to swear.” This is also the Bach's text. On that reading the wording is repetitive, because the sentence already began with an oath imposed by one witness. The correct reading found in the other editions is “or there is one witness.” The later editors unnecessarily changed “or” to “if.” The meaning is that the rule of payment for inability to swear applies in a one-witness case only where two witnesses would directly impose monetary liability without assistance. But where even two witnesses would not impose payment until the claimant swears, as with a hired worker who collects only upon oath despite witnesses to the hiring, or where there is a single witness supporting the claimant so that he collects without an oath, the defendant who says, “I do not know,” is not treated as one who cannot swear and must pay. This reading also indicates that Ramah holds that a supporting witness exempts the claimant from oath. The Beit Yosef's contrary inference is not compelling. In any event, the dispute between Ramah and Rabbi Matityahu does not necessarily depend on whether a supporting witness exempts, as I explain in siman 46. The same analysis applies to the robbed party in siman 90 and to the shopkeeper discussed in the preceding seif katan. Since the claimant would ordinarily collect only upon his own oath, the employer's uncertainty combined with one witness does not create payment through inability to swear.
