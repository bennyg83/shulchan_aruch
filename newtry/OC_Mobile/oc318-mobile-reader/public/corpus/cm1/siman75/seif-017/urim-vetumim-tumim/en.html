Where a person has a doubtful monetary claim against another, he may sometimes retain money already in his possession, because his own conscience tells him the debt is genuine even though beit din cannot adjudicate on uncertainty alone. This explains cases such as the coin that fell from two people. The possessor may say that in his own mind he is certain the other party owes him, even though the court lacks proof. However, the distinction is critical. If he merely tells beit din, "I have a counterclaim," without explaining its doubtful basis, his possession can protect him because from his own perspective he is certain. But if he explains to beit din that his entire basis is a known doubtful claim, such as the uncertain fallen coin, beit din must order return. A court can rule only on established claims, not on a person's private conviction. There is strong proof from Rosh. Rosh compares the doubtful fallen coin to the butcher-broker case, where the broker swears he did not know of the animal's defect and then the butcher must pay the reduced meat value. Yet the butcher is already holding his own money before the dispute arises. If doubtful possession automatically helped even after the basis was disclosed to beit din, the butcher should simply retain his money and say, "I am certain you knew of the defect; I do not care about your oath." Rosh does not allow that. The necessary distinction is that once the basis of the retention is openly before beit din and is itself only a doubtful inference, beit din cannot authorize seizure on that basis. Private possession without judicial adjudication is different because the holder acts according to his own asserted certainty. This distinction is correct and resolves the sources.
