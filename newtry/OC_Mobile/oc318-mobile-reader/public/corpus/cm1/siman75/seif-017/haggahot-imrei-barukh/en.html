(Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 9) It is difficult, for if so, with a document signed by two witnesses, etc. Note: This difficulty was already raised by Penei Yehoshua and Hafla'ah, particularly from the Gemara in Ketubot at the end of chapter 2 on the Mishnah, 'These are believed to testify in adulthood,' where the Gemara states, 'provided that an adult is with him.' This shows that authentication does not become effective through a single witness. Independently, it is impossible to say that the Shach thought a document could be authenticated through one witness. In my opinion, the Shach means only that with mamranot in the debtor's possession, authentication by one witness is effective, and the debtor is not believed to say 'I paid' through a migo that he could have claimed forgery, because he cannot brazenly contradict a witness who recognizes his handwriting and knows with certainty that his claim of forgery is false. But with a note signed by two witnesses, the debtor can say, 'I know with certainty that I never borrowed anything from this person, and therefore I necessarily know that this document is forged.' The statement of a single witness who recognizes the signatures does not obligate him to believe the witness and pay a debt he knows he never incurred. Such a claim does not display brazenness toward the witness, for he does not positively contradict the witness; he merely does not accept the witness's statement. His principal certainty is that he never borrowed, and therefore the claimant must have forged the note, whether by forging the signatures or by finding false witnesses. If the document is authenticated by two witnesses, he would have to pay even though he himself knows he owes nothing. But so long as authentication rests on only one witness, he is not required to pay what he knows with certainty he does not owe, and such a claim is not considered brazen toward the witness.
