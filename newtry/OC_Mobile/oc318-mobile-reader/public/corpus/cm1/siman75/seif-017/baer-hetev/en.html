Doubt. In the name of the drone, if this prosecutor does not know how much harm he has harmed is not satisfied with this lawsuit, and he has nothing but a boycott of anyone who knows that he has harmed his death and how much he is. A:
It seems to me. If one says, 'It seems to me that you owe me one hundred,' and the other replies, 'I repaid you,' while the claimant says, 'I know with certainty that you did not repay me,' Responsa Mabit, part 1, siman 263, rules that the defendant must take a hesset oath. This requires examination, for the defendant should seemingly be believed through a migo that he could have said, 'I owe you nothing,' in which case he would be exempt, as I wrote in siman 66, seif 39, in the name of Ritva citing some of his teachers, who exempt such a case even from hesset. It is not comparable to the end of siman 88, because there the claimant has now remembered with certainty that the debt exists, whereas here he has not. See there carefully. Shach.
Since. This refers to what immediately precedes it, namely that for this reason he need not take even a hesset oath. It does not refer to the statement that he need not pay in order to satisfy Heaven, because even without this reason he has no such obligation when the defendant too makes a definite claim. So write the Sema and Bach. This is unlike Responsa Maharit, siman 151. See there. Shach.
Jesting. Gidulei Terumah asks that a claim of 'I was merely jesting with you' applies only where one was first sued and then admitted. There is no difficulty, for the case can be where the claimant initially said, 'You have one hundred of mine,' even though he himself was not certain, and the defendant admitted it. The Bach explains likewise. In Sefer HaTerumot itself the matter is independently resolved, because it holds that the defense of jesting applies even where one admits on his own, as stated in its name in siman 81, seif 5. There remains a question, however, because beit din does not assert the defense of jesting for a defendant unless he himself raises it. It appears that this requirement applies only where the claimant asserts with certainty that he lent the money. Here the claim itself is doubtful, and beit din does not impose an oath on a doubtful claim. We therefore say that the defendant may assert that he was jesting. This appears to me the correct practical ruling. Shach.
You admitted. The Sema derives from the Mechaber's extended wording, where he could simply have written, 'But if he claims that he said, You are my witnesses,' and from several changes in wording, that the first clause concerns an admission that the defendant took property belonging to the claimant. The novelty is that although he cannot say that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy, because the admission followed the claimant's demand, it still is not considered a definite claim. The latter clause teaches that although the defendant admitted on his own, where the defense that he admitted merely so as not to appear wealthy would ordinarily apply, and is such a strong defense that beit din asserts it for him, once he said, 'You are my witnesses,' the claim is treated as definite. The Shach challenges this. According to the Sema, one must force the Mechaber's unqualified wording to disagree with Rambam, cited in siman 81, seif 14, who holds that where one admits before the claimant he cannot say that he did so merely to avoid appearing wealthy even without saying, 'You are my witnesses.' Minor redundancies in wording do not justify departing from the plain meaning. Rather, the entire passage concerns the defense of jesting, and this law is according to all opinions, because to defeat the defense of jesting one must say, 'You are my witnesses,' even when the admission was made before the claimant. Shach.
Grounds. Likewise, if an item fell from someone and only one other person was present, the owner may require that person to take a hesset oath, because this is treated like a definite claim since no one else was there. This appears from the Rosh in Bava Metzia, chapter Elu Metziot, concerning one who saw a coin fall from one of two people. Mordechai's statement that Rashi agrees is not proved by Rashi's words. Rashi only means that the loser does not despair because he thinks he can make the other person swear. In truth the law may not be so. Rambam writes similarly that one does not impose a hesset oath on a doubtful claim, such as: 'A certain item was stolen from me, and no one was there but you, so in my eyes you are likely responsible,' when the defendant says, 'I have nothing of yours.' He is exempt even from hesset, and the same applies to similar cases. It appears that such matters depend upon the judge's assessment. If there are grounds indicating the claim is likely true, he may impose a hesset oath. In any event, Maharik, cited by the Rama in siman 15, seif 4, writes that a judge may impose an oath even where the strict law does not require it when he does so to clarify the truth. Shach.
Hesset oath. Shimon cannot shift the oath onto Reuven by requiring Reuven to swear that he knows Shimon took a certain amount from his box and then collect it, because Reuven did not see the taking with his own eyes. This resembles what the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write in siman 92, that where the claimant is disqualified from taking an oath, the defendant cannot shift his hesset oath onto him, and likewise a person who owes an oath to a minor. Nevertheless, Shimon is exempt with a hesset oath. This is unlike the rule in siman 357 concerning people found inside another's house, because there are much stronger suspicious circumstances there. The Sema adds that if the owner of the box seized Shimon's property without witnesses, the seizure is effective. He cites as proof Bava Metzia and the Tur in siman 262 regarding an item seen falling from one of two people, where we say that the loser certainly did not despair. The Rosh writes that if the loser seizes the other's property, the other cannot recover it even through a hesset oath. The Shach rejects this: how can seizure transfer money on the basis of doubt? In siman 262 we mean only that the loser thinks he may later seize the other person's property and assert a definite claim, causing the finder to admit. Certainly, seizure based on a doubtful claim is ineffective. Responsa Maharashach, part 2, siman 2, likewise writes that even according to Raavad, who requires an oath where one claims, 'My father told me,' as discussed below in seif 21, seizure is ineffective even without witnesses so as to allow the claimant merely to swear that his father told him. The Shach brings further proof from the end of siman 134 concerning a purchaser or heir of a craftsman who claims that the craftsman purchased the item in his presence. Mordechai compares an open box to the claim 'My father told me.' The Taz likewise challenges the Sema. He also cites Mordechai's report of a question to Maharam concerning one who bought land from another and holds a deed, where within three years the seller claimed, 'You sold it back to me.' From this the Taz derives that hesset was not instituted upon a claim alleging something that is highly unusual. See there.
