Every claim based on uncertainty, etc. The Beit Yosef cites Ritva: if a claimant does not know how to assess the amount of damage done to him, we do not impose an oath upon the denial of such an unquantified claim. The defendant is subject only to a general ban upon anyone who knows that he damaged his fellow and knows the amount.Even to fulfill one's duty toward Heaven, etc.
The Tur likewise writes this. What is stated, 'since the claimant says perhaps,' explains why no oath, even hesset, is required. It does not explain the exemption from a heavenly obligation, because that follows independently where the defendant himself makes a definite denial.But if he claims against his fellow, 'You yourself admitted to me that you owe me, etc.' The Tur uses the same wording.
The lengthy formulation teaches several distinctions. In the first case the defendant admitted only after being confronted by the claimant, who said, 'Did you not take mine?' and he answered, 'I did.' Although he cannot then say that he admitted merely in order not to appear wealthy, because the admission followed a direct claim, it is nevertheless not treated as a definite claim against him. In the latter case he admitted spontaneously, where ordinarily he could say that he admitted only to avoid appearing wealthy; nevertheless, because the claimant says that the defendant said, 'You are my witnesses,' it becomes a definite claim. This also explains why the first case is phrased, 'you took mine,' while the latter says, 'you owe me,' a formulation often used in admissions made to avoid appearing wealthy.
In the first case, although such words would not normally be admitted falsely, we can still say he was joking when he responded to the claimant's demand.And I said to the witnesses, 'You are my witnesses.' Above, siman 81, seif 14, the Mechaber rules according to Rambam that if he admitted directly before the claimant, it is unnecessary for him to say 'you are my witnesses.' The Rosh disagrees, and the Mechaber cites that dissenting view there. Here the Mechaber follows the Tur's wording.He may impose a hesset oath. Shimon cannot reverse the oath onto Reuven and require Reuven to swear that he knows Shimon took the stated amount and then collect, because Reuven did not see it with his own eyes but only has circumstantial indications.
This resembles the rule in siman 92 that where the claimant is suspected of false oaths, the defendant who owes only a hesset oath cannot reverse the oath upon him, and likewise one obligated to swear to a minor.To impose a hesset oath upon him. At least with a hesset oath he is exempt. This is unlike Tosafos in Bava Kamma regarding one who recognizes his utensils in another's house, where one may recover from known thieves present there, because there are much stronger adverse indications.
Nevertheless, if the owner of the chest seizes property from Shimon without witnesses, the seizure is effective. Proof comes from Bava Metzia concerning an object that fell from two people, where the loser does not despair because he thinks: no one else was here, so the other person must have taken it, and today or tomorrow I will seize from his property. The Rosh writes that if he later seizes, the other cannot recover even by taking a hesset oath.
