(See the Taz in the responsum printed at the end of the sefer.) A person purchased grain with a binding handshake and paid the price definitively. Several days later he took the grain and afterward paid the full price, one hundred reinish, using the bank notes commonly used today. After he left, the seller examined the notes when he gave them to another person and saw that each note was only for five reinish. There were ten notes, leaving an error of fifty reinish. The seller immediately sent a messenger after the buyer. The expense of the mission was approximately five gold pieces. The buyer returned and said that he was uncertain, because he did not know the accounting of his money. It was possible, he claimed, that there had been twenty notes. He had placed them on the table and gone outside to his wagons, and the seller too had left the table before taking them. Perhaps someone had taken some of the notes. The seller stated with certainty that the buyer had given no more than ten notes, each for five reinish, and that there had certainly been an error in the calculation. I obligated the buyer to pay the fifty reinish. According to the Urim VeTumim in siman 75, where a person should have known, such as where he failed to inspect a counterfeit coin, he is liable according to all opinions, and there is no dispute there between the Taz and Maharshal or Maharshal's cited view. The same applies here, because it was easy to distinguish whether each note was for five or for ten reinish. He should have known this with certainty. From the accounting of his own money he likewise should have known whether one hundred or only fifty reinish was missing, and from the number of notes he should have known whether he had given twenty or ten. The buyer himself admitted that he had not given notes of ten reinish each. The language of the Taz there indicates that his ruling rests partly on the majority presumption that coins are not counterfeit, whereas here a counting error is not so unusual. The fact that the notes were left on the table does not establish definitively that the seller acquired them before they actually came into his hand, even though they were in his house and on his table. Especially since the seller left the money before taking it, there is considerable indication that he did not yet acquire it until he later took it, and until then it remained the responsibility of the giver. Therefore there was no basis even to impose an acceptance of a ban upon the seller. The buyer's principal doubt arose from the possibility that notes were taken from the table, but the seller bore no responsibility for this. He was not even an unpaid custodian, because the buyer did not say, "Place them before you and guard them," but left of his own accord. According to the buyer's own account, the seller also left before him. The circumstances likewise suggested that the buyer presented himself as doubtful merely to avoid embarrassment and suspicion that he had intentionally tried to deceive the seller. The immediate dispatch of a messenger strongly supported the seller's version. Nevertheless, I did not obligate the buyer to pay the messenger's expenses, because he can be judged favorably as having made an innocent mistake and having been effectively compelled by that mistake. We cannot extract those expenses from him on the mere doubt that he acted intentionally. The seller also did not demand an acceptance of a ban regarding this point. I therefore exempted the buyer entirely from those expenses.
