Since the claimant says, “Perhaps,” and the defendant, etc. The phrase “since the claimant says perhaps” explains why no heset oath is required. It does not explain why the defendant has no obligation to satisfy Heaven, because even without that factor there is no such obligation where the defendant himself asserts certainty. This is the Sema's and Bach's explanation, contrary to Maharit.
For he can say, “I was jesting with you.” Gedolei Terumah objects that the defense of jesting applies only where the claimant first demanded payment and the defendant admitted, as explained in siman 81, seif 5. There is no difficulty. The case is one where the claimant first said, “You owe me one hundred,” although he himself was uncertain, and the defendant admitted. The Bach explains it this way. In the Baal HaTerumot's own view, there is an additional answer, because he holds that the defense of jesting may apply even to a voluntary admission. One may still ask that beit din does not raise the jesting defense for a litigant unless he himself pleads it, as stated in siman 81, seif 3. The wording here does not sound as though the defendant expressly says, “I was jesting.” It states generally that the claimant's assertion is not definite because the defendant could say he was jesting. The answer is that an express plea is required only where the claimant asserts a definite loan. Here the claim itself is uncertain, and beit din does not impose an oath upon an uncertain claim because the defendant could have been jesting. This appears correct as a matter of law.
But if he claims, “You voluntarily admitted to me,” etc. The Sema and Bach explain this as a case of an admission intended to avoid the appearance of wealth. Their reading requires one to say that the Mechaber here follows the Tur and Rosh, rather than the Rambam's view cited in siman 81, seif 14, that one who admits directly before the claimant cannot later say that the admission was made only to avoid the appearance of wealth, even without saying, “You are my witnesses.” In my opinion the words should be read simply. This clause, like the opening clause, concerns the defense of jesting. There is no reason to depart from the plain meaning because of minor redundancies. The ruling then follows all opinions, because everyone agrees that even an admission before the claimant may be dismissed as jesting unless the claimant said, “You are my witnesses,” as explained at the beginning of siman 81.

When there are grounds supporting the matter, etc. Likewise, if an object fell from someone and only one other person was present, the owner may require that person to take a heset oath, for this is treated as a definite claim since no one else was there. This appears from the Rosh in chapter Eilu Metziot regarding one who saw a coin fall from one of two people, and the Mordechai there cites Rabbi of Shuzina similarly. The Tosafot there also seem to imply this, and the Mordechai indicates that Rashi agrees. The Agudah quotes Rashi as saying that the claimant may impose a heset oath because he advances a definite claim, and infers that one who accuses another of theft may require an oath. In truth, however, nothing can be proved from Rashi. His intent may be only that the loser does not despair because he thinks he can impose a heset oath, although the law does not actually permit it. When three people are present, by contrast, he despairs. Baal HaTerumot, gate 36, part 3, cites Rabbi Avraham son of Rabbi Yitzchak to this effect. He explains that “I do not know” is not a definite claim, and that the explanation attributed to Rabbeinu Chananel concerning a coin that fell from two people merely means that the loser does not despair because he imagines he can impose an oath. The object therefore reaches the finder before despair, though legally no oath may be imposed. The Mordechai likewise cites Rabbeinu Baruch that no heset oath is imposed in the case of the coin that fell from two people. The Rambam similarly rules in the first chapter of Hilchot To’en that a heset oath is not imposed on an uncertain claim, such as: “It appears that a certain object was stolen from me and only you were there, so I suspect you stole it,” or, “I counted my money and found it short; perhaps you misled me in the calculation.” If the defendant says he has nothing belonging to the claimant, he is exempt even from heset. It appears that these matters depend upon the beit din’s assessment. When the judges themselves see grounds supporting the suspicion, they may impose a heset oath. Perhaps the Rambam and the other authorities spoke only where the claimant himself says that he has grounds for suspicion. Where the beit din sees objective grounds, they agree that an oath may be imposed. The case of the broken-open chest means that the judges see that the chest was breached and find supporting circumstances. The same applies in other cases. Moreover, Maharik, at the end of responsum 186, writes that a judge may impose an oath even where no oath is legally required when this is done to clarify the truth, as the Rama rules above in siman 15, seif 4.
He may require him to take a heset oath, etc. The Sema writes that if the owner of the chest seized property belonging to Shimon without witnesses, his seizure is effective. He proves this from Eilu Metziot and from the Tur in siman 262, seif 6: when one sees an object fall from one of two people, the loser certainly does not despair, because he thinks, “No one else was here; this person surely took it, and today or tomorrow I will seize something of his.” The Rosh writes there that if he later seizes the other’s property, the other cannot recover it even by taking a heset oath. This is difficult. How can seizure extract money on the basis of doubt? In siman 262 we say only that the loser does not despair because he expects to seize and assert a definite claim, thereby causing the finder to admit. But where he asserts only uncertainty, seizure is certainly ineffective; the other party takes an oath and recovers his property. Rabbi Shlomo Cohen likewise rules in his responsa, book 2, siman 2, that even according to Raavad, who obligates an oath below in seif 21 when one says, “My father told me,” seizure does not help, even without witnesses and even if the claimant swears that his father told him so. A proof may also be brought from the end of siman 134 concerning a purchaser or heir of an artisan. If he claims that the artisan purchased the utensil from its owner in his presence, he is believed. But if he merely says that the artisan told him he had purchased it, he must return the utensil. The Mordechai in Shevuat HaDayanim compares the case of the breached chest to one who says, “My father told me.”
