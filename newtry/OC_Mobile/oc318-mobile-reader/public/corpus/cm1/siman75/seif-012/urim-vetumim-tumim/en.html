Even though rabbinically he is obligated. Tosafot often indicate that the rule "one obligated to swear who cannot swear pays" does not apply to a merely rabbinic oath. There is, however, a difficult Tosafot in Bava Metzia 94. According to Rabbenu Tam, even a custodian claiming unavoidable accident needs partial admission for a Torah oath, yet Chazal imposed an oath on the accident claim alone. Tosafot's discussion of the borrowed-versus-rented animal seems at first to imply that inability to swear even such a rabbinic oath can produce payment. This can be explained. According to one stage of Rabbenu Tam, Chazal may have modeled the special custodian oath with full Torah-like force in this class, because the Torah already imposed unusually strong oath obligations on custodians. Where the rabbinic oath was deliberately made like the Torah oath, its inability rule can follow the same model. But this should not be generalized. The broader Tosafot tradition is that ordinary rabbinic oaths do not produce payment merely because the person cannot swear. The Talmudic questions can also be resolved by noting that the early stages of the sugya may assume a different configuration of animals and a true Torah oath, or that according to the relevant authority the accident oath itself occupies Torah-level status through its connection to the main custodian oath. The practical rule therefore remains: an ordinary rabbinic oath does not trigger the doctrine that inability to swear requires payment, unless Chazal expressly gave that oath Torah-like consequences.
