Who lent him. Although the witness does not testify that the loan remains unpaid or that it is still within its term, the plaintiff asserts with certainty that the debt remains, while the defendant says, "Perhaps you never lent me," and does not contradict the witness who says that the loan occurred. The defendant must pay. The Sema explains that although ordinarily a definite claim met by "I do not know whether I borrowed" ends with a shevuat heset and exemption, here one witness joins the plaintiff's definite claim. The Torah treats one witness like two until the defendant contradicts him by oath, and this defendant cannot swear because he says he does not know. The Shach describes this as the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so must pay, even if he has a migo. Responsa Maharshdam 60 supports this against Mabit I, 151, which required the witness to testify also that repayment had not occurred. Maharshdam adds that the witness must testify with certainty to an actual loan, not merely that he saw money counted out, and the Shach regards this as obvious.
I paid you. Meaning that the defendant claims payment and one witness testifies that he did not pay. Earlier authorities struggled because one who says "I do not know whether I paid" is liable even without a witness. The Shach explains that the case is where the plaintiff's claim itself rests on the witness, as discussed later in seif 23. The Taz offers another explanation.
And pays. Gidulei Terumah infers from the failure to mention a general cherem here, unlike seif 13, that when one witness supports the plaintiff there is no cherem because we do not suspect both unlawful collection and false testimony. The Shach rejects the inference. The cherem in seif 13 applies to all the preceding cases as well.
