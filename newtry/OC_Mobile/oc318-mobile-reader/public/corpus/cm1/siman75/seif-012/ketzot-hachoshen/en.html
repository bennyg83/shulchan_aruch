And one witness says he lent to him. The Sema explains that even if the witness does not testify that the debt remains unpaid, the defendant who says, "I do not know whether you ever lent me," must pay. The Shach explains this through inability to swear. His wording might suggest that if the defendant instead said with certainty, "You never lent me," the migo that he could have said, "I borrowed and repaid," would exempt him from swearing against the witness. That is not correct. Ran states explicitly that one witness is to an oath as two witnesses are to money. Just as migo does not defeat two witnesses, it does not defeat one witness before the defendant takes the required oath. Therefore a defendant who says, "I never borrowed," must swear against the one witness even though the witness does not testify about repayment. The same principle resolves the Tur's formulation, which discusses both "I do not know whether I borrowed" and "I do not know whether I repaid." If he is uncertain about the loan itself, the one witness creates the Torah oath and he cannot swear. If he is uncertain about repayment, he is liable in any event because a certain debt plus uncertain repayment obligates him. Even if his uncertainty concerns both stages, the result is the same: the witness establishes the loan sufficiently to remove the uncertainty about whether there ever was a debt, while his remaining uncertainty concerns repayment, against which the creditor's certain claim prevails. This remains true even if the claimant himself is uncertain in some configurations. The rule of inability to swear can apply where the Torah oath exists independently of the claimant's certainty, as shown by the sugyot of borrowed versus rented property. The decisive point is that one cannot satisfy a Torah oath by saying, "I do not know."
