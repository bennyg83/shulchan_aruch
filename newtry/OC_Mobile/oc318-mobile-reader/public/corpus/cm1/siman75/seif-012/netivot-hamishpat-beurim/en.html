He is obligated to swear and, being unable to swear, must pay. See Shach, seif katan 38, who concludes that the case is where the creditor's claim is based on the witness. Even so, absent the doctrine that one who cannot swear must pay, such as where the witness is a relative, the borrower would not be liable merely because he says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," because the creditor himself does not know whether he was paid. Although the creditor makes a definite assertion based on the witness, and such an assertion is considered definite below in seif 23 for the purpose of imposing an oath on the opposing party, it is not considered a sufficiently definite claim to impose monetary liability. Knesset HaGedolah, in his glosses to Tur, gives another distinction: if liability is based on "I do not know whether I repaid you," the defendant can later say, "I remembered that I paid," as above, whereas if liability arose from being unable to swear against one witness, he cannot later say that he remembered. At first glance this is difficult. Why should remembrance not help? How is it different from an ordinary case of uncertainty about repayment, where he may say that he remembered, or from siman 59, where he is believed to do so? It appears that ordinarily, whenever one first says, "I do not know," and later says, "I remembered," he may change his statement, because moving from lack of knowledge to knowledge is not considered retracting and making a new claim. Ran in Shevuot, Perek Shevuat HaEdut, writes that even in testimony, going from "I do not know" to "I know" is not treated as prohibited retraction of testimony. Knesset HaGedolah's rule applies only where one witness testified that the debt was not paid and the borrower responded that he did not know. At that moment, because the borrower did not contradict the witness, the witness's statement became legally equivalent to the testimony of two. This resembles the rule in prohibitions that when one witness is accepted first, a later single witness cannot overturn him because the first has already been accorded the force of two. So here, once the witness said that the debt was unpaid and the borrower said he did not know, the witness became like two with respect to nonpayment. The borrower cannot later say that he remembered paying and thereby contradict testimony that has already acquired the force of two.
