Seif 27. I bought it for such and such. If a seller says that he is reselling merchandise for the amount he paid and states a specific figure, but it is later discovered that he paid less, the purchaser appears to have the upper hand. He may choose to pay the figure expressly stated before the truth was discovered, in which case the sale stands, or he may refuse and say that he relied on the seller's representation that the figure reflected the actual cost and therefore will not pay more than the true cost. The seller, however, may argue that he never agreed to sell for less than the stated amount. His false statement about his own cost weakens his ability to compel the purchaser to pay that amount, but it does not necessarily permit the purchaser to compel him to sell for a lower amount he never expressly accepted. The sanctions of mi shepara and the disapproval attached to failure to keep one's word may likewise not apply to enforcing the transaction, because the seller's earlier false statement has already undermined the agreement. This requires study. Where the transaction is expressly an amanah sale, meaning resale at the seller's own cost, if the seller both states a specific amount and says that this is what he paid, but later it is discovered that he paid less, the purchaser may retract as above. If the expressly stated amount itself involved onaah, perhaps the seller may also retract, because the amanah formulation may be treated as merely weakening the purchaser's obligation to pay the stated amount rather than fixing an alternative lower price. If the seller explains that his stated 'cost' included interest or business charges he paid on money borrowed from a non-Jew to finance the purchase, the purchaser may still argue that ordinary language meant the actual purchase price. Yet the seller may perhaps rely on his own understanding of 'cost' to prevent the purchaser from using onaah against him when the purchaser is willing to pay the expressly stated figure, since with those financing expenses included his statement may retroactively have been accurate enough to make it an amanah sale. The reverse case likewise requires study. If the seller mistakenly stated a figure lower than his true cost, he may perhaps choose either to sell for the actual amount he paid if the purchaser agrees or to cancel the sale as a mistake. Even though the larger amount includes the smaller, the purchaser too may say he consented only to the figure stated to him, and if the buyer is not bound there may be no fixed sale. If the purchaser can cancel but the seller cannot where the purchaser agrees to pay the true cost, onaah may still not apply even if the market price had risen by one-sixth, because once the true cost is established the transaction is revealed as an amanah sale and the misstated figure drops out. If the error is not proved by two witnesses but the first seller supports the claim and is at least a single witness, the purchaser may become one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and therefore must pay, even if he presently possesses the money. It may not help to say that he could not have known, because he could have checked with the first seller from the beginning. Even if he regards that seller merely as a single witness, there is some practical inference that had the information been presented earlier the second seller would not have stated the mistaken figure. In some circumstances even a relative or other person not qualified as a witness may create enough practical basis to affect whether the matter is classified as something the purchaser should have known. On the other hand, the Taz at the end of siman 75 regarding a counterfeit coin writes that once the parties separated without an established debt, the later uncertainty is like 'I do not know whether I ever borrowed,' and the same reasoning may apply here. If an amanah sale concerns measured or weighed goods and a quantity error is discovered, onaah may apply. The seller may argue that his quantity statement was merely an estimate because the purchaser saw the bulk goods with his own eyes and that the essence of the agreement was resale at cost, not a sale by exact measure. See siman 218; perhaps land and movables differ. If the seller must make up the shortage and the market price fell in the meantime, where the goods were originally purchased at a known market rate it may be that he owes the monetary value corresponding to the shortage at the original sale date. But in a transaction whose value could not have been known precisely, such as sale of an annual right to millings where the seller estimated that a certain quantity of grain was already due and less was later found, any required supplementation may perhaps be valued at the later, lower rate. If a purchaser buys a specified number of hides to be chosen later from a much larger existing stock, the sale is not an amanah or rough-estimate sale exempt from onaah merely because the particular hides were not yet identified. The overall stock already exists and the uncertainty is only which individual hides will later be selected. If after selection the chosen hides create one-sixth onaah, the party disadvantaged cannot simply demand that inferior hides be substituted to erase the onaah if the original bargain fixed the selection method. Where the parties appointed an expert to choose an appropriate number of hides against a fixed sum without requiring him to choose the best, the selector should choose so as to minimize onaah to either side as much as possible. If no perfectly balanced selection exists, he should choose the combination with the smallest disparity. If inferior hides would cause less-than-one-sixth onaah to the purchaser and better hides less-than-one-sixth to the seller, with no neutral option, the selector may perhaps choose either, resembling shuda dedaynei, though he may not accept a bribe from one side to tilt his judgment. This may not be the Torah prohibition of judicial bribery, but it is still improper because he was chosen to exercise independent judgment. If the agreed selector later leaves and it is discovered that he selected badly, a selection involving less than one-sixth onaah should remain valid. If he selected goods causing one-sixth or more where he could have chosen goods with little or no onaah, the transaction may be void rather than treated as an amanah or rough-estimate sale, because the bargain contemplated a reasonable selection and imposed on him a duty to minimize disparity. If the amount exceeds the cancellation threshold, the transaction is fully cancelled and the advantaged party cannot force the other to accept some different assortment after the agreed selection has failed. If the selected goods involve only ordinary refundable onaah, the selection may stand and the excess is returned as in any sale. The selector himself may be able to retract his selection upon discovering his mistake. If no selector was appointed at the time of sale and the parties later agree on one whose selection results in cancellation-level onaah, they may perhaps agree on another selector until a valid selection can be made. Value is measured as of the time of sale, and this does not present a problem of bereirah because the later selection merely reveals which existing items satisfy the original agreement. A further question arises in an amanah sale of goods the seller will acquire in the future if he bought them with one currency and the currency later appreciated or depreciated before the purchaser's payment. The result may depend on whether the seller acts as agent or independent buyer, whether the goods already existed, whether a market rate had been fixed for purposes of mi shepara, and whether the parties knew payment would occur at a specified later time. These distinctions require further study.
