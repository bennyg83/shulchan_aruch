Likewise, if it became known that such a bargain came into his hands. Sema says this must be known clearly. If the parties dispute whether the seller later encountered a comparable bargain, even if the purchaser says with certainty that he did, the seller prevails and may collect. Sema explains that a person does not ordinarily buy in order to retract, so we presume the seller did not know earlier. Shitah Mekubetzet cites a similar ruling from Ramach. In my view the deeper reason is that waiver is an inferior plea. People do not ordinarily waive a monetary claim. Therefore the purchaser must prove that the later bargain actually came to the seller and that the seller knowingly waived. Why is the seller not believed in waiver through a migo that he could say he already repaid the amount of overreaching? Because once the sale occurred he ordinarily would not pay back overreaching before beit din appraised the transaction and held him liable. He can say, "Who says the Sages will obligate me?" Thus repayment is not an equally available plea. If the amount the seller originally paid is known only from the purchaser's admission, however, the purchaser can be believed that the seller later encountered a comparable bargain and waived, through a migo that he could have said the seller originally bought without overreaching. In that configuration the migo is genuine.
