For the merchandise is in his possession. Netivot HaMishpat writes that if the purchase was effected by a kinyan sudar while the object remained with the seller, the positions reverse: the seller who was overcharged cannot rescind because the object remains in his possession, whereas an overcharged buyer may rescind because the object is not yet in his possession, and his period for showing it to a merchant begins only after delivery. Pitchei Teshuva disagrees in part. According to the authorities cited above, a person who remains in possession of the relevant property may say that he did not investigate because he relied on what he still held. Thus a seller who still physically holds the object should likewise be able to say that his delay does not prove waiver. Netivot follows his own view that possession helps only through migo, and in a case where the seller has no migo, for example where witnesses presently see the object in his hands, Netivot would not believe him. But according to Mishneh LaMelech and the other authorities who make possession itself the reason, the seller also retains the claim. Rabbi Akiva Eiger further explains that even one who rejects this principle where the sale was completed by a full kinyan may agree where only money was given and the buyer did not yet take possession. Since the seller still has a right to withdraw subject to mi shepara, his delay is no proof of waiver; he may rescind even after the ordinary period without incurring mi shepara.
And certainly in a case of cancellation of the sale. Responsa Rishmei Sheelah, siman 57, considers a buyer who already resold the item before the original sale was voided for excessive onaah. It asks whether the second sale is automatically void as a sale of property not truly owned, permitting the first seller to compel both later parties to unwind it. The author's father answered that the second purchaser is not required to believe the first seller and buyer that their transaction was void, since they may be colluding or may have known and accepted the price. Accordingly, if the first buyer accepts a solemn ban that he did not know of the excessive onaah, he had resold under color of ownership and need not pay the original seller more than the amount he actually received. Had he destroyed the property or given it to a non-Jew in good faith, he likewise would not pay more from his own assets. If he cannot make that affirmation, however, he must pay the first seller the item's full value. See also Netivot above.
