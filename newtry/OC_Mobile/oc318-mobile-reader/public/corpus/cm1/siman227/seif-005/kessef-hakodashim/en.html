Seif 9. The market price changed. Siman 227, seif 9, makes clear that a change in price after the sale does not itself create onaah. I have heard a question whether the rule differs when merchandise was already being transported from a nearby place at the time of the sale and arrived immediately afterward, thereby lowering the market. Some bring proof from Ketubot 97a, where ships already delayed in the bends are treated as though they had effectively arrived. In my view the cases are not comparable. There the sale was voided because of disclosed intent, and the existence of the ships supported that intent: had the party known the facts he would not have bought. Onaah, however, is measured only by the place and time of sale, not by merchandise already on its way there. Since in movables mere disclosure of intent is ineffective without a condition, as stated in siman 207, seif 3 and the Rama there, the approaching shipment does not change the onaah calculation. At the moment of sale the prevailing price was what it was. This is my view, though it still requires study. If in an onaah dispute the seller cannot state his claim with certainty but the purchaser claims certainty, perhaps the purchaser may still be required to swear even though he is in possession, because the circumstances themselves provide some indication against him. The same may be true if the purchaser admits some onaah but claims that it was below the threshold requiring return, because below that threshold the law treats the excess as waived and the claim is effectively a definite denial. The time limit of showing the purchase to a merchant may perhaps not apply in the ordinary way to a transaction whose value is inherently speculative, such as a year's rental of a mill where future grain prices can alter the value. Since the parties know that later market movement may make the apparent onaah disappear, the ordinary appraisal period may not fit such a transaction.
