Seif 27. One who conducts business on trust, etc. Note: The Netivot HaMishpat in this siman innovated, on his own reasoning, that where the seller already knew that he had overcharged, the buyer has a claim of ona'ah because the seller violated the prohibition of "You shall not wrong." See there. In my humble opinion, this ruling is neither clear nor correct. For even where the discrepancy is less than one sixth, even by the slightest amount, one violates "You shall not wrong," and nevertheless there is no claim of ona'ah. Necessarily, then, the matter does not depend upon the prohibition itself. Although there the reason is that up to one sixth a person waives the discrepancy, the same is true in a sale made on trust. It cannot be said that here he did not know and therefore did not waive it. That reasoning applies where one buys at market value, because his intent concerns the value of the object, and the money he gives is payment for that value. There one can say that he did not know and therefore did not waive the discrepancy. But in a transaction on trust, his intent is not directed to the value of the object, as the poskim write, and as the Netivot HaMishpat himself states. If so, the money is not truly the purchase price of the object; rather, it is as though he gives the money as a gift on condition that the other party give him this object as a gift. Since the money is given as a gift, he knowingly waives any discrepancy. Granted, a defect voids the sale even in such a case, because if there is a defect the object is no longer regarded as the same thing, but rather like a different object. It is comparable to one who stipulated that wheat be given to him and barley was given instead. But if it is the very same object without defect, and the mistake is only in the price, since his intent was not directed to the object's market value it is as though he knew and waived it, for he gives this money as a gift and the other gives him the object. A further proof is that if the return of ona'ah were based on the violation of the prohibition, then even an unwitting violator would have violated it, for a person is always deemed forewarned. Necessarily, the return of ona'ah is not because of the prohibition. This is clear in my humble opinion, contrary to the Netivot on this point. Moreover, the burden of proof rests upon one who seeks to establish such a novel rule. Examine this carefully. Seif 29, these are the matters to which ona'ah does not apply, etc. Note: See Tosafot in the chapter HaKotev, s.v. "one who sells a promissory note to his fellow and then forgives it." It is evident there that even one who sells a promissory note merely as paper has no claim of ona'ah. It requires study why the poskim did not record this law. There, in the gloss, and some say specifically up to one half, etc. Note: See my Torah commentary on Parashat Chayei Sarah, year 5607, where I brought proof from the Midrash that a discrepancy of one half constitutes ona'ah. I also concluded there that although the Sema writes that this constitutes cancellation of the sale, that applies only when one Jew buys from another Jew. But when a Jew buys from a gentile, where money does not effect acquisition and serves only to remove the gentile's ownership, after which the Jew acquires by chazakah, if the discrepancy is one half the gentile must return the amount of ona'ah to the Jew, as the Shach writes above that a gentile who wrongs a Jew must return the ona'ah. There is no cancellation of the acquisition, because once the gentile withdrew his ownership the property became ownerless and the Jew acquired it from ownerless property. The acquisition therefore remains valid, and only the ona'ah is returned. See there, where I wrote an appropriate analysis with Hashem's help.
