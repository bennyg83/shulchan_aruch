For the merchandise is in his possession. Netivot HaMishpat writes that according to this, if the transaction was made by kinyan sudar while the merchandise still remained in the seller's possession, the rule is reversed. If the seller was the one overreached, he cannot retract because the merchandise is still in his hand. If the purchaser was overreached, he may retract because the merchandise is not yet in his possession, and his inspection period begins only when the item reaches him. In my humble opinion, in such a case the seller too can retract, according to the authorities cited above, seif 7, seif katan 2, who hold that where the buyer performed meshichah but has not yet paid, the buyer may retract even after the ordinary inspection period because, while he remained in possession of his money, he had no reason to investigate and his silence is not proof of waiver. The same logic should apply here in reverse: since the seller still possesses the merchandise, he had no need to investigate and his silence is no proof of waiver. Netivot HaMishpat, however, follows his own view that this rationale is not accepted and that the rule depends instead upon migo, as stated above in his name. In siman 232, seif 3, he likewise disagrees with Mishneh LaMelech and holds that without migo one is not believed to say that he did not waive. Thus, the present case would be one where the seller lacks a migo of having returned the property, such as where witnesses now see it in his possession or where its possession is publicly known, similar to the rule above, siman 72, seif 18. According to Mishneh LaMelech and the other authorities who hold that even without migo one may say, "I did not waive," the seller who retains the merchandise would likewise be protected. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in his novellae to Bava Metzia 49b, appears to agree. He further explains that even one who rejects the broader rule that possession alone preserves the ona'ah claim might do so only where the sale was completed by a fully effective kinyan such as meshichah or kinyan sudar. In that case one might argue that since the party no longer had power to retract and had nothing else upon which to rely, silence proves waiver. But if the sale was not completed by a final kinyan, and money was given without meshichah or kinyan sudar, the seller can certainly retract even after the inspection period without accepting Mi Shepara on account of ona'ah. Since the transaction was not complete and was binding only to the extent of Mi Shepara, the delay is no proof of waiver, because the seller could rely upon his existing right to retract subject to that sanction.
