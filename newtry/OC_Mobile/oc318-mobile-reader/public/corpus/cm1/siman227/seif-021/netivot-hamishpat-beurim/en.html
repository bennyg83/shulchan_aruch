On condition that you have no claim of ona'ah against me. Tosafot in Ketubot 56, s.v. Harei Zo Mekudeshet, explain that where the seller merely says that no law of ona'ah shall apply, meaning that the buyer will not be able to sue him for the overcharge, the buyer nevertheless retains the ona'ah claim. Nimukei Yosef explains that a present waiver has no substance because the buyer does not yet know what he is waiving. Consequently the condition itself cannot accomplish the result, because it is a condition contrary to Torah law. This also resolves the words of the Levush cited by Sema, seif katan 37, and the questions raised there by the Sema, Machaneh Ephraim, and Ketzot. If, however, the seller says, "on condition that you will waive any ona'ah," this is a genuine condition. Its meaning is: if, once the buyer learns of the ona'ah, he then waives it, the sale will remain effective; if he refuses to waive it, the sale will be void. Therefore, even where the ona'ah is exactly one sixth, where ordinary law says the sale stands and the overcharge is returned, the seller can cancel the sale because the condition was not fulfilled. A person can, under Torah law, actually forgive a monetary ona'ah claim once it exists. The condition therefore does not contradict Torah law. By contrast, when the seller says "on condition that you have no ona'ah claim against me," he seeks to make the claim already forgiven now by force of the condition even if the buyer never later waives it. That is a condition against Torah law. The Gemara's statement that "he did not know and therefore did not waive" does not mean, as Ketzot suggests, that any such waiver would be void as a mistaken waiver. Tosafot themselves ask concerning a ketubah that the wife does not know whether her husband will die first, yet a wife can certainly expressly forgive her ketubah. Rather, where she does not know, she has not actually formed an intention to forgive. The matter therefore depends solely upon the condition, and the condition is contrary to Torah law. The Rashba in his newer responsa and the Rashbatz, cited by Machaneh Ephraim, explain similarly. This is unlike Ketzot's approach. Tosafot in Makkot 3, s.v. Al Menat, write, "that she will not demand the ona'ah, but will waive it." This too can be understood as a purported waiver taking effect now, before the buyer knows what he is waiving. Therefore the ona'ah claim remains, and where the disparity is one sixth the seller must return the ona'ah while the sale itself remains valid. The Gemara's phrase that Rav says "he has against him the law of ona'ah" also implies that the ordinary ona'ah law applies in its normal form, unlike Ketzot's explanation.
On condition that there is no ona'ah in it. See Sema, seif katan 38, who indicates that if the disparity is less than one sixth, the sale is not cancelled even according to Rashi. The reason is that a disparity below one sixth is not legally called ona'ah, unlike the Bach. Accordingly, if the seller says, "I know there is ona'ah in it," and the Sema in seif katan 39 allows him to argue, "I did not know the ona'ah was so great," that argument should fail where the disparity is exactly one sixth. The term ona'ah does not ordinarily refer to a disparity below one sixth, so his condition naturally covers an exact one sixth disparity and is effective.
