On condition that you have no overreaching claim against me. Sema says that unlike a condition against Shemittah, where monetary rights can be waived knowingly, this condition fails because the buyer does not know the amount of the overreaching he is supposedly waiving. Ir Shushan instead explains that the condition fails because it is a condition against what is written in the Torah. Sema rejects that, saying the seller merely asks the buyer to waive the monetary claim. Nimukei Yosef supports Ir Shushan. He writes that once waiver fails because the buyer does not know what he is waiving, the condition also fails because it then operates as an attempt to uproot the Torah's law rather than as a knowing personal waiver. Rashba explains Rabbi Yehudah's general rule that a monetary condition can stand because the condition is understood as, "on condition that you waive." Therefore, where no knowing waiver is possible, that route disappears. If instead the seller says, "on condition that the law of overreaching shall not apply," the defect is even clearer. That directly attempts to negate Torah law and is ineffective. Thus Ir Shushan's explanation is well founded. I discussed in siman 209 that even a formulation excluding the legal right can sometimes be understood as a personal monetary waiver rather than an attempt to uproot Torah law. Nevertheless, where the buyer does not know what he is waiving, the same lack of knowing consent remains decisive.
Because he does not know how much the overreaching is in order to waive it. The Gemara says that one who tells another, "On condition that you have no claim of overreaching against me," is subject to the dispute of Rav and Shmuel. Rav holds the buyer still has an overreaching claim, because Rabbi Yehudah validates monetary conditions only where the beneficiary knew the right and waived it. Here he did not know what he was waiving. It follows that if there is exactly one sixth overreaching, where the sale ordinarily stands and the excess is returned, the seller who stipulated "on condition that you have no overreaching claim" can himself retract from the entire sale if the buyer insists on recovering the excess. According to Rabbi Meir, a condition against Torah law is void while the act stands. Tosafot asks why failure of the condition does not cancel the act and answers that because this is not a valid condition modeled on the condition of the tribes of Gad and Reuven, the condition is simply ignored and the act remains. But we rule like Rabbi Yehudah in monetary matters. Here the condition itself is not inherently against Torah law. It is a condition that the buyer waive a monetary right. The buyer's attempted waiver fails only because it was made in error or without knowledge. The condition, however, still expresses that the seller agreed to sell only if no overreaching claim would be asserted. Once the buyer asserts the claim, the seller can say that his condition was not fulfilled and cancel the transaction. The same follows from the alternative theory, that in monetary matters the Torah itself gives the right only subject to the parties' agreement. Even if the buyer cannot waive an unknown amount conclusively, the seller's conditional agreement remains a valid condition. Therefore the buyer may choose to recover the overreaching, but the seller can then cancel the sale. Sema understood that the sale simply proceeds as though no condition had been made. In my view that is incorrect regarding the wording, "on condition that you have no overreaching claim against me." If instead the seller says, "on condition that this transaction has no law of overreaching," Tosafot understands that as an invalid condition against Torah law. In that formulation the condition itself is void and the act stands, so with exactly one sixth the sale remains and the excess is returned. Rashi, however, understands the phrase differently, as a factual condition that there shall in fact be no overreaching. If overreaching exists, the condition fails and the seller can retract. The Shulchan Aruch says "all the more so if he said, on condition that there is no overreaching," implying the two formulations are governed alike. According to my analysis, this supports Rashi: in either formulation, where the condition fails, the seller may cancel. According to Tosafot the two cases differ. This is another reason to read the practical law like Rashi.
