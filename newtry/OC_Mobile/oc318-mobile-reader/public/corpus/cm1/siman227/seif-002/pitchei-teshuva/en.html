And not to claim his ona'ah. See Baer Heitev in the name of Shach, who writes that this implies the purchaser already paid the money, and Hagahot Ashri states the same. Ketzot HaChoshen comments that the unqualified language of the poskim implies that even if he did not pay, the sale remains valid because there is a presumption that he waived the ona'ah. The purchaser's migo would then be a migo against a strong presumption, meaning a presumption equivalent to direct proof. See above, siman 99, seif 1, second gloss, and Baer Heitev there, seif katan 8. Netivot HaMishpat, however, adopts Shach's ruling. He must understand the presumption here as not so strong, comparable to the presumption that a person does not repay within the term, above siman 78, seif 5. Pilpula Charifta, chapter HaZahav, siman 20, letter 40, also clearly takes Shach's approach. On Sema's statement, seif katan 14, cited by Baer Heitev above, that a purchaser who still retains the purchase money cannot say that he does not waive less than one sixth because his individual view is disregarded, Pilpula Charifta writes that this is only for less than one sixth. But at exactly one sixth, where he waited longer than the time required to show the item to a merchant and ordinarily can no longer claim because we say he certainly waived, if he still holds the money he can say that he did not waive, through the migo that he could say he already paid. Shaar Mishpat likewise agrees with this practical result and writes that even where the purchaser lacks a migo of payment, he may retract so long as he still holds the money, not for Hagahot Ashri's reason but because while he retained the money he had no reason to hurry to investigate the amount of ona'ah. The Gemara in Bechorot 13b, concerning one who purchases scrap from a non Jew, expressly indicates that where money has not been paid we do not say that he should have examined the matter immediately. Tosafos, Ketubot 58a, s.v. Hanehu, likewise write that wherever payment has not yet been made the argument "he should have checked" does not apply. The same is true here. So long as the buyer did not pay, it is not customary to rush to investigate whether he was overreached. This is strong proof for Hagahot Ashri's ruling even without migo. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Mechirah, chapter 16, halachah 3, appears to agree. Maggid Mishneh there, cited by Sema, siman 232, seif katan 10, brings an opinion that if one sells an item with a defect that can be immediately examined and tested, the purchaser cannot later retract. Mishneh LaMelech comments that if the purchase was on credit and the purchaser still holds the purchase money, he can say that this is why he did not immediately test the item and that he never waived. He brings proof from Baal HaTerumot, gate 7, in the rule of one who claimed wheat and admitted barley, cited above, siman 88, seif 12, in the gloss and Sema there, seif katan 27. Mishneh LaMelech implies that the same applies to ona'ah after the ordinary period for inspection. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer, siman 93, seif 13, writes similarly. Where the money has not been paid, there is no need to rely on migo; the case resembles Choshen Mishpat 88:12, where if the defendant was already in possession at the time of the claim, his failure to claim barley is explained by his existing possession. He notes that the migo rationale itself can be questioned in light of Sema there, seif katan 28, and Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer, seif katan 23. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in his novellae to Bava Metzia 49b, likewise appears to hold that one who retains his money has no duty to show the item to a merchant and his silence is no proof of waiver. He too cites Tosafos, Ketubot 58a. He adds, however, that if at the time of purchase the buyer had not yet paid and waited beyond the inspection period, but afterward paid and only then sought to retract, then even if less than the full inspection period passed after payment, we say that because he had time to show it before paying and nonetheless paid without doing so, he thereby demonstrated waiver. See there. Responsa Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 48, was likewise asked about Reuven, who purchased a ship from Shimon. The ship lay in port about three parasangs from the city. Before purchase Reuven sent an expert in ships, who told him that it was worth the agreed price. Reuven relied upon him, purchased it, and paid most of the price, leaving a fixed sum unpaid until a specified date. It was later discovered that the price involved ona'ah greater than one sixth, but more than the normal inspection period, approximately three or four weeks, had passed. Reuven claimed that he had not known the true value clearly until then and wished either to retain the unpaid money against his loss or to annul the sale. Shimon argued that the time for inspection had passed and Reuven had waived his claim. Mishkenot Yaakov cites Shach and Ketzot HaChoshen. He questions Ketzot, because Rambam and the Mechaber do imply Shach's position, as does the wording of the Mishnah and Gemara, where the inspection period is said concerning recovery of ona'ah from one who already possesses the wrongfully received money. The Rosh likewise indicates that there is no true presumption of waiver, only a rule against extracting money from the possessor, and Responsa Maharit, part 2, siman 19, supports this. He therefore concludes that the practical ruling follows Shach despite Shach's own final expression of doubt. He adds that the migo rationale is unnecessary. The basic rule is that as long as the ona'ah amount remains in the buyer's hand, the seller cannot extract it merely from the buyer's silence. The buyer can say, "Why should I have protested? My money remained in my hand, and I intended not to release it until I investigated the matter." Thus there was no waiver. He offers a further rationale: can a person say to another, "I waive whatever you will steal from me," and thereby make theft permissible? Waiver is effective only concerning an obligation already in the other's possession, and even then particularly concerning a debt. What remains in the claimant's own hand cannot be removed by a mere waiver, as explained by the poskim. See above, siman 241, seifim 2 and 3, and Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 163, cited in part above, siman 156, seif 5, seif katan 7. All the more so here, where waiver is only inferred from silence. Mishkenot Yaakov then cites Mishneh LaMelech's explicit reasoning that where the purchase money remains with the buyer there is no proof of waiver. He therefore rules in the ship case that because Reuven still retains a fixed amount of the price, he may keep it against the ona'ah even after a long time. In such a case there is no inspection period and silence does not prove waiver. One who compels him to surrender the money would violate the prohibition of ona'ah and assist wrongdoing, as Ibn Ezra explains the plural wording of the verse "you shall not wrong," which includes those who assist. At first glance, the facts of Mishkenot Yaakov's case, as stated, show that before purchasing the ship Reuven sent an expert and relied upon his valuation. If so, there should have been no need for the reasoning that Reuven retained part of the money. Even if he had paid in full, he should be able to retract long afterward because he purchased in reliance on an expert, as I will write in the following seif katan. One must therefore say that after he learned that the expert had erred, he then waited an additional inspection period. The phrase "but he delayed longer," then, refers not from the date of purchase but from the time the error became known. The wording is not clear and requires study. As to the basic rule agreed upon by these authorities, that where the buyer retains the money he may claim ona'ah even after the inspection period, the authorities differ only in rationale. Hagahot Ashri, Pilpula Charifta, Shach, and Netivot HaMishpat rely on migo. Shaar Mishpat, Mishneh LaMelech, Beit Meir, Rabbi Akiva Eiger, and Mishkenot Yaakov reason that while the buyer retained the money he had no need to investigate and his silence is no waiver. In my humble opinion there are practical differences. Under the migo rationale, the buyer should have to swear a heset oath that he did not agree to the ona'ah. Even though the seller cannot contradict him with certainty, his credibility depends upon the migo that he could have claimed payment, and one claiming payment would have to swear heset, as Shach writes in siman 126, seif katan 56, cited in part by Baer Heitev there, seif katan 45. Under the second rationale, no oath is required at all. Another difference concerns a case where at the time of purchase the buyer paid cash, but only after the inspection period did he seize money from the seller without witnesses. Under the migo rationale, the later possessor still has a migo and can say that he never accepted the ona'ah. Beit Meir expressly states this. Under Mishneh LaMelech's rationale, however, since the buyer was not initially in possession, he should have investigated. His delay shows waiver, and a later seizure cannot help him, as Sema, siman 88, seif katan 28, indicates. Conversely, where the buyer purchased on credit but after the inspection period the seller seized the purchase money, under the migo rationale the buyer now lacks the migo and may no longer claim ona'ah. Under the second rationale, however, because he originally retained the money, his failure to investigate remains explained, and he may still recover the ona'ah from the seller as though still within the inspection period. In such cases the practical law should follow the party presently in possession, who may say that he relies upon the authorities supporting his position. I saw in the recently printed Responsa Amudei Or, siman 108, a case where Reuven sold an item to Shimon, received part of the money, and fixed a later date for payment of the balance. Shimon immediately took the item. After the due date Reuven demanded his money, and Shimon replied that he had been overreached by more than one sixth and had witnesses. Reuven asked why he had not claimed within the inspection period, and Shimon answered that he saw no need to litigate because Reuven's money remained in his hands. Amudei Or ruled for the seller. According to what has been explained, this ruling is difficult. Shimon's argument is legally sound. Amudei Or adds a supposed support from Sema, siman 126, seif katan 39, explaining that there the claimant might have thought there was no reason to demand before the due date. But Sema's actual language is that the defendant could say, "I thought to myself that during the term he cannot demand from me because my time has not yet arrived." The same reasoning applies here. This requires study. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 187, concerning Reuven, who served Levi for many years and, toward his wages, took from Levi's shop the clothing needs of himself, his wife, and children. Eventually he left Levi's service. Levi then demanded twenty Reichsthaler shown in his ledger as the amount Reuven had taken in excess of his wages over twelve years. Reuven answered that the ledger arithmetic was correct, but during those years he had received merchandise worth more than one hundred Reichsthaler at prices greatly exceeding its true value and ordinary retail value. He had not protested because he thought that since he was not paying cash but merely offsetting the purchases against his wages, he need not be exacting. Now that Levi sought actual payment of the balance, he challenged all the prices, arguing that if the goods were valued correctly he owed nothing. Chavot Yair rules for Reuven because the ona'ah exceeded one sixth. Even though much time had elapsed, waiver is not inferred where he gives a reason for his silence, whether he says that he knew immediately or learned soon afterward. Even if Levi had explicitly told him, or any seller tells a buyer, "This article I sell you for two hundred is worth only one hundred, but I will not sell it for less than two hundred," if the seller did not add "on condition that you have no ona'ah claim against me," the buyer can still retract. Even a slight explanation for the buyer's silence, either at purchase or upon learning of the value, is sufficient. All this applies even if the purchaser already paid. Certainly in the case before him, if it is established that the seller overreached by more than one sixth, the sale is void. Since the clothing was already worn out, or even if the fabric had merely been cut to make garments, and the seller now demands the price, the buyer pays only the appraised fair value according to ordinary merchant sales. This applies only to merchandise still capable of identification so that one knows it was sold with ona'ah. He cannot reopen all prior transactions generally, because the seller faces no definite claim as to who appraised each item and for how much. See there. However, Chavot Yair's statement that even after the garments were worn out or fabric cut, the buyer need only pay appraised fair value appears incorrect in my humble opinion.
