How much constitutes overreaching. Ritva writes that if an item is worth five but is sold for six, and that particular buyer genuinely values it at six, there is no overreaching for him. However, if he agrees to the excessive price only because he is under pressure or in urgent need, an agreement made under distress is not considered a binding valuation. Ritva proves this from the law of a woman pressured to obtain chalitzah from an unsuitable brother in law. She may promise him two hundred zuz, yet after he performs the chalitzah she can say, "I was merely playing with you," like one escaping prison who promises an exorbitant fee to be ferried across a river. Such a price fixed under compulsion is not binding. He therefore concludes that one who buys medicines or similar necessities for an excessive price because of illness need pay only their true value. Several points require study. First, the accepted law grants overreaching even with jewels and other objects people specifically wish to match, which implies that an individual's unusual desire does not eliminate objective market value even when the item is particularly useful to that buyer. Second, regarding the person escaping prison, the Shulchan Aruch rules that if he has already paid the exorbitant amount, it is not taken back. Rama similarly rules in the chalitzah case. All the more, if the buyer already paid the excessive price, it is difficult to say he can always recover it. Third, sale differs from hiring. The ferryman case rests on a mere verbal promise for services and the promisor can say he was joking. A completed purchase is supported by a kinyan. If the ferryman agreement itself had been made by a binding kinyan, the result might likewise be different. Ritva's broad extension to sale therefore requires study.
