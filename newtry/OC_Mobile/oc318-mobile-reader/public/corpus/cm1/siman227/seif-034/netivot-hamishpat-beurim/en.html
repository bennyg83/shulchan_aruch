He hired him to sow his land. See Sema, seif katan 62. The words printed in parentheses, "and he is a land contractor," should not be deleted; this is precisely the explanation of Kesef Mishneh on Rambam. One should not ask why the Gemara did not answer simply that the ona'ah concerned the worker's wage and the issue was whether he is a contractor in land, who has no ona'ah, or a contractor in movable property, who does. That question was already obvious. The Gemara's uncertainty concerns a more specific point: the worker's task was to plow and sow land, so as a worker he is plainly a land contractor. But the actual error lay in the quantity of wheat needed for the land. The question is whether that wheat should itself be treated as land for ona'ah purposes or as movable property. This also answers Ramban's objection that the case should be governed by "If I leave it fallow and do not work it, I will pay the best expected yield." Tosafot in Bava Metzia 56, discussing Rashi's explanation, ask that if the seller said, "My field is properly sown," when it was not, that seems like a mistaken transaction rather than ona'ah. Tosafot answer that the owner said truthfully how much seed he placed, but both sides erred regarding how much improvement that amount would produce. The same can be said here. Suppose the owner hired the worker to cultivate a field of ten cubits for six gold pieces, the standard wage for such work. The owner mistakenly believed a ten-cubit field required only five measures of seed and instructed the worker to sow five. The worker was not negligent; he did exactly what the owner directed. But the wage itself was computed on the normal assumption that a worker receiving six gold pieces for such a field would sow six measures. Since he sowed only five, he should receive only five gold pieces. The error therefore lies in the wage, but arose from the mistaken quantity of seed. The Gemara asks whether those seeds are treated as part of the land or as movable property. Kesef Mishneh's answer that this is unlike leaving a field fallow because the worker nevertheless improved it somewhat is very difficult. If a worker sows only part of a field, he can plainly be liable for the portion left unworked. Ramban gives another interpretation that Sema mentions without explaining. According to Ramban, the ona'ah was in the market value of the actual wheat. The question is still whether wheat that is being incorporated into land is treated as land or as movable property for ona'ah. This produces a practical difference. If one sells attached produce and the buyer knows the quantity and condition of the produce but errs only about its market price, Ramban would treat the ona'ah as ona'ah of land, while according to Tosafot it would be ona'ah of movable property because the error was in the market value of the produce itself.
Likewise we do not impose an oath. See Sema, seif katan 63, who gives the example that the claimant says, "I gave you six se'ah to sow and you sowed only five," while the worker says he sowed five and a half. This is very difficult, as Shitah Mekubetzet already notes. Such a denial concerns what was done with the claimant's property and resembles a claim, "I repaid half by placing it in the land according to your instruction." Ramban and Ran therefore explain differently, such as where the claimant says, "You sold me six se'ah of produce that had not yet taken root," and the defendant admits only five.
