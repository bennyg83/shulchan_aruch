Even the one who committed the ona'ah. See Sema, seif katan 8, who says that once the injured party later accepts the transaction, it is treated as though he consented from the outset. According to this, if the buyer uses the item to betroth a woman while the sale is still voidable for ona'ah, and the buyer, who is the party who committed the ona'ah, wishes to cancel the sale and thereby the kiddushin before the seller accepts the transaction, the kiddushin would be void because one cannot betroth with property that is not his. But once the seller has accepted the sale, Sema’s retroactive theory would validate the kiddushin. In truth, Sema’s question from a seller who repeatedly demands payment does not compel his conclusion. Ona'ah is different. The wrongdoer’s inability to retract is based on the principle that he may not profit from his wrongdoing. The injured party can say: “Had you not overreached me, you could not have retracted. Shall your wrongdoing now give you a right to withdraw?” Tosafot in Bava Metzia 50 therefore hold that the wrongdoer cannot retract at all until the injured party demands the ona'ah. Other authorities hold that so long as the injured party himself may cancel, the transaction remains incomplete and even the wrongdoer can cancel. Once the injured party can no longer cancel, however, they too agree the wrongdoer cannot withdraw, so that a sinner not profit. On that approach the sale is not truly complete until the injured party accepts it, and kiddushin made in the interim should not take effect. Nevertheless, Sema’s conclusion appears correct in practice: if the original seller never actually retracts, his later acceptance reveals retroactively that he never intended to cancel, and the original sale stands from the outset. There is some support from the law of commerce “in trust,” where even if the seller himself was overreached the sale can remain valid, and if the original seller does not cancel, the first buyer’s title is treated as sufficiently established to validate the onward sale. We do not say that the second sale was necessarily void because at the moment it was made the first buyer was selling something not yet his. This indicates that nonexercise of the first seller’s cancellation right can confirm the first sale retroactively.
