The source of this ruling is the Mordechai, who writes that if the buyer knew of the overcharge and remained silent until later, and then summoned the seller to court, his silence is not a waiver. The proof is that Rava exempted the seller there only because he was a private householder, not because the buyer knew at the time of purchase that he had been overcharged. The Beit Yosef writes that he does not understand these words, for they appear contrary to the Mishna's rule concerning the time within which restitution must be demanded. The phrase “remained silent until later” was understood by the Beit Yosef as meaning until after the time allowed for showing the article to a merchant or relative. He therefore asked: just as ordinarily, once that time has elapsed, we assume that the buyer knew of the overcharge and waived it, so too here, where he knew from the outset. His only basis for recovery is that he later sued, which must mean within the time allowed for showing it, for afterward the court would not order restitution once he knew and waived. Nor can one say that he relied upon his initial declaration that he would not waive anything, and therefore even after the allotted time that declaration helps him. If so, he could have paid five and a half and relied upon his declaration that he waived nothing. What difference would there be between immediate waiver of an overcharge of less than one sixth and waiver of one sixth after the allotted time? Necessarily, he relied only upon the ordinary law allowing him to sue within the allotted time. This is why the Beit Yosef questioned the Mordechai's words “until later.” The Rama corrected the matter by writing here that the claim was made before the time for showing the article had elapsed. The Mordechai also speaks of this, and “later” means later than the sale, but still within that allotted period. I have written this to exclude the view I saw disputing the Rama. The Rambam further rules that one need not accept the deficient coin back unless one accepts it as an act of piety. The Maggid Mishneh questioned this and left it requiring study. It appears to me that the Rambam's inference is from the latter clause of the Mishna, which calls one who refuses to accept it a person of bad character. This refers to a coin that can still be spent, as the Gemara states explicitly, and the first and latter clauses concern the same kind of coin, as the language of the Mishna implies. Yet the first clause speaks of a coin that is not ordinarily spendable, for the Gemara explains that the period for claiming overcharge on a sela is twelve months because “it does not circulate,” which Rashi explains as not being accepted in ordinary payment. We must therefore say that it can be spent only with difficulty, and “does not circulate” means without difficulty. It follows that only regarding such a coin did the Mishna set the period until Friday or twelve months, and concerning that case the tanna said that as an act of piety he should accept it even after twelve months. We may nevertheless infer that if the coin cannot be spent at all, the recipient must accept it back as a matter of law, because he was given nothing but a shard. This applies whenever he recognizes it as the very coin he gave, as the Rambam writes. Although the Mishna states “whenever he recognizes it” regarding the act of piety, the tanna did not intend to distinguish recognition in law from recognition in piety, but only to distinguish the Friday period from twelve months. Regarding recognition there is no distinction. Even where one is legally obligated to accept the coin because it cannot circulate at all, he must recognize that it is his coin. The Tur's intent is likewise this when he writes, “provided that it can circulate with difficulty.” This qualifies the first clause, which says that the injured party is not obligated to exchange it, meaning where it can circulate with difficulty. If it cannot circulate even with difficulty, anyone must exchange it as a matter of law. In my humble opinion, the author of the Ir Shushan understood this correctly. As for the Sema in seif katan 31, who writes that if it cannot circulate at all there is not even an act of piety, and cites proof from the Maggid Mishneh and the Tur, I have not understood his reasoning. According to the Maggid Mishneh, the Mishna itself concerns a coin that cannot circulate at all, and even there it is an act of piety to accept it. How, then, could he write that according to the Tur there is not even an act of piety? I also know of no source for saying that where it cannot circulate at all there is no act of piety in accepting it, and this is not mentioned in the Rosh or the Kitzur Piskei HaRosh. Rather, the Tur and Rambam should be interpreted consistently, as I have written: whenever he recognizes the coin, even after twelve months, he must accept it if it cannot circulate at all; if it can circulate with difficulty, accepting it is an act of piety. See also the proof I brought from this passage for another matter at the end of siman 75.
