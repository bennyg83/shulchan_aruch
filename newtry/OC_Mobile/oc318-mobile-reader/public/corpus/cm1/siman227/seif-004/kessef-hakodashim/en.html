Seif 8. But a seller. If a seller claims that he was overreached by more than one-sixth and the purchaser says that he has already sent the movable goods to a distant place, perhaps the purchaser must immediately bring them back at his own expense so that they can be appraised. If the appraisal then shows no ground for voiding the sale and no refundable onaah, the seller who demanded the inspection should bear the expense. Even an overcharge below the refundable threshold is prohibited initially, but if the purchaser says that he did not know of any refundable onaah, and people commonly err by less than one-sixth, he should not be charged any part of the expense merely because the seller's demand caused it. Before ordering the purchaser to incur the expense, he should be warned that if he knows with certainty, whether from his own knowledge of the market or from trustworthy people, that there is refundable onaah or grounds to return the sale, he must accept the expense if his claim proves unfounded. If he is genuinely uncertain and refuses to accept that risk, he should accept a cherem that he does not know of refundable onaah and should include that the goods are not nearby. If he refuses the cherem, he should bear the expense himself. If the purchaser argues that because he did not know of the onaah he should remain exempt from expenses even if refundable onaah is later established, one may answer by analogy to the time limit of 'enough time to show it to a merchant': once that period passes, we presume the buyer learned of the onaah and waived it. A claim that he shipped the goods away before the period expired ordinarily does not excuse him, because he should not have sent them away before obtaining an appraisal. This requires study. If either party claims that the goods can be valued without bringing them back because many people saw them closely and can testify as though they were present, those witnesses should be questioned. If they can state with certainty that there is refundable onaah or that there is none, that should suffice. Once one knowledgeable person states that there certainly is no refundable onaah, the claimant may need two valid witnesses to extract money. If the other side has two such witnesses, perhaps one additional expert can resolve the appraisal as in other valuations, though this requires study. If the witnesses can say only that there is certainly some refundable onaah but that a physical inspection might show an amount large enough to void the sale, the earlier rules concerning expenses apply. If the seller does not believe that the purchaser sent the goods far away and the purchaser says that he can produce them later, beit din may grant the normal time to bring evidence or property, and no cherem is immediately required because he undertakes to produce the goods. If there is concern that by the time the goods return people will forget the market value or the value of the currency on the day and place of sale, every possible measure should now be taken to preserve that evidence. If that cannot be done, the purchaser may have to accept a cherem that he did not know of refundable onaah when he sent the goods away. This cherem may be appropriate even where several doubts combine, such as perhaps there was no onaah at all and perhaps the market price will not be forgotten. All this requires careful study. In a barter of one merchandise for another, where commercial practice values one item especially low and the other especially high so that the exchange balances, and each party naturally praises the value of his own merchandise, a party may perhaps say even after the time for showing it to a merchant that he assumed the merchandise of his own still in the other party's possession offset the apparent onaah.
