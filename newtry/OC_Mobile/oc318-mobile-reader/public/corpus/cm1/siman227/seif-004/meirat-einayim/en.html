The injured party may return the item. But he cannot demand merely repayment of the ona'ah, because the party who caused the ona'ah can say, "Either cancel your purchase or keep it on the terms on which you bought it." The Tur writes this.
But the party who caused the ona'ah cannot retract. A wrongdoer should not profit from his wrongdoing. Had he not caused the ona'ah he would have had no right to void the sale. See below, seif 10.
Even the party who caused the ona'ah can retract. Since the injured party has the right to retract, the transaction was not fully finalized, and therefore the other party can also retract, unless the injured party once affirmatively accepted the transaction. At that moment the sale is completed and the other party can no longer retract. The Tur writes at the end of the preceding siman and in siman 190 that where a seller repeatedly demands payment, once the sale is void as to the seller it is also void as to the purchaser, so even if the seller later agrees to maintain the sale, the purchaser may still retract, although his position is weaker. That case differs. The seller's repeated demand for payment immediately reveals that but for his need for money he would not have sold, so the sale becomes void at once when the money is not paid. His later willingness to maintain the sale cannot deprive the purchaser of a right to retract from a transaction already voided. Here, by contrast, the injured party did not initially reveal a desire to retract. Therefore, if he later accepts the transaction, it is treated as though he accepted it from the outset, and the party who caused the ona'ah can no longer retract.
For one calculation it is voiding the sale, etc. For example, if he sold property worth sixty for fifty-one. Measured against the merchandise value, the error is less than one sixth, since one sixth of sixty is ten and the seller lost only nine. But measured against the money received, the discrepancy is more than one sixth: dividing fifty-one into six equal parts gives eight and one half dinarim per part, while the seller lost nine, half a dinar more than one sixth of the money. Thus according to the money calculation the transaction would be void. If he sold property worth fifty-one for sixty, the reverse applies: measured against the merchandise the discrepancy is more than one sixth, while measured against the money paid it is less than one sixth, since one sixth of sixty is ten and the purchaser overpaid only nine.
We follow the merchandise, etc. It follows that selling sixty for fifty-one is treated as waiver, while selling fifty-one for sixty is treated as voiding the sale, according to the approach just explained.
Because people err, etc. Therefore, if the disparity is less than one sixth when measured against the merchandise, even though it is more than one sixth measured against the money, we do not say that people commonly err in counting the money itself. Rather, we say he waived that difference. This explains Mordechai's view. It is difficult, however, because it does not align with the Maggid Mishneh cited above. Maggid Mishneh holds that if property worth sixty is sold for fifty-one, it is not treated as waiver until the price reaches fifty-one and a half, because only then is the discrepancy less than one sixth of the money by one fourteenth of a dinar, as explained above. Since the Mechaber carefully ruled in seif 3 according to the Maggid Mishneh, as I explained, and Rama did not object there, Rama should not here have stated Mordechai's rule without qualification as though everyone agreed. It is strained to say that Mordechai's statement that selling sixty for fifty-one is "waiver" is imprecise and merely means it is not void as a sale even though the discrepancy exceeds one sixth from the money side, and that since from the other side it would be waiver, we instead classify it as ordinary ona'ah. The central point would then be missing. The reverse difficulty also arises: Maggid Mishneh writes that if property worth fifty is sold for fifty-eight and one third, which is one sixth of the merchandise, it is ona'ah. The same is true if it is sold for fifty-eight and a half, fifty-nine, or sixty; all are treated as ona'ah even though the purchaser's overpayment increases, until the price exceeds sixty, when the sale becomes void. Mordechai, however, holds that fifty-one sold for sixty is void even though the overpayment is only nine. We must therefore say that Mordechai holds that whenever one of the two calculations gives exactly one sixth, whether based on the merchandise value or the money, the law is ona'ah, even if the other calculation gives less or more than one sixth. But where neither calculation yields exactly one sixth, he follows the discrepancy as measured against the merchandise. Rambam and the Tur, who write that selling property worth sixty for fifty plus a perutah is waiver, in my opinion can likewise agree with Mordechai if they mean specifically this case, where the discrepancy is neither one sixth of the merchandise nor one sixth of the money. Therefore they follow the merchandise side, where it is less than one sixth, and treat it as waiver. They do not write that selling property worth fifty plus a perutah for sixty is waiver, because there too neither side is exactly one sixth, but measured against the merchandise the discrepancy is more than one sixth and the sale is void under Rambam's view. Maggid Mishneh, however, cannot be reconciled with Mordechai in this manner. It follows that Rama's presentation of Mordechai immediately after leaving the Mechaber's ruling, which follows Maggid Mishneh, unchallenged presents two inconsistent approaches in the same matter and requires study.
