One who exchanges utensils for utensils. Sema writes that this applies even where the utensils were appraised before the exchange. See Nachalat Tzvi, where I cite many authorities who disagree. I therefore concluded in practice that with utensils, if they were appraised beforehand and the party claiming ona'ah is currently in possession, property is not removed from him because he may say, "I rely upon the authorities who rule in my favor." See there at length. Baer Heitev writes that for many years he was uncertain whether the rule applies even where the objects were intended for commerce, and he concluded that it does. Responsa Radbaz, part 4, siman 269, does not rule so. He holds that specifically householders who exchange one utensil for another or one animal for another have no ona'ah claim, but utensils and animals held for commerce are subject to ona'ah even according to Rambam. Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 12, cites this Radbaz as practical law, but adds that this is specifically where the objects were appraised either before or afterward. If they were never appraised in money, neither before nor afterward, there is no ona'ah claim, for the case is no worse than produce exchanged for produce. Shitah Mekubetzet at the end of the chapter HaZahav, Bava Metzia 46, cites Responsa Ri Migash: one who exchanges merchandise with another for merchandise without appraising either in money cannot claim ona'ah, because our principle is that barter is not subject to ona'ah. According to this, Radbaz and Rashdam need not disagree, as Bnei Chayei assumed when cited by Baer Heitev. Rashdam can be speaking where the items were not appraised, and Radbaz where they were. This also resolves Machaneh Ephraim's objection in Hilchot Mechirah, siman 8, to Radbaz from Shitah Mekubetzet in the name of Ri Migash, which indicates that even merchandise exchanged for merchandise has no ona'ah. There is no contradiction once Radbaz is understood as speaking specifically after monetary appraisal. In my humble opinion, in such a case, if the ona'ah exceeds half the value, the transaction can be rescinded. For goods held for commerce, Rambam's reasoning that each party may prefer the particular item he receives does not apply. The exemption must instead derive from the scriptural exclusion cited by the students of Rashba. If so, where the ona'ah exceeds half, the exchange should be void just as with land. See Nachalat Tzvi, s.v. Ein Lahem Ona'ah. If one party acquired his utensil for personal use and the other acquired the other utensil for commerce, the law resembles exchange of a utensil for produce, which will be discussed in the next seif katan. It also appears that if the parties dispute their intent, the claimant saying, "I took it for commerce," and the other saying, "You took it for personal use," the matter may be learned from siman 232, seif 23. This requires study. See Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 1, siman 166, concerning partners who exchanged a ring with Shimon for produce. The produce was worth about twenty gold coins at the market, but when Shimon showed the ring to a merchant it was appraised at only six. Shimon sought to annul the transaction because he had been overreached by more than one sixth, while the partners argued that barter has no ona'ah. Shav Yaakov replied that one cannot resolve the matter merely from Rambam's wording, "one who exchanges utensils for utensils," implying that utensils for produce are subject to ona'ah, because one could infer the opposite from Rambam's later phrase "one who exchanges produce for produce." Nevertheless, in such a case ona'ah does apply. Rambam's reason must be understood carefully. If the reason utensils exchanged for utensils are exempt is merely that one person may prefer a needle to armor, then the same should be true where he buys a utensil for money, and there too ona'ah should never apply. Rather, the special case is where each has a utensil he does not need and desires the other's utensil, and neither has or wishes to use cash. It is relatively uncommon for two people to meet who each happen to need exactly what the other wishes to exchange. Because of this unusual mutual preference, the law does not apply ona'ah in the ordinary way. This also explains why the poskim do not distinguish between a utensil commonly available in the market and one that is not, as Rambam distinguishes in Hilchot Pikadon and Choshen Mishpat, siman 72. But where one purchases a utensil for money, or even for produce that has an established market price and therefore functions like money, the transaction is ordinary and ona'ah applies. People commonly say that with money one can buy anything. Therefore, in Shav Yaakov's case, where a utensil was exchanged for produce, ona'ah applies and the transaction was void for excessive ona'ah. These are his words. Radbaz, however, at the end of siman 269, writes differently. He says that where one exchanges a utensil for produce or other merchandise, the party who receives the utensil has no ona'ah claim because he needs that specific utensil, but the owner of the produce does have an ona'ah claim, for he can say, "Why should I lose my legal protection merely because the owner of the utensil has none?" Radbaz therefore clearly assumes that the recipient of the utensil remains exempt. Nevertheless, Shav Yaakov's reasoning appears persuasive. His wording, however, seems to speak where there was no appraisal, which requires study, because where there was no appraisal even produce exchanged for produce is not subject to ona'ah, as Kesef Mishneh and Sema, seif katan 36, write. One must say either that the ona'ah in his case exceeded double, as discussed in Nachalat Tzvi, s.v. Ein Lo, or that he follows Machaneh Ephraim's interpretation of Rambam in Hilchot Mechirah, siman 8, as explained in Nachalat Tzvi, s.v. HaMachlif. There is also room to consider a case where one exchanges a utensil for another utensil plus money, for example where one utensil is appraised at ten gold coins, the other at five, and the latter party adds five gold coins. At first glance, the law should be the same as pure barter of utensil for utensil, which has no ona'ah even after appraisal, at least where the alleged wrongdoer is in possession, as discussed in the preceding seif katan.
