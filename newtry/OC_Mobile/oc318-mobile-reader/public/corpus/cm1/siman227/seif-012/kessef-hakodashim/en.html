I further found. A wife who regularly conducts her husband's business may perhaps be treated more favorably than an ordinary agent with respect to onaah below one-sixth. The Shach writes regarding partners that, because of market stability, a partner's act can bind the partnership even where he deviated somewhat. With onaah below one-sixth he apparently need not reimburse his partner either, even though partners are agents and paid custodians for one another, because such a minor pricing error is practically unavoidable and people commonly err by that amount without objecting. Perhaps even if partners are liable for some accidents, this kind of commercial error is still exempt because they agreed that either partner may sell, and if every such sale exposed the acting partner to liability no partner would be willing to transact. Even if the partners had publicly declared that they revoke one another's agency for any deviation whatever, a purchaser who acquires with less-than-one-sixth onaah may resemble one who buys from a thief not publicly known as such, so that market-protection principles still apply. The same may be true for wives who conduct their husbands' business. One cannot simply treat the wife as a co-owner merely because perhaps the property is hers, is property over which her husband has no control, is her nichsei melog, or because she owns a small portion of the merchandise. With respect to her husband's property she has no ownership merely because he must support her. Perhaps there is a presumption that all the business property is his until it becomes publicly established that she has separate nichsei melog with which she trades. Even if in most such households some property belongs to the wife, monetary law does not follow a majority against a possessor, though perhaps the ordinary legal subordination of her hand to his creates an additional presumption. Still, market-protection principles may validate her ordinary transactions. Yet even if women today are generally considered authorized business operators, it has not been established that they may sell without their husbands' knowledge and consent in situations involving a doubtful onaah, perhaps even below one-sixth. An ordinary agent, despite broad authority, cannot knowingly make even a small deviation where the principal has not consented, and a transaction may therefore be reversible. A partner is stronger because he unquestionably sells his own share and can ordinarily reimburse the other partner for any loss; a wife may lack that ability. This requires further study. As to my statement that a partner need not reimburse for a possible onaah below one-sixth, if commercial practice in that market was such that he should have known the true value, liability may be different. One cannot automatically extend the Sages' fixed rule that onaah below one-sixth is waived to every internal question between partners.
