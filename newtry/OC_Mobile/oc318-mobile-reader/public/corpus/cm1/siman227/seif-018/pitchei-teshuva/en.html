In a transaction conducted on trust, there is no ona'ah claim against him, and so forth. See Netivot HaMishpat, who cites Responsa Maharit, part 2, siman 19, as asking why this should differ from one who did not say, "on condition that you have no ona'ah claim against me," who remains liable for ona'ah as stated above, seif 21. Maharit answers that one who buys and sells on trust is treated as though the seller became the purchaser's agent to buy the item. Netivot HaMishpat regards this as forced and offers a simpler distinction. According to his reasoning, if the seller already learned that he himself had been overreached and then sold the item on trust while overreaching the next purchaser, that purchaser may retract. See there further. At first glance, this should apply specifically to the formulation of Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, where the seller says, "I purchased this item for such and such amount," because there the exemption requires Netivot's reasoning. The seller does not know the object's true value; he actually paid that amount for it, and the Torah did not prohibit trading honestly on the basis of what he paid. Consequently, if he learned beforehand that he had been overreached, he may no longer overreach the next purchaser, and restitution applies. But according to Rabbeinu Chananel, the alternative view cited by Tur, the seller ordinarily does not disclose at the time of sale what he paid. The purchaser says only, "I trust whatever you tell me you paid, and I will add such and such profit." In that form, Maharit's question does not arise, as Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchot Ona'ah, at the end of siman 16, explains. The purchaser plainly does not rely on his own estimate, because he does not yet know the amount. He accepts whatever result emerges, whether high or low. It may therefore be that even if the seller knew beforehand that he had been overreached, the later purchaser cannot retract, because he knowingly accepted the transaction on that basis. This requires study. See Responsa Galia Masechta, siman 1, who discusses this at length and advances a novel analysis. In essence, he argues that Rabbeinu Chananel and Rambam address two separate situations. According to Rabbeinu Chananel, the baraita's statement that "he has no ona'ah" refers to ona'ah suffered by the current seller when he bought from the first seller. He therefore interprets the case specifically as one in which the new purchaser does not ask at the time of purchase for the purchase price, but trusts whatever the seller will later say and obligates himself to pay that amount plus the agreed profit, whether the seller's original price was high or low. Rambam, by contrast, interprets the seller as saying, "I bought this item for such and such," and understands the sugya not as addressing ona'ah in the seller's original purchase, but rather the seller's agreed profit above the amount he paid the first seller. The profit itself is exempt because the transaction resembles one where the purchaser expressly says, "I know it is worth only one hundred," and nevertheless agrees to pay more. He discusses this at length and concludes that under modern merchant practice, where a wholesaler tells a shopkeeper on his trust that he paid a certain amount and they agree on a stated percentage profit, if it is later discovered that the wholesaler himself was overreached when he bought the goods, the shopkeeper may recover the ona'ah. According to Rambam this is clear, because the exemption applies only to the disclosed profit, which the shopkeeper knowingly waives, not to the principal price itself. Even according to Rabbeinu Chananel, ona'ah applies here because a shopkeeper ordinarily asks the amount of the principal purchase price. In siman 3, however, Rabbi David, Av Beit Din of Mir, responds to all of Galia Masechta's arguments and concludes that the practical law is clear: in merchant transactions conducted on trust in the customary manner, ona'ah does not apply at all. This follows the early authorities and leading later authorities, and we may not depart from their words based on novel reasoning without clear proofs. He writes that this was the consistent practice of batei din. Galia Masechta then writes again to defend and strengthen his position, praising his own analysis as the correct understanding of Rambam and asserting that one who rules otherwise risks error in an explicit law. Although his discussion is sophisticated and well reasoned, it opposes the position of numerous early authorities and leading later authorities, and we certainly may not depart from them. Rabbi David of Mir further writes that even according to Rambam, the exemption should perhaps apply specifically where the seller regularly conducts business on trust, and the purchaser and others know this, as among contemporary merchants. There it is reasonable that neither principal nor profit is subject to ona'ah, because the purchaser does not base the transaction on market value at all; he knows the seller prices according to what he himself paid. But where a person who does not ordinarily trade on trust happens to pass through the market and says, "I bought this item for such and such and want to profit such and such," and the purchaser agrees, this does not necessarily prove that the sale was truly concluded on a trust basis. The purchaser may simply have erred and believed that the item was worth the amount stated. His declaration that he trusts the seller may merely be empty language if in his own mind he assumes the market value corresponds to the seller's statement. If ona'ah is later discovered, the purchaser may therefore retract. Rabbeinu Chananel can agree with Rambam in practical law. His explanation of "trading on trust" means that the transaction itself was demonstrably conducted on trust, which is certainly the case where the purchaser does not know the purchase price and simply says, "I trust whatever amount you state, and I will add such and such." All the more so where the seller regularly trades this way. Although Rabbi David regarded this distinction as clear in his own reasoning and found no authority who expressly disagreed, he refrained from ruling conclusively because the unqualified language of the poskim does not distinguish, and appears to exempt all transactions made on trust.
