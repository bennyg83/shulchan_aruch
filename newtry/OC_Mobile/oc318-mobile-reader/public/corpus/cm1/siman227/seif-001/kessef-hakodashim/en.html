Seif 2. How much must the overcharge be? If a purchaser buys pieces cut from old garments in order to make a garment for himself, and the seller cuts the needed pieces from larger remnants, the appraisal of onaah may sometimes have to include not only the value of the pieces delivered but also the loss in value caused to the larger remnants by cutting them, where the purchaser saw that the seller was cutting those pieces specifically for him. If the purchaser did not know whether the pieces came from larger remnants or whether the seller already had pieces of exactly the needed dimensions, even though he knew they were old cloth rather than new, it appears that only the value of what he received should be appraised. If he reasonably should have anticipated that cutting would reduce the seller's remnants, the matter may resemble the rule that where both parties know the relevant circumstance the worker bears the loss, but one must still determine whether possession or the presumption sustaining the sale controls. Regarding the period of 'enough time to show the merchandise to a merchant,' where appraisal requires examining the reduction in value of remnants that remain with the seller, the purchaser may be treated like a seller who can claim onaah indefinitely because the relevant property is not in his possession and he cannot obtain a meaningful appraisal without seeing the seller's remaining cloth. By contrast, where appraisal concerns only the pieces in the buyer's hands, but the buyer immediately delivered them to a tailor because of urgency before Yom Tov or similar circumstances known to the seller, the time limit may begin only when the garment is returned to him. Although the tailor may sometimes understand such values, the buyer may not regard that particular tailor as a reliable appraiser, and the tailor may have an interest in completing the work rather than encouraging cancellation. The beit din must evaluate the circumstances. If one buys the meat of a live animal with an agreed price structure under which there would be one-sixth onaah if it proves kosher but no onaah if it proves treifah, the different possible values may perhaps be treated together because the parties knowingly accepted both possibilities in one transaction. The internal organs, whose fat content was unknown, are likewise part of the same overall animal sale. Onaah in meat must also account for the costs and losses involved in retail cutting and selling, including trimmings and weight loss. Since the sale may take effect only upon slaughter at a specified place, valuation may perhaps follow the time and place of slaughter. With a money transaction stipulated to take effect after thirty days, no 'from now' is needed even if the money has been spent, but with situmta or a handshake it appears the acquisition may not operate for a future time unless made effective from now. Kinyan sudar, however, ordinarily operates as if effective from now. If the purchaser later takes the animal for slaughter but says it may be slaughtered only in his presence and it is slaughtered in his absence, perhaps he never acquired it. If no time for slaughter was fixed, valuation may follow the time he actually receives the meat, though ordinarily within a few days for kosher meat. Where they did not specify whether hindquarters would be kashered, local practice controls, and where most are or are not kashered one must remember that monetary law does not generally follow a majority against possession. In a sale 'from now and after thirty days,' onaah is measured at the time of the sale, because the later date is only a condition unless he retracts. In a sale effective only after thirty days, valuation appears to follow the later date. Advance payment until that date may also raise ribbit concerns if a known price premium is charged for waiting. Where a small unspecified portion is sold from a larger mass and the seller retains the right to choose what to deliver, so that risk has not yet passed, perhaps the sale does not take effect until selection. I have discussed this elsewhere, and the relative market values may provide evidence. If wheat infested with insects is sold and moved to another house where the insects are no longer visible and its value increases, and without the insects it would have been worth one-sixth more, the sale may be void if on the day of sale it could readily have been moved within the same city to a clean place where the insects would not appear. A simple relocation within the city is not considered a different market. In villages, value is often measured by the nearest city. If on that day there was no practical way to make the wheat clean, valuation by a later day's condition may be improper unless it was already certain that ordinary relocation would clean it. If a buyer receives only part of a varied lot and the rest remains with the seller, the time limit for showing the goods to a merchant may not run where the buyer cannot determine whether the undelivered portion would alter the overall one-sixth calculation. This is especially strong if the buyer still holds the money. Where the goods are animals or perishable merchandise and the buyer slaughtered or resold them before discovering the onaah, that use does not waive the claim unless he knew of the defect or overcharge before using them. He may have been compelled to sell after slaughter or because of spoilage. Even if he could estimate onaah while the animals were alive, he might reasonably wait to see whether they prove kosher and thereby change the economics of the transaction, though perhaps once he knew that there was already onaah in their live value he should have rescinded. This requires careful assessment. If the seller undertook to deliver merchandise to a specified place at his own risk, the onaah measure ordinarily follows the place and time where the sale itself took effect if the acquisition was already effective from now and the delivery obligation was merely ancillary. Even an express condition of delivery can cancel the transaction if unfulfilled, but does not necessarily shift the valuation date when ownership was already acquired.
