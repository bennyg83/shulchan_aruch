Seif 2. How much constitutes onaah? Note: A case came before me involving a grain merchant who purchased thirty kor of wheat and milled them into flour. After the flour had already been produced, he sold it to another person by calculating its price according to the value of each kor of wheat, so that the purchaser would pay according to the price assigned to thirty kor of wheat. Both seller and purchaser knew that the wheat had already been milled. The purchaser later claimed that those thirty kor yielded unusually little flour, so that he would sustain a large loss from the transaction. I ruled as follows. If one sells wheat while it is still wheat, the seller has no responsibility for how much flour it later yields. He sold wheat, not flour. We therefore evaluate the wheat itself. If experts say wheat of that quality was worth the sale price, or only slightly less, below the statutory measure of onaah, the purchaser cannot void the sale or claim onaah merely because the milling yield was poor. But in the case before us the wheat no longer existed at the time of sale; only flour existed. It is impossible to say that the sale attached to wheat that was no longer in the world. The sale necessarily applied to the flour. If calculating the payment by reference to thirty kor of wheat produces a price exceeding the flour's value by the measure of onaah, then onaah is returned at one-sixth and the transaction is void when the excess is more than one-sixth. This is clear in practice. I remain somewhat uncertain about another point. Suppose the purchaser relied upon the seller's representation that the original quantity had been thirty kor and later says he no longer believes that statement. If he did not expressly agree to rely upon the seller's personal credibility, he can certainly later say that he does not believe the quantity was as stated. Even if at the outset he stipulated that he was purchasing on the seller's credibility that there had been thirty kor, it still appears that he may retract so long as there was no formal kinyan sudar creating that evidentiary commitment. Payment, a customary handshake undertaking among merchants, or physical taking can acquire the tangible merchandise that exists, but if in truth there were fewer than thirty kor, the nonexistent quantity cannot be acquired through taking or payment. Liability for the missing amount would arise solely because he accepted the seller as trustworthy, comparable to the rule "my father is trustworthy to me" and similar evidentiary stipulations. Such acceptance requires a formal kinyan under siman 22. Without it, he may retract. This appears clear and is a useful general principle in many cases. There, seif 7: Until when may he retract, etc.; until he can show it to a merchant. It requires study why the authorities omitted Tosafot in Bava Metzia 49b, s.v. "within the time needed to show it to a merchant." Tosafot ask that this would make the law depend on variable distances, since sometimes merchants are far away and sometimes nearby. They answer that there is one fixed standard based on the ordinary amount of time in which most people can show the item to a merchant or relative. If in a particular case merchants are unusually close or unusually distant, the period is neither shortened nor extended. This is an important rule, yet the codifiers omitted it. Tosafot Yom Tov cites it. It is even more difficult that Taz here writes that if no merchants were present and later arrived, the period is measured from their arrival. That appears contrary to Tosafot. I have not seen anyone who expressly disputes Tosafot, and the matter requires great study. There, gloss: If the purchaser knew, etc. See my Torah writings to Parashat Toldot, year 5614, where I wrote that this distinction applies to a purchaser. A seller who knows that he is selling below value is deemed immediately to have waived the deficiency. The reason is that Rabbi Yehudah already holds that a householder selling his personal utensils is not subject to onaah, because he sells only under pressure and therefore consents despite the loss. Although we do not rule fully in accordance with that opinion, where the seller actually knew he was underpricing his property, we may say that because of his need he consciously accepted the loss and waived it. See there. I later found that Or HaChaim on that parashah cites Maharik to a similar effect.
