And he did not receive payment for the appraisal. Rashbatz was asked about a buyer who showed a parcel of ore to an expert goldsmith and said, "Examine whether it is pure gold. If it is pure I will give you half the profit as your fee." The expert said it appeared pure based on its weight. The buyer relied on him and bought it, but upon smelting it proved to be copper. Was the expert liable as a paid expert? Rashbatz ruled he was exempt. Liability for negligent professional advice is stronger when the expert receives a fixed fee for the appraisal. Here, if the material were false, there would be no profit and therefore no fee. The expert's supposed compensation was only a share of a profit that would exist if his conclusion turned out correct. When his negligence causes the loss, he receives nothing. Such a contingent share is not the type of fee that makes him a paid professional for liability purposes. Rashbatz compares Ramban's discussion concerning orphan funds and concludes that reliance alone does not make the unpaid expert liable.
A gloss on that responsum objects from Tur in siman 306, where slaughterers who receive the innards of kosher animals are considered paid even though they receive nothing from tereifah animals. If so, perhaps this expert too should be treated as paid because, had the gold been genuine, he would have received his share. The printed gloss appears to contain an error and should read, "because if it had been good, he would have received his fee." Nevertheless, Rashbatz is correct. A paid custodian or slaughterer is promised compensation for performing the task properly. If he guards properly or slaughters properly, he receives his fee. If through his negligence the object is lost or the slaughter is invalid, he must pay and loses his fee. The fee is therefore compensation for proper performance. Here, however, the goldsmith was promised nothing merely for examining carefully. If he had examined perfectly and thereby discovered that the material was counterfeit, he would receive no fee at all because there would be no profit. His compensation was not for the quality of his appraisal but only a share of a profit that would exist if the property happened to be genuine. Proper performance itself did not guarantee compensation. That is fundamentally different from an ordinary paid professional. Therefore he remains unpaid for liability purposes and is exempt. This distinction is clear.
