See Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, siman 85, concerning an enactment established in his community long before, eliminating ona'ah claims in trade in precious stones and pearls. The enactment stated in substance: Since it is common for people to raise ona'ah claims in transactions involving precious stones and pearls, and most members of the community earn their livelihood from that trade, and because such claims make it impossible to conduct the business properly, the community sought rabbinic guidance how to regulate the matter without violating Torah law. The sages ruled that an ona'ah claim in the trade of precious stones and pearls would remain available only while the merchandise was still in the seller's hand. Once the purchaser took the goods from the seller and left, even if he had not yet paid and had not signed a bill of exchange, he would be obligated to retain the merchandise at the agreed price and would have no ona'ah claim. A case later arose where the seller himself had been overreached by more than half and sued the purchaser. Knesset Yechezkel writes that the enactment appears to have been made only concerning purchasers, for the commercial reason stated, not sellers, who remain governed by ordinary Torah law. Moreover, even for a purchaser the enactment applies only where the ona'ah is less than half. If he paid twice the true value, the enactment does not apply. The text itself expressly says that the community did not wish to legislate against Torah law. How, then, could the sages have authorized an enactment contrary to Torah law? Rather, they must have adopted the view of Rabbeinu Chananel, who rules like Rabbi Yehudah, and this is within the authority of sages to do for social regulation, as the Rosh writes that sages may select one recognized halachic opinion for the sake of public order. According to Rabbi Yehudah as well, however, there is ona'ah where the price is double, as stated explicitly in the chapter HaZahav. Therefore, he concludes without hesitation that the seller remains governed by ordinary Torah law, and if the purchaser was overreached by double the value, the sale is void even at the jubilee fairs.
