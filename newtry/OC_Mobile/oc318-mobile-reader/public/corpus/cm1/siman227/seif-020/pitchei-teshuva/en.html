One who exchanges utensils for utensils. Sema writes that this applies even where the utensils were appraised before the exchange. In Nachalat Tzvi I cited many authorities who dispute this and concluded that if the utensils were appraised and the aggrieved party is in possession, money is not extracted from him because he may plead kim li. Radbaz IV, siman 269, limits the no-onaah rule to private owners exchanging one utensil or animal for another for personal use; merchandise or animals held for trade remain subject to onaah even according to Rambam. Netivot HaMishpat adopts this, but adds that if neither side was appraised before or afterward there is no onaah, based on Shitah Mekubetzet citing Ri Migash that a pure barter transaction without any monetary valuation is not governed by onaah. Thus Radbaz and Rashdam may not disagree: one can refer to appraised goods and the other to unappraised barter. Pitchei Teshuva adds that where the goods are held for commerce and the only exclusion is the special law of barter, a discrepancy greater than half should still void the transaction, analogous to land. If one party takes the utensil for personal use while the other takes the exchanged item for trade, the case resembles exchanging a utensil for produce. Where the parties dispute whether the item was taken for trade or personal use, the issue may be analogous to siman 232:23. Shevut Yaakov I, siman 166, discusses partners who exchanged a ring for produce worth about twenty gold coins, while the ring proved worth only about six. He rules that there is onaah. Rambam's reason for exempting utensil-for-utensil exchange is that each party may subjectively prefer the particular utensil, and such a mutual coincidence of needs is unusual. That rationale does not apply where a utensil is bought for money or for produce having a known market price, which functions like money. Therefore the ring-for-produce exchange was a mistaken transaction and could be rescinded. Radbaz had suggested that the party receiving the desired utensil may have no onaah claim while the party giving produce does, but Pitchei Teshuva finds Shevut Yaakov's reasoning more persuasive. He notes, however, that if no valuation at all was made, even produce-for-produce barter generally has no onaah, unless the discrepancy is extreme. He also raises the unresolved case of exchanging one utensil for another plus a cash equalization.
He has no claim of onaah. Radbaz IV, siman 269, states that even cancellation for a discrepancy greater than one sixth does not apply to true utensil-for-utensil barter, because Rambam's example of a needle for armor necessarily contemplates an enormous difference in value. Radbaz also initially suggested that a defect in the article would not cancel barter, but that is untenable. Radbaz himself later concludes that where the needle or armor has an actual defect, the transaction may be rescinded because the purchaser can say, "I needed an intact utensil." Netivot HaMishpat likewise states simply that claims of defect unquestionably remain, since a person does not ordinarily accept defects, and Galya Masechta agrees.
After the sale. Netivot HaMishpat writes that this applies only while the parties are still engaged in the same matter. There is no reason for a valuation made after the transaction has ended to retroactively affect what already occurred.
