The market price changed. At first glance this means only that if there was no ona'ah at the time of sale, the buyer cannot later claim that because during the inspection period the price moved to a level that would have constituted cancellation, the original transaction should now be treated as incomplete. A later market movement is not ona'ah. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat siman 19, however, explains more broadly: even where witnesses know there was ona'ah at the moment of sale, if the market price changed before the injured party protested, he can no longer cancel. Ordinarily, if the party truly discovered a comparable sale during the claim period, one expects him to protest immediately. If he remained silent while the price stayed the same, we infer he had not yet encountered such a comparison. But once the market changes, his present protest may be motivated only by the new price. It is possible he already discovered the ona'ah earlier but did not care enough to cancel until the market moved. According to this explanation, Rama’s following rule requires analysis, as discussed below.
The injured party must bring proof. Ketzot cites Mordechai that this differs from R. Nachman’s rule that where a person is presently healthy he bears the burden of proving that he had previously been a shechiv mera. There we follow the present state because most people are healthy; here market prices commonly change, so the current price creates no comparable presumption. Ketzot asks from Even HaEzer siman 46, where if we do not know whether an item was worth a perutah at kiddushin but it is worth a perutah now, the kiddushin can be treated as certain, even though prices change. In my view there is no difficulty. We do not destroy a presently established acquisition because of mere doubt. When the woman is now before us as one whose kiddushin item is worth a perutah, and she has already been treated as betrothed, we do not manufacture a doubt that would undermine the existing legal status. Taz in siman 103 and Rashba regarding contradictory witnesses similarly state that an established kinyan is not undone because of uncertainty. Mordechai’s distinction regarding a shechiv mera addresses the opposite situation. There, when the deed first comes before beit din, the very wording leaves it uncertain whether it was made as a healthy person’s deed or a shechiv mera gift. The recipient has not yet displaced the prior owner’s chazakah. If present health nevertheless allowed the recipient to overcome prior ownership, that would show a very strong present-state presumption. Mordechai says market price lacks such force because prices ordinarily fluctuate. Thus the existing ownership is not disturbed merely by current market value. In addition, we do not ordinarily presume people acted as thieves; absent evidence, an ordinary sale is presumed not to contain ona'ah. According to Maharit’s interpretation that witnesses know there was ona'ah at the sale and the issue is only whether the party discovered it in time, one might think the wrongdoer should bear the burden, because cancellation-level ona'ah is already established. Perhaps the answer follows Maharit’s logic: ordinarily we assume a comparable transaction becomes known during the inspection period. If the injured party protested while prices remained unchanged, his silence until then supports the inference that he had not yet encountered a comparison. But where he protests only after a market change, there is a competing obvious explanation for his protest, namely the changed price. We then presume that he had already had an opportunity to discover the original ona'ah and chose not to act until the market moved.
