But if the market price changed. Panim Meirot II, end of siman 8, writes that where a seller undertook to supply hides he did not yet possess and, before supply, the market rose or fell enough to create onaah, the ordinary rule that a later price change does not permit rescission may not apply. That rule concerns goods already in existence and owned by the seller at the time of sale. Here the seller undertook to acquire future supply; if no payment was given and the obligation was created by deed, the binding undertaking may still be subject to onaah when the goods are later acquired and their then value is measured. If the buyer paid the full price at the outset, by contrast, the transaction is treated as economically fixed at that time, so later appreciation or depreciation belongs to the buyer. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 4, is more restrictive: if at the time of contract both parties knew prices could rise or fall and they made a fully binding kinyan, ordinary fluctuations were accepted in advance and do not create onaah. Only an extraordinary change that beit din determines was beyond reasonable contemplation may justify relief. If he sold because of his distress. Responsa Rishmei Sheelah, siman 53, discusses a woman who, while her husband was away, sold his tallit for one third of its value, explaining that she acted out of necessity. Years later the husband returned and sought to void the sale. At first glance he cannot, because Even HaEzer 70:5 rules that a wife whose husband went overseas may sell property for support, and Beit Shmuel states that the husband cannot later recover what she validly sold. Moreover, women who manage the household commonly have authority to sell ordinary property, especially minor items, and siman 227 provides that one who knowingly sells under financial pressure cannot later rescind for onaah. Yet the responsum distinguishes: a person may choose to dissipate his own property, but it is not clear that a wife has authority to sacrifice her husband's property at a grossly inadequate price when she could have sold through beit din. If she made no effort to determine the item's value and simply sold immediately to one buyer, that may resemble negligence by a custodian. Her credibility concerning whether she acted negligently may require an oath. The responsum concludes that if the decisor finds the opposing considerations balanced, a compromise is appropriate.
