Seif 20. One who exchanges utensils for utensils, etc. Note: There is room to question the law where utensils are exchanged for produce. Does onaah apply? The opening clause, which specifically says utensils for utensils, implies that utensils for produce are subject to onaah. The closing clause, which says produce for produce, could imply the opposite. The wording therefore proves nothing. By reasoning, onaah should apply. The rationale that one person may value a needle more than armor applies only when both sides of the exchange are utensils, each desired for its particular utility. If one side is not a utensil, that rationale cannot be used. Otherwise every sale of a utensil for money would escape onaah on the claim that the purchaser wants the utensil more than the money. Since a monetary sale is subject to onaah, an exchange of a utensil for produce should likewise be subject to onaah, because produce itself is subject to valuation and onaah and there is no basis to say each side simply desired the other's unique object more. For this reason I ruled that if one sells an object on condition that the purchaser perform a certain amount of labor for him, the transaction is subject to onaah. It cannot be exempted as barter, because the labor side is not an article and is even more analogous to produce. It is comparable to merchandise, regarding which onaah applies, as Radbaz rules and the later authorities cite. The same applies where merchandise is sold for an object or vice versa. One must also distinguish this from the rule that a hired worker is not subject to onaah. We follow the principal nature of the transaction. If the principal agreement is one of employment and the employer promises an object as the worker's wage, the law of employment governs and there is no onaah. But if the principal agreement is a sale of the object, and in payment of its price the purchaser undertakes to perform work, the transaction has the law of sale and is subject to onaah. This appears clear in practice.
