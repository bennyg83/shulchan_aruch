Or where he purchased through a broker. Sema explains that even if the buyer knew the goods belonged to a householder, once the householder handed them to a broker to sell to whoever came, the householder must have assumed the buyer would not know they were his and therefore delivered them to the broker subject to the ordinary right to recover ona'ah. Taz finds this astonishing. Perhaps the householder instructed the broker not to sell except for a fixed price above market value and the broker did exactly that. Since the buyer knew the proceeds would reach the householder who sent the broker, the buyer knowingly caused his own loss.
Sema's view appears correct. Even if a buyer knows at the time of purchase that an item is not worth the price and nevertheless pays more, he can still claim ona'ah within the time needed to show it to a merchant, as Rama rules in seif 7. See what I wrote in seif katan 4. The essential rule is that where the seller knows the buyer understands the merchandise and its true value and nevertheless demands more than its value, the seller is effectively stipulating that the sale is on condition of waiver, and the buyer likewise purchases on that understanding. But if the seller does not know that the buyer knows the true value, the seller has not made any such condition, because he assumes the buyer is unaware of the market value. In that case, even if the buyer actually did know the value, he has not waived his claim and can recover the ona'ah. When buying directly from a householder, both know the circumstances and the buyer purchases with awareness of the householder's implicit condition of waiver. Through a broker, however, the broker may not know that the buyer recognizes the merchandise as belonging to a householder. Therefore, even though the buyer himself knows the ownership, the case is like Rama's seif 7, where the buyer knows the value but can nevertheless claim ona'ah. This is precise.
