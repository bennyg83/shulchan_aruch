And consecrated property. See Sema, who writes that property consecrated for the poor or for a synagogue has the law of ordinary property. See Responsa Shav Yaakov, part 1, at the end of siman 166, who cites Responsa Be'er Esek, siman 3, as writing in his case that there could be no ona'ah claim because consecrated property is one of the categories exempt from ona'ah. The exemption is derived from "when you sell a sale to your fellow," excluding consecrated property, and he argued similarly that "a man with his brother" excludes property of the community. Shav Yaakov sharply rejects this as an unheard of novelty. To the contrary, precisely because consecrated property is specifically excluded by the word "your fellow," ordinary property is subject to ona'ah even when owned by many people. Moreover, the Rosh and those who follow him omit the exemption for consecrated property because in our times there is no Temple consecration in the relevant sense, while property dedicated to the poor or synagogue is treated as ordinary property and is subject to ona'ah even though it belongs to a group. Otherwise one could similarly expound the prohibition of oppression and robbery, which says "do not oppress your fellow and do not rob," to exclude communal property, which is plainly untenable. Nevertheless, it appears to me at first glance that where the property belongs to multiple individuals, even if they are partners, the wrongdoer is not obligated to make restitution unless each partner's share of the ona'ah is at least a perutah. See above, seif 5. See also below, siman 262, seif 2, which states that even a lost object of substantial total value need not be returned if it belongs to so many partners that no individual share reaches a perutah. However, according to Levush there, who explains that although the phrase "and you found it" applies, the phrase "that which is lost from him" does not apply separately to each partner, that case may not prove ours.
