Seif 2. Fruit for fruit. An exchange of one kind of produce for another is subject to onaah according to the values expressly assigned by the parties to each side. Although barterers often agree to an inflated value for the other's produce so that the other will reciprocally value theirs high, unexpressed intentions have no legal force; the calculation follows the stated values. If the onaah is large enough to void the sale, the disadvantaged party may say, 'I agreed to give my produce only in exchange for yours; now that you are not accepting mine on the agreed basis, my transfer too is void,' because where part of a single transaction is void the whole may fail. If the onaah is only of the kind where the sale stands and the excess is returned, the excess is returned from the side in which it occurred according to the stated valuation, and the rest of the exchange remains intact. If at the time of the transaction they expressly said that each side's produce was not truly worth the stated figure but that they were nevertheless exchanging at those values because each regarded the other's produce as worth less to him, then perhaps onaah does not apply. If one later demands return of onaah, the other may be able to cancel the entire exchange based on the parties' disclosed intent, according to Shaarei Ephraim's view that even with movables a possessor may rely on the opinion that disclosed intent can be effective because of the dispute in siman 230.
