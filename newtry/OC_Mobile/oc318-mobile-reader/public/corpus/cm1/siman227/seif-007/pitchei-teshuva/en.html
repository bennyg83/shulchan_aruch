Ketzot HaChoshen argues that the silence of the other authorities implies that even where payment remains unpaid, the sale stands after the period for showing the item to a merchant, because there is a strong presumption of waiver and a migo cannot overcome such a presumption. Netivot HaMishpat, however, follows the Shach, and Pilpela Charifta likewise states that where the buyer still holds the purchase money he may say that he did not waive his claim, relying on the migo that he could claim payment. Shaar Mishpat reaches the same practical result for a broader reason: so long as the purchaser still holds the money, his delay is no evidence of waiver, because a person does not hurry to investigate an overcharge while his money remains in his own hands. Mishneh LaMelech, Beit Meir, and Rabbi Akiva Eiger similarly hold that possession of the money explains the delay. Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds that if the buyer delayed while still holding the money and only afterward paid, payment itself can show that he waived, because he had time to investigate before surrendering the money. Mishkenot Yaakov, siman 48, applies this to a purchaser of a ship who retained part of the price. Although several weeks passed, he could withhold the retained balance for an overcharge exceeding one sixth, because his continued possession of the money meant that silence did not prove waiver. Mishkenot Yaakov also argues that waiver cannot permit one party to commit onaah regarding money still in the other party's possession. Pitchei Teshuva notes that in that particular case the buyer had first sent an expert to value the ship, which itself may extend the period for rescission; the chronology therefore requires clarification. The authorities thus agree substantially that a buyer who still holds the money may raise onaah after the ordinary period, but they disagree why. Hagahot Ashri, Tosafot Yom Tov, Shach, and Netivot rely on migo; Shaar Mishpat, Mishneh LaMelech, Beit Meir, Rabbi Akiva Eiger, and Mishkenot Yaakov rely on the absence of any inference of waiver while the buyer remains in possession. Pitchei Teshuva identifies practical differences. Under the migo rationale the buyer must take a hesset oath, as one who pleads payment would. Under the possession rationale no oath is required merely to deny waiver. If the buyer paid in cash and only later seized money from the seller without witnesses, the migo rationale may help him but the possession rationale does not, because he should have investigated while he was not in possession. Conversely, if the purchase was on credit and the seller later seized payment after the period expired, the possession rationale may still preserve the buyer's claim even though no migo remains. In such disputed configurations, Pitchei Teshuva suggests that the party presently in possession may plead kim li. He then rejects a contrary ruling in Amudei Or involving a buyer who retained part of the price until a fixed date and later alleged onaah. According to the principles above, the buyer's explanation that he did not litigate while the seller's money remained in his hands is legally sufficient. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 187, likewise ruled for an employee who over many years took clothing and goods from his employer against wages at greatly inflated prices. When later sued for a balance, he could reopen the earlier prices because he plausibly explained his silence by the fact that the goods had been charged against wages rather than paid from his pocket. Where the overcharge exceeded one sixth, a plausible explanation for silence can prevent a finding of waiver. Chavot Yair held that for identifiable goods still subject to valuation the buyer should pay only fair value, but Pitchei Teshuva questions applying that rule after the goods were used up, since use itself may create an obligation to pay their assessed value. Netivot objects to Ketzot's statement that where both parties clearly know of the onaah, it is as though they expressly stipulated waiver, citing Bava Metzia's requirement that one say explicitly "on condition" even after stating, "I know it is worth only one hundred." Responsa Galya Masechta, end of siman 4, defends Ketzot. Where knowledge and waiver are genuinely clear and open, no formal "on condition" is required. The case of "I know it is worth only one hundred" is different because the buyer may think the seller is merely praising his merchandise and that the object is actually worth more, so waiver is not conclusively established without an express condition.
