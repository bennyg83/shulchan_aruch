And if he waited longer. If he has not yet paid the money, he is also believed to say, by migo that he could have said “I paid,” that he did not consent to the price. This requires study (Shach). See Beurim: with an overcharge of less than one sixth, such a migo is ineffective.
And if he proves it. If after the purchase he had an unavoidable circumstance that prevented him from investigating, then even after the circumstance ended, although he did not afterward show the item to a merchant, he may nevertheless retract and explain that because he was initially prevented by circumstances, it did not occur to him to investigate further (Sema). The Taz disagrees and maintains that only where he claims that he was unable to establish the unavoidable circumstance, and therefore delayed until now, may he retract; otherwise he may not retract. See Beurim: if he showed the item to a merchant and the merchant erred and said that there was no ona'ah, and it was later established that there was ona'ah, he may retract even after a long time.
We do not say that he waived it. Nevertheless, he can demand restitution of the ona'ah only within the period required to show the item to a merchant or relative. The principal reason we do not say he waived is that he can say that he remained silent so that the transaction would remain in force and he could demand the amount of the ona'ah. But once that period has passed, he knows that he can no longer demand restitution of the ona'ah, and if he remains silent he is presumed to have waived it (Taz). According to this, where the disparity is sufficient to void the transaction, the reasoning that he remained silent in order to void the transaction does not apply, for if so, why did he purchase it at all? Consequently, even within the period required to show it, he cannot void the transaction. This is the principal ruling, unlike the Sema. See Beurim.
