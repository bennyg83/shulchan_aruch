Within the time needed to show it to a merchant. See Shach, seif katan 4, through his statement that since he has a migo to say "I paid," he can also make the other claim. This applies specifically to ona'ah of less than one sixth and after the period for showing it to a merchant has elapsed. With exact one sixth, where the rationale is as the Sema writes in seif katan 20, namely that normal commercial practice does not require a buyer to investigate immediately in the same manner, even a migo does not help. Machaneh Ephraim likewise writes this. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 42, s.v. Amar Abaye, imply that it is permissible intentionally to overcharge another by less than one sixth when the seller intends later to return the overcharge, doing so only so that the transaction itself will remain binding at the proper value.
He was prevented by an unavoidable circumstance. If the buyer proves that he actually showed the item to a merchant within the allotted time, and the merchant told him it was worth what he paid but the merchant was mistaken, the buyer can still claim ona'ah. There can be no greater unavoidable circumstance than this. The Sages themselves fixed the period so that the buyer could show the item to a merchant, and he did exactly that. What more could he do if the merchant erred? Shach in siman 306, seif katan 12, explicitly says, "but here we see that he showed it to a money changer," supporting this. The same follows from the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur here concerning parties who agreed to finalize a transaction according to Levi's appraisal.
The buyer knew. See Sema, seif katan 18, citing the Mordechai from the Gemara, and Bach, who asks that even where the price disparity is so great that no reasonable person could err, the Rosh rules that we say the buyer knew and waived, as in Bava Batra 78. All the more so, where it is established that at the time of purchase he knew an item worth five was being sold to him for six, one should say that he knowingly waived the overcharge. The principal view appears to be the Taz's. He explains that after the period for showing the item to a merchant has elapsed, the buyer can no longer recover even though he already knew of the overcharge at the time of sale. Otherwise, why would the period for showing it to a merchant be relevant to a person who already knew? His initial right exists because we assume that although he knowingly entered the sale, he intended to claim the overcharge later in beit din. Once the statutory period passes and beit din will no longer order repayment, his continued silence together with his prior knowledge demonstrates waiver. According to this, in a case of cancellation of the entire sale the buyer certainly cannot later retract. There the Taz necessarily disagrees with the Sema, because if the buyer intended from the beginning to sue later to cancel the whole sale, there was no reason to enter the transaction at all, and his knowledge therefore indicates waiver. This also removes the question from Bava Batra, which concerns complete cancellation. A difficulty remains from Tosafot in Bava Metzia 51, s.v. Bameh Devarim Amurim. Tosafot write that where one expressly conditions, "on condition that you have no claim of ona'ah against me," it is as though he said, "I know it is not worth this amount," and they explain the matter according to Rabbi Yehudah. Maharsha writes that according to Rabbi Meir, even one who says "I know" still has an ona'ah claim because he is attempting to uproot Torah law. This shows that even when the buyer knows at the time of sale that the object is not worth the price and nevertheless pays it, he can have a claim of ona'ah. The case there even involves a disparity sufficient to cancel the sale. How can this be reconciled with Bava Batra? It appears to me that an express condition makes the case worse. Even where he says "I know," when he couches the arrangement as a condition he is attempting to alter the Torah's legal rule of ona'ah. Tosafot in Ketubot 56, s.v. Harei Zo Mekudeshet, likewise imply that forgiveness attached to a condition can be weaker than simple forgiveness. They ask whether a person would ever be able to forgive a debt by condition, implying that straightforward forgiveness can work even where the analogous condition cannot. This gives room for the Sema's position, as I wrote above in siman 64. Bach explains that the case in Bava Metzia concerning the cloak dealers is specifically where the buyer disclosed before witnesses that he intended to claim the ona'ah later. The principal view, however, is the Rama's, that even without such an express disclosure the buyer can say that this was his intention. Since the issue is doubtful waiver, the seller cannot retain money that he is otherwise obligated to return, comparable to one who is unsure whether he repaid an existing debt. Ketzot challenges the Bach: if absent disclosure we presume waiver, then a disclosure should not be enough to overcome that presumption unless the witnesses also recognized a coercive circumstance, just as the Rama in siman 205 says that even where no moda'ah is required for a sale below value, the witnesses must at least know of the coercion. The cases are not comparable. There the seller seeks to invalidate the sale itself, and if the witnesses do not know the coercion we can say that perhaps he truly wished to sell. Here the buyer does not seek to cancel the sale. He keeps the purchase and merely asserts that he did not forgive the existing debt of ona'ah. A disclosure that he does not forgive that monetary claim is therefore sufficient. Nor is it correct to say that where both parties know the overcharge the case is automatically as though they made a formal condition, whereas where only the seller knows and the buyer does not, it is not. Why should knowledge alone create a condition? Conditions have formal legal requirements, and there is no compelling presumption here that the parties intended one. Moreover, Bava Metzia itself, in the case where one says "I know it is worth only one hundred," requires the further words "on condition that you have no ona'ah claim," and even that is effective only according to Rabbi Yehudah, not Rabbi Meir, as Tosafot explain.
