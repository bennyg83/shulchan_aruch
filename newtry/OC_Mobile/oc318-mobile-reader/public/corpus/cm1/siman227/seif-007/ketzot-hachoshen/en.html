The Shach leaves this difficult. The silence of the other authorities indicates that even before payment the sale stands and the waiver is presumed. The reason is that a person ordinarily does not complete a purchase while intending immediately to undo the agreed price. The presumption of waiver therefore defeats the migo. The purchaser knew. Mordechai proves from the case of a man selling an ornament worth five for six that a knowledgeable purchaser can still claim overreaching. The buyer knew that if he offered five and a half the seller would accept, but he said, "If I give five and a half, the difference will be too small to recover. I will give six and sue him for overreaching." Rava ruled that if the seller were a merchant the buyer would indeed have an overreaching claim, but because the seller was a private householder, whose personal possessions are sold only reluctantly, the special rule for a householder applied. Bach limits this. If the purchaser merely knew the true value but did not declare before witnesses that he intended to sue later, his purchase at the excessive price is treated as a gift or waiver. Only where he stated in advance, "I will pay six and sue," is it clear that he did not waive. Bach further concludes that such a purchaser can sue even long after the ordinary time for showing the item to a merchant, because the reason for that time limit is to give an unsuspecting buyer time to discover the value, whereas this buyer knew the value from the outset and expressly preserved his claim. In my view Rama and Sema are correct in rejecting this limitation. If prior knowledge itself normally creates waiver because the buyer knowingly accepted the price, then merely announcing, "I will later sue," cannot preserve the claim where he is under no compulsion. Even a formal moda'ah does not defeat a voluntary sale unless the witnesses recognize actual coercion. Rivash says that even where the transferor receives only a small benefit and expressly says he is not acting willingly, without coercion the transaction stands. Thus a buyer who freely pays the price cannot turn an otherwise valid waiver into a nonwaiver merely by private protest. The true distinction is different. Where the disparity is so extreme that no reasonable person could mistake the value, both parties necessarily know the true value and the seller necessarily knows that the buyer knows. Their transaction is therefore equivalent to an express stipulation that the buyer waives overreaching. The buyer acquires on those terms and cannot later recover. Where the disparity is within the range that people might reasonably mistake, however, the seller does not necessarily know that this buyer knows the true value. Since the seller did not expressly condition the sale on waiver, the law does not treat the transaction as made on such a condition. The knowledgeable buyer likewise does not automatically acquire on a waiver condition that the seller never imposed. He can therefore claim overreaching even without having declared beforehand that he intended to sue. Shitah Mekubetzet states this explicitly: even if the buyer knew of the overreaching when he purchased, he can recover unless the seller expressly told him that the object was not worth the price and the buyer accepted that fact. This supports the present explanation. The case of an extreme disparity is different because both parties' mutual knowledge makes the transaction effectively equivalent to an express disclosure and waiver. The special rule concerning purchase from a private householder also fits. Since both sides know that a householder ordinarily does not sell personal possessions except for a premium, the transaction itself carries an implied condition that ordinary overreaching rules do not apply. Once that implied condition exists, even saying in advance, "I intend to sue," does not help absent actual coercion. This resolves the source and supports Rama and Sema.
