The purchaser knew at the time he bought it. Darkei Moshe cites Mordechai. Mordechai brings proof from the cases in the Gemara where purchasers bought an item worth five for six. Had they offered five and a half, the seller would also have sold, but the purchasers knowingly paid six with the intention of later claiming the one sixth overcharge while keeping the item. If they had paid only five, the seller would not have sold. If they had paid five and a half, they could not have recovered the half because it would be below one sixth and therefore waived. The Gemara rules in favor of the purchasers. From this Raavyah learns that the same applies whenever we know the buyer was aware of the overcharge but do not know why he remained silent. We do not assume the silence was waiver. He may still claim within the time allowed to show the item to a merchant. For this reason Rama states generally that knowledge and silence do not establish waiver. At first glance the same reasoning could apply even where the buyer knowingly paid seven for something worth five, an amount sufficient to void the sale. Even there he could retract within the relevant period, because mere silence is not admission. If the rule were limited to exactly one sixth, because there the Gemara's particular rationale applies that he wished to preserve the sale while later recovering the overcharge, Rama should have stated that limitation. Still, it is possible that Rama's phrase 'claim his overcharge' indicates specifically the one sixth case. In any event, the Ir Shushan's distinction is incorrect. He writes that the purchaser can recover only if he learned of the overcharge after the sale was completed, but if he knew before completion and nevertheless bought, we say he knowingly waived it. The Gemara and Mordechai prove the contrary.
