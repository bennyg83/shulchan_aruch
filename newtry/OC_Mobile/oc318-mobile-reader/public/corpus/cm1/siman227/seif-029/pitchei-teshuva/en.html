Slaves, documents, and land. Sema, seif katan 51, cites Maharshal that although these categories are excluded from the monetary remedies of onaah, they are no less subject to verbal oppression and deception and remain within the prohibition against wronging one's fellow, unless the seller himself did not know of the disparity. The quoted wording "do not wrong each man his brother" appears to be a scribal error and should read "do not wrong each man his fellow," the verse applied to verbal oppression in Bava Metzia 58b. It also requires study why Sema did not mention Ramban, cited by Raah in Sefer HaChinuch, mitzvah 337, who holds that one violates the same Torah prohibition of monetary onaah in these categories and that they were excluded only from the restitutionary law. See Nachalat Tzvi.
Documents. Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 126, seif katan 9, writes a novel distinction. The exemption of documents from onaah applies specifically to sale of a written debt instrument by writing and delivery, because the document itself is not intrinsically money and is excluded by the interpretive rule requiring an object whose body has monetary value. An oral debt transferred through maamad sheloshtan, however, should be subject to onaah because the debt itself is monetary value. Its inability to be consecrated stems from a different rule, that it is not presently in the owner's possession, not from lack of monetary substance. Divrei Chaim, laws of loans, siman 36, questions this. The Gemara needs the "body is not money" rationale for documents because a document is movable and transferred hand to hand. An oral loan, by contrast, is already excluded by the verse concerning something acquired from the hand of one's fellow, since it is not transferred hand to hand, no better than land. Chavot Daat, Yoreh De'ah 173:8, likewise appears to assume simply that sale of even an oral debt is not subject to onaah, since when valuing a deferred obligation the debt itself must be appraised and sale of a debt instrument is exempt from onaah. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that Chavot Daat's underlying ruling itself requires study in light of Shitah Mekubetzet, Bava Metzia 65.
And consecrated property. Sema writes that property dedicated to the poor or to a synagogue has the law of ordinary property. Shevut Yaakov I, end of siman 166, cites Responsa Beer Esek, siman 3, which argued that communal property is also exempt from onaah because the verse speaks of a sale "to your fellow," excluding consecrated property, and similarly "his brother" would exclude a community. Shevut Yaakov rejects this as an unheard-of innovation. On the contrary, the Gemara's need to exclude consecrated property from "your fellow" implies that ordinary property, even when owned by many people, remains subject to onaah. Moreover, the Rosh and his school omitted the law of Temple consecration in contemporary practice because present-day dedication to the poor or synagogue is treated like ordinary property and is subject to onaah despite being communal. Otherwise one would similarly have to exclude communal property from prohibitions such as oppression and robbery merely because the verses speak in the singular, which is untenable. Pitchei Teshuva nevertheless suggests that where property belongs to several private partners rather than to the public, restitution may not be required unless each partner's share of the onaah reaches a perutah. He compares the rule in siman 262 concerning a lost object jointly owned by many, though the Levush's rationale there may make the comparison inconclusive. The matter requires study.
