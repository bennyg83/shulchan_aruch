In a transaction conducted on trust there is no claim of onaah. Netivot HaMishpat cites Maharit II, siman 19, who asks why this differs from one who merely says there shall be no onaah without an effective condition. Maharit answers that a trust-based sale is as though the seller acts as the buyer's agent to acquire the goods. Netivot finds that strained and offers a simpler distinction: where the seller truly does not know market value and merely states the amount he himself paid, Torah law did not prohibit honest dealing on that basis. If, however, the seller already knows that he himself was overcharged and then passes the goods to the buyer on trust, the buyer may have a right to rescind. Pitchei Teshuva suggests that this distinction is specific to Rambam's formulation, where the seller says, "I bought this article for such and such." Under Rabbeinu Chananel's formulation, the purchaser says in advance, "I trust whatever amount you later tell me you paid, and I will add a fixed profit." There the purchaser knowingly accepts the uncertain acquisition cost, whether high or low, and even the seller's prior knowledge of his own overpayment may not create a claim, because the buyer accepted that risk. Galya Masechta develops a novel distinction between Rambam and Rabbeinu Chananel and argues that contemporary shopkeepers who buy from merchants on stated cost plus a percentage may claim onaah in the merchant's original acquisition price. R. David, av beit din of Mir, forcefully rejects this and rules that established commerce conducted on trust has no onaah, in accordance with the Rishonim and major later authorities and the practice of batei din. Pitchei Teshuva praises Galya Masechta's analysis but concludes that one cannot depart from the consensus of earlier and later authorities. R. David further qualifies Rambam's rule. Where a merchant is known always to trade on a cost-plus trust basis and the buyer knows this, there is strong reason to exempt both principal and profit from onaah because the buyer is not bargaining over market value at all. But where an ordinary person happens to say, "I paid this amount and want this profit," and the purchaser agrees, the buyer may still have relied on an assumption that the item was actually worth that amount. Rabbeinu Chananel's classic trust case is clearest where the purchaser does not yet know the seller's cost but expressly undertakes to pay whatever cost the seller later reports plus a fixed addition. R. David nevertheless leaves his qualification short of a final ruling because the authorities generally state the rule without distinction.
He has no claim of onaah against him. See Baer Heitev regarding cancellation of the sale. Shevut Yaakov III, siman 169, writes that in a transaction conducted on trust, if an actual defect is later discovered the buyer may rescind. The Gemara and poskim exempt only onaah, not a defect that makes the purchase a mistaken transaction; otherwise they should have stated the greater novelty. This applies where the defect could not have been discovered at the time of sale. He found the same ruling in Responsa Maharit Tzahalon, siman 25, with the concurrence of Maharam Galanti. Galya Masechta, siman 10, independently reaches the same conclusion.
I bought it for such and such. Responsa Radbaz I, siman 57, discusses Reuven who bought merchandise in Shimon's presence. Shimon said, "I will give you one dinar profit; give it to me," and Reuven did so. Radbaz rules that Shimon has no claim of onaah. This is all the more so where Shimon relied on Reuven's good faith rather than independently on market value. Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation of trust-based commerce shows that the buyer accepts the transaction based on the seller's actual cost. Here too Shimon relied on what he himself saw Reuven pay, not on an independent valuation of the merchandise. Shaar Mishpat cites this briefly, and Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 19, rules similarly.
