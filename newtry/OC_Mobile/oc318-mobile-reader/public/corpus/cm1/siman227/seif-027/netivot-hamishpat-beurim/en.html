One who conducts business on trust has no ona'ah claim. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat siman 19, asks why this should be better than one who says, "on condition that you have no ona'ah claim against me," where the condition does not work. The same question arises from the homeowner case in seif 23, where Mechaber requires the exact amount of the ona'ah to be stated. There is a further difficulty. Why does one who conducts business on trust have no ona'ah because neither party entered the deal on the basis of the object's market value, while in the case "sell me for these" in seif 19 some hold that ona'ah does apply? Maharit answers that in a trust transaction the seller is effectively acting as the buyer's agent in acquiring the item. This is strained. In my view the distinction is straightforward. In a condition waiving ona'ah, the parties did enter the transaction on the basis of market value; the seller merely attempted to exclude the legal consequence of ona'ah. Nimukei Yosef explains that because the injured party did not know the amount he was waiving, the condition resembles a stipulation against what is written in the Torah. Once the condition fails, the seller has violated the prohibition of ona'ah and must make restitution. By contrast, in a homeowner sale or a trust transaction, the parties never made market value the basis of the bargain. The homeowner is selling his own personal object for the amount at which he is willing to part with it, and in a trust transaction the seller says truthfully what he paid and does not purport to know the market value. The Torah's prohibition of ona'ah does not apply to a transaction that was never framed around ordinary value. In "sell me for these," however, the seller may have at least an approximate understanding of the object's value, as Rashi explains in Bava Metzia. Therefore ona'ah can still apply. But if neither side can reasonably estimate the value at all, even approximately, there is no ona'ah, because the transaction was never made on the basis of value. It resembles gambling, where because each wants the chance to acquire, each fully resolves to transfer. It follows that if, after the trust transaction was completed, it becomes known to the seller that the buyer was materially overreached, the injured party can retract. Once the error is known, it is forbidden to continue benefiting from another's mistake and the prohibition of ona'ah applies from that point. If the seller himself had earlier bought the item from a Jew and had been overreached in that purchase, then resold it on trust, it might appear that the second sale can be undone because the first sale can be voided and the second seller thereby sold property that was not truly his. But if the first seller has already accepted and affirmed the first sale in a manner preventing retraction, as explained above in seif 4, then it is treated as though the sale stood from the outset and the second sale cannot be undone on that basis. Similarly, before the first seller has affirmed the first transaction, one might say the second buyer can cancel because the seller's title remains vulnerable and the true owner may recover the object. Although siman 226 says the seller can sometimes tell the buyer, "Show me your dispossession," that may apply especially to land, where the buyer can use and take produce until actual recovery. With movable property that remains legally the original owner's and has not passed through despair, no one may use it without permission, because unauthorized use is theft. Since the buyer purchased it to use or resell, one might therefore think the transaction is immediately void. Yet Sema in siman 225, seif katan 5, indicates that even with stolen movable property the seller may say, "Show me your dispossession," and the buyer cannot cancel until the original owner actually recovers it. Therefore even if use is presently forbidden, the second sale does not automatically fail before an actual legal recovery. In an ona'ah case, moreover, even the person who caused the ona'ah can continue using the item until the injured party actually retracts, because perhaps the latter will never do so.
