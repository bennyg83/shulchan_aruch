He swears. He takes a Torah oath as one who admits part of the claim, and the lender collects the other half from unencumbered property, but not from property sold to purchasers. The purchasers can say, "We relied on the witnesses." Even if the note contains a trust clause equivalent to two witnesses, siman 71, seif 19, explains that such a clause is ineffective against purchasers. If the borrower later retracts and says, "I now remember that I paid the entire debt," he may make that claim because witnesses testify in support of him, as stated in siman 79, seif 3. Shach. The Sema cites the Rashba, brought by the Beit Yosef in siman 75, concerning Shimon who produced a promissory note against Reuven. Reuven claimed it had been paid and offered as proof that he had omitted this debt from the inventory of his assets when he swore for tax purposes. The Rashba ruled for Shimon, because the omission from the tax inventory is no proof; perhaps Reuven forgot it. The Taz comments: This is difficult in light of siman 81, seif 28, where we do not accept a claim, "I forgot and swore," because a person is presumed to be careful regarding an oath. One may answer that where a person swears specifically about a particular matter, the seriousness of the oath causes him to focus and remember. Here, however, he merely failed to include this item among the rest of his assets, and such forgetfulness is common. The Shach likewise writes this in siman 85, seif katan 2.
