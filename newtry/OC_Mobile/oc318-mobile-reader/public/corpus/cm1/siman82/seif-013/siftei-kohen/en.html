Witnesses testify that the entire debt was paid. This means that the witnesses did not testify until after the borrower admitted half. If they had already testified, the borrower would be believed without an oath, because his admission of half would be the return of a lost object. Sema writes that this is proved by Tur. Tur says that he is not treated as one returning a lost object because he admitted only out of fear of the note. Once witnesses have already testified that the note was paid, however, there is plainly no fear of the note, since their testimony completely voids it. Tur therefore agrees in practice with Rashbam, Tosafot, and Rabbeinu Yerucham. This is the correct ruling, unlike Bach and Imrei Binah, page 82b, who write that according to Rambam and Tur the borrower must swear even when the witnesses testified first. This also resolves Tur with Rosh, unlike Bach's claim that Tur rejects Rosh because the Gemara chose a case involving a note rather than an oral claim. Tur would not silently reject Rosh. Bach's question is also unfounded. In an oral claim, the borrower is plainly exempt even without the doctrine of returning a lost object, because witnesses support him. With a note, by contrast, the instrument stands against him and his own admission partly contradicts the witnesses. It is therefore as though there were neither a note nor complete supporting testimony, and he must swear. Ran explains that although the witnesses testify to full payment and their testimony includes partial payment, the borrower himself disqualifies their testimony to some degree by saying that only half was paid. He is believed against himself, but does not fully invalidate them, because he may simply have forgotten part while they remember more. Their testimony therefore suffices to cancel the note, but not to exempt him entirely. The creditor's remaining claim becomes an oral claim unsupported by witnesses, and a defendant who admits part must swear. Nimukei Yosef explains similarly. This reasoning applies specifically where a note also supports the creditor and creates the argument, “Why is your note in my possession?” In an oral claim, the witnesses are not contradicted in this manner and the borrower does not swear. This is clear, contrary to Bach's implication that even an oral claimant would require an oath according to Rashbam and Tosafot.
The borrower swears. This is a Torah oath, like the oath of one who partially admits, and the creditor collects half from unencumbered property. He does not collect from encumbered property, because the purchasers can say: we relied on the witnesses. Even if the document contains a clause of credibility equivalent to two witnesses, it was explained above in siman 71, seif 19, that such credibility does not help against purchasers. So writes the Tur, based on Baal HaTerumot at the end of Gate 8.
The creditor collects half. This applies when the borrower maintains his original statement. If he retracts and says, “I now remember,” he may advance that claim because witnesses testify in his support, as above in siman 79, seif 3. Maggid Mishneh writes that some explain Rambam this way. Although he then offers another distinction based on the existence of the note, Tur above in siman 40 and Rama above in siman 79, seif 7, imply that there is no distinction. Baal HaTerumot asks why this case differs from an ordinary borrower who says that a note was paid, where the creditor swears and collects. He first answers that when the borrower claims the entire note was paid, his claim is weak because the creditor still holds the note. Here, since the borrower admits half, the creditor had reason to retain the note and the borrower's account is plausible. This first answer is difficult, because the Gemara and poskim show in many places that a borrower is not believed even to say that half of a note was paid. The correct answer is that of Rav Yosef HaLevi: witnesses testify that the entire debt was paid, thereby voiding the note and reducing the claim to an oral loan. The borrower is then an ordinary partial admitter who swears and is exempt.
Because I saw that Darkei Moshe and Sema record several rules of migo in this siman, I too shall set out the laws of migo here. 1. Some hold that a migo cannot be used to extract money. Nevertheless, where the litigant is already in possession of land or its produce, a migo may operate, since this is not treated as extracting from another. This is the meaning of Rivash and is discussed below in siman 150. Where a valid note supports the claim, a migo may also permit collection. In a case of a definite claim against an uncertain denial, as with a woman who says she was violated after betrothal, the combination of a definite claim and migo may permit collection. A ketubah, however, is not always equivalent to a note representing money unquestionably belonging to the claimant, because the husband's obligation arises from the ketubah enactment itself. Thus the woman's definite claim remains essential. See also the places cited below concerning migo used to extract money. 2. A migo cannot override an established local custom. In relation to custom, the claim is treated like a migo against witnesses. One who alleges that the parties changed the custom bears the burden of proof. Even possession does not enable him to establish a departure from the custom, although possession may help when he claims that an unavoidable accident prevented compliance. This follows Rav Nachman and the Geonim. The Mishnah in Peah likewise shows that one is believed only regarding distributions people ordinarily make. Thus one may say that wheat was given as poor tithe, but not that flour or bread was given, because people do not ordinarily distribute flour or bread. The implausible departure from custom is like contrary testimony. Although Rav Yehudah may allow a migo in a special sharecropping case, the law follows Rav Nachman. The same principle applies where neighboring owners ordinarily share the cost of a wall: possession of the stones does not establish that one owner built it alone. 3. A migo is not derived from the possible claim, “I do not know.” 4. Ordinarily, two persons cannot rely upon a migo based on a coordinated alternative claim, because neither knows what the other will say. Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef state this concerning two witnesses. An exception may exist where both face the same monetary exposure and can confidently be expected to give the same exculpatory account. After the enactment of the heses oath, even that migo may cease to apply. 5. A migo cannot stand against witnesses. This includes witnesses who directly contradict the present claim and, all the more so, witnesses who contradict the alternative claim upon which the migo rests. Rosh writes that even disqualified witnesses may sometimes defeat the migo, though later authorities question the scope of that ruling. 6. Darkei Moshe and Sema cite a view that no migo is made from a brazen claim to a less brazen claim. The broad formulation is incorrect. The Gemara and many authorities apply such a migo to retain money. The goats that ate peeled barley are the central example: the possessor may claim their value through the migo that he could have said that he purchased them, although the purchase claim would be more brazen. Similar examples include property said to have belonged to the claimant's father, objects ordinarily lent or rented, and a deposit retained against a debt. The distinction is this. A weak migo involving brazenness may retain money, but does not ordinarily exempt a person from an oath. In a classic denial of a deposit, loan, or partial admission, a person does not brazenly deny one who originally trusted him, so such a migo cannot remove the Torah oath. Where no initial trust existed, or a later independent dealing arose, the alternative claim is not treated as the same kind of brazen denial. Rivash, Ramban, Ran, Rosh, Tosafot, Mordechai, and other authorities support this distinction. Therefore the general statements of Nimukei Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema that a migo of brazenness is never said are not accepted. 7. Mordechai writes that a migo is said only from an alternative claim that is open and available. His formulation requires careful limitation and should not automatically be extended beyond its context. 8. A migo does not operate retroactively. The alternative claim must still be available within the time for an immediate retraction. Thus a woman who first says, “I am married,” cannot much later say, “I am unmarried,” unless she gives a satisfactory explanation. The same rule appears in many monetary contexts. 9. A migo is not applied where the litigant believes he is reporting the truth, but the law suspects that he is honestly mistaken. A woman who says that her husband died in war may be describing what she assumes occurred after seeing him struck, and she is not believed through the migo that she could have said he died in bed. Likewise, a claimant who impaired her ketubah may be accurately recounting what she thinks happened without having checked carefully. This is not an ordinary liar's-choice migo, because she does not intend to lie. The cited Tosafot must be understood in this limited sense. 10. Whether a migo prevails against a presumption depends upon the strength and nature of the particular presumption. The Gemara leaves unresolved the migo against the presumption that a person does not pay before a debt falls due. Other presumptions are weaker or operate differently. The case of a yevamah who says after thirty days that consummation did not occur is governed by two opposing presumptions: a woman does not brazenly lie to her husband, yet a man ordinarily cannot restrain himself for more than thirty days. The presumptions offset one another, so she cannot compel him. One may not equate every presumption with every other. 11. A migo may operate against a note. A custodian who gave a written receipt may say that he returned the deposit through the migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable accident. We do not automatically say, “Why is your receipt still in the other party's hand?” 12. A migo against witnesses is unavailable even where the opposing testimony creates liability only when joined to the litigant's own admission. The case of the road by the Pekod River demonstrates this point. 13. A migo from one item of property to a different item is not categorically rejected. Some early authorities read the divided garment case that way, but the accepted law allows a person to retain one object against a separate debt when he could have denied holding it. If the property is already in the other party's possession, the problem is extraction, not merely that it is a different item. 14. As a general rule, a migo retains property but does not extract it. The ketubah case initially cited for the opposite rule rests on additional strength, such as a definite claim against an uncertain one, or a valid note. Earlier authorities dispute the precise boundaries. A valid note ordinarily supports a migo used to collect, but a ketubah obligation may differ where the very basis of liability is disputed. 15. A migo may extract money where the claimant states a definite claim and the defendant only an uncertain one. This explains the ruling concerning a woman who says that the loss of virginity occurred after betrothal. The rule is not derived from the ketubah alone, because if the husband asserted a definite contrary claim, the note or court-imposed ketubah obligation would not necessarily suffice. 16. A migo against a presumption remains an unresolved or context-dependent question, as in payment before maturity. 17. A migo must generally be asserted within the time of immediate speech. This is shown by the cases “I was captured but remain pure,” “I was married but am divorced,” and witnesses who authenticate their signatures but say they acted under compulsion. Later retraction requires an adequate explanation. 18. Where the later statement merely explains the original words rather than retracting them, it may be accepted even after more time has passed. The father who says, “I gave my daughter to this man,” and then identifies the man is the example, because the second statement interprets the first. 19. A migo based on the option to remain silent may apply even to two witnesses. Their silence does not require coordinated alternative testimony; each could simply refrain from creating the evidence. The wording of the earlier authority is imprecise insofar as it speaks of the two agreeing upon another account. 20. By contrast, two witnesses ordinarily cannot rely on a migo that they could have given a different joint account, because neither knows what the other would say. Thus witnesses who say they signed under financial compulsion are not believed through the migo that they could have said they signed under threat to life. Where both witnesses face the same monetary claim and their alternative account would exempt both, Tosafot recognize that they may be sufficiently coordinated, subject to the later enactment of the heses oath. 21. Even a silence-based migo is unavailable where there is a plausible reason the witness chose to speak. One witness who says that a woman was divorced cannot necessarily say, “I could have remained silent,” because he may have spoken in order to disqualify her from the priesthood. 22. It is disputed whether a migo may rest on a highly unusual alternative claim. Some language of Tosafot suggests that an uncommon claim, such as coercion under threat to life, is too remote. Other authorities and several clear rulings show that a borrower or custodian may rely upon an alternative claim of unavoidable accident or death through ordinary use, although uncommon. The better distinction is that when a person is known to be telling the truth, we do not assume he would choose an unusual lie merely to obtain an incidental benefit, as in entitlement to a penalty payment. But where we do not know whether his present account is true, the ordinary “why would he lie” reasoning can extend even to an uncommon alternative. Accordingly, the categorical exclusion stated by Rabbeinu Shimshon requires study. 23. A migo of brazenness is not used in classic partial-admission cases to avoid an oath. A borrower who admits three when the lender claims five cannot rely on the possibility of denying everything, because complete denial would be brazen. This is the special law of denial and partial admission. Outside that setting, a migo involving a more brazen alternative may operate to retain money, as explained above. 24. A person is not believed through a migo that would require him to make himself wicked, nor is he believed to make himself wicked through a migo based on a non-wicked claim. The cases of eating forbidden fat and voluntary sodomy illustrate the rule. A person's statement may be accepted to avoid bringing an unconsecrated offering into the Temple courtyard, because there he seeks repentance rather than legal self-disqualification. 25. A migo may fail where the present claim would require no oath but the alternative claim would require an oath. The case of agents who say they paid the creditor through the migo that they could have said they returned the money to the borrower changed after the enactment of the heses oath: the agents swear that they paid and the creditor swears that he did not receive payment. 26. “Why would he lie?” applies where the litigant may be lying and could have chosen a stronger lie. It does not apply where he may sincerely believe a mistaken account. Thus a woman reporting death in war is not believed through the alternative of reporting death in bed, because she may be speaking from an inaccurate assumption rather than choosing between lies. 27. A migo is not effective when one witness contradicts the alternative claim and the litigant would need a Torah oath to overcome that witness. In the case of the seized ingot, the possessor admits seizure but claims it was his own. He cannot rely on the possibility of denying the seizure, because that denial would itself require an oath against the witness. Since he cannot escape the Torah oath through the alternative claim, the migo does not arise. 28. A migo may operate even where both the present claim and the alternative require oaths. A custodian who issued a receipt may swear that he returned the item through the migo that he could have sworn a Torah custodial oath that it was lost by unavoidable accident. The key is that the present claim already requires an oath, so the migo moves from one oath-bearing claim to another. This differs from using an oath-bearing alternative to support a present claim that otherwise requires no oath. The rulings of Rambam, Semag, Baal HaMaor, and other authorities support this distinction. 29. A migo may apply even when the present claim is more favorable than the alternative claim. One who admits that a field belonged to the claimant's father but says he bought it is believed through the option of denying the father's ownership, although the claimant may know that the latter denial is false while lacking knowledge whether a sale occurred. 30. A migo is not readily accepted where the alternative claim is obscure or unaccustomed, such as a later assertion of computational error. Rabbeinu Tam required clear proof for such an error and did not allow it merely through the migo that payment could have been claimed after an admission. I discussed and challenged the breadth of this rule above in siman 81, seif 23, seif katan 60.
31. Responsa Mahara ibn Sasson and Mabit indicate that a migo is not used where the alternative depends upon choosing one side of a disputed legal question and asserting “I rely upon the authorities who rule in my favor.” 32. Finally, many passages reject a proposed migo on a relatively slight distinction. One must therefore exercise great care before deciding monetary law through migo, and especially before extracting money. Unless the cases are truly parallel, a small difference may defeat the inference. Responsa Mahari ibn Lev likewise collects many rules of migo in the cited sections of his three volumes.
