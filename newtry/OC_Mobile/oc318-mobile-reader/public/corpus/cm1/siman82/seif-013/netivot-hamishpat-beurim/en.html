It is. This rule is from Tosafot in Ketubot and from Nimukei Yosef in chapter Chezkas HaBatim concerning two groups of witnesses who contradict one another. I answered in the main text Tumim's question concerning three witnesses, and according to what I wrote his first question is likewise no difficulty. Tumim also challenges Mahari Mintz, who proved this rule from Tosafot's question regarding witnesses who say, "Our statement was subject to a protest," where Tosafot ask why they are not believed by a migo that they could have said the document was forged. Mahari Mintz asks that we do not say a migo involving two persons. Tumim objects that we do say a migo that the witnesses could simply have remained silent. Tumim further asks on Tosafot: why should they be believed by a migo of silence? Perhaps the case is one where the possessor held the land for three years and then produced this document. If the witnesses say that there was a protest, his chazakah is void because it rests upon the document and is a chazakah without a valid claim. If they remain silent and the document is treated as uncertified, however, the person who held the land for three years is believed that it is a valid document by a migo that he could have suppressed the document and merely claimed that he purchased and possessed the land for three years. That would not be a migo used to extract, because a migo is effective to retain land. In my view there is no difficulty. Tosafot do not mean a migo that the witnesses could have remained entirely silent. Rather, they mean that they could have refrained from saying that this is their handwriting and instead have testified orally that their statement was subject to protest. In that case the possessor would have no migo to say that it is a valid document by claiming that he could have suppressed the document and relied only on three years of possession. If the witnesses had withheld certification, they would certainly have been believed to say that there had been protest or coercion, because the dispute over whether witnesses may say so applies specifically to witnesses signed on a certified document, where this may be considered retracting from prior testimony. Ordinary witnesses are certainly believed to testify to protest or coercion even if the document is later shown, so long as it has not been certified, just like other witnesses who are not retracting from prior testimony. Tumim also asks what migo there is, since perhaps the witnesses do not wish to cause the purchaser to lose his purchase money. If they remain silent and thereby invalidate the document, he will lose even the purchase money. According to what I wrote, this too is no difficulty. Their migo is that they could refrain from certifying the signatures and instead testify orally that they know of the sale, the purchase price, the coercion, and the protest. The purchaser would then not lose the purchase price. On this basis the words of Mahari Mintz are also correct. This does not resemble an ordinary migo of silence because they must in any event give testimony that they know of the sale and the protest in order not to cause the purchaser to lose the purchase price. It therefore becomes a migo involving two persons: perhaps the witnesses would not agree on one false story, or perhaps one thinks that the other will not say that he knows of the sale, which would cause the purchaser to lose the purchase price. They therefore chose instead to say, "This is our handwriting," and there is no migo. From this Mahari Mintz proved his rule that where the testimony of either witness would be effective even without the other's agreement to the same extent as the testimony he gives now, we do say a migo, as I wrote in the main text. For this same reason, if one witness thinks the other will say, "This is my handwriting," then even if he himself says, "This is not my handwriting, but I know of the sale and the protest," the seller will still be required to return the purchase money.
