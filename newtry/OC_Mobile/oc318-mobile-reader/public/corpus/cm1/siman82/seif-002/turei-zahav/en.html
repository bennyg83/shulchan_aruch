(Seif 3) “The borrower swears that he has no property.” The Tur and Sefer HaTerumot then cite the Geonim, who say neither party can compel the other to swear first. The defendant owes nothing until the claimant swears, while the claimant need not swear until assets are available for collection. If the claimant chooses, he may swear; if the debtor then pays, well and good, and if not, the debtor swears that he lacks property. The Beit Yosef does not comment but the Mechaber follows the Rambam’s contrary first view, as he often does where neither Rif nor Rosh expressly disagrees. The Sema’s assertion that later authorities agreed with the Rambam is not found in the Beit Yosef. The Rama should have cited the Geonim, especially since the Tur and Sefer HaTerumot present their view last. A responsum of the Rosh supports them. Where a nephew claimed that his deceased uncle instructed him to give books to charity, and the daughter produced a large debt instrument against the estate, the Rosh required the nephew to swear first that the instruction was made. Only then would the daughter swear that she inherited no other assets sufficient to satisfy the debt. Until the nephew swore, his claim was mere words and could not impose an oath on her. All the more so here, where the debtor denies the creditor’s claim. The creditor’s oath benefits him even if the debtor presently has no property, because assets may later be found or acquired. The debtor’s oath, by contrast, may be entirely pointless if the creditor never swears. Since the authorities dispute the matter, the burden of proof lies on the creditor, and practical halakha follows the Geonim: he cannot compel the debtor to swear until he himself swears first.
