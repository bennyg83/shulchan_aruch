And even if it contains an explicit clause of trust. See the Shach, who wrote that there is no doubt here, and that in every respect this is equivalent to the case of a purchaser. I am uncertain, however, whether, if an earlier creditor collected movable property rather than land, an explicit clause of trust might be effective. Such a clause is equivalent to the debtor saying now, "I have not repaid." With movable property, why should he not be believed? It is within his power to give all of it to that creditor as a gift. If the answer is that the other creditor has a lien upon movable property acquired together with land, nowadays the market protection enactment applies. If so, when the debtor says, "You are believed by me," why should he not be believed? It is as though he were presently standing before us and saying, "I have not repaid." This is understandable with land, for if he gives the land away, the later creditor can still collect it, so he cannot impair the later creditor's rights. But with movable property, if he gives it away the later creditor has nothing. Why, then, should he not also be able to confer trust upon the first creditor? This requires study. For Tosafot in the chapter Mi Shehaya Nasui, Ketubot 90, s.v. "we learn from this," wrote that the dispute below on 94 between the first Tanna and Ben Nanas, whether the last creditor must swear in a case where a later creditor preceded an earlier creditor and collected, concerns movable property, for with land, according to all opinions, the later creditor's collection is ineffective. There on 94, s.v. "which was found," Tosafot ask, according to the first Tanna, why she should not have to swear under the law governing one who seeks payment from orphans' property. They answer that the case is one where the debtor exempted her from an oath. It must be that even though he exempted her, with respect to a later creditor she must nevertheless swear, and the clause of trust and exemption is ineffective. Yet this is difficult, for the case concerns movable property, and it also concerns a situation where no lien of movables acquired together with land was written, since otherwise the movable property would again have the status of land. It thus appears that the clause of trust is nevertheless ineffective. One must distinguish between a debtor who states at the time of collection that he has not repaid, in which case he certainly has the migo described above, and a debtor who grants trust prospectively. Perhaps such a migo does not apply to a prospective clause.
