If the borrower was established as a denier, etc. The Bach asks what this adds beyond the explicit Gemara and the rule above at the beginning of siman 79. He suggests that a written instrument is weaker because, even when its signatures are authenticated, perhaps the creditor forged them so expertly that the witnesses mistakenly recognize them. Therefore, unlike the oral case, the borrower may issue a general ban before payment, and after payment may sue for double payment and compel a hesset oath. This is incorrect. The case is the same as siman 79: one who denied the loan has thereby admitted that he did not repay, and cannot afterward compel a hesset oath regarding prior payment. Gidulei Terumah and the language of the Tur and Mechaber support this. Raavad, as quoted by Baal HaTerumot, expressly says that after payment the borrower may issue a general ban against whoever took his money unlawfully, but does not say that he may compel an oath. The novelty is that although the Gemara says the established denier must pay, one might have thought the creditor must at least swear. Baal HaItur indeed holds in the analogous oral case that the debtor may compel an oath after payment. Raavad teaches that even an oath is unnecessary. The rule applies equally to an oral debt, but is stated here because the present subject is an instrument. Alternatively, one might distinguish an oral denial, “It never happened,” which clearly means, “I never borrowed,” from a denial of a document, which might mean only, “This instrument was never validly made.” If so, perhaps even after he is proven false regarding the instrument, the creditor should still swear that the debt was not paid. The rule teaches that saying the instrument never existed is treated like saying the loan never occurred. All this concerns an alleged payment before the denial. If the borrower claims that he paid only afterward, the creditor need not swear before collection because the borrower was established as a denier, but after paying the borrower may compel a hesset oath, as explained above in siman 79, seif 8, seif katan 18.
