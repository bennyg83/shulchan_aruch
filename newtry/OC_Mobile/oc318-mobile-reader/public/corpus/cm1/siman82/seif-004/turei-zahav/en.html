(Seif 6) “But if he seized property.” The Beit Yosef asks that according to the Tur the same rule applies even to an ordinary person. He answers in the name of Rabbi Aharon HaLevi of Trani that after seizure an ordinary person is placed under a ban, whereas a Torah scholar is not. This distinction is unnecessary. Rather, with an ordinary person the borrower may compel him either to tear up the note, so that he cannot later seize on its basis, or to swear. A Torah scholar cannot be compelled in this manner, and if he later seizes, his seizure stands. Yet all these explanations are difficult. The Gemara asks, “Because he is a scholar, may he strip people of their cloaks?” and answers, “Rather, we do not attend to his case.” This shows that a Torah scholar receives no monetary advantage over an ordinary person, for one law applies to all Israel. The Beit Yosef’s question, why the Rosh had to state that seizure is effective for an ordinary person, therefore presents no difficulty; it is self-evident for the reason just stated. The conclusion of the Gemara appears to be as follows. When an ordinary creditor presents his note and the borrower claims payment, the court immediately adjudicates between them: the noteholder swears and collects. With a Torah scholar, however, the court does not issue a ruling between them. It defers the matter and does not tell the scholar, “Swear and collect,” because that is beneath his dignity. The parties are left as they are until the scholar can enforce his own right by seizure. This is the precise meaning of the Gemara’s conclusion, “we do not attend to their case.” Accordingly, if both parties are Torah scholars, the law is as though neither were a scholar. Were the court to defer out of respect for the claimant, it would disgrace the defendant by implying that he is not a scholar; therefore their equal standing must be recognized. The Tur and Rosh should also be understood this way. In truth, seizure is effective for every Jew and no oath is required, because one does not impose a ban upon a person who has already seized. The Beit Yosef’s proof for Rabbi Aharon of Trani from siman 108, seif 10, where the court places him under a ban until he swears, is no proof. The oath here is exceptional leniency, since it is imposed only after the borrower asks that the creditor swear. Nevertheless, it is clear that seizure here dispenses only with the stringent Mishnaic oath required of one who swears and collects. If, after payment, the borrower sues the creditor for having received payment twice, the creditor must take a heset oath, even if he is a Torah scholar. See what I wrote in siman 86.
