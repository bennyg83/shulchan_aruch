The creditor was suspected. Rambam writes that the defendant takes only a hesset oath. He does not require the defendant to take the severe oath ordinarily shifted to the opposing party when the original swearer is suspected. This shows that here we do not make one rabbinic enactment upon another. The creditor loses because he seeks to extract money and is unable to take the rabbinic oath required of him. His right therefore falls away entirely. The defendant, who remains in possession, need only take hesset. This explains the sugya in Shevuot, which says concerning rabbinic oaths that we do not enact one rabbinic institution upon another, and appears to include all rabbinic oaths. The Shach uses that sugya against a responsum of Maharshal that allowed a possessor to say, "I follow Rambam." According to Rambam, however, the distinction is internal to rabbinic oaths themselves. With a hesset oath, if the defendant is suspected the claimant does not receive a shifted oath and simply loses. With an oath imposed upon one who seeks to collect, the claimant likewise loses his collection right if he cannot swear, and the defendant takes only hesset. Thus we never create a second special oath enactment for the opposing party. The Shach cites Rashi in Perek HaKotev as proof against Rambam. Rashi explains a case of a woman obligated in an oath and suspected as one where another person sued her for money and she denied it, apparently excluding the possibility that the oath was her ketubah oath. Yet this is not a proof. In that sugya Rabbah shifted the oath to the opposing party. Under Rambam's approach, where the original obligation was only a rabbinic oath to collect, there is no such full shifting. The defendant merely takes hesset. Therefore Rashi had to establish a case of a defendant's oath. Rif's responsum, cited by the Rosh in Perek HaKotev, does appear to disagree. It rules that a suspected person who impaired his own document can have the oath shifted to the other side, who swears and is exempt. That implies a full shifted oath. According to Rif, the rule that one rabbinic enactment is not made upon another must be limited, perhaps mainly to hesset rather than to oaths of those who collect. According to Rambam, however, the sugya can apply to all rabbinic oaths as explained.
