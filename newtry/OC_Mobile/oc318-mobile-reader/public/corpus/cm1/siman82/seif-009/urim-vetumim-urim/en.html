Either swear and collect. Ordinarily, when one party swears and collects, the opposing party accepts a general ban that he is not forcing a needless oath. Here the borrower says that he does not even wish to accept that ban; otherwise there would be little practical difference, because some form of ban would remain. So writes the Sema. This is not conclusive. It may be that the borrower is uncertain whether he paid. He can therefore accept a ban that he is not knowingly forcing a false oath, because he is genuinely uncertain, but he does not want to accept a stronger formulation amounting to a declaration that he definitely paid, since he has some doubt.
And we proclaim a ban even outside his presence. Such a ban takes effect even if the person is far away at sea. It appears that this was true when the practice was to issue a general ban. In our time, when the custom is for the litigant personally to accept a ban upon himself in place of the general ban mentioned by the authorities, beit din plainly compels him to accept it expressly. This is how Jewish courts rule.
