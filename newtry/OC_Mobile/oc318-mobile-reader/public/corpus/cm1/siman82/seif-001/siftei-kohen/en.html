The same applies to every similar claim, such as a claim that the instrument was one of trust, etc. Beit Yosef correctly writes that this appears in the second chapter of Ketubot. Rav Yehudah states that one who says, “This was a trust instrument,” is not believed. Rava explains that the borrower is speaking and that the ruling follows Rav Huna, who holds that one who admits writing the instrument need not have it authenticated. It follows that according to the authority requiring authentication, the borrower is believed to say that it was a trust instrument. Gidulei Terumah’s lengthy objections to Beit Yosef, including the claim that Tosafot distinguish trust from payment and the criticism of Ran’s wording, are all mistaken. The Gemara necessarily equates a claim of trust with a claim of payment for this purpose, and no authority disagrees.
It was a trust instrument, or I wrote it in order to borrow, etc. This applies not only where the borrower claims that the witnesses did not know the instrument was merely one of trust or was written before any loan. Even if he says the witnesses knew, so that according to his account they acted improperly, he is nevertheless believed while the instrument cannot be authenticated, because he has a migo. The same follows from Nimukei Yosef and the Rama’s case in which the borrower says he was a minor. Witnesses who signed for a minor would have acted improperly, because they were required to examine him. Yet the borrower is believed where the instrument cannot be authenticated. Baal HaItur and Baal HaTerumot say the same concerning an antedated instrument. Above at the end of siman 46, witnesses who authenticate their signatures and then say, “It was a trust instrument,” or “He was a minor,” are not believed because a person cannot render himself wicked. We divide their statement: they are believed that the signatures are theirs, but not that they acted improperly. Here, however, the borrower says the instrument was one of trust, and the witnesses are not present to authenticate it. He is therefore believed through his migo. If the witnesses later appear, authenticate their signatures, and say that it was a trust instrument, the instrument becomes authenticated and their self-incriminating explanation is rejected.

It is treated like all other instruments, and collection may be made upon it. The Bach explains that the novelty is collection retroactively from encumbered property, not collection after the borrower has already sworn that he paid. Otherwise, says the Bach, why would beit din initially administer an oath without concern that the creditor may later authenticate the instrument and render the oath unnecessary? Rambam himself rules that where a claimant first sues orally and the defendant says, “You have an instrument against me,” the instrument must be nullified before an oath is administered. The Bach further invokes Ran’s rule that beit din does not both leave the creditor holding an instrument and compel the debtor to swear. In my opinion this is incorrect. Even after the borrower swore that he paid, the creditor need not return the unauthenticated instrument. Rambam expressly writes that if a creditor produces an instrument he cannot authenticate and the borrower says, “I paid,” or “It was a trust instrument,” the borrower takes a hesset oath and is released; if the creditor later authenticates it, it is treated like any other instrument. Baal HaTerumot states the same. Even if the borrower says, “I will not swear until you return the instrument,” we do not listen. The creditor may say, “Swear now because at present I cannot authenticate it. I will retain it in case I later find authentication.” There is no unnecessary oath. If the creditor never authenticates it, the oath protected him; if the creditor later authenticates it, the instrument then acquires its ordinary force. Were the borrower able to avoid the oath by demanding the instrument’s return, Rambam and Baal HaTerumot would not have ruled categorically that he must swear. The case above at the end of siman 75 is different. There the creditor sues orally, while the debtor knows that the creditor already holds an authenticated instrument and will later collect with certainty. The debtor may therefore say, “Why should I swear now for nothing?” Here the creditor may never be able to authenticate. The case instead resembles the rule that where the creditor has witnesses whom he cannot presently produce, he may still demand an immediate oath. Ran’s rule cited in siman 58 is also different. There the creditor wishes to retain the instrument only so that he may later seize property, even in the presence of witnesses. Beit din will not force the debtor to swear while leaving such an instrument over him. The debtor may insist upon retaining the protection of his oath just as the creditor retains his paper, and may use every lawful means to prevent seizure. Here, however, if the creditor later authenticates the instrument, the debtor will owe even without seizure. The debtor therefore must take the hesset oath now, while the creditor may retain the instrument and collect if he later authenticates it. This is clear, unlike the Bach. He is not believed afterward to say, “I paid.” The Sema explains that this includes a claim of payment after the instrument was authenticated. He therefore says the practical consequence is that the creditor need not swear even if the borrower demands an oath, because once the borrower was established as a denier he is not believed until the creditor admits payment or the borrower brings witnesses. This complication is unnecessary. Nimukei Yosef’s own case is one in which the borrower later brings witnesses that he paid. If he first claimed that the instrument was forged and it was later authenticated, he cannot bring witnesses that he paid earlier, because one who says, “It is forged and I never borrowed,” is treated as having said, “I did not pay.” But if, after authentication, he claims that he paid afterward and brings witnesses to that later payment, he is believed.
