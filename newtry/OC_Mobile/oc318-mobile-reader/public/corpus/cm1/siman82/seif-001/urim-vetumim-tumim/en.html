A trust deed, etc. The Shach, seif katan 2, wrote that even if the borrower says that the witnesses knew it was a trust deed, thereby rendering them wicked, the borrower is nevertheless believed by a migo that he could have claimed that the deed was forged, even when he says that the witnesses themselves knew of the arrangement. He brought proof from the Nimukei Yosef that one is believed to say that he was a minor. There is no need to bring proof from the Nimukei Yosef, for this is compelled by the Gemara there in Ketubot 19, where Rav Huna says that one who says, "This is a trust deed," is not believed, and the Gemara establishes this as a statement by the borrower. Rav holds that one who admits that he wrote a deed need not have it authenticated. Why did the Gemara not establish the case as one where he says that it was a trust deed and that the witnesses knew of it, in which case according to all opinions he should not be believed, rather than making the ruling depend specifically on the view that authentication is unnecessary? It is strained to say that this was done merely because Rav independently rules that way as halachah. See Tosafot there. Necessarily, in such a case, according to the view that the deed must be authenticated, he is believed. The point is therefore compelled.
And if he subsequently found, etc. The Bach, both in his work and in responsum siman 20, ruled that if the borrower already swore that he repaid and the lender subsequently found witnesses who could authenticate the deed, the borrower has nevertheless already been discharged, since the witnesses do not testify clearly that he did not repay. For this reason he may say, "I will not swear until you tear up the deed." But one who pleads that the deed is forged cannot in truth demand that it be torn up, and if it is later authenticated he has been contradicted by witnesses and must pay. The Shach and the Taz challenged the Bach; see there. I add a question against the Bach's position. How can the borrower be believed that the debt was paid by a migo that he could have claimed forgery? What migo is there? He knows himself that the deed is not forged, and he must therefore be concerned that witnesses may later be found to authenticate it. It is thus better for him to plead payment, since even if witnesses later appear, according to the Bach he has already been discharged, whereas if he pleads forgery and witnesses appear he will have to pay. Rather, the Bach's ruling cannot stand, and the position of the Shach and Taz is correct. One should not ask, even according to their view, what migo exists, since he may fear that witnesses will appear, and if he pleads forgery he will be contradicted by witnesses and become disqualified as a suspect person, whereas if he pleads payment, even if he must ultimately pay, he will not be contradicted by witnesses. He could instead plead, "I do not know whether this deed is genuine," in which case collection likewise cannot be made because of the doubt, and he would not be contradicted at all. Tosafot similarly wrote regarding witnesses that there is no migo of two persons, since they could remain silent, meaning that if beit din were to ask whether this is their handwriting they could answer, "We do not know."
And some disagree, etc. From Tosafot in Bava Metzia 13, s.v. "He says it never happened," it appears like the first view. Tosafot ask, in a case where the borrower claims that there never was such a debt, why, if the deed contains a lien on property, should it not be returned? Either way, if the lender cannot authenticate it he will never collect, and if he does authenticate it he is legally entitled to collect. See there, where Tosafot discuss this at length in their answers. This is difficult. What follows from the borrower's having pleaded forgery? If the deed is authenticated, he should still be able afterward to say, "I wrote it intending to borrow, but I never borrowed," and he is believed so long as the deed remains in the finder's possession, as Rashi wrote on "this works well." This is unlike the case where it is returned to the lender and then authenticated, for once the lender holds his deed the borrower can no longer plead that he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed. Necessarily, Tosafot maintain that even while the deed remains with the finder, once the borrower initially claimed that it was forged and it was then authenticated, he can no longer say that he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed, because he has established himself as a false denier. One cannot argue that although he has established himself as a false denier, how can we return a deed containing a lien on property, since perhaps he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed, and what does authentication accomplish regarding purchasers? Even if he admitted that he owes the debt, we would still be concerned on behalf of purchasers. This is incorrect for two reasons. First, if so, why was it necessary to say that with a deed lacking a lien Rabbi Meir holds that one cannot collect even from the debtor's free property? With respect to the debtor himself, he has established himself as a false denier. Therefore, either way, if the deed is authenticated, the law requires collection from his free property, and if it is not authenticated, returning it causes no harm. Second, Shmuel is not concerned about collusion at all. Since the borrower has established himself as a false denier, it is as though he admitted that he did not write the deed merely intending to borrow and then fail to borrow. This admission is effective even with respect to purchasers. Tosafot in Ketubot 92b, s.v. "the law," likewise wrote in the case of one who pleads forgery that he can no longer plead payment, because one who says, "I never borrowed," etc. The matter is explicit. However, according to this, the Rashba's rulings appear contradictory. The "some disagree" cited by the Rama is the view of the Rashba in a responsum brought by the Beit Yosef in siman 80. Yet the Rashba, in his novellae to Gittin 9 on the passage concerning one who brings a bill of divorce in Eretz Yisrael, also cited the question of Tosafot above. Initially he considered saying as I wrote, that even though the borrower first said, "I did not write it," we may nevertheless be concerned that perhaps he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed, because he did not pay attention to formulate his first plea precisely, or because he thought, "If I say that I wrote it intending to borrow, perhaps they will not believe me, so I will say that I never wrote it." But he ultimately wrote that it is difficult to say that with respect to his own liability we should assume that he had a different plea but did not disclose it. See there. His words indicate that in his final view, even in such a case he cannot advance a different plea afterward. It may therefore be said that there the rule is different. Everyone presumably knows the law concerning a found deed, namely that if the borrower pleads that it never reached the lender and that he wrote it intending to borrow but never borrowed, the finder plainly has no authority to return it. Reason itself dictates this, for whom should the finder heed, the lender or the borrower? The matter remains doubtful and the deed is held indefinitely. If so, why would the borrower go to the trouble of claiming forgery when he could simply make that plea? Necessarily his forgery plea is false, and he therefore establishes himself as a false denier. Here, however, with an ordinary deed, he knows that if he pleads payment he will not be believed. Even if witnesses might be brought, perhaps such witnesses were not then available. Thus he can properly say, "Because I feared they would not believe me, I instead pleaded forgery," exactly as the Rashba wrote explicitly in the novellae cited above. This is satisfactory. Accordingly, what I wrote above in siman 69, seif katan 6, from reasoning alone is compelled by the Rashba's own words, without any concern that the Rashba contradicts himself, and the matter is correct. Nevertheless, from the unqualified language of Tosafot, especially Tosafot in Ketubot, it appears as I wrote initially. Otherwise, Tosafot should not have raised the question and distinguished merely by an unstated rationale; they would have explained the distinction in their usual manner.
