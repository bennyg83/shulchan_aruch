We do not impose an oath on a doubtful claim. Even if both lender and borrower are uncertain whether a written debt was paid, the lender can ordinarily collect. From heirs, however, collection may not be possible because beit din advances claims for heirs as though definite. Alternatively, independently, one cannot collect from heirs without an oath, and since the lender cannot swear he cannot collect. The same issue arises where payment certainly did not occur but the lender is uncertain whether language of waiver included this document, even where the rule favoring the holder of the receipt does not apply, and even where he is uncertain whether any waiver occurred. In the oath concerning nonpayment, other matters such as waiver are included through gilgul. Even according to those who hold that 'one obligated to swear who cannot swear must pay' does not apply to gilgul, a claimant seeking to collect cannot collect until he can make the required oath including all matters incorporated in it. Where he himself doubts whether he waived the debt, that doubt may approach the status of doubt about payment. If he seized property, even after the doubt arose and even after death, since without a migo no oath is otherwise imposed on him, one can argue that the claims we advance for heirs are not treated as absolute and the document remains in its presumption. If one witness testifies that he waived the debt, it is obvious that he cannot collect, because the witness obligates him to swear that the debt remains unpaid and he cannot swear. Even if his seizure was with permission, the witness may render the opposing claim like a definite claim, and he cannot collect on a document while saying, 'I do not know whether I was paid.'
