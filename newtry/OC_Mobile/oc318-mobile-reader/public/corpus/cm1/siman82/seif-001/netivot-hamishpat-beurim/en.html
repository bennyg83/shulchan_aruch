The Shach. In his rules of migo, the Shach questions the Gemara in Ketubot 12, which asks, "What difference is there between this migo and that migo?" The Shach asks why the Gemara compares them at all. In the case of a woman who says, "I was raped after betrothal," the migo would be used to extract money, while in the case of one who says, "This field belonged to your father," the migo merely allows him to retain land already in his possession. See the Shach's strained answer. In my view there is no difficulty. Ketubot 76 discusses one who exchanged a cow for a donkey and cites the case of a bride. The Gemara concludes that where the defect is found while the bride is still in her father's house, the father must bring proof, even concerning the kiddushin money. This shows that when the Mishnah states generally that the father must prove his claim, it includes recovery even against money already held under the kiddushin. Even according to the conclusion there that the party in whose domain the uncertainty arose must bring proof, the first clause, where the defect is discovered in the father's house, requires the father to prove his position even against the kiddushin money. The same is true in Even HaEzer 39. Accordingly, in the case of "after betrothal I was raped," Rabbi Yehoshua says, "We do not live by her mouth until she brings proof for her words," which implies that this applies even against the kiddushin money. He married her on the assumption that she was a virgin and she was found not to be one, which constitutes a mistaken transaction like any other defect. Even according to the conclusion that the party in whose domain the doubt arose must bring proof, here the absence of virginity was discovered at the first intercourse after marriage, which necessarily means that the condition changed sometime during the period of betrothal. The doubt thus arose while she was in her father's domain, and the father bears the burden of proof even against property already held by him. Tosafot on 75, in the passage beginning "the first clause, here it was found," write that the case can involve a possibility that she was assaulted after the chuppah, but they say this only according to Rava. According to Rav Ashi's answer, which does not use Rava's principle, that assumption is unnecessary. There is another way to answer Tosafot even according to Rava. The woman's claim "after betrothal I was raped" is accepted, according to one approach, because of a migo, as the Gemara on 12 explains that where one side makes a definite claim and the other an uncertain one a migo can even extract money. Since Rabbi Yehoshua's ruling applies even against the kiddushin money and he nevertheless rejects the woman's migo, it follows that in his view even a migo used to retain property is insufficient in this setting. The Gemara therefore properly asks what distinguishes one migo from the other. I later found that Tumim elsewhere gives a similar answer. He writes that the Gemara could have formulated Rabbi Yehoshua's concession specifically with respect to the kiddushin money, but it did not need to because the explanation just given is correct. Tumim further questions Ramban, who holds that a migo may be used to extract money, from Ketubot 87 concerning a woman who reduces the amount of her ketubah. The Gemara asks that the document is merely a worthless shard, and Tosafot explain that the issue likewise involves a migo to extract money. If so, Ramban's rule seems contradicted by that sugya. In my view there is no difficulty. According to the opinion that a migo may extract money, the Mishnah in Bava Metzia 2, where one says, "The entire garment is mine," and the other says, "Half is mine," raises the question why the latter is not believed through a migo that he could have claimed the whole garment. One must answer that we do not recognize a migo from one monetary claim to a different monetary claim. See Shach, rule 13. Rabbah's statement explaining why one who partially admits a claim must swear does not contradict this, because there the migo is used merely to defend against the same demand. Accordingly, Ramban can say that in the case of an allegedly forged document, a migo may be used even to extract because both alternatives concern the same money. In the reduced ketubah case, however, there is no valid migo either way. If she were to say that the document for one thousand is genuine but that the excess was already paid, she would become one who admits partial payment of a document. If instead she claims that the document is fully genuine and all of it remains due, that would be a migo from one monetary claim to a different monetary claim. Tumim further questions the rule, derived from Tosafot concerning one who recognizes his utensils or books in another person's possession, that we do not accept a migo to extract the object itself even when the claimant returns the purchase price. Tosafot ask why the owner is not believed to say that the items were stolen through a migo that he could say he lent them to that person, and answer that a migo is not used to extract property, even though the claimant repays the purchaser's money. Tumim asks that according to our ruling this answer cannot work, because we rule that where one side says definitely and the other uncertainly a migo can extract. Here the purchaser says only "perhaps." In my view there is no difficulty. Ketubot 16 asks whether the anonymous Mishnah follows Rabban Gamliel and answers that Rabban Gamliel ruled only in a definite versus uncertain case, not where both claims are definite. The Rosh asks that in the case of a widow the heirs make only an uncertain claim, and answers that because beit din advances their defense for them, their position is treated as a definite claim. Thus even though in the case of "after betrothal I was raped" the husband himself had no reason to know and his position is technically uncertain, heirs for whom beit din supplies the claim are treated as definite claimants, and Rabban Gamliel concedes in such a case. The same applies to a purchaser. Since beit din advances the purchaser's defense, the case is treated as definite versus definite, and a migo cannot be used to extract from him.
