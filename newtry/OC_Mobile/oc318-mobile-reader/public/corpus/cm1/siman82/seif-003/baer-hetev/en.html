We say. The wording seems to imply that beit din itself advances this claim for the lender even if he did not raise it, and Beit Yosef attributes that view to Rambam. The Tur's language does not imply so. The Sema suggests that even Rambam may mean only where the lender is not presently suing for payment, but the borrower is demanding return of the document because he says it was paid and offers to pay again if the lender will swear otherwise. In that posture the borrower is the claimant seeking return of the document, and perhaps even the Tur would agree that beit din may articulate the lender's defense.
His money. In siman 93, seif 18, the Tur cites Responsa Rosh implying that collateral may suffice and actual cash need not be brought. The Shach says perhaps that case differs because the party there does not remember and the oath arises through reversal, whereas here the lender holds a document and his oath is merely rabbinic, so the borrower may need to bring actual money.
The borrower. The Taz notes that Tur and Baal HaTerumot cite Geonim who hold that neither party can force the other to swear: the defendant owes nothing until the plaintiff swears, while the plaintiff need not swear until the money is ready. Beit Yosef does not reject this view, yet rules like Rambam. The Taz questions the Sema's statement that Beit Yosef says later authorities agreed with Rambam and likewise questions why the Rama omitted the Geonim's position, especially since Tur and Baal HaTerumot cite it last, suggesting acceptance. The Taz adds that where there are two opinions, the burden of proof should remain on the lender, who therefore should not be able to force the borrower to swear until he himself swears. Maharam Recanati also rules like the Geonim, and the Taz regards that as the practical law. See the Shach.
