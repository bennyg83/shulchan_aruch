We say to him. See Baer Heitev, through its statement that only the borrower demands that the lender return the document, etc. See Responsa Galia Masechta, end of siman 8, which notes that this requires consideration in light of the Sema's own ruling in siman 72, seif katan 49, in the name of the Ran, concerning collateral: when the lender does not demand payment and it is instead the owner of the collateral who demands its return, the lender swears only a hesset oath. Perhaps there is nevertheless a distinction in this respect between a document and collateral. See there, and see the Shach there.
Bring his money. See Baer Heitev, through its statement that one can say he must specifically bring cash. Urim VeTumim writes that if the borrower says he has no cash, it is obvious that collateral suffices, since when he says he has no cash he may discharge the debt with movable property, as stated above in siman 101, and he is not required to swear at all that he has cash. Netivot HaMishpat likewise agrees. Responsa Galia Masechta, siman 12, however, writes that here payment with movable property does not suffice even when the borrower says he has no cash. According to the Sema, cited in the preceding Baer Heitev, the case is one where the lender is not pressing the borrower for payment at all; rather, the borrower demands that the lender return his document, and for this reason beit din advances the lender's claim for him. Where the lender is not seeking payment, siman 74, seifim 5 and 6, already establishes that the borrower cannot force him to accept an appraisal of property. See the Sema there. Consequently, here the borrower must specifically tender cash. As for the Shach's challenge from the Tur, siman 93, seif 18, although there the lender is the claimant, that case concerns an oral claim and he prevails only if the borrower swears. Since the borrower who reverses the oath is seeking to free himself from his oath, he is the one seeking to complete the matter and can therefore be regarded as the party seeking affirmative relief. See there at length. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, concerning a creditor who sued on a document and the borrower demanded, 'Swear to me that I have not repaid you.' The creditor replied, 'Bring the money and I will swear.' The borrower said that he had no cash, only land, and offered to designate the land as an apotiki from which the creditor could collect after swearing. The creditor refused the land and said he would wait until cash was available. The borrower objected that delay caused his property to depreciate and damaged his prospects of remarrying because the public report of the debt harmed his standing, a circumstance that Knesset HaGedolah, siman 24, treats as depreciation of assets. Beit Ephraim discusses at length the source of the rule that the defendant must prepare money before the claimant swears, and then analyzes the Shach's statement that collateral is insufficient and actual cash must be brought. He writes that this is difficult to accept. The reason to require money before the oath is that otherwise the creditor may say, 'Why should I swear, perhaps he will then fail to pay and my oath will have been for nothing.' If the borrower tenders adequate collateral, there is no concern that the oath will be for nothing, so the lender should swear. Afterward, the borrower may indeed have to swear that he has no cash according to the Rosh cited by the Shach in siman 101, seif katan 5, while according to other poskim he need merely accept a ban. But there is no basis to force him now to swear or accept a ban that he has no cash before accepting movable property or land. Perhaps the Shach speaks only where the borrower actually has money but chooses to offer collateral instead; in that case the Shach requires cash. If he says he has no cash and has only collateral, however, we do not compel him to sell it and produce money. This accords with the Tumim and Netivot cited above. All of this applies where the lender is pressing the borrower for payment and is prepared to swear if cash is tendered. Where the lender is not pressing at all and the borrower instead demands that the lender swear or return the document, it appears that the lender may answer that he does not wish to swear now, because the rule is that beit din ordinarily attends first to the claimant. Even though where the defendant's assets are depreciating beit din may attend first to the defendant, that may be only where the defendant says, 'Swear, and then I will satisfy you according to law with cash.' Where he says that he has no cash and only land, the lender may say, 'I do not wish to swear now; I will wait until you have money,' as explained in simanim 74 and 101 in the name of a responsum of the Rosh. Thus, even though the borrower's assets depreciate, the lender may seemingly refuse land. Responsa Rashba III, Livorno edition, siman 43, however, records a dispute among the Rashba's teachers where the borrower's assets depreciate because of the delay, and the Rashba himself inclines toward those who require the lender in such a case to accept land. Accordingly, in the case described above, where the borrower says that his assets are depreciating, he plainly may compel the lender and say, 'Return my document, or swear and take the land, because I have nothing else with which to pay and my assets are losing value.' One cannot say that the lender is regarded as the party in possession for this purpose and may plead that he follows the opposing opinion. The dispute concerned an undisputed debt. Here the borrower protests that the document is being retained although he owes nothing, and says that according to the lender's own claim he should swear and take payment. If the lender's concern is merely that his oath might prove useless, the land stands ready. It therefore appears that even the opposing view would agree that where the debtor suffers actual loss through depreciation and the debt itself is disputed, the lender must accept land. The Rosh's concern about closing the door before borrowers does not apply in the same way where the borrower denies the debt. Even if one says that concern still applies because after the oath the debt will become established and the lender will find only land to collect, the borrower can still say: 'Because my assets are depreciating, you must swear now. I contend that you will not be able to swear at all, because nothing is owed. No one forces you to take land after you swear; once the debt is established, you may wait if you prefer. I merely refuse to remain tied to you in litigation when I say that no debt exists.' Beit Ephraim therefore concludes that in the case before him the law favors the borrower. At the end of the responsum he explains that this is where the document states that payment is to be made in cash. He further writes that all this applies where the document contains no clause of credibility. If it contains a credibility clause, so that strictly speaking the creditor collects without an oath and the hesset oath imposed upon him is comparable to an oath after payment because the borrower brings payment to beit din, then the law favors the creditor. The creditor can say, 'I hold an established debt. If you pay me according to law in cash I will accept it, and afterward I will swear the post payment hesset oath. If you have no cash, I prefer to wait.' Although according to the Rashba cited above even where the document contains a credibility clause the borrower may perhaps compel the creditor to accept land if his assets are depreciating, in this setting the creditor may be considered in possession and may rely upon the view of the Rashba's teacher and the plain meaning of the Rosh cited by the Tur in siman 74, namely that even where the assets depreciate the borrower must satisfy him specifically with cash and the lender may elect to wait. Since the document contains a credibility clause, the debt is established, and the lender can say, 'If you want me to swear, first satisfy me in cash; afterward you can demand that I swear hesset as after payment.' If, however, the borrower wishes to exchange or transfer land and the lender prevents the transaction by lodging a protest in beit din, it is possible that the borrower is then considered the party in possession for this issue and may say, 'The land is before you; swear and take it,' in accordance with the opinion cited by Rashba that where the borrower's assets depreciate the lender cannot insist on waiting. Beit Ephraim also cites Ateret Tzvi, and similarly Maamar Kaddishin here, who distinguish the Shach's rule about collateral according to whether the document expressly requires payment in cash, but he writes that this distinction lacks sufficient basis. See there at length.
The borrower shall swear. See Baer Heitev in the name of the Taz, who agrees with the Geonim cited by the Tur that the lender cannot compel the borrower to swear so long as the lender himself has not first sworn. The Shach agrees instead with the Rambam and the Mechaber, distinguishing the Geonim's case as other persons who swear and collect without a document, such as one who was robbed, one who was injured, or a shopkeeper relying on his ledger. Such claimants have no Torah right to collect even with an oath, but collect by rabbinic enactment. Here, by contrast, the lender holds an authenticated document and under Torah law collects without an oath. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat agree. Galia Masechta writes that as a matter of logic the ruling of the Geonim appears correct: the lender cannot compel the borrower to swear until the lender has first sworn and maintained the document's enforceability. This applies, however, only when the lender is the one suing and pressing for payment, which is the case addressed by the Geonim and by the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Taz. Where the lender is not pressing at all and the borrower instead demands return of the document, which is the Rambam's case according to the Sema, because the borrower wishes to free himself from appearing indebted and thereby regain the ability to conduct business on credit, even the Geonim would agree that the borrower must first swear that he presently lacks means. He then remains free from collection until he later acquires funds, at which point he may compel the lender to swear or return the document. Responsa Beit Ephraim similarly distinguishes: if the borrower demands return of the document, the borrower must swear first; if the lender is the claimant, the lender cannot compel the borrower to swear until the lender swears first, in accordance with the Geonim and the conclusion of the Taz. Beit Ephraim also questions the Shach for grouping a shopkeeper relying on his ledger with the other persons who swear and collect, because in siman 92, seif katan 14, the Shach himself accepts the Baal HaMaor's view that such a shopkeeper would by strict law collect without an oath. He further questions the Taz's proof from the responsum of the Rosh and cites Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 3 of the laws of hiring. It also appears from his words that a general ban should nevertheless be imposed upon the defendant, because we suspect that he may merely be delaying by falsely saying, 'I have nothing,' in order to postpone the lender's oath, knowing that the lender will not swear needlessly where there is nothing to collect. He need not swear concerning this, however; acceptance of a general ban suffices. See there further.
