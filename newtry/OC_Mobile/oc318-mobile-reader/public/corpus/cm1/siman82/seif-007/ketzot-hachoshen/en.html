You waived it for me. Waiver is not independently a strong defense against a document. Its force comes from the migo that the borrower could have claimed payment. This therefore appears to be a case where a migo is used to impose an oath on the creditor. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 97 writes that we do not ordinarily say migo merely to impose an oath. Gidulei Terumah ties the issue to the dispute whether migo can exempt from an oath: according to the authority who does not allow migo to remove an oath, perhaps it likewise cannot create one. If the creditor himself is uncertain whether he waived, while the borrower says with certainty, "You waived the debt," it appears the borrower's certain claim should prevail. Even though the creditor holds a document, the borrower also has the presumption of possession of his money. Where a certain claim is joined to possession, certainty can prevail over uncertainty, as in the first chapter of Ketubot where Rabban Gamliel relies on a supporting presumption. This differs from a claim, "You owe me another debt equal to this one." Such a counterclaim does not extinguish the present debt. Each party can collect separately. Waiver, by contrast, directly extinguishes the documented debt. Therefore a certain waiver claim combined with possession is stronger against an uncertain creditor.
