Where there is a document, we say migo even to extract. Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim proves this from the case of "another debt" in Perek HaKotev. The Shach likewise assumes that all authorities agree that a fully valid document allows a migo to extract. This is not entirely necessary. Ramban explains the "another debt" case differently. The creditor's migo is not being used to prove the debt written in the document. That debt remains standing. The migo concerns only the money actually received, as to which the creditor says it was payment of another debt. Since he is already in possession of that received money, the migo is not extracting anything at that stage. The fact that this in turn leaves the written debt collectible is only a consequence. Accordingly, even with a valid document one could maintain that a migo does not directly extract. This explains the students of Rabbeinu Yonah, who ask why a woman who admits part of her ketubah is required to swear and answer that a migo of complete denial would be a migo to extract. The existence of a ketubah does not automatically turn every migo into one supporting collection. Those authorities who do allow a migo to extract with a formal document can explain it by treating the document as though the amount were already collected for this limited purpose. Even though we generally rule like Beit Hillel that a debt awaiting collection is not legally collected, Ramban in responsum 100 says the document can have enough strength for the migo to function as retention rather than extraction. In any event, the special force belongs only to a document collectible from encumbered property. A paper that does not carry land responsibility is not treated as already collected even according to Beit Shammai's broader theory. This follows Gittin concerning Shemittah, where only a document with property responsibility can be treated as collected. Therefore a migo to extract should not gain special strength from a document that does not collect from purchasers. The Shach also writes that in an oral loan where the borrower admitted private payment but the creditor says the money was for another debt, the creditor cannot use the "another debt" migo. According to Ramban's explanation, however, even there the creditor's credibility concerns the money he actually received, not extraction of the original debt. If the receipt of the money is admitted and the only dispute is how it was applied, Ramban's logic can apply even without a formal document, depending upon the exact structure of the underlying oral obligation.
