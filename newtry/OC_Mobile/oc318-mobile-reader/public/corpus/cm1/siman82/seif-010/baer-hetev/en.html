Remains in force. R. Yehudah, son of the Rosh, writes of Reuven who held against Shimon a promissory note containing a clause of trust, and also held a separate document recording that Shimon had sworn to repay him. When Reuven demanded payment, Shimon produced the torn document containing his oath as proof that he had paid. The ruling was that he nevertheless had to pay by virtue of the promissory note still held by Reuven. Shach.
He shall pay. Baal HaTerumot writes that this applies even where the note contains no clause of trust. That is specifically where the lender stands by the note and says that it contains no interest. But if the lender admits that the transaction contained interest and claims only that it was arranged permissibly, the Rama writes in Yoreh Deah 169 that the lender must take a stringent oath while holding a sacred object. The reason is that by his own admission he has impaired the force of the note. Sema. See the Shach there in Yoreh Deah, seif katan 81; Tekfo Kohen, siman 121; Responsa Rama, siman 86; Responsa Maharashach, part 2, siman 158 and part 3, siman 30.
Hesset. This is the law of all defendants where there is no written note. If, by contrast, we accepted the borrower's demand that the lender swear before collecting, the lender would have to take a stringent oath while holding a sacred object, as one who swears and collects. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes that the Tur initially includes the claim that the note is forged, as does the Mechaber. Yet when discussing a claim made after payment, the Tur gives examples such as a trust document or interest and omits forgery. It appears that where the claim is forgery, the borrower cannot require an oath even after payment, since the note had been authenticated. Moreover, an oath claim must address the money itself. This appears correct.
