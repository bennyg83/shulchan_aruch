If the creditor admits that the note contains interest but claims that it was arranged permissibly, Rama writes in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 169 that he must take an oath while holding a sacred object before collecting. The reason is that his admission weakens the note. Before payment, the claim “I have not collected” is stronger; after payment, the claim “I collected permissibly” is stronger. In this way Rama's rulings here and in Yoreh Deah do not conflict, as Sema writes. See what I wrote there in Shach, seif katan 79, where I explain that Rama's ruling requires study for another reason.
If he denies it, he must take a heses oath. Tur initially includes the claim that the note is forged, as Mechaber does. Yet when discussing a claim raised after payment, Tur mentions only that it was a trust note, that it contained interest, or that the borrower had never borrowed, and says that after payment he may sue and, if the creditor denies it, the creditor takes a heses oath. Tur omits forgery. It appears that after payment the borrower cannot require an oath on a forgery claim, since the note had been authenticated.
