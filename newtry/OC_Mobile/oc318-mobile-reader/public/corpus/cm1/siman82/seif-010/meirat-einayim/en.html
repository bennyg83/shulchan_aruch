And the lender stands upon his document, etc. Rabbi Yehudah son of the Rosh ruled concerning Reuven, who held against Shimon a promissory note containing a credibility clause, and also a separate document stating that Shimon had sworn to repay. When Reuven demanded payment, Shimon showed him the torn oath document as proof that he had paid. He ruled that Shimon must nevertheless pay by virtue of the promissory note still in Reuven's possession. See Darkei Moshe.

Rather, he must pay, etc. The Baal HaTerumot writes that this applies even where the document contains no credibility clause, specifically where the lender stands on the document and says there is no interest in it. If the lender admits that interest was included but says it was taken permissibly, for example through a gentile or the like, Rama writes in Yoreh Deah siman 169 that a lender seeking to collect on the document must first swear while holding a sacred object. The reason is that by his own admission he has impaired the force of the document.
And if he denies it, he takes an oath of hesset. This is the ordinary rule for a defendant sued without a document. Here the lender must take hesset only if he denies and says that he never took interest. If he admits that he took interest but says it was permitted, Rama writes at the end of Yoreh Deah siman 169 that he need not even take hesset, because there is a presumption that a person does not abandon a permitted course and consume a prohibited one. Thus, after the borrower has already paid and then demands return of alleged interest, the lender's claim, 'I took it permissibly,' is stronger than a bare denial. By contrast, when the lender comes with his document to collect and the borrower says it contains interest, the lender's denial that there was any interest is stronger than his admission that there was interest but that it was permitted. This resolves Rama's rulings without contradiction.
