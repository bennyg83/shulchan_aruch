The borrower shall swear. See Baer Heitev in the name of the Taz, who agrees with the Geonim cited by the Tur that the lender cannot compel the borrower to swear so long as the lender himself has not first sworn. The Shach agrees instead with the Rambam and the Mechaber and distinguishes the Geonim's case as other persons who swear and collect without a document, such as one who was robbed, one who was injured, or a shopkeeper relying on his ledger, whose right to collect is by rabbinic enactment. Here, by contrast, the lender holds an authenticated document and under Torah law collects without an oath. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat likewise agree. Galia Masechta writes that logically the ruling of the Geonim appears correct when the lender is suing and pressing for payment: he cannot compel the borrower to swear until he first swears and maintains the document. This is the case addressed by the Geonim and by the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Taz. But where the lender is not pressing at all and the borrower demands return of the document, as in the Rambam's case according to the Sema, because the borrower wishes to free himself from appearing indebted so that he can transact on credit, even the Geonim would agree that the borrower must first swear that he has nothing. He is then free of the lender until he later acquires means, at which point he may compel the lender to swear or return the document. Responsa Beit Ephraim likewise distinguishes between a borrower who demands return of his document, who must swear first, and a lender who initiates collection, who cannot compel the borrower to swear until he himself swears first, in accordance with the Geonim and the Taz. Beit Ephraim also questions the Shach for grouping a shopkeeper relying on his ledger with the other persons who swear and collect, since the Shach himself in siman 92, seif katan 14, agrees with the Baal HaMaor that by strict law such a shopkeeper collects without an oath. He also questions the Taz's proof from the responsum of the Rosh and cites Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 3 of the laws of hiring. It appears further that the defendant should nevertheless accept a general ban, because we suspect that he may merely be delaying by saying he has nothing in order to postpone the lender's oath, knowing that the lender will not swear for nothing when there is nothing to collect. A formal oath is unnecessary; a general ban suffices. See there further.
