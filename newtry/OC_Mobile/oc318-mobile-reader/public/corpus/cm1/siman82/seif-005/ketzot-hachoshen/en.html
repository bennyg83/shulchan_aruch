He collects without an oath. The Sema asks from Even HaEzer siman 96, where a suspected widow does not collect her ketubah; rather, the heirs swear and are exempt. The Shach distinguishes a debt lien, which is biblical, from the ketubah lien, which is rabbinic. The Sages who created the ketubah requirement of oath can say that if she cannot swear she loses. The Taz in Even HaEzer makes the same distinction. There is a difficulty from the Rosh in Perek Kol HaNishba'in. The Rosh proves that a suspected creditor seeking to collect from orphans cannot collect from the law of "one cannot bequeath an oath to his children," and then adds proof from Rav, who did not collect a ketubah for a widow whom he regarded as suspected. If ketubah is fundamentally different because its lien is rabbinic, what proof is there from ketubah to an ordinary debt? This question is raised in Pnei Yehoshua. The Taz's other proof from Rashba concerning an insane widow is likewise inconclusive. Rashba speaks of a widow who became mentally incompetent and whose heirs sought to collect her ketubah from the husband's heirs. He rules for the husband's heirs because a widow collects from orphans only with an oath. The same stringency applies in ordinary debts collected from orphans. Thus the case does not prove a special distinction between ketubah and debt. There is also reason to question the Taz's assumption that an ordinary creditor who becomes insane can necessarily collect from the borrower without an oath. If the borrower says with certainty, "I paid," and the creditor is unable to make a certain claim, this may resemble siman 59, where a certain borrower defeats an uncertain creditor. The creditor's insanity may be treated as a very weak uncertainty that cannot be repaired by advancing claims for him personally. Heirs are different because beit din advances the decedent's claims for them. For the creditor himself, we generally do not supply a factual certainty he does not possess. If, however, an incompetent creditor is treated like one for whom beit din must "open the mouth of the mute," perhaps collection remains possible. The sugya concerning a deaf mute or mentally incompetent woman in Ketubot suggests such a possibility, though Tosafot there limits the principle. Therefore the law of an insane creditor requires separate study. In any event, Rambam's reason in the suspected creditor case appears to be a penalty or loss of the rabbinic right to collect without an available oath, not the Torah rule of inability to swear, because that latter rule does not ordinarily apply to rabbinic oaths. Thus an innocent person who cannot swear for reasons such as mental incapacity may not be treated the same as one suspected of false swearing.
