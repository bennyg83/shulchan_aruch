(Seif 8) “Some say that the creditor collects without an oath.” This is the view of the Ramah cited by the Tur: since he holds a validated note and the Sages alone imposed an oath upon him, he should not lose because he is unable to swear. The Tur concludes that this is reasonable. Although the Ramah’s formulation would also include other suspected claimants who ordinarily swear and collect, such as one who admits partial payment on his note, the Tur adopted his ruling only for this oath, where the creditor says that he received nothing and does not impair his note, but the debtor merely demands, “Swear to me that I did not repay you.” Since this oath is a leniency for the debtor and is administered only at his request, payment should not be delayed where the creditor cannot swear. By contrast, an impaired note is subject to an oath even without the debtor’s request, because the impairment itself creates a presumption of weakness. The Tur therefore did not decide like the Ramah in that case when he cited the dispute between the Rambam and the Ramah in siman 92, seif 13. A further proof is that in Even HaEzer, siman 93, the Tur rules that where a woman who has impaired her ketubah is suspected, the opposing party swears and is exempt. The Sema distinguishes there between a woman’s ketubah and other notes, but this is exceedingly difficult. What basis is there for such a distinction? Moreover, the rule that one who impairs a note must swear is learned only from the Mishna in HaKotev concerning a woman who impairs her ketubah, and from there the Gemara in Shevuot derives the rule for an ordinary note. If a woman’s ketubah were different and more stringent regarding a suspected claimant, how could the law of an impaired debt note be derived from it? Plainly, that distinction has no foundation. According to the explanation given here, everything is resolved, with God’s help.
