But if he seized, etc. Everyone wondered what distinction there is between a Torah scholar and an ordinary person, since with every rabbinic oath the rule is that if he seized property, we do not remove it from him. The Shach, seif katan 17, wrote that from the words of the Rosh in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim, in the incident of Rav Mesharshiya, it is evident that although we do not remove the property from him, we nevertheless place him under a ban. If so, this is the distinction regarding a Torah scholar: even a ban is not imposed upon him. See there. That an ordinary person is placed under a ban is indeed correct, as is explicit in the Rosh. But to say that a Torah scholar is not subject even to a ban requires study. The Rosh wrote this in Chazakat HaBatim regarding the statement of Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya, "The Sages said that one who comes to collect from the property of orphans may collect only with an oath." From there the Rosh proves that although one who seized property need not return it, he is nevertheless placed under a ban, as in the case of a hesset oath. But if a Torah scholar is not even placed under a ban, the difficulty remains: why was Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya so alarmed? As a Torah scholar, nothing would have been done to him, and the Rosh's question remains unresolved. One cannot say that the distinction stated between a Torah scholar and an ordinary person applies only to the oath demanded by a debtor who says, "Swear to me that I have not paid you," since that oath is not expressly mentioned in the Mishnah, whereas an oath expressly mentioned in the Mishnah, such as that of a claimant who admits partial payment, and certainly the oath of heirs, is imposed even on a Torah scholar. If that were so, this distinction would properly have been cited by the decisors only concerning that particular oath and not concerning the oaths of the Mishnah. For then why did the Rosh labor there to ask that even if Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya would be exempt from the oath required of one who collects from orphans, he would nevertheless be liable to a hesset oath? See there. This is difficult, for from hesset he would be exempt as a Torah scholar, whereas from the oath required to collect from orphans he would not be exempt. Certainly hesset is more lenient than the oath imposed when the debtor says, "Swear to me," since the latter is an oath originating in the period of the Mishnah, and in the latter case he is a claimant whereas in hesset he is a defendant. The Baal HaMaor likewise wrote that it is more reasonable to exempt a defendant than a claimant. There is a greater difficulty. According to this approach, the enactment of Rav Nachman establishing a hesset oath would have no application at all to a Torah scholar, since beit din would neither descend to his property nor place him under a ban. We find no such proposition anywhere, that a hesset oath does not apply to a Torah scholar. Certainly, as I wrote, hesset is more lenient than the oath demanded by a debtor who says, "Swear to me," as the Rosh wrote that there is an opinion considering that oath merely hesset, whereas he himself concluded that it is an oath imposed while holding a sacred object; see the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. The Ran likewise wrote that an oath imposed on a defendant is more lenient. If one says that although a Torah scholar is not placed under a ban, beit din nevertheless tells him, "You are transgressing the enactment of the Sages who instituted this oath," and that this is why Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya feared being, Heaven forbid, within that category, then hesset can indeed apply to a Torah scholar. But if so, it remains difficult how the Rosh knew that an ordinary person is placed under a ban. His proof is from Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya, but perhaps Rabbah feared the oath merely so as not to be called a transgressor. It is strained to say that this itself is the Rosh's intent: since we see that a Torah scholar is called a transgressor, for otherwise what did Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya fear, we move one level upward and say that although beit din does not descend to an ordinary person's property, it does place him under a ban. For if an ordinary person too were not placed under a ban but were merely called a transgressor, what distinction would remain between a Torah scholar and an ordinary person? Yet the Gemara in Shevuot says that with a Torah scholar "we do not attend to the claim against him," which implies that an ordinary person is placed under a ban. This interpretation of the Rosh is strained, since the essential proposition that a Torah scholar is within the category of a transgressor but is not placed under a ban is entirely absent from the Rosh's words, though it would be the foundation of the ruling and ought to have been stated explicitly. Moreover, the Tur below in siman 87, concerning hesset, nowhere explains that there is any distinction between a Torah scholar and an ordinary person. How could he omit a rule that arises every day? It is strained to say, as the Taz wrote, that a stringent oath, namely an oath of the Mishnah, is not administered to a Torah scholar, whereas the more lenient hesset oath is administered even to him. It would still be difficult why the Rosh asked that Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya would be liable to hesset, for perhaps his concern was precisely to avoid the more stringent oath. Still, that difficulty can be answered; see what I wrote below in siman 87. The principal point, however, remains strained. The reason we do not attend to the demand for an oath against a Torah scholar is that we do not suspect him of retaining a paid deed. Why should we then suspect him sufficiently to impose hesset? Does the fact that he does not swear while holding a sacred object remove that suspicion? And what are we to say according to those who make no distinction in this regard between an oath of the Mishnah and hesset? Initially I thought to resolve the difficulty above along the lines of the Sema, but with further clarification. It would not mean, as the Sema wrote, that the debtor may tell the claimant, "Either swear or tear up the deed," for we do not find it stated that the debtor can compel the lender in that manner. Rather, one may say that seizure is effective for an ordinary person, and likewise for a Torah scholar, only if he seized before beit din ruled, "Go and swear." Once beit din has already imposed the oath, there is nothing after the ruling, and subsequent seizure is no longer effective because judgment has already been rendered. See below in siman 108 concerning the rule that one cannot bequeath an oath to his children. Where seizure is effective, the Shulchan Aruch writes, "if they preceded and seized," meaning before a final judgment, since after judgment seizure is ineffective. Therefore, with an ordinary person beit din attends to the matter and issues a ruling that he must swear, after which he can no longer seize. With a Torah scholar, beit din does not attend to the matter, specifically so that he remains able to seize, for so long as no judgment has issued he may seize. This precisely fits the Gemara's wording, "we do not attend to the claim against him." This seemed correct as a matter of law and precisely reflected the wording of the Gemara. Nor would there remain the objection raised by the Taz that a Torah scholar would thereby strip another person's garment from him, because the law itself is not changed. Beit din merely refrains from issuing a ruling so that seizure remains possible. Yet the matter remains difficult if the law is that when the Torah scholar seizes, he is placed under a ban. Why should we devise a means enabling him to seize and thereby violate a rabbinic ban? If he must then return the property, what has this accomplished? What benefit is it to the Torah scholar that he can seize if doing so subjects him to the Sages' ban?
Therefore it appears more correct that even with an ordinary person there is greater severity when he seeks to collect through his deed and the debtor says, "Swear to me," than when the other person sues him claiming, "You have one hundred in my possession," where the applicable oath is hesset. The reason is that in the latter case we suspect him of lying. It is therefore more severe to allow a person to extract money through his own assertion than to allow him to retain money already in his possession. Accordingly, even in the period of the Mishnah, when hesset did not yet exist for one already in possession, the Sages nevertheless imposed an oath on one who came to collect by deed and thereby extract money. A Torah scholar, however, is not suspected of deliberately making a false claim. The concern is only that through forgetfulness or inadvertence, because he is preoccupied with his studies, he may have forgotten, as the Gemara says, "How much more so the Master, who may have forgotten." That concern applies when he has no deed in hand. But when he has a deed, the deed supports his assertion that he was not paid, since had he been paid he would immediately have torn it up, for he would not allow injustice to remain in his dwelling. Forgetfulness therefore does not apply in the same way. Thus this oath concerning a deed is more lenient for a Torah scholar, to the extent that he is not placed under a ban for declining it, than an ordinary hesset oath. In the latter case one can still say that perhaps he forgot, and the oath will cause him to focus and remember. Accordingly, there is no distinction in hesset between a Torah scholar and other people. The Rosh therefore properly argued that if Rabbah bar Rav Mesharshiya would not sue on the basis of his deed but would instead say that the property had been acquired by him, he would have to take a hesset oath, and regarding hesset there is no distinction between a Torah scholar and others. Nevertheless, the matter still requires study. At least as a matter of law it appears clear that the oath of heirs and similar oaths certainly make no distinction between a Torah scholar and other people. Likewise, regarding hesset we find no distinction between a Torah scholar and others. But where a claimant holds a deed, the opposing party may demand, "Swear to me," and the Torah scholar had seized beforehand, whether he is placed under a ban or not remains unresolved as a matter of law. It also appears correct to me that if a final judgment has already been issued, seizure in the presence of witnesses is no longer effective.
