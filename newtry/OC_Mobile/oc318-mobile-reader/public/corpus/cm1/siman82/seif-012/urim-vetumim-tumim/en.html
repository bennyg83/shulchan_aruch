The lender swears and collects. The Shach wrote that this follows the opinion that even when the witnesses' handwriting is authenticated from another source, they are believed to say that there was a condition, since this is a separate matter and resembles a plea of payment. According to the dissenting opinions, however, how can the borrower be believed even to impose an oath? Such a plea resembles a trust deed. His words are nearly compelled. Otherwise, how can we say that witnesses are not believed, when their handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere, to say that there was a condition? The borrower himself could plead that there was a condition, thereby requiring the lender to swear. How could the lender swear when the witnesses contradict him? If we say that in such a case the witnesses are certainly lying and are not believed, so that we assume there was definitely no condition, why should the borrower be believed sufficiently to impose an oath? It is strained to say that the rule that the witnesses are not believed applies only where the borrower is not alive to make the plea, and that for heirs we do not plead an entirely uncommon matter. This plea must indeed be entirely uncommon, for otherwise even within the term we would plead for the heirs that perhaps there was a condition, as discussed above in seif katan 7. That may suffice where there are no witnesses saying so, but where witnesses themselves say that there was a condition, why should we not plead for the heirs a matter for which there is substantial indication? Still, I am uncertain about this. Certainly the witnesses themselves are not believed, because they should have written the condition into the deed. How could they write the deed without qualification and omit the principal condition upon which it depends? See the Ran, who explicitly writes, according to the authorities who hold that the witnesses are not believed when their handwriting is authenticated elsewhere, that the reason is that it is improper for them to conceal the condition from the deed and fail to record it. The Ran asks that if so, even where their handwriting is not authenticated from another source they should not be believed, just as with a trust deed. According to that view one must answer that since the deed itself was written properly and merely required the addition of the condition, so long as it has not yet been authenticated in beit din the witnesses may complete their testimony, since their testimony is not yet finalized. In any event, the reason they are not believed is that their conduct would be improper. If so, where the borrower says that there was a condition known to the witnesses and they failed to record it, the Shach's position is reasonable, for such conduct would be improper and witnesses are presumed to act properly. Why should the borrower be able to impose an oath on that basis? But where he says that the condition existed privately between the parties and was never known to the witnesses, so that they had no reason to write it, there is no impropriety at all. See above in siman 46, where if the witnesses say that they learned of the condition only afterward, they are believed even though their handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere. The same should therefore apply where the borrower says there was a condition unknown to the witnesses.
Because we do not say migo to extract money. The Shach, seif katan 21, challenges this, for the migo here stands together with a fully valid deed, and we do say migo to extract where there is a fully valid deed. This is indeed a fully valid deed. What difference does it make that it was initially conditional? It is now fully valid. If a deed was initially given as a trust arrangement on condition that the lender later lend the money, and afterward he actually lent it, would it not become a fully valid deed? The Shach's question is strong. It appears to me that if the condition was, for example, that the borrower perform a certain act before Tishrei, Tishrei passed, and the lender says that he did not perform it, so that there is no longer any opportunity to satisfy the condition and the deed now has full force without qualification, then the Shach is certainly correct. It resembles a deed that was initially a trust deed and later became effective through the actual loan, and is now a fully valid deed. But if the condition was that whenever the borrower performs a certain act the deed is void, without any time limit, and the borrower says that he already performed it while the lender says that he has not yet performed it, then the lender himself admits that his lien remains precarious. The borrower can still satisfy the condition at any time if he wishes. Such a deed is not fully valid in the relevant sense. According to the Shach, the reason we allow a migo to extract money where there is a deed is that the deed is regarded as though already collected. This deed cannot be regarded as collected, because the borrower can satisfy the condition at any time. How can it be considered already collected? Therefore the authorities formulated the condition specifically in this manner and not in the reverse form. They did not describe a case where the borrower says, "I gave you the deed on condition that if you speak to the ruler on my behalf it will belong to you, and if not it will not, and you did not speak to him," while the lender responds, "It is true that this was the condition, but I did speak to him." In that case, according to the lender's own words, the deed has become fully effective, and his migo would stand together with a fully valid deed, making him believable. In the form they wrote, however, there is no fully valid deed, because at every moment the borrower retains the power to annul the obligation and lien. This is the intent of the Baal HaTerumot, who wrote: although elsewhere we say that where there is a fully valid deed one may rely on a migo to extract money, here too, since the lender admits that it was conditional, and if the borrower fulfills his condition the lien never takes effect, while the borrower claims that he did fulfill it, the lien never took effect. His precise language is, "and if he fulfills his condition," not "and if he had fulfilled his condition," showing that it still remains within the borrower's power to satisfy the condition. The language of the Shulchan Aruch likewise indicates this, for it says that the borrower "has not yet fulfilled it." The word "yet" indicates that time remains for him to fulfill it. Therefore this is not a fully valid deed regarded as already collected. This is precise.
And if one witness says that it was conditional, etc. The Shach wrote in the name of the Ran that if one witness says the deed was paid, even where the witnesses' handwriting is not authenticated from another source, we say that both witnesses testify to a fully valid deed, while one of them separately says it was paid, etc. But from the Ramah whose words are cited by the Tur in siman 46 it appears that even where the witness says it was paid, the deed is nullified. Nevertheless, it appears that even the Ramah said this only where the witnesses' handwriting is not authenticated from elsewhere. If it is authenticated from elsewhere, it is unreasonable to say that the witness is believed, for otherwise we would find one witness able to establish a monetary result. These are the Shach's words. I did not understand them. Why does he treat it as a matter of reasoning that where the handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere the witness is not believed? This is an explicit Mishnah: if one witness testifies that the deed was paid, the claimant must swear. The oath is understood as being only to appease the debtor's mind. What difference is there between that witness and any other witness once the handwriting is authenticated from elsewhere, especially regarding payment, which has no connection to the original testimony in the deed at all? Even where the witnesses' handwriting is not authenticated from elsewhere, the law follows the Ran: the witness is not believed to say that it was paid so as to nullify the deed. He only obligates the claimant to take the oath of the Mishnah. There is no migo that the witness could have remained silent and refused to authenticate his handwriting. What would that accomplish? There would still be a deed supported by one witness, namely the second witness, and the borrower would then have to contradict that witness. The first witness does not wish to force him to do so. If the borrower admits the debt, he can no longer plead payment, as above in siman 51 concerning a deed supported by one witness. One cannot say that the reason is only that such a borrower is one who cannot swear and therefore must pay, whereas here he has a supporting witness. In a case where we apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, a supporting witness does not help. The witness therefore has no migo except that he could force the borrower to contradict the other witness. As for the Shach's proof from the Ramah, the Ramah wrote only of a case where one witness says that the deed was paid and the other says it was not paid, and ruled that we do not say that the statement of one witness is as nothing against two. His intent is merely that the claimant is obligated to take the oath of the Mishnah. One should not say that because one witness contradicts the debtor's claim, that witness becomes a supporting witness exempting the claimant from the oath, as I wrote above in siman 46 in explaining the Ramah's reason. He did not write that the deed is nullified. Alternatively, the Ramah may follow the Rashba's view, which disagrees in siman 51 and holds that even where there is one witness on a deed, the borrower can plead payment. If so, the witness has a migo that he could remain silent, in which event the borrower would be believed to say that he paid. He is therefore believed now to say that it was paid. According to the Rambam's rule above in siman 51, however, the witness is certainly not believed to nullify the deed. Still, it is possible that even according to the Ran, the witness is believed to say that the deed was paid so far as collection from encumbered property is concerned. With respect to encumbered property he has a migo that he could have remained silent. If he remained silent, the result would merely be a loan evidenced by a deed with one witness, which is not collectible from encumbered property. If one argues that the witness intended through his testimony to prevent collection even from the debtor's free property, and therefore a migo based only on encumbered property should not suffice, see below in the rules of migo whether such a migo is effective. Since the Shach set out at the end of seif 7 a series of principles governing migo, and the Darchei Moshe and Sema also wrote several rules of migo in seif 7, while the later collector, author of Knesset HaGedolah, who lived after them, added in this siman many rulings concerning migo gathered like roses from responsa and earlier works, I decided to present his material according to the numbered order he established, one by one and concisely. His collection includes all the principles gathered by the Shach. I will write alongside them what appears correct in my humble opinion, sometimes supporting, sometimes rejecting, sometimes raising a question, and sometimes resolving one where there is room to do so.
