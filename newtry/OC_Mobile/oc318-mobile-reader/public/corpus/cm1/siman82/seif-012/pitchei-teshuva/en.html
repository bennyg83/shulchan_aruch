Because the borrower has not yet fulfilled it, the lender must bring proof. See Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat siman 17. He raises a difficulty from the law below, siman 241 seif 10. There the Sema explains that where a gift is conditioned on the recipient’s refraining from an act, we presume the prior state continued and therefore require proof that the condition changed. This appears to imply that if a person obligates himself to another but stipulates that he will be exempt if he performs a certain affirmative act, the obligor should have to prove that he performed it. Otherwise the obligation remains, because the prior state is presumed to continue. If that principle applies equally to an affirmative condition, why in our case must the lender prove that the borrower did not fulfill the condition? Here the borrower undertook an affirmative condition. The apparent answer is that because the borrower is in possession, he is believed to say that he fulfilled it. We would then have to distinguish between a passive condition, where the prior state can be relied upon even to extract money, and an affirmative condition, where that presumption alone does not extract money. He then notes, however, that the Shach later in this seif, citing the Bach, explains a case where there are witnesses that the transaction was conditional by saying either that the borrower is prepared to fulfill the condition or that there are witnesses that he already did. This implies that the borrower himself is not believed merely to say, “I fulfilled the condition,” which seems inconsistent with the first half of the seif. Mayim Chayim therefore suggests that the Bach and Shach do apply a presumption of nonperformance even to an affirmative condition. Their reasoning is not simply that people generally fail to perform affirmative conditions, for experience shows that people often do perform them. Rather, where fulfillment of an affirmative condition is what releases a person from an obligation, one normally designates witnesses at the time of fulfillment so that there will later be proof of discharge. If he produces no witnesses, we infer that the condition was not fulfilled, for had he fulfilled it he would ordinarily have done so before witnesses. Accordingly, the first half of our law concerns a case where there were no witnesses to the existence of the condition and the lender himself admits that the obligation was conditional. The borrower is then believed to say that he fulfilled it, because there was no practical reason for him to arrange witnesses. If the lender later denied that any condition existed, witnesses to fulfillment alone would not help the borrower, since the lender could deny the very condition to which the performance related. In the latter case, however, where witnesses establish the condition, the borrower is not believed merely to claim that he performed it. Had he performed it, he should have done so before witnesses. From this Mayim Chayim derives a broader possible rule: wherever an affirmative condition is established by witnesses, the party whose release depends upon performance must bring proof that he fulfilled it, and absent proof money may even be extracted from him. He bases this on the inference that if the condition had truly been fulfilled, it would have been fulfilled before witnesses. He later questions this conclusion from Chelkat Mechokek in Even HaEzer siman 38 and leaves the law requiring substantial further study. See also Shaar Mishpat above, siman 46, and Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 113.

And the borrower is exempt even without an oath. See Responsa Mayim Chayim, siman 17, which explains that this is not difficult in light of the opening clause. There, although the borrower is believed, he nevertheless requires an oath, because that case concerns parties who contradict one another as to whether the condition was fulfilled. Therefore, even though the document is void, the borrower must still take the oath applicable to an oral claim. Here, however, they do not dispute whether the condition was fulfilled. It may be that the lender admits that the borrower fulfilled it, or at least does not know whether he did. An oath is not imposed upon a doubtful claim. The lender's principal assertion is only that there never was any condition, and witnesses contradict him. He is therefore not believed, and the borrower is exempt even without an oath. This teaches that although the witnesses signed an unqualified document, we do not treat their signatures as testimony that there was no condition, such that the testimony of other witnesses that there was a condition would create two against two and require the borrower to swear regarding fulfillment. Rather, the borrower is exempt even from an oath. The statement that there was a condition is treated as a separate matter, as explained there. This interpretation is also compelled by the Bach as cited by the Shach in seif katan 33. See there. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 113, however, explains the ruling literally, that the borrower is exempt from an oath concerning fulfillment of the condition even where the parties also contradict one another regarding fulfillment. The distinction from the opening clause is that here the lender has been established by witnesses as having denied the truth concerning the existence of the condition. The borrower is therefore believed without an oath even concerning its fulfillment, as stated in siman 79 seif 8. Beit Meir adds that the Bach and Shach cited above require study.
