Reservation. See siman 46, seif 37 in the gloss, where if witnesses say that there was a condition, they are believed to explain that the words in the document, "without any reservation," were not written to negate the condition, but only to state that there was no reservation in the substance of the sale once the condition would be fulfilled. One might say that the same applies here to a loan. It appears more correct, however, to distinguish. In a sale it is natural to say that the intent was that there was no reservation in the substance of the sale, because people commonly reserve for themselves some right in property they sell. That concept is not applicable in the same way to a loan. Sema. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 293, and Gidulei Terumah, page 106, column 4.
The lender. The Shach writes that the Tur and Baal HaTerumot, who state this rule, follow their own position that witnesses are believed to say that there was a condition even where their signatures can be authenticated from another source, because a condition is a separate matter and resembles a claim of payment. But according to what I write below, that witnesses are not believed to assert a condition where their signatures can be authenticated elsewhere, because they thereby uproot their own testimony and it is as though they say the loan never occurred, contrary to what their document states, then here too the lender need not swear concerning the alleged condition.
The borrower. The Taz writes: We do not say that the lender should swear and collect, because according to the lender's own account this note is defective, since it was made subject to a condition. The note has therefore lost its ordinary force. See there.
Through a migo. The Shach writes that this ruling does not appear correct, although the Tur states it from Baal HaTerumot. What does it matter that the lender admits the note was made subject to a condition? According to the lender's present account, the note is now valid. This is exactly analogous to a claim that a payment was applied to a different debt and similar cases, where the lender is believed even according to the opinion that a migo cannot be used to extract money. It would be no worse than if he claimed that it was a trust document. I would not have dared dispute Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, but the Ramban, Rashba, and other later authorities already disagree with the underlying rule regarding a forged or trust document at the end of siman 83. They hold that whenever the lender has a migo, he is believed even though he admits that the document itself is ineffective. All the more so here. It therefore appears clear to me that the law follows the lender. See Responsa Maharit, simanim 70 and 105. The Sema writes in the name of the Rivash that even according to the view that a migo cannot extract money, it may be used against a claim to land, and we do not say that land remains in the possession of its owner. See siman 150. Nimukei Yosef writes that a migo cannot be used to defeat an established local custom. The Ran discusses the laws of migo at length and writes that one does not say a migo based on an alternative claim of "I do not know." Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim writes that a migo is not used jointly by two people. See there for further distinctions, and Responsa R. Yehudah Mintz, which discusses the matter at length. For the general rules of migo, see the Shach at the end of this siman, where he explains the distinctions extensively.
To extract. See Responsa Ramban, beginning of siman 100, which writes that although a migo is generally not used to extract money, where there is an independent document supporting the claim, a migo is used even to extract. It follows that if one owes another an oral debt in circumstances where he cannot claim payment, such as where the lender stipulated, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," or the like, and the debtor later made an undesignated payment without calling it repayment, the creditor cannot claim that the money was a gift on the strength of a migo, according to the Mechaber and those who follow him in siman 58, seif 2. This is also evident from siman 70, seif 4. Shach.

Authenticated elsewhere. The Shach writes: I am astonished that the Mechaber states the Tur's view without qualification, for the Rambam, Ramban, and Rashba disagree and hold that where the witnesses' signatures can be authenticated from another source, they are not believed. The Beit Yosef himself writes this at the end of siman 29, and the Mechaber himself rules so in siman 46, seif 37. I likewise concluded this in siman 29 as practical halachah, and showed that Rashi, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Nimukei Yosef, and the Ritva agree, and that the Rosh does not disagree.
One. The Sema writes: From here it is proven that when the Mechaber writes in siman 46, seif 37, "and here there is only one witness," he does not mean that the litigant must take a Torah oath against that witness, as the Ramah there understands. Rather, the person who says there was no condition has only the force of one witness, and there is one witness against him; he therefore need take only a hesset oath. This excludes the Ramah's view there that the one who says there was no condition has the force of two witnesses. As to whether one who is otherwise obligated in a Torah oath is exempted by a single witness supporting him, the Mechaber records a dispute in siman 75, seif 2, and siman 87, seif 6: some say the witness exempts him and some say he does not. The Tur rules throughout that one witness does exempt from an oath. See what I wrote in siman 29, seif katan 3. If one witness testifies that the debt was paid afterward, see the Shach, who cites a dispute among the authorities whether the same rule applies as in the case of a condition.
