Or without any, etc. Either formulation is sufficient. He may write either "without any condition" or "without any reservation." Sema.
The borrower is not believed. The Sema asks from siman 46, where the witnesses themselves may explain that the phrase "without any reservation" referred only to the substance of the sale. Why then may the borrower not make the same explanation here? The Sema distinguishes a sale from a loan: in a loan, if there is no reservation in the underlying obligation, the borrower remains bound to pay. According to that distinction, if the borrower can plausibly identify what particular matter the phrase "without reservation" referred to, he should be believed. It appears to me, however, that in siman 46 the witnesses themselves are able to explain their testimony, because witnesses may say, "We signed concerning this point but not concerning that point." A litigant, by contrast, is not believed to force a remote meaning into the wording of a document. Witnesses may clarify their own words even through a less obvious interpretation without violating the rule that once a witness has testified he cannot retract, as explained above in siman 29. This is clear.

For we do not say a migo. See Tumim. I concluded that if the condition required the borrower to perform a particular act by a fixed deadline and that deadline has passed, so that according to the claimant the condition was never fulfilled and can no longer be fulfilled, the lender is believed through a migo even though the migo is being used to extract money. In conjunction with a document we do accept this type of migo. Likewise, if the condition worked in the opposite direction, namely that the document would take effect only if the lender performed a certain act, and the borrower says he did not, while the lender admits the condition but says he already fulfilled it, the lender is likewise believed through his migo. But if the condition was that the debt would be waived whenever the borrower fulfilled a condition, no deadline was fixed, the borrower says he already fulfilled it, and the lender says he did not, then according to the lender himself there remains time for the borrower still to satisfy the condition. The document is therefore not yet a fully absolute obligation, and the lender's migo is an ordinary migo used to extract money. For the remaining rules governing such a migo, see the general rules of migo.
