A migo to extract the object while paying its value. The Shach in siman 192 writes that Tosafot does not treat this as a true migo to extract because the possessor pays the object's market value under the market overt enactment. Rabbeinu David, cited by Tosafot, treats even that as a migo to extract. The opening Mishnah of Bava Metzia supports the stricter view. In a disputed purchase where both paid the seller and one says, "All of it is mine," while the other says, "Half is mine," the latter cannot claim half through a migo that he could have claimed all, because as to the disputed half the first party is equally in possession. Tosafot calls this a migo to extract. Yet the dispute concerns ownership of the object, while the money paid to the seller is not lost. Thus paying value does not necessarily remove the character of extraction. Tosafot's leniency in the case of recognizable stolen utensils can be explained differently. There the owner makes a certain claim and the purchaser only an uncertain one as to the theft. Tosafot follows the authorities who allow a migo to extract in a case of certain versus uncertain, as Sefer HaKeritut and Mordechai derive from the ketubah case of one who says, "I was raped after betrothal." The purchaser's uncertainty is weak even where he could not have known. Tosafot also holds that heirs and purchasers are exempt in "fifty I know and fifty I do not know" primarily because they could not have known, yet this does not necessarily make their uncertainty equal to a certain claim supported by a migo. Therefore the stolen utensil case does not prove that payment of value alone makes the migo nonextractive.
