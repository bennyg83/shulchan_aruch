If the creditor admitted. The Shach rejects this ruling. Even if the creditor admits the document was originally made subject to a condition, he says that according to the creditor the condition was fulfilled and the document is now valid. This is no worse than the familiar "another debt" case, where a creditor can rely upon the document even though he admits receiving money. He compares it also to an accommodation instrument: if a creditor admits the document was initially an accommodation but says he later lent money on it, why should he not be believed? The comparison to an accommodation document is not sound. A document created as an accommodation was not authorized for writing as an operative debt instrument. As explained in siman 70, a document not authorized for writing lacks true document status. Even if witnesses later establish that the lender actually advanced money on it, the borrower can claim payment because the original paper was never a valid formal document. Therefore if the creditor himself admits it began as an accommodation instrument, he cannot rely on a migo from that paper to extract money. A conditional document is different. Such an instrument was properly authorized for writing. Once the condition is fulfilled it can collect even from encumbered property. Ran explicitly distinguishes between an accommodation document and a condition. The Shach's question is therefore substantial in the conditional case. The answer is that we use a document to support a migo to extract only where the existence of a valid document is not itself the very point in dispute. If the borrower admits the document is a valid obligation and merely says it was later paid, while the creditor says the payment was for another debt, the document stands in its ordinary strength and the creditor's migo concerns only the money received. But here the borrower says the condition was never fulfilled and therefore no debt ever arose under this document. The creditor says it was fulfilled and the document became effective. The dispute is therefore whether there is any valid document at all. A migo cannot use the disputed document itself to overcome the borrower's possession. This is like the ketubah dispute of one who says she was raped after betrothal while the husband says she was already a nonvirgin beforehand. The very existence of the ketubah obligation is disputed, so the document cannot itself transform the migo into one that extracts. Baal HaTerumot states this point explicitly: the borrower claims that the condition was performed in a way that prevented the lien from ever attaching. Thus according to the borrower's account there is no document. In such a case the creditor's migo remains a migo to extract. This resolves the Shach's question while preserving his distinction between a true conditional document and an accommodation instrument.
