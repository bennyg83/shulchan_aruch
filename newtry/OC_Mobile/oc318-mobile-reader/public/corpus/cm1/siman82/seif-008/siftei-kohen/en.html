["And this is the more reasonable view. The Sema asks that in Even HaEzer, siman 96, seif 12, the Tur rules regarding a woman who is suspected of false swearing that the opposing party swears and is exempt. He answers that this applies specifically to a woman who has property of her husband in her possession, for there is concern that she took from her husband's property or that her husband entrusted funds to her in advance. Therefore, the opposing party swears and is exempt. Here, by contrast, the discussion concerns ordinary promissory notes. It appears to me that the distinction is instead between a ketubah and an ordinary note. Regarding a loan, the Tur holds that the lien is of Torah law, as explained above, siman 39, seif katan 2. Even according to the view that the lien is rabbinic, that concerns the lien upon property, but it is obvious that by Torah law the borrower must repay what he borrowed by means of the note even without an oath. The oath is only a rabbinic enactment, and when the creditor is suspected of false swearing we restore the matter to the Torah rule. A ketubah is the reverse. By Torah law the woman has no ketubah claim at all; the Sages instituted a ketubah for a woman, as the Tur writes in Even HaEzer, siman 66, that a woman's ketubah is rabbinic. Therefore, the same Sages who instituted it also ruled that she may not collect except with an oath. If she is suspected of false swearing, we restore the matter to Torah law and she has no ketubah claim. See Beit Yosef, Even HaEzer, siman 92, seif 13, where you will find support for my explanation. A practical difference exists between my answer and the Sema's answer. According to the Sema, the same rule applies even where the husband owes her ordinary money; according to me it applies specifically to a ketubah. It is possible that even according to the Sema we are concerned that the husband set aside funds in advance only with respect to a ketubah. Note carefully. I later saw that the Derishah, Even HaEzer, siman 96, seif 28, writes that in any event there is no difficulty, because only a creditor collecting from a borrower is ordinarily not required to swear by strict law, and therefore the Sages were lenient when he is suspected. In these other oaths, however, even if the heirs or husband do not demand an oath, beit din advances that claim on their behalf, and therefore even a suspected claimant is not exempted from swearing. This does not appear correct to me at all. There is no such distinction between a creditor and a woman. A divorced woman also need not swear by strict law unless the husband says, 'Swear to me.' An widow collecting from heirs must swear, and likewise a creditor collecting from heirs must swear. If one witness testifies that the debt has been paid, or if she admits partial payment, or in similar cases, a creditor also must swear, as stated in the Gemara and all the authorities. According to the Derishah, one would therefore be forced to distinguish even within an ordinary loan between a case in which the borrower says, 'Swear to me,' and a case in which the creditor admits partial payment. There is no reason for such a distinction, since in both cases the oath is a rabbinic enactment. The Tur and Mechaber here and below, siman 92, plainly imply that the same law governs all these cases, even regarding a person suspected of false swearing. In Even HaEzer the case must concern a living husband, since it says that if she is suspected, the husband swears and is exempt, and if the husband too is suspected, there is neither an oath nor payment. When one seeks to collect from heirs and is suspected, an ordinary creditor likewise does not collect, and the heirs are exempt, as stated at the end of siman 92. See there."]
