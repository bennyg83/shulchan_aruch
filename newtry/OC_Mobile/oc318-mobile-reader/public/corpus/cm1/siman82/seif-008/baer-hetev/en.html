It is reasonable. The Sema asks that in Even HaEzer 96, seif 12, the ruling regarding a woman who is disqualified from taking an oath is that the opposing party swears and is exempt. He answers that this applies specifically to a woman who has property of her husband in her possession, where there is concern that she took her husband's money or that her husband entrusted funds to her. Therefore the opposing party swears and is exempt. Here, however, the discussion concerns ordinary promissory notes. The Shach writes that another distinction appears more correct. A ketubah differs from an ordinary debt instrument. With a loan, the obligation is a Torah obligation, for the lien is of Torah force as stated in siman 39. Even according to the opinion that liens are rabbinic, that concerns only liens on property. It is obvious that by Torah law the borrower must repay what he borrowed under a note without any oath, and the oath is only a rabbinic enactment. Therefore, where the lender is disqualified from swearing, the law reverts to the Torah rule. A ketubah, by contrast, has no Torah basis at all, but was instituted by the Sages. Thus, the Sages who created the obligation also required that she collect only with an oath; if she is disqualified from swearing, the law reverts to the Torah rule and she has no ketubah claim. See Beit Yosef, Even HaEzer 92, seif 13, which supports this explanation. The Shach discusses the matter further there. The Taz also distinguishes along similar lines; see there.
