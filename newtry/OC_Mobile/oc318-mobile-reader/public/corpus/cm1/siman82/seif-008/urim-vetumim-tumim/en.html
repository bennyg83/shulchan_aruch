And this is the more reasonable view, and the Tur likewise ruled so. The Sema asked that in Even HaEzer, siman 96, seif 12, the Mechaber and the Tur rule that if a woman is disqualified from taking an oath and seeks to collect her ketubah after divorce, the husband swears and is exempt. The Sema's answer is strained. The Shach answered by distinguishing a loan, where the debt is d'oraisa, for a rabbinic oath cannot come and uproot a Torah debt. Therefore, if the claimant is disqualified from swearing, he collects without an oath. A ketubah, however, is rabbinic. The Sages who established the obligation also established its conditions, so that a rabbinic oath reverts in effect to the status of a Torah oath, and a woman who is disqualified from swearing loses her claim. The Taz gave the same answer that the Shach also cited in the name of the Derishah, namely that this ruling applies specifically to the oath arising when the debtor says, "Swear to me," which is not mentioned in the Mishnah. There, if the claimant is disqualified from an oath, the Tur and Mechaber rule that he does not lose his claim. In Even HaEzer, however, the ruling concerns oaths of the Mishnah, such as where she admits partial payment or one witness testifies that the ketubah was paid. In those cases, if she is disqualified from swearing she loses, because such an oath has force comparable to a Torah oath. The Shach wrote that this distinction is not plausible. It appears to me that both answers must be combined, and neither is sufficient by itself. The Rosh in Ketubot, on the Gemara at 88 that says, "If he is clever, he brings her to a Torah oath," wrote in the language of Tosafot that the practical difference is according to Rabbi Eliezer, who says that the oath returns to the one obligated to take it, and the halachah follows him, so that where one cannot swear, one pays. But regarding a rabbinic oath, if the person obligated to swear is disqualified, he does not pay, as is evident in the chapter Kol HaNishba'in. In this matter, the Rif wrote in a responsum that one who admits partial payment on his deed and is disqualified from swearing loses against an opposing party who swears and is exempt. We do not say that the Sages did not institute one enactment on top of another except regarding a hesset oath, not regarding an oath of the Mishnah. See Tosafot there, s.v. "he brings her," who likewise wrote substantially the language of the Rosh, though they did not cite the Rif's responsum. The Maharsha and the Bach explain that Tosafot and the Rosh agree with the Rif's responsum, and that the case of "if he is clever" concerns a situation where both husband and wife are disqualified from swearing. Thus, regarding a rabbinic oath, since both are disqualified, the husband must pay. But if he brings her to a Torah oath, she must repay to her husband what she collected twice. According to this, the Shach's answer is difficult. How could the Tur rule here, and decide that this is the more reasonable view, that the holder of the deed collects without an oath, when the Rosh explicitly ruled in accordance with the Rif's responsum that the claimant loses? There it must concern a ketubah obligation beyond the basic one hundred or two hundred zuz, namely an additional ketubah obligation and nichsei tzon barzel, which are like an ordinary loan, as the Mishnah says, "her ketubah was one thousand zuz," referring to the additional obligation. For if it concerned the basic ketubah, what practical difference could there be between a rabbinic and Torah oath? According to the Shach, with a ketubah even a rabbinic oath has the status of a Torah oath because the Sages both created the obligation and fixed its conditions. Even where both are disqualified, she would lose, as is explicit in Even HaEzer. Yet the Rosh nevertheless rules like the Rif. The difficulty therefore remains. I initially considered explaining Tosafot and the Rosh differently from the Bach and Maharsha. It is difficult for me to say that "if he is clever" refers to a case where the husband too is disqualified from swearing. Why would the Gemara call that cleverness? Where is his wisdom in becoming disqualified by beit din? That would be shameful folly, not cleverness. Furthermore, according to the Rif, the question in Shevuot, "What is the difference between a Torah oath and a rabbinic oath?" and the answer that one difference is where the person is disqualified from swearing, in which case the opposing party does not swear and collect because the Sages do not make one enactment upon another, must refer only to hesset and not to an oath of the Mishnah, as the Rosh explicitly writes in the Rif's name. If so, the other differences mentioned there, regarding reversal of the oath and descent to property, would likewise concern hesset alone, not an oath of the Mishnah, whose law according to the Rif is like that of a Torah oath. How then could Tosafot and the Rosh add another practical difference in the case of "if he is clever" regarding reversal and descent to property? With an oath of the Mishnah those distinctions should not apply, since according to the Rif its law is like that of a Torah oath. Tosafot in Shevuot 46, s.v. "and the Rabbis," also imply that the statement made earlier regarding a disqualified person, that with a rabbinic oath we do not create one enactment upon another, concerns an oath of the Mishnah. See there.
Therefore it appeared that Tosafot disagree with the Rif and follow the Tur's ruling here, that where the holder of a deed is disqualified from swearing, since the oath is rabbinic we do not create an enactment upon an enactment, and he collects his deed. This is clear in Tosafot's reasoning. With the Rosh, one could perhaps say that although in his question concerning whether Rav and Shmuel hold that the claimant loses, and certainly pays, since the heirs of the borrower also must swear, it is possible that for practical halachah, even though we follow Rabbi Eliezer, the lender who is disqualified can still lose if the opposing party is prepared to swear. The fact that one who follows Rabbi Eliezer may do so would then relate to the circumstance that the borrower's heirs also cannot swear, making payment preferable. Tosafot, however, answer that if Rav and Shmuel held like Rabbi Eliezer they should have said that one pays half, because the borrower's heirs also cannot swear. They therefore treat both sides as balanced. If so, according to the accepted ruling following Rabbi Eliezer, why should the claimant lose even half? Necessarily, they maintain that according to Rabbi Abba we do not say that a claimant who cannot swear simply loses. If so, why should a disqualified claimant lose? He should not lose at all. Thus the Maharsha's explanation requires study, for he combined Tosafot's view with that of the Rosh although there is a significant distinction between them. Perhaps the Maharsha followed the version of Tosafot in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, which reads concerning "if he is clever" that Rav Nachman said, "If he is clever." Rav Nachman holds that the parties divide, which Tosafot explain as a penalty. According to this, when the holder of a deed is disqualified and the opposing party is not, he too ought to be penalized by losing. Therefore, according to Rav Nachman, the Rif's rule is certainly correct, and the Gemara can be explained as involving two disqualified parties. Since both fall within the penalty, the law reverts to the Torah rule, under which there is no oath at all and payment is required. This is precise. There was also room to distinguish a deed because it stands ready for collection and is therefore regarded, for several purposes, as though already collected, even according to Beit Hillel. See Tosafot in Sotah. If so, it is difficult to remove that presumptive status. See the discussion of that sugya, where the Maharsha and Ritva write that it resembles the presumption of the original owner. Therefore the holder does not lose through inability to swear. A ketubah, however, may never come to collection at all, so perhaps it is not regarded as collected and she therefore loses. This requires study. This law depends upon the dispute in Shevuot concerning two parties who are both disqualified from swearing, whether the oath returns to the one obligated to take it or returns to Sinai. The basis of that dispute, as Tosafot and the Rosh explain, is that the rule that the opposing party swears and collects is rabbinic, and the Sages imposed an oath upon the claimant. Therefore, according to the view that the oath returns to Sinai, even regarding a rabbinic oath a claimant who cannot swear loses. According to the view that the oath returns to the one obligated to take it, and that one who cannot swear must pay, the claimant's oath, being rabbinic, does not generate a rule that inability to swear causes loss. Since we rule like Rabbi Abba that the oath returns to the person obligated to take it, the law is decided that inability to take a rabbinic oath does not cause the claimant to lose. This, then, is the intent of Tosafot and the Rosh in the case of "if he is clever." The woman is disqualified from swearing, while the husband is not, so the term "clever" is entirely appropriate. They write that the practical difference is according to Rabbi Abba, whose ruling is halachah, that the oath returns to the person obligated to take it and, where he cannot swear, he pays. That rule applies to a Torah oath. With a rabbinic oath, however, even if she is disqualified, she does not lose. This means that according to Rabbi Abba this is the law, whereas one who disagrees with him could hold that even with a rabbinic oath she loses. Tosafot therefore precisely refer to Rabbi Abba, whose ruling is halachah. Consequently, when only a rabbinic oath is imposed on her, she collects without taking the oath. If the husband cleverly brings her to a Torah oath, the opposing party swears and collects from her, since the husband himself is not disqualified. Everything is consistent. The words of Rabbi Abba are specifically the proof. From the statement at the beginning of Shevuat HaDayanim that in the case of a disqualified person we do not establish one rabbinic enactment upon another, there would be no decisive proof, because it could be explained as the Rif did in his responsum, as referring to hesset. Therefore, the Rosh's proof is precisely from Rabbi Abba, who concerns an oath of the Mishnah. The Rosh can likewise be understood this way. When he adds, "In this matter the Rif wrote in a responsum," etc., he does not mean to support his own words from the Rif. Had that been his intent he should have said, "The Rif likewise wrote." Rather, he means to disagree with the Rif: in this matter the Rif ruled that even with a rabbinic oath she loses, but this is incorrect, because Rabbi Abba's position shows that she does not lose through inability to take a rabbinic oath. This is what initially appeared to me to be the intent of Tosafot and the Rosh, and it would agree with the Tur's ruling here. But what can we do with the fact that the Tur, in his abridged rulings there, expressly rules as halachah that if the holder of the deed is disqualified, the defendant swears and is exempt, and if both are disqualified, the holder of the deed collects? This agrees with the Maharsha and the Bach. My interpretation of the Rosh cannot stand against their understanding. Regarding Tosafot, however, it remains possible to explain as I wrote. Tosafot in Shevuot 40, s.v. "because," explicitly write that according to Rabbi Abba, with a rabbinic oath the claimant does not lose. But in the Rosh the explanation must be as the Maharsha and Bach say. As for the difficulties from "if he is clever" and from the principle that we do not create one enactment upon another, I will, with Hashem's help, explain these matters properly in siman 84 and siman 87. Nevertheless, according to the Shach's approach it remains difficult how the Tur ruled here against the conclusion of his father, the Rosh, who agrees with the Rif. According to the Taz's resolution, however, this point is answered. The Rif and Rosh spoke only of one who admits partial payment and similar cases, not of the oath arising from a debtor's demand, "Swear to me." Therefore, in the case of partial payment the Tur indeed rules in Even HaEzer in accordance with them. Yet this creates the opposite difficulty: how can the Tur and Mechaber rule there in Even HaEzer that if both husband and wife are disqualified from swearing, there is neither oath nor payment? That is contrary to Tosafot and the Rosh, who explain that when both are disqualified in a rabbinic matter, the husband pays, and that this is precisely the cleverness in bringing her to a Torah oath, which causes her to lose. At first glance, this requires study. We must therefore combine the two answers. The reason the Tur rules here unlike the Rosh is that concerning the particular oath arising from the plea "Swear to me," everyone agrees that the Sages do not institute one enactment upon another. There is a strong reason for this. Otherwise, every deed held by a person disqualified from swearing would effectively be destroyed. In every case the borrower could say, "Swear to me," then the borrower himself would swear and be exempt, turning every deed of such a creditor into an oral loan. The scribe's pen and the witnesses who wrote the deed would have labored for nothing. By contrast, where the creditor himself admits partial payment, or in similar circumstances, he caused the problem himself by impairing his deed. This does not affect all deeds generally. See below in siman 92, where I discuss this further. As for the question why Even HaEzer rules that where both are disqualified the woman loses, the answer is the Shach's: a ketubah is rabbinic, and the Sages who established it also fixed its conditions. Accordingly, the rabbinic oath receives the effect of Torah law in this context, and inability to swear causes loss. The Tosafot case involving a ketubah must concern the additional ketubah obligation, as explained above. Everything is thus resolved without difficulty. It appears possible to bring proof for this distinction between a rabbinic ketubah and a Torah debt evidenced by deed. In Shevuot 48, the Gemara challenges Rav and Shmuel, who hold that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children, from several mishnayot in Ketubot implying that when a woman dies after her husband, her heirs nevertheless collect the ketubah. The Gemara resorts to strained resolutions. Why is there a difficulty? The ruling of Rav and Shmuel depends on whether we say that one who cannot swear must pay. Tosafot already asked on Shevuot 47, s.v. "because," from the Mishnah in Bava Metzia where one says, "the borrowed animal died," and the other says, "I do not know," and payment is required, proving that one who cannot swear must pay. Tosafot answer that Tannaim disagree whether we apply that principle. Rav and Shmuel establish the Mishnah in Bava Metzia according to the Tanna who applies it, while they themselves rule like the other Tanna, who does not. If so, there should likewise be no difficulty from the ketubah mishnayot. They could be established according to the view that one who cannot swear must pay, and according to that view it is obvious that the rule of Rav and Shmuel, that one cannot bequeath an oath to his children, does not apply. Rav and Shmuel themselves would rule like the opinion that does not impose payment when one cannot swear, and therefore maintain that the oath cannot be bequeathed. According to what was explained, however, the matter is clear. The connection between Rav and Shmuel's rule and the principle that one who cannot swear must pay is based on Tosafot's reasoning: the borrower's heirs cannot swear that their father paid, and the lender's heirs likewise cannot swear that their father did not receive payment. Logic therefore dictates that the borrower's heirs should lose, because with respect to them the rule of inability to swear causing payment applies. That is so with a Torah debt evidenced by deed. With a rabbinic ketubah, however, the Sages who established it also established that it may not be collected from orphans except by an oath. Therefore, according to everyone, if there is any room for Rav and Shmuel's reasoning, one could say that the ketubah heirs do not collect. It is reasonable according to all opinions that the heirs of the ketubah claimant should lose rather than the husband's heirs, because the ketubah was instituted from the outset on condition that an oath be taken. The anonymous mishnayot in Ketubot surely follow the view that ketubah is rabbinic, like the anonymous Mishnah at the end of the chapter Shnei Dayanei Gezeirot concerning one who married in Cappadocia; see the Rosh at the beginning of Ketubot. The Gemara therefore properly challenges Rav and Shmuel. It must be that their underlying reasoning is entirely rejected, namely that inability to take the oath does not make the heirs lose, and the Sages did not institute the oath for one collecting from orphans in a manner that would cause loss where the oath cannot be taken. This is contrary to Rav and Shmuel. Otherwise, with a rabbinic ketubah, everyone should agree that she loses. This is satisfactory. It is true that the earlier difficulty remains with the baraita from which the Gemara challenges Rav and Shmuel, which states, "the son's power is greater," etc., and which the Gemara was compelled to establish according to Beit Shammai. Why not establish it according to the Tanna who holds that one who cannot swear must pay? The baraita mentions nothing about a ketubah. At first glance this requires study. One must say that the Gemara infers from the structure of the baraita. It seeks examples where the son's power is greater than the father's and includes a rabbinic oath. Why did it not simply list the case where the father says, "I know about fifty, and about fifty I do not know," in which he must pay because he cannot swear, while a son making the analogous plea is exempt, since the verse is expounded as "the oath of Hashem shall be between them," but not between their heirs? Necessarily the baraita follows Shimon ben Tarfon, who uses that verse for another exposition and holds that even the father in such a case is exempt, because he does not accept the principle that one who cannot swear must pay. The Gemara therefore properly challenges Rav and Shmuel, and for this reason is compelled to establish the baraita according to Beit Shammai. This is satisfactory. There is, however, another point requiring precision. The Rosh in Kol HaNishba'in wrote that a disqualified person who seeks to collect by deed from orphans loses, and brought proof from the fact that Rav would not collect a ketubah for a widow because she was suspected of swearing falsely. This is difficult. A ketubah is different everywhere. Even regarding the oath of one who admits partial payment, she loses because the ketubah is rabbinic. What bearing does a rabbinic obligation have upon a Torah obligation? One might first ask independently how the Rosh can bring proof from Rav's refusal to collect the ketubah. Rav plainly holds that in every context inability to swear causes the claimant to lose, because Rav does not accept the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. Therefore he maintains throughout that the claimant loses, both regarding one who admits partial payment and all other oaths imposed on claimants, as Tosafot and the Rosh themselves write. Rav finds it more reasonable that the holder of the deed should lose than collect. For our halachah, however, we say concerning Rav and Shmuel, "apply their rule but do not extend it." Thus perhaps this case too is an impermissible extension, as the commentators wrote. One must say that the proof is not specifically from Rav but from the Mishnah itself, which states that they refrained from administering the oath. Why should suspicion of false swearing prevent the widow from collecting her ketubah? Rashi explains that they refrained both from administering the oath and from allowing collection of the ketubah. According to one who does not rule like Rav, how is this Mishnah understood? Necessarily, a disqualified claimant loses because his own conduct caused the problem, and even Rabbi Eliezer agrees. If so, according to the opinion that ketubah is d'oraisa, would that opinion disagree with the enactment of Rabban Gamliel the Elder? The Rosh could therefore properly prove that even where the underlying debt is from Torah law, a disqualified claimant whose own conduct caused his inability to swear loses. Moreover, according to Rabbi Eliezer there is no distinction in this respect between orphans and other people. How, then, could the Tur rule here that a disqualified holder of a deed collects? In truth, however, there is no proof. According to Rabbi Eliezer, the Mishnah can be established in accordance with Shmuel, who says that within beit din they did not administer the oath, but outside beit din they did. The same can be said according to the view that ketubah is from Torah law. The Rosh's proof from Rav therefore cannot rest on Rav alone, since Rav can certainly be following his own general position as explained above. Rather, the proof can be drawn from Rabbah bar Rav Huna, who came before Rav Nachman, where Rav Nachman said, "Come, let us uproot Rav and Shmuel's ruling," and was answered, "Apply it, but do not extend it." In any event, both initially and ultimately, Rav Nachman did not agree with Rav's general position that wherever a person cannot swear he loses, for he initially wished to uproot even Rav and Shmuel's rule concerning heirs. Nevertheless, in Gittin he likewise did not seek to collect a ketubah for the widow, following Rav. From him the author of the glosses and the Rosh could properly derive the rule. The Rosh abbreviated because he relied on the Gemara's sugya. If so, however, the earlier difficulty returns. Perhaps a ketubah is different. One might answer that regarding an individual disqualified claimant, it is reasonable to say that because the ketubah is rabbinic, the Sages instituted the ketubah and also required the oath. If she cannot swear, their enactment does not allow collection. But Rav's general refusal to collect any widow's ketubah is different. If all widow oaths were abolished because vows had become treated lightly, Rav would thereby uproot the Sages' ketubah enactment itself. The Sages instituted that a widow collect her ketubah by an oath or vow. If Rav nullifies both methods and makes collection entirely impossible, he nullifies their enactment completely. If inability to swear under Torah law itself causes loss, one can say that Rav held that the Sages did not strengthen their ketubah enactment beyond Torah law. But if under Torah law the claimant would not lose, why should the ketubah claimant lose here? The principle that the Sages who established the obligation also established its conditions cannot explain it, because here their own enactment was that she should collect her ketubah with an oath, not that no widow should ever collect. They certainly did not intend their ketubah enactment to become incapable of taking effect. How then can one say that they both created and restricted the obligation in this manner? This requires study. I will write more on this in siman 92.
And this is not difficult on the ground that, according to the above, a woman disqualified from swearing should not be permitted to remain married to her husband, because then divorce would be too easy in his eyes, since he could claim that he had paid her ketubah. Apart from the fact that he must take a hesset oath, and we do not suspect him of lying under oath, even before the institution of hesset he was not presumed willing to rob his wife and falsely deny her claim. This does not render divorce easy in his eyes. Especially before divorce, the debt is still within its fixed term. At the time of divorce she can also warn him not to pay her except before witnesses. The Rosh explicitly wrote similarly at the beginning of Bava Metzia, according to the initial assumption that in a place where no ketubah document is written, the husband may plead payment. See there. Nor is there a difficulty against the Taz's answer from the question in Shevuot 41 regarding the oath demanded by the debtor, "Swear to me that I have not paid you": what difference is there between this and one who admits partial payment on his deed? There would seem to be a major difference if the claimant is disqualified from swearing. With the former oath he collects his deed, whereas with partial payment the opposing party swears and is exempt. One answer is that according to the view rejecting Rabbi Abba, so that wherever one cannot swear one loses, perhaps in both cases the claimant cannot collect, and the Gemara sought a distinction valid according to every opinion. Furthermore, the distinction we made arises only from the final conclusion, where in the case of partial payment beit din itself raises the oath. This shows that the underlying claim is stronger, and therefore a disqualified claimant loses. In the case of "Swear to me," beit din does not raise the oath on its own, showing that the claim is weaker, and therefore the law does not require the claimant to lose. At the Gemara's initial stage, where the two oaths were treated as equivalent, this distinction could not possibly be given.
