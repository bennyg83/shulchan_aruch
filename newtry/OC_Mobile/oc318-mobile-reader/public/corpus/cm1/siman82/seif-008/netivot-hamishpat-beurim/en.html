The source. The source of this rule is Responsa Rosh, klal 66, siman 8. Tumim asks that if so, why does the oath concerning testimony not apply to relatives, since their testimony can have practical value regarding a migo, just as one is liable for an oath concerning testimony in the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba. See there. In my view this is no difficulty. Ran in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim writes that even if one accepted a relative as having the credibility of two witnesses, the relative is not liable for an oath concerning testimony, because by Torah law he is not a person qualified to testify. All the more so here, where the relative is not within the category of testimony at all and is not believed by virtue of testimony. His statement is treated as false absent the opposing party's admission. His presence merely prevents the claim from being considered a migo based on unwillingness to be brazen. This differs from the single witness in the case of Rabbi Abba's ingot. So long as the defendant does not swear against him, even if the litigant says that he does not know, the Torah gives the witness the force of two witnesses for this purpose, and he is therefore within the law of an oath concerning testimony. The other questions raised by Tumim there, however, are very difficult. Above in siman 29 it is stated that the ban does not apply to relatives because their testimony is of no benefit, whereas according to the Rosh it can benefit with respect to a migo. Likewise, in Bava Batra, chapter Chezkas HaBatim, the Gemara says, "If there are witnesses, let us see what the witnesses say." Perhaps it could be speaking of disqualified witnesses. It is also very strained to say that every place that says that witnesses defeat a migo intends even disqualified witnesses. It therefore appears that the Rosh's case concerns relatives who signed a document. Their disqualification is not because they are inherently not believed, but because Scripture decreed their disqualification, as explained in Bava Batra, chapter Get Pashut. Since they signed a document, the witnesses to the document certainly generate publicity. The Rosh therefore properly compared this in his responsum to a migo in the face of a public report. See there. This applies specifically where the claim is that the document was void from its inception, as in the case of the responsum where he claimed that the debt arose from gambling. But he can certainly claim, "I paid," since one is not concerned about leaving such a document outstanding. The reason the Rosh did not believe him by a migo that he could have claimed payment is that there was already one valid witness, in a case where the valid witness was not disqualified by combination with the invalid one. See Sema, siman 45 seif katan 34, and Taz, siman 36 seif katan 1. According to Rambam's view in siman 51, that one cannot claim payment against a document attested by one witness, this is clear. By contrast, against relatives who testified orally, we do say a migo. Likewise, against witnesses who became disqualified by a ruling of beit din, although every act of beit din generates publicity, if such witnesses signed a document we still say a migo, because persons who are not believed are not capable of creating legally significant publicity. Tumim brings proof from Eduyot, chapter 8, concerning the young woman who was held as security in Ashkelon. The Sages said, "If you believe them that she was held as security, believe them that she was not defiled." Raavad explains that this concerns disqualified witnesses. Tumim asks how disqualified witnesses can testify, and therefore concludes that she herself also said, "I was held as security and I remained pure," yet she is not believed by a migo in the face of disqualified witnesses. In my view that proof can be answered. There, she was certainly held as security in Ashkelon before many witnesses. Since the disqualified witnesses said that she had been held there before valid witnesses, there was a public report predating the marriage that would prohibit her, as explained in Even HaEzer. Since they also said she was not defiled, however, it is a report accompanied by its own refutation. Although in the ordinary case of captivity even a report predating marriage does not prohibit her, because the Sages were lenient regarding a captive woman, being held as security is more stringent than ordinary captivity, as explained in Ketubot.
