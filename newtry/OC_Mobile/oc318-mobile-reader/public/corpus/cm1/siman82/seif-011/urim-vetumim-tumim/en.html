You forgave , etc. One may ask that whatever the debtor himself could plead, we plead on behalf of his heirs, even a matter by which he himself could not impose an oath, as Tosafot write in Shevuot 41, s.v. "and what," etc. Accordingly, if he could plead, "You forgave me," we should likewise plead for the heirs that perhaps the creditor forgave the debtor. If so, what is accomplished by the established rule that within the term of a deed one may collect from heirs without an oath? Perhaps the creditor forgave the debtor, and regarding forgiveness there is no distinction between within the term and after the term. One must say that this is an entirely uncommon occurrence. If the debtor himself pleads it he is believed, but since it is exceedingly uncommon, according to all opinions we do not raise it on behalf of heirs. The rule in Even HaEzer that a woman swearing on her ketubah must swear that she did not forgive it is due to gilgul shevuah. Alternatively, one may say that regarding a wife and her husband, since her feelings are close to him, everyone agrees that forgiveness is a concern. This can resolve Tosafot's question why a widow must swear, since the ketubah debt is within its term. According to what I wrote, there remains a concern that she may have forgiven the ketubah, and she must therefore swear. We then include by gilgul shevuah that she was not paid and did not receive pledged funds. According to this answer, however, Tosafot's statement that where the husband died suddenly there is no concern for pledged funds and she needs no oath is rejected, for she must still swear that she did not forgive. This requires study. I discussed this at length in Even HaEzer, because regarding the basic ketubah perhaps one cannot say that she forgave it, since a man may not remain with his wife without a ketubah. If so, Tosafot's question remains where she has only the basic one hundred or two hundred zuz: why must she swear?
