The correct reading is that when the guarantor brings witnesses that he paid, the borrower may say to the guarantor, 'I repaid you'; with a mere written acknowledgment of receipt he cannot say 'I repaid you,' but without this he cannot collect. Shach concludes that a mere acknowledgment of receipt is ineffective and that the debt instrument must be transferred by writing and delivery. He interprets the Rosh, who cites Rabbeinu Yonah in Bava Batra 32 and speaks of an acknowledgment of receipt, to mean that the creditor gave the guarantor a written assignment together with delivery. This is very difficult, as Tumim already asks. If so, what function does the acknowledgment serve? Why does the Rosh discuss specifically a guarantor, when writing and delivery transfers a debt instrument even to any stranger? And why does the Rosh say that without the acknowledgment the borrower can argue that perhaps the instrument is merely deposited with the guarantor or was only handed to him, if according to Shach the principal reason is that a creditor would not leave such an instrument in another's hands? Tumim therefore concludes that the Rosh means that an acknowledgment of receipt alone, without a written assignment and delivery, suffices. In truth, the Rosh's intent in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah clearly accords with the other poskim that an acknowledgment alone suffices. Yet the Rosh still requires explanation. Why does he say that without an acknowledgment the borrower may claim that perhaps it is merely deposited with the guarantor or was only handed over to him? The Gemara itself speaks of a case in which the borrower is not believed to say that he repaid the guarantor. If there were no acknowledgment, he would certainly be believed, as ruled here in Shulchan Aruch. The answer is as follows. Sema, seif katan 15, writes that even when the creditor collects from the guarantor, the debt instrument remains effective against the borrower and his property so that the guarantor may recover from him. Sema further writes that ordinarily a person does not throw away his money for nothing, so the guarantor is treated as having purchased the debt instrument from the creditor. Certainly, if the guarantor pays expressly in order to extinguish the document, the document is cancelled. But ordinarily we assume that he did not throw away his money and paid on the understanding that the document should remain effective, effectively purchasing it from the creditor. Such a document can, in my view, pass from the creditor to the guarantor by delivery alone, without writing. The principal reason that sale of debt instruments, which is rabbinic, generally requires writing and delivery is that a creditor cannot sell the personal lien upon the debtor, allowing the debtor to say, 'You are not my litigant.' But when the guarantor pays, the debtor's body and property automatically become obligated to the guarantor. The creditor can therefore transfer the document to him by delivery alone. The poskim dispute only whether this is enough to preserve liens against property later sold to third parties. This applies specifically when the creditor collects from the guarantor as payment, or the guarantor actually pays the creditor in discharge of the debt, for then the debtor's personal obligation to the guarantor arises and the document may be delivered to him. But where the creditor cannot yet demand payment from the guarantor, such as before the due date, or the guarantor refuses to pay before the borrower is first sued, and the creditor says, 'I do not want to collect from you as guarantor. I want only to sell you this debt of one hundred for fifty as one sells it to another, so that I will not have to sue the borrower first,' the guarantor is like any other purchaser and requires writing and delivery. Before he pays as guarantor, he has no personal lien upon the borrower. Accordingly, Rashba's statement cited by Shach, that the creditor must write an acknowledgment of receipt and give the guarantor his rights, means that the payment should not be made to cancel the instrument, but that the creditor should transfer the instrument to the guarantor by way of acquisition. According to Sema, we ordinarily presume this intent because a person does not throw away his money. Once the instrument is transferred at the time of payment or afterward, Sema holds that delivery alone suffices. Therefore, even witnesses who merely testify that the guarantor paid generally establish the same result, because the money is presumed to have been paid with intent to acquire the instrument. This explains Sema, seif katan 14, that such testimony is equivalent to an acknowledgment of receipt, unless the witnesses state expressly that the payment was made to extinguish the instrument and that the creditor ordered it torn up. In that event the instrument cannot later be sold to the guarantor. The Rosh can therefore be read precisely. He speaks of an acknowledgment of receipt witnessed and accompanied by transfer of the creditor's rights, meaning that the instrument was acquired by the guarantor. Without such an acknowledgment, even if the creditor admits that the guarantor paid and that he himself has no further claim against the borrower, the borrower may still say: perhaps you did not pay as guarantor at all, but rather purchased the debt instrument from the creditor for less than its face value before the due date, in which case delivery without writing would not transfer the instrument. The borrower is then believed to say that he repaid the guarantor. This also explains why the Rosh had to give a reason for the borrower's claim of repayment. True payment by a guarantor would ordinarily transfer the document to him, so the borrower could not simply say 'I repaid you.' The Rosh therefore says that the borrower can argue that perhaps the document was transferred for reasons other than payment. It further appears that a debt instrument passes to a guarantor by delivery alone, when he pays without the borrower's knowledge, only according to those who hold that if the borrower merely said 'Be my guarantor,' the guarantor who later pays is entitled to reimbursement even without the borrower's further instruction. On that view, once the guarantor pays, the borrower's personal obligation to him arises automatically. But according to the opinion that even after saying 'Be my guarantor' the borrower is exempt unless he said 'Be my guarantor and pay,' a payment made without the borrower's knowledge does not make a mere acknowledgment of receipt sufficient. The guarantor must acquire the document through writing and delivery. This is the position of those who require writing and delivery specifically. The Rosh in Bava Batra 32 says that an acknowledgment of receipt suffices because he there deals with a kablan. There is therefore no real dispute among the poskim. Shach's citation in the name of Ramban that an acknowledgment is ineffective is also difficult, because Ramban there indicates the opposite. Ramban further writes that if the guarantor notified the borrower or the heirs that he was paying on their behalf, he certainly has the rights of the written instrument.
