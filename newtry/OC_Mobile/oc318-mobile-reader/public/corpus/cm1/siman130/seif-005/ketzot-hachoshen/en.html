The borrower must reimburse the guarantor because the guarantor was negligent if he paid on a written debt without taking the document. The Shach notes that Baal HaTerumot rules similarly even where the debtor reminded the payer that a document existed, unless he expressly instructed him to take it. A guarantor is different because he intends to return and collect from the borrower, so he should protect himself by obtaining the instrument. Where the original loan was oral, however, a guarantor who pays without witnesses is not thereby negligent. There is no debt instrument he should have obtained, and ordinary oral debts are not commonly paid before witnesses. Ran derives this from the Mishnah of the shopkeeper's ledger. The employer tells the shopkeeper to pay the workers. The shopkeeper pays without witnesses, and when the workers deny receipt both the shopkeeper and workers swear and collect from the employer. We do not say the shopkeeper was negligent for paying without witnesses. Shitah Mekubetzet states explicitly that the rule requiring proof of payment where a document exists does not extend to an oral debt. Accordingly, if a guarantor says he already paid the creditor and the creditor says he has not been paid, while the borrower had authorized the guarantor to pay, the case resembles the shopkeeper's ledger. The creditor and guarantor can each take the appropriate oath and collect, and the guarantor is not disqualified merely because he paid without witnesses. Under Baal HaMaor's theory, the shopkeeper's right is strict law because we presume an agent carries out his mission. That presumption is less suitable for a guarantor. Even when the borrower says, "Become my guarantor," the guarantor is not necessarily a formal agent commanded to pay. The borrower mainly gave him permission to discharge the debt. Therefore the presumption that an agent certainly performed his agency may not apply. But even according to the authorities who ground the shopkeeper rule in strict law, they generally agree that where that strict presumption is missing, the rabbinic reimbursement enactment can still apply to one who incurred expenses with authorization. Thus a guarantor who paid with the borrower's permission can still swear and recover. If the guarantor himself is suspected and cannot swear, however, he does not collect merely through the presumption, because here the special agency presumption is weak or absent. Therefore the suspected guarantor is not treated exactly like the suspected shopkeeper under opinions that allow the latter to collect.
