When he wrote to him... As Rashbam explains there, s.v. "for this reason." Rashbam does not read in the Gemara the phrase concerning the borrower admitting the debt, and most commentators likewise omit it.
There is an authority who says... See Tosafos there, who challenges Rashbam because even a lender holding a document cannot collect from minor heirs. Raavad therefore explains the case as concerning adult heirs. They object that the phrase "orphans are not subject to..." does not fit adults. He follows Ramban's explanation that the case indeed concerns minors and the dispute is whether liability arises when they become adults. Rav Papa holds that payment of a creditor is a mitzvah; since the minors are currently exempt and the guarantor voluntarily preceded them and fulfilled the mitzvah, they remain exempt even after attaining majority. Rav Huna holds that because the guarantor paid, it becomes an oral loan, and they likewise remain exempt even after majority. The practical difference is where the borrower admits the debt. The challenge from the baraisa, "A guarantor... may collect," means after the heirs become adults. According to Rav Huna, since the creditor wrote "I have received payment," the borrower or heirs cannot claim that the borrower had already paid, because we do not advance for heirs a claim their father could not have made. The guarantor clearly paid intending to collect from the heirs, not merely to discharge their father's debt.
The same applies if... See Tosafos there. Their suggested explanation requires difficulty to say that even minor heirs are proceeded against in such a case, and the sugya in Arachin could then have been established in that situation. According to Raavad and Ramban, however, it is straightforward because the collection occurs after majority. Thus it says, "from adult heirs..." and also "from purchasers," as Tosafos cites in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel, for he reads that it is like a document and explains that the borrower obligated himself to the guarantor. But in the first case, where the creditor merely wrote that he had received payment, it is certainly not equivalent to a document, as Tosafos itself asks. Ramban, however, cites an explicit Tosefta saying that the creditor wrote "I have received," and explains it as above. Tosafos instead explains the Tosefta as meaning that the creditor instructed them to write it.
