When the creditor writes to him, "I have received the amount of the debt," etc. Rama writes in Darkei Moshe: See siman 116 whether the creditor may write such a receipt after the debt has already been paid and the lien of the document has been extinguished. This implies that Rama compares the present case to that one, so that if the creditor did not write the receipt before the guarantor paid, writing it afterward would no longer help. It would also follow that the creditor cannot be compelled to write such a receipt for the guarantor in order to place him in the creditor's position. In my view the cases should be distinguished. There the purchaser knowingly bought without warranty, despite knowing that the creditor had a lien upon the field he purchased from the borrower. Therefore the Tur rules that if the creditor refuses to transfer his rights, beit din cannot compel him. A guarantor is different. From the outset he obligated himself to the creditor on behalf of the borrower on the understanding that if he ultimately had to pay, he could return against the borrower. The borrower can defend against the guarantor by claiming payment or similar defenses, and in such a situation beit din should compel the creditor to protect the guarantor by writing him a receipt that places him in the creditor's position. For the same reason, even if the guarantor already paid without first stipulating that the creditor write such a receipt, the creditor can still write it afterward and it should be effective, because the guarantor plainly paid on the understanding that he would have recourse against the borrower. It is as though he had expressly stipulated this. No authority, moreover, states that the receipt must be written before payment. The very language "I have received" naturally refers to something already received. Otherwise the enactment should have required the creditor to write, "I shall collect from him." It further appears that even according to Rabbeinu Yonah, who requires that the guarantor receive a transfer of the promissory note in order to collect from purchasers, that transfer should be effective even after the guarantor has paid. We assume he paid on that understanding, because a person does not throw away his money for nothing. This resembles an unrelated purchaser of a promissory note who pays the price first and later receives the formal writing transferring the note and all its liens. Since the payment was plainly made for that purpose, the later transfer is effective. A guarantor is the same. This differs from a purchaser who expressly bought land without warranty, where one cannot imply a condition contrary to the express terms. Furthermore, only in the case of a purchaser is it appropriate to say that the lien of the note was extinguished, because the note was written against the borrower and his property, and once the creditor collected from the borrower's very property in the purchaser's hands, that lien was satisfied. Here, however, the note remains directed against the borrower and his property. When the creditor collects from the guarantor, the note remains in force against the borrower and his assets so that the guarantor can return against him or against purchasers from him. Similar distinctions appear in siman 77 where the creditor forgives one of several borrowers or guarantors. Examine this carefully.
