When he wrote to him, I have received, etc. The Sema in seif katan 15 challenges the Darkei Moshe, which holds that the notation I have received is effective only if written at the time the guarantor pays, not afterward. The Sema maintains that it is effective even if written later. In my view this depends on the dispute between Rashbam and Rabbenu Yonah. According to Rashbam, the words I have received convert the guarantor's claim into one having the force of a documented loan; without them it is only an oral loan. If so, the notation plainly must be written immediately at the time of payment. The moment the guarantor pays, his claim against the borrower arises as an oral debt and the lien of a document is absent. How can the creditor later convert that already existing oral claim into a documented claim and revive a lien that never arose? The Sema's point that the borrower is paying only a guarantor does not solve this, for the legal lien nevertheless lapsed at the moment of payment. Everyone agrees that absent the notation, the guarantor has only an oral claim. According to Rabbenu Yonah, however, the notation serves only to prevent the borrower from claiming payment. The reason must be that since the borrower sees that the creditor took the trouble to write I have received, he understands that the guarantor is not relying upon him casually and would not leave such a document in the guarantor's hands after reimbursement. On that view the Sema is correct. What difference does it make if the creditor wrote it somewhat later? Ultimately, once such a document is in the guarantor's hands, the borrower would not leave it there after paying him. All the more so according to those who hold generally that one cannot plead payment against a document whose original lien has been extinguished. Even those who disagree there can agree here, for by the time the borrower pays, the notation I have received already exists in the guarantor's hands. According to Rabbenu Yonah, the notation creates no lien at all; it merely weakens the borrower's plea of payment. In my opinion, even the Rama agrees to this. The Darkei Moshe's statement that the notation must be written at the time of payment should be understood only according to Rashbam's approach. This is clear.
From purchasers who bought from the borrower. In my view this ruling follows Rashbam's opinion that documentary rights are acquired by delivery alone, as is well known from the decisors. When the creditor writes I have received, he deliberately indicates that the guarantor's payment was not intended to extinguish the borrower's underlying obligation, but rather that the guarantor is to pursue the borrower. The document has therefore been delivered to him as an acquisition, and according to Rashbam documentary rights are acquired by delivery. Indeed, according to Rashbam one must otherwise ask why the words I have received are needed at all. Since the document is in the guarantor's hands and we rule like Rava that one need not prove how it came into his possession, simple delivery should suffice. The answer is that absent I have received, the guarantor himself admits that the creditor handed him the document merely as part of repayment, and the original lien would therefore be extinguished. By writing I have received, however, the creditor shows that the lien is not being extinguished but is intended to remain available to the guarantor, for he took the trouble to write such language so the guarantor may pursue the borrower. Delivery therefore acquires the documentary right. But according to our law that acquiring a document requires both writing and delivery, how could one imagine that the guarantor may collect from purchasers merely because the creditor wrote I have received? What proprietary right did the guarantor receive through those words? The creditor did not write, Acquire this document and all its rights, as the law of document transfer requires. What difference is there between a guarantor and any other person to whom the creditor writes I have received? Would such a person thereby gain power to collect from encumbered property? Why should the guarantor have greater rights? The borrower's assets were never independently encumbered to the guarantor, and with respect to purchasers he is like any stranger. It is therefore clear that Rashbam follows his own view and that this ruling is not practical law according to our position. Examine this carefully, for in my view it is correct.
Who is not believed to say, I paid. Gidulei Terumah asks against the Tur and Rosh, who require an admission that the person was guarantor, for otherwise how do we know he was guarantor? As the Shulchan Aruch writes in seif 3. If so, why is the borrower not believed to say, I paid you, through a migo that he could have denied that the claimant ever became guarantor, similar to the rule that one who admits writing a document may still require its authentication? In the Rosh and Tur one can answer because they hold that one who pays another person's debt without his knowledge is entitled to reimbursement. According to them, why is an admission that he was guarantor needed at all? It must be as the Shach writes in seif katan 3: if he was not guarantor, then even the borrower is believed to say, I paid you, according to everyone, because the creditor's notation I have received has no evidentiary force unless the claimant stood in the position of guarantor. On that understanding there is no valid migo. A migo applies where the alternative plea would itself exempt the litigant without requiring his present plea. For example, if by saying, You were never my guarantor, he would be entirely exempt without also needing to say, I paid you, then his present claim of payment could gain force from that alternative. Here, however, even if he says, You were never my guarantor, that alone does not help, because under the Rosh and Tur one who paid another's debt without his knowledge is still entitled to reimbursement. He would still have to add the separate plea, I reimbursed you. Only by telling two lies, namely that there was no guaranty and that he later paid, could he succeed, while now he admits the guaranty. That is not a migo. We do not say that because a litigant could have told two falsehoods, including one more brazenly in his opponent's face, therefore his present single plea is believed. Such a migo is nowhere recognized. The question therefore disappears for the Rosh and Tur. For the Shulchan Aruch, however, which rules above that reimbursement requires the borrower's admission because one who pays another's debt without his knowledge is not reimbursed, Gidulei Terumah's question remains. There the borrower has a true migo: he could simply deny the guaranty and be exempt. It is strained to answer that because liability for unauthorized payment is itself a dispute among the authorities we do not recognize a migo resting on one side of that dispute, though some authors say so. It is more satisfactory to say that the Mechaber's earlier statement in seif 3 that an admission suffices concerns the ordinary case where the borrower merely claims uncertainty whether the guarantor paid. In the specific law of Rabbenu Yonah, where the borrower affirmatively claims, I paid you, the creditor's notation I have received alone does not defeat him unless there are witnesses to the guaranty, because otherwise he has the migo identified by Gidulei Terumah. Alternatively, the Mechaber's reference to an admission means that the borrower had already admitted the guaranty before later asserting payment. In that event the migo is already lost. Gidulei Terumah writes similarly.
The same applies if the borrower wrote to the guarantor, etc. Tosafot on Bava Batra 174, s.v. Ve'im katav lo, implies that when the borrower writes such language to the guarantor within the document, the guarantor is treated as a kablan and the creditor may demand payment from him first. See Tosafot there. Tosafot are compelled to explain this way in order to resolve the wording of the Tosefta that states, And if the creditor wrote, I have received. But according to Ramban's interpretation, and according to what I wrote above in explaining Rabbenu Yonah, the baraita's phrase wrote to him, I have received does not mean that the borrower writes such wording to the guarantor. The rule that such writing makes the guarantor's claim comparable to a documented loan is independently reasonable even without proof. But the further rule that it makes him equivalent to a kablan as against the creditor requires study.
