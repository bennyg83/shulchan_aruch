And if the creditor wrote to the guarantor, I have received, etc. The Sema in seif katan 13 explains that even if the borrower claims, I paid the guarantor, this applies, and likewise where the creditor wrote to the guarantor, I have received. The Shach in seif katan 8 discusses this at length and understands that most decisors disagree with the Tur. My understanding is otherwise, and I will clarify the matter. The Shach explains the Rosh's citation of Rabbenu Yonah, who says the case is one where the creditor wrote to the guarantor, I have received, as meaning that the creditor also transferred the loan document to him by writing and delivery. This cannot be correct. First, if there was a valid written assignment and delivery, what role is left for the words I have received? The assignment alone transfers the document, and the guarantor then stands exactly like the original creditor. The Gemara should not describe him as a guarantor who paid by virtue of guaranty at all, but as a purchaser of the document. Second, the Rosh's language indicates that Rabbenu Yonah does not dispute what the Rosh wrote immediately beforehand, that the case involves witnesses to the guaranty or the borrower's admission that he was guarantor. Rabbenu Yonah merely adds that the creditor must also write, I have received. If Rabbenu Yonah means a complete assignment by writing and delivery, there is no need to establish that he was originally guarantor. Remove the guaranty entirely; he is simply purchaser of the document and proceeds under his acquired document. The Rosh should then have said that according to Rabbenu Yonah none of the previous conditions are necessary. Third, if I have received means a full written assignment and delivery, while the mere words I have received do not prevent the borrower from saying, I paid you, as the Shach holds, because the borrower does not care to leave the original document in the guarantor's hands even after repayment since it is not written in the guarantor's name, then the reason given by Rabbenu Yonah is inexplicable. He says that without I have received the borrower can claim, Perhaps you never paid the creditor at all and the document is only deposited with you, or perhaps he merely delivered it to you and a document is not acquired by delivery alone. That reason would not explain why the words I have received without formal assignment fail. Once the creditor wrote I have received, it is evident that the document was not left as a deposit or transferred by bare delivery, but was handed over because the guarantor paid and the creditor gave him the document as proof. Why should formal assignment then be needed? If Rabbenu Yonah's real concern were that the borrower could still claim payment because he does not care to leave a document not written in the guarantor's name, that is the reason he should have stated. Necessarily, the Tur's understanding is correct. Without the words I have received, the borrower may say, I paid you, because the document is not written in the guarantor's name and the borrower is not particular to retrieve it from him even after payment. But where the creditor took the trouble to write I have received, the borrower likewise would not leave such a document in the guarantor's possession after repaying him. The guarantor can therefore answer, What is your document doing in my hand? The words of Rabbenu Yonah are simple and compelling. Rabbenu Yonah derives this from the Gemara's wording, Give me the money, and here is the document, which implies that the guarantor sought recovery through the document. Yet the document itself gives him no collection right, for perhaps it is merely deposited with him. Why then does the Gemara say, Here is the document? Necessarily, the creditor had written I have received upon the document, and this is what the phrase Here is the document refers to. From this Rabbenu Yonah infers that the case involves I have received, and consequently the borrower cannot plead that he paid the guarantor, as the Tur explains the Gemara. This is exactly the position of the Nimukei Yosef. He explains that without I have received the guarantor cannot collect even if the original loan document is in his possession, because the document creates a lien only for the creditor, not for the guarantor, and therefore it is no proof to say, Your document is in my hand, since perhaps it is merely deposited with him. If, as the Shach reads the Nimukei Yosef, formal written assignment and delivery were required and the words I have received alone did not suffice, the Nimukei Yosef's reason would make no sense. Once the creditor wrote I have received, how can the document be merely a deposit? The Nimukei Yosef should instead have given the other reason discussed above. Necessarily, he too agrees with Rabbenu Yonah that the words I have received suffice. When the Nimukei Yosef begins by saying that the creditor wrote I have received and gave him his right, this does not mean, as the Shach suggests, that he formally assigned the document by writing and delivery. Rather, by writing I have received and delivering the loan document, he gave the guarantor his practical standing, so that the guarantor stands in his place, as Rashbam and Rabbenu Chananel explain in Tosafot below on Bava Batra 174. This is clear and compelling. Baal HaTerumot, gate 35, part 1, law 45, likewise clearly agrees with the Tur. He writes that the guarantor must bring proof that he paid, and if he brings no proof, or if the borrower claims, I paid you, the borrower is believed. The mere fact that the loan document is in the guarantor's hands does not make his claim a documented loan, as the baraita teaches: if the creditor wrote to him I have received, he may collect, but otherwise not. He explains the case in Chezkas HaBatim, where the guarantor says, Give me the money and here is the document, as referring to the document bearing the creditor's notation I have received. This clearly means the words I have received alone, not a formal assignment. If Baal HaTerumot required writing and delivery, there would have been no reason to establish the case in Chezkas HaBatim as one where I have received was written; it would be enough to say that the guarantor produced a document transferred by valid assignment. Moreover, Baal HaTerumot proves the rule from the baraita saying that if the creditor wrote I have received the guarantor may collect. There, according to Ramban and Baal HaTerumot, the case concerns adult heirs, and everyone agrees that the words I have received suffice without formal assignment, as Ramban writes in his novellae and Gidulei Terumah states explicitly. If our case required formal assignment, how could proof be brought from that baraita? Indeed, what proof would be needed at all? Baal HaTerumot concludes that nevertheless a guarantor or kablan cannot collect from property sold by the borrower until the creditor delivers the loan document and transfers it by the legal mode of acquiring documents. This itself proves that the earlier discussion, where I have received prevents a plea of payment, does not mean formal written assignment and delivery. Otherwise, what novelty would remain in his concluding requirement for collection from purchasers? If even against the borrower himself formal assignment were needed merely to defeat a plea of payment, this would already have been stated. Every reader can see that the earlier I have received is not a formal assignment. In part 2, law 3, Baal HaTerumot writes that for collection from adult heirs and purchasers it helps if the creditor wrote I have received and gave him the document by writing and delivery. One might infer that even against adult heirs formal assignment is required. Gidulei Terumah already explains that the statement is divided: against adult heirs the words I have received suffice, while against purchasers formal assignment is required. The wording itself supports this, for he mentions both I have received and writing and delivery. If writing and delivery alone governed both, the separate phrase I have received would be unnecessary. Thus, the Rosh, Rabbenu Yonah, Nimukei Yosef, Baal HaTerumot, Tur, and Rivash all support the Tur's rule. Although Rashba and Rabbenu Yerucham disagree, we follow the former group, and there is no basis for the Shach's surprise or lengthy objection. Still, one question remains: from where did Rabbenu Yonah derive that once I have received is written the borrower cannot claim, I paid you? It is difficult to say he stated this purely from his own reasoning. The inference must be from the Gemara's phrase, Here is the document. Yet one could say that the guarantor himself had a written guaranty document, or that the creditor's notation I have received was written into the guaranty document, as Tosafot later explains. Nor can the proof be from the baraita that if the creditor wrote I have received the guarantor may collect. According to the Tur, a guarantor cannot collect from minor heirs, though he may collect from adults. The Tur understands the entire sugya at the end of Get Pashut concerning this guarantor as dealing with minor heirs. Regarding minor heirs he rules that the guarantor may collect only through one of three specified methods, and he does not state that I have received alone suffices, because why should it? Even a full loan document cannot generally be collected from minor heirs. It must therefore be that the Tur had the Gemara's text reflected by the Nimukei Yosef, Rashbam, and Rabbenu Chananel, where the Gemara challenges from the baraita that I have received is effective. According to Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua, where the debtor admitted the debt, that is understandable. Tosafot's answer is then needed that the words I have received are necessary so the borrower cannot say, Perhaps you never paid the creditor. Yet that does not itself prove that I have received prevents a claim, I paid you, since according to our ruling like Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua the debtor's admission is the main point and I have received only establishes that the guarantor actually paid the creditor. The Gemara's principal difficulty is that even a document cannot ordinarily be collected from minor heirs, so how does I have received help? Tosafot therefore explain the baraita differently, that the borrower wrote the notation to the guarantor, which has no connection to a creditor writing I have received to a guarantor. It seems to me that Rabbenu Yonah does not follow Tosafot's interpretation. Rather, he holds that at the stage of the Gemara's question it did not yet assume that I have received prevents the borrower from claiming payment. The question is then strong because the baraita necessarily concerns collection from heirs. It cannot merely concern collection from the borrower himself, as Ramban writes in his novellae. If the borrower says, I paid you, what does I have received prove? And if he does not deny the debt and admits he never reimbursed the guarantor, then it is obvious that he must pay, since the creditor admits the guarantor paid and the borrower admits he did not reimburse him. Thus the baraita must concern heirs. If the debtor admitted the debt, the baraita is meaningful because it rejects Rav Pappa's view that minor heirs are not subject to the mitzvah of paying their father's debt. That is the Gemara's question: this works according to Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua, but according to Rav Pappa it cannot be placed with heirs because they would be exempt, while if it concerns the borrower himself it is obvious. The Gemara answers that it does concern the borrower himself where the debtor does not admit the obligation. One might then ask why he cannot plead payment. The answer is that this is exactly why the creditor troubled himself to write I have received: it gives the guarantor the force of a documented claim for this purpose and prevents the plea, I paid you. This is Rabbenu Yonah's rule, and the sugya is then coherent. It also resolves Tosafot's question. Rabbenu Chananel, whom Tosafot cite as reading the Gemara's question as a case where the debtor admitted and its answer as one where I have received strengthens the claim like a document, may likewise be understood this way. There is no need to say, as Rashbam does, that the dispute applies only to an oral loan, a view that appears to contradict the clear implication in Arakhin noted by Tosafot. Rather, Rabbenu Chananel can be read in accordance with Rabbenu Yonah, and his words are clear. According to Rashbam, one would have to explain that the implication in Arakhin that Rav Huna and Rav Pappa disagree even with a document belongs only to the Gemara's initial assumption, before the rule concerning heirs who consumed property that was not theirs is introduced. After that rule is known, Rav Pappa's statement can be limited to an oral loan, while the Bava Batra passage follows the conclusion. This would also answer Tosafot's question why the case specifically involves minors and a mitzvah rather than a simple plea of payment: Tosafot's question must be directed to the Arakhin passage, because in the incident of the guarantor there is a document and the guarantor paid after the borrower's death while the heirs were minors. The baraita itself does not state when the guarantor paid and indeed concerns a case where the debt was admitted. Thus Tosafot ask from Arakhin, where according to Rashbam the case is an oral loan: why invoke the mitzvah rather than the plea that the debt was paid? According to this, Maharshal's question why one cannot answer that the debtor admitted is likewise resolved, because if he admitted, there would also be no concern for money set aside for payment. Still, all this is strained. It is preferable to explain Rabbenu Chananel according to Rabbenu Yonah, and everything fits without difficulty. Since Rabbenu Chananel also supports Rabbenu Yonah, and his words carry great authority, the law should unquestionably follow them. Examine this carefully. From all this, however, one point emerges clearly. It is specifically where the creditor wrote I have received that the borrower cannot plead payment, because the creditor's deliberate written notation gives the guarantor's possession of the document special force. According to Rashbam, it even creates the force of a documented loan, as explained above. But where there are merely witnesses that the guarantor paid the creditor, then if the borrower admits he has not yet reimbursed the guarantor he plainly must pay. If he claims that he already reimbursed the guarantor, however, why should the guarantor have the status of a creditor holding a document? Nothing was written to him. I have not seen any authority say this. All of them specifically mention the creditor's written I have received, not merely witnesses to payment. Baal HaTerumot is explicit: the guarantor must bring proof that he paid, but if he brings no proof, or if the borrower says, I paid you, the borrower is believed, until the creditor writes I have received. This means that even when the guarantor has proof of his payment, if the borrower claims that he later reimbursed the guarantor, the borrower is believed unless I have received was written. If witnesses alone were enough, the baraita should have taught that witnesses suffice, and I have received would follow all the more so, since a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. There is no proof from Tur, siman 132, that where the guarantor paid before witnesses the borrower cannot thereafter plead payment. There the issue is only whether the guarantor may demand reimbursement at all. Certainly he may. The borrower is obligated to pay him, but that does not mean the borrower is not believed to say that he has already done so. We find that special consequence only where the creditor wrote I have received. By what Torah principle should mere witnesses to the guarantor's earlier payment convert his later claim against the borrower into a documented loan? This is clear.
