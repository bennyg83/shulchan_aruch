Even if the debt instrument is in his possession. The Sema explains that this is the correct understanding of the Tur, Mechaber, and Baal HaTerumot, unlike the Levush, who says that even a written acknowledgment of receipt does not help. It is true that the Tur and Rivash, siman 178, imply the Sema’s view, but nothing is proved from the Baal HaTerumot. On the contrary, his words imply the opposite, as I shall explain. Nor is anything proved from the Rambam or Mechaber. The Sema also cites the Maggid Mishneh as explicit support, but in my humble opinion the Maggid Mishneh implies the opposite: only where the lender wrote an acknowledgment of receipt and transferred his right, meaning that he assigned the debt instrument through writing and delivery in the manner explained above in siman 66, may the borrower no longer plead that he paid the guarantor. Otherwise, the borrower may say, ‘I paid you.’ This is why the Maggid Mishneh concludes that the guarantor’s position cannot be weaker than that of an unrelated purchaser. A mere acknowledgment of receipt would certainly not suffice for an unrelated purchaser without a full assignment of the instrument. The Bach correctly explains the Ran cited by the Beit Yosef in the same way, namely that the lender wrote an acknowledgment and transferred his right by writing and delivery. Nimukei Yosef in Chezkat HaBatim implies the same. The Bach also cites the novellae of the Rashba to Get Pashut as holding that a mere acknowledgment does not prevent the borrower from pleading payment; the work meant is in fact the Ramban’s novellae. Maharshal, Responsa siman 93, likewise rules that even if it is known that the guarantor paid, the borrower may say, ‘I paid you.’ The Bach later objects that an acknowledgment plainly permits recovery, but this contradicts the authorities he himself cites here. Get Pashut supplies no proof, for it may concern a case in which the debt instrument was assigned by writing and delivery, as explained by the Baal HaTerumot and other authorities. Alternatively, the ‘acknowledgment’ there may mean that the borrower wrote to the guarantor, ‘I regard myself as though I received the money from you,’ as explained by Baal HaMaor, Tosafot, Raavan, and the Rashba. Rabbenu Yerucham states expressly that even if the lender wrote an acknowledgment, the borrower may plead payment unless the debt instrument was transferred by writing and delivery. Responsa Mabit likewise concludes in the name of the Rashba, Rabbenu Yonah, and other authorities that only an acknowledgment combined with assignment by writing and delivery bars the plea of payment. R. Aharon Sasson also explains the Ran and Maggid Mishneh this way and rules accordingly. It is also likely that Rabbenu Yonah, cited by the Rosh in Chezkat HaBatim, refers to an acknowledgment together with transfer of the right, as the Rashba reports in his name. The Tur cannot be interpreted that way, because the Tur separately requires that the guarantee be mentioned in the instrument and says that the guarantor may not collect from encumbered property. If the document had been fully assigned to him, even an unrelated purchaser could collect from encumbered property. Rabbenu Yerucham expressly understands Rabbenu Yonah to require acknowledgment and assignment by writing and delivery. The Bach’s suggestion that Rabbenu Yonah merely bars the borrower from saying that perhaps the guarantor did not pay, while still allowing him to plead that he later paid the guarantor, is impossible, for the Gemara and Rosh expressly address a borrower who says, ‘I paid you,’ and reject that plea. According to what has been explained, it is clear that even where the guarantor brings witnesses that he paid the lender, the borrower may still say to the guarantor, ‘I later paid you.’ If a written acknowledgment does not defeat that plea, witnesses to the guarantor’s payment certainly do not, for a litigant’s admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. The Tur’s contrary ruling follows his own view that a mere acknowledgment bars the plea of payment. The Levush is inconsistent: after ruling that a mere acknowledgment does not bar the plea, he nevertheless follows the Tur where witnesses prove the guarantor’s payment. The Ramban’s novellae to Get Pashut state explicitly that when the guarantor sues the borrower, if there are no witnesses that the guarantor paid, the borrower may plainly say, ‘I paid you.’ Even if witnesses prove that the guarantor paid the lender, the guarantor may recover only if it is also known that the borrower did not thereafter pay the guarantor. Similarly, if the lender wrote an acknowledgment, the borrower may still say that he later paid the guarantor, since the guarantor’s claim against him is not itself a documented loan. Nimukei Yosef and the Baal HaTerumot imply the same. The Baal HaTerumot writes that the guarantor must prove that he paid, but if he brings no proof, or if the borrower pleads, ‘I paid you,’ the borrower is believed, and the instrument in the guarantor’s possession does not convert the claim into a documented loan. His proof from the Mishna’s phrase ‘If he wrote him an acknowledgment, he collects’ concerns an acknowledgment joined with assignment by writing and delivery. Otherwise, even proof that the guarantor paid would not bar the borrower from claiming that he later paid the guarantor. This is the correct practical ruling.
