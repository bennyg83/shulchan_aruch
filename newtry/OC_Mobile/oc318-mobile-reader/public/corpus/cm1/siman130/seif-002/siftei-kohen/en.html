In Perishah I wrote that even according to the view imposing liability in such a case, the guarantor is exempt if the borrower claims that he later paid the lender. Otherwise, why would the Tur record these laws when he rules above, siman 128, in the name of Rabbenu Tam and the Rosh that one who pays another’s debt without authorization is entitled to reimbursement, even where the lender proved that he had not been paid at the time the guarantor paid him?
Who entered the guarantee on his own initiative. The Tur and the Baal HaTerumot write that liability exists only where the borrower said, ‘Become my guarantor.’ In that case the guarantor is entitled to reimbursement even if he paid on his own before the lender brought the borrower to judgment. If the borrower did not say, ‘Become my guarantor,’ the borrower is exempt. This appears to me primary. The Maggid Mishneh presents the contrary possibility only tentatively, and we do not abandon what the Baal HaTerumot and Tur understood with certainty in the Raavad because of the Maggid Mishneh’s doubt. Moreover, the Maggid Mishneh himself agrees with the Rambam, as do the Semag and Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 5, who writes that this is Rav Saadiah Gaon’s view. The Beit Yosef’s discussion in seif 7 concerning Nimukei Yosef also appears to support the Baal HaTerumot and Tur’s understanding of the Raavad. In my humble opinion, this is likewise evident from Rashbam, Bava Batra 32b, the Rosh there, siman 14, and Nimukei Yosef concerning the incident of the guarantor, for they explain that he was a kablan. The same implication appears in Responsa Mabit, part 1, end of siman 42.
