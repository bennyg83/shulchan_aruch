But if he informed them. See Responsa Neot Deshe, siman 61, concerning Reuven, who borrowed three hundred gold coins from the local nobleman on a debt document bearing annual interest. The nobleman refused to lend unless Shimon signed together with Reuven, and they both signed. Reuven received the money. At the end of each year they paid the interest and asked the nobleman to continue the loan for another year, which he repeatedly agreed to do. In the middle of the fourth year Reuven died, leaving adult orphans. When that year ended, the nobleman summoned Reuven's widow and Shimon to pay the debt. Shimon said: you know that I received no benefit from this money and signed only as guarantor. Reuven's wife responded that half of the debt belonged to Shimon. The nobleman sent them to Jewish judges to determine from whom he should collect. The widow and orphans then admitted that the entire sum had in fact gone to Reuven and that they knew this clearly. Their only claim was that on one occasion they heard Reuven say that he had claims and counterclaims against Shimon amounting to approximately one hundred and fifty gold coins, and that when he had time he would sue him and perhaps prevail. Shimon denied owing Reuven anything from their business dealings. The question was whether, because Shimon was signed on the document together with Reuven, strict law treats Shimon as borrower for half the amount, as though Shimon himself borrowed one hundred and fifty from the nobleman and then lent it to Reuven. If Reuven were alive and asserted his claims while Shimon denied them, one might have required Shimon to pay half the debt to the nobleman, after which Shimon would be the claimant against Reuven, who could swear and be exempt. Perhaps the same claim should now be pleaded for the orphans. Neot Deshe explains at length that the orphans' claim has no substance. In his view, even if Reuven were alive and asserted with certainty that Shimon owed him money from another matter, Reuven would not be believed. The Shach in siman 75, seif katan 22, proves that the claim "I have money of yours in my hands" can be asserted only where the defendant has a migo that he could deny the original transaction or claim repayment. Here Reuven could not have claimed repayment. As explained at the end of siman 130, only where the guarantor paid before informing the heirs are we concerned that the borrower may have given the guarantor the money. Once the guarantor informed them first, we are not concerned, because a borrower ordinarily pays the creditor directly. Moreover, Reuven died during the term, in the middle of the fourth year. The Shach in siman 78, seif katan 2, rules that even where the lender graciously extended the time, we retain the presumption that a person does not pay before the due date, contrary to Maharshadam. In this case even Maharshadam would agree. Furthermore, the nobleman still held the debt document, which itself creates a presumption against payment. Nor could Reuven have used a migo that he initially borrowed only half and Shimon borrowed half for himself, because it was publicly known throughout the town that Shimon had signed merely as guarantor, a fact stronger than ordinary public reputation. Thus, if Reuven himself could not have asserted "I have your money in my hands" because he lacked a migo, all the more so do we not plead this for his heirs. Many authorities in siman 108, seif 4, hold that even a claim of unavoidable accident is not pleaded for heirs because it is uncommon, and all the more so a claim of waiver or "I have your money in my hands," which is even less common. Even the authorities there who do plead unavoidable accident for heirs, as the Shach concludes, agree regarding waiver or a counterclaim of this kind. See what I wrote at the end of siman 108, seif katan 5. Thus there are two independent reasons the heirs' claim fails even if they had said that their father told them with certainty that Shimon owed him money from another matter: first, their father himself would not have been believed without a migo; second, this is an entirely uncommon claim that we do not plead for heirs. If the heirs themselves had some migo and said that their father told them this with certainty, they might be believed with an oath, as is evident in siman 297 in the Rama, concerning heirs who say, "Our father told us that this belongs to him." But here the heirs have no migo and are not believed even if they assert certainty. All the more so where they heard only that their father himself was uncertain. Even if they had some migo, such an uncertain statement would not help. Neot Deshe adds that there is another reason the heirs' claim has no substance. We never find that a certain debt is displaced by a doubtful repayment or similar uncertainty. That is why one who says, "I do not know whether I repaid you," is liable. Orphans who do not know whether their father paid are ordinarily exempt because they were not expected to know, as the Tur writes in siman 75. But this applies only where the matter was certain to the father and doubtful only to the heir. Where the father himself was uncertain, we do not plead for the heir that perhaps the father's doubt might later have been resolved in his favor. A doubt cannot displace a certain debt. He brings proof from Bava Kamma 112, where Rava had to reinterpret a baraita. He develops one further possible reason to obligate the heirs. Even if their father could have asserted a counterclaim and even if such uncommon claims are generally pleaded for heirs, perhaps we still do not plead a doubtful counterclaim of "I have your money in my hands." When we plead for heirs against a certain debt, we do so with claims that directly undermine the debt itself, such as "I repaid," "I returned it," "it was lost through unavoidable circumstances," or "I purchased it." Such claims make the very debt doubtful, and money is not extracted from heirs on doubt. A counterclaim that the creditor separately owes the debtor money does not attack the debt itself. In such a case one may say that a doubtful counterclaim cannot displace a certain debt. He supports this from Rashi in Bava Kamma there, but concludes that practical action should not be based on this novel rationale alone, especially to extract from heirs. Nevertheless, because of the earlier reasons, he concludes clearly that Shimon need only accept a general cherem that he owes Reuven nothing from their business dealings and other matters, and the nobleman may collect the entire debt from Reuven's estate. Although Shimon technically has the status of borrower regarding half the debt, the heirs would immediately have to reimburse him. There is no purpose in making the money travel in a circle. Nor is there a practical difference regarding the standard time beit din grants for payment, because Shimon would have no need to raise funds elsewhere. His reimbursement would come only from the remaining estate assets, which are still held in the estate. Therefore the judges should tell the nobleman to collect from Reuven's estate.
