Who entered, etc. If one voluntarily became guarantor and paid, he may recover from the borrower. This is the Maggid Mishneh's understanding of the Raavad. One may ask that this appears to be one who paid another person's debt without his knowledge, whom we rule is not reimbursed. What difference is there between paying immediately and first becoming guarantor? In either case everything was done without the borrower's knowledge. Gidulei Terumah raises this difficulty. It can be answered in two ways. One possibility is that the rule exempting the debtor when another paid his debt without his knowledge applies only where the debtor did not know of the payment at all. If he sees another become guarantor and pay on his behalf and remains silent, his failure to protest indicates consent and he is liable. This, however, is difficult, for regarding one who vowed not to benefit another, the other may pay his debt because such payment is treated as paying another's debt without his knowledge. The decisors should then distinguish whether the payment occurred in the debtor's presence. If in his presence, his silence would show consent, making him liable and thus benefiting from the person from whom benefit was forbidden. The better explanation is that the cases differ fundamentally. Where the debt already existed and another merely pays it, the debtor can say, You only drove a lion from my property; I might have appeased the creditor or otherwise avoided payment. The Yerushalmi states another reason, that nothing came into the debtor's hand, so on what basis should he reimburse? But where the lender originally refused to lend until this person became guarantor, and because of the guaranty the loan was advanced, the borrower plainly must reimburse the guarantor if the latter later had to pay, even though the guaranty was undertaken voluntarily without the borrower's request. Ultimately, the borrower received the money because of him, and without the guaranty he would not have received it. The claim, I could have appeased the creditor, has no application, because without the guarantor the creditor would never have lent. Once the person became guarantor he was legally compelled to pay, and therefore the borrower must reimburse him. It is possible that according to the Maggid Mishneh the Raavad agrees that the guarantor should not pay until the creditor first presses the borrower, and the phrase he paid voluntarily means only that the borrower never requested that he become guarantor, so both guaranty and payment were initiated by him. Alternatively, even if he paid without first pressing the borrower, the borrower remains liable because he received the benefit created by the guaranty. It is as though the borrower authorized the guaranty, for one may confer a benefit upon a person without his knowledge. Since the guaranty benefited him, it is treated as authorized; and according to the Raavad, one who was authorized to become guarantor is also authorized to pay without further consent. On this basis, even a voluntarily assumed guaranty is deemed authorized, since ordinarily a person is pleased to receive such a benefit. In a loan, the debtor cannot say, I could have appeased him, because without the guarantor there would have been no loan at all. Perhaps this is why the Mechaber adopts the Maggid Mishneh's view and does not focus on the Tur's interpretation that the Raavad requires the borrower to say, Guarantee for me, though he need not say, Pay. The Tur follows his own view that one who pays another's debt without his knowledge is liable for reimbursement. His discussion therefore concerns the different issue addressed by the Sema, whether the borrower who later paid the creditor must nevertheless reimburse the guarantor as well. On that issue the Tur could hold that if he never told the guarantor, Guarantee for me, there was no duty upon the borrower to ask whether the guarantor had paid, since he never authorized the guaranty and had no reason to concern himself with it. Thus the Tur requires that he said, Guarantee for me, so that according to the Raavad the borrower had the duty to ascertain whether the guarantor had paid before paying the creditor himself. It may therefore be that the Tur and Maggid Mishneh do not disagree in law at all. Concerning the basic rule that voluntary guaranty is not treated as unauthorized payment of another's debt, the Tur may agree with the Maggid Mishneh because the loan was made only due to the guaranty. Concerning a later duplicate payment by the borrower, however, the Maggid Mishneh could agree with the Tur that where the guaranty was undertaken entirely voluntarily, the borrower had no duty to inquire and is exempt. The Mechaber speaks only to the first issue, since above in siman 128 he rules that one who pays another's debt without his knowledge is exempt. Thus the ruling is consistent.
