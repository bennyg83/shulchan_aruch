But if he informed them. Responsa Neot Deshe, siman 61, discusses Reuven, who borrowed three hundred gold pieces with interest from the local prince only after Shimon signed together with him. Reuven received all the money, and each year they paid the interest and obtained an extension. During the fourth year Reuven died, leaving adult orphans. At year's end the prince demanded payment from Reuven's widow and from Shimon. Shimon said that he had derived no benefit from the loan and had signed only as guarantor. The widow initially claimed that half the debt belonged to Shimon, but she and the heirs later admitted clearly that the entire principal had been Reuven's. Their only response was that they once heard Reuven say that he had some business claim against Shimon for about one hundred and fifty gold pieces and might eventually sue him and perhaps prevail. Shimon denied owing Reuven anything. The responsum rules that the heirs' assertion cannot defeat the clear debt. Although Shimon, by signing jointly, is treated vis-a-vis the prince as a borrower for half, so that if Reuven were alive and could assert a valid counterclaim Shimon might pay the prince and then seek reimbursement, Reuven himself could not have relied on a bare claim of 'I have money of yours in my hands' without a migo. Here Reuven could not plausibly claim that he had already paid Shimon, because where a guarantor informs the borrower before paying, we do not suspect that the borrower had previously given him the money; moreover Reuven died during the agreed extension period, and a person is not presumed to pay before the due date, even where the creditor voluntarily extended the term, and the prince still held the deed. Nor could Reuven claim that from the outset Shimon had borrowed half for himself, because the entire town knew that Shimon signed only as guarantor. Since Reuven himself lacked a migo for such a counterclaim, certainly beit din does not advance that unusual claim for his heirs. Even where the heirs report that their father stated a definite claim, they are not believed without their own migo; here they heard only a doubtful possibility. Neot Deshe further develops the principle that a certain debt is not displaced by a merely doubtful, collateral counterclaim. The rule protecting heirs who do not know whether their father paid applies to uncertainty concerning the debt itself, such as payment, return, accident, or purchase, not to a speculative independent claim of setoff. He offers additional proofs but is reluctant to rely on that novel reasoning alone to extract from orphans. On the other grounds, however, he concludes clearly that Shimon need only take an oath while holding a sacred object that he owes Reuven nothing from their business dealings, and the prince should collect the entire debt from Reuven's estate. Although Shimon formally has the status of borrower for half as against the prince, requiring him to pay and then recover the identical amount from the estate would be pointless circuity. Nor is there a beit din payment period to be granted, because he can recover only from the estate assets already present. Neot Deshe notes that he later found a similar ruling in Perach Mateh Aharon.
