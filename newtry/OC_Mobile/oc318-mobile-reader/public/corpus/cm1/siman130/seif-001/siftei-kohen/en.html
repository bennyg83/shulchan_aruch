["If he paid before it became known, etc. The Sema writes that this refers to an oral loan. The Bach writes that the same applies to a written loan if the borrower can prove that a receipt exists against it."]
["And the borrower claims that he already paid. Meaning, even if the borrower brings no proof but merely asserts payment. So writes the Sema, and this is also implied by the continuation: but if he paid, meaning after the lender brought proof, etc."]
["He owes the guarantor nothing. See Sema seif katan 4. The Sema suggests a difficulty from this rule and then says that seif 2 proves that the phrase, he did not authorize him to pay, refers to the guarantor. I do not understand what this resolves. It still follows that a kablan is exempt if the borrower claims that he paid. If not, why would the borrower owe him nothing? The kablan could say: even if you tell me in my presence that you paid, I remain liable to the lender. Nor does seif 2 prove otherwise. There, the phrase, he did not authorize him to pay in an effective manner, concerns a case where the guarantor paid without authorization, yet the borrower is exempt because he never authorized payment except where the debt was still unpaid."]
["The borrower must give, etc. See Sema seif katan 7, who writes that a guarantor is different because his payment is treated as though made with the borrower's authorization, and he repeats this in seif katan 10. In my view, where the underlying debt is oral and the borrower claims, after you paid I went back and paid the lender because I did not know of your payment, the borrower is exempt even if the guarantor proves that he paid the lender and the lender had then proved that he had not previously been paid. This remains true even if the borrower had said, become my guarantor. No authority, including the Raavad, disputes the borrower's right to claim later payment. The guarantor had authority to pay and is therefore not merely one who paid another's debt without authorization, but his position is no stronger in this respect than that of any other person. He should have informed the borrower that he paid on his behalf. The borrower had no reason to assume this had occurred, and if the guarantor failed to notify him he caused his own loss. The borrower may therefore say, I later paid the lender a second time because I did not know of your payment. If the loan was evidenced by a note and the guarantor has witnesses, then plainly even if the guarantor paid on his own initiative and entered the guaranty without the borrower's request, the borrower cannot credibly say that he later paid the lender, for why would he have paid without receiving back his note? Unless the borrower produces a receipt from the lender showing that he paid, in which event the case is treated like an oral loan and he is exempt from the guarantor if he says he paid later without knowing of the guarantor's payment and that the lender told him the note was lost and gave him a receipt. The Tur must therefore follow his own view below that where the guarantor paid the lender and proves the payment, the borrower cannot later claim that he paid the lender, because the instrument held by the guarantor together with proof of payment gives the guarantor the status of a written creditor. Thus the Tur teaches here that the borrower must pay the guarantor, whereas with an ordinary third party the borrower could say, I later paid you. He then cites the Rambam and Raavad to teach that according to the Raavad, if the borrower told him, become my guarantor, and the guarantor proves payment and took the note, the borrower must reimburse him and cannot say, I paid you. According to the Rambam, however, since he has only the status of one who paid another's debt without authorization, even holding the note does not prevent the borrower from claiming repayment. This appears to be the Tur's view."]
