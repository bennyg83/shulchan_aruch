Before witnesses. Without witnesses he is in any event not believed, as explained in seif 3. Yet witnesses are not literal; the same applies if the creditor wrote him a receipt, as explained in seifim 3 and 4, or if the borrower admits that the guarantor paid.
If he paid him before it became known, etc. In the Perishah I wrote that this concerns an oral loan. If the creditor held a document, that itself would count as knowledge that the loan had not been paid, as explained in siman 129, seif 8. What the Tur and Mechaber conclude, 'whether he became liable to him by document, etc.,' refers not to the opening clause but to the latter clause; see there.
And the borrower claims that he had already paid him. Meaning, even if the borrower brings no proof that he paid, but merely claims so. This is also why the earlier wording is 'before it became known whether he paid,' rather than 'before it became known that he paid.'
He owes the guarantor nothing. The Tur concludes with the reason: he authorized him to pay only if the debt was still unpaid, not if it had already been paid. From the wording 'he authorized him to pay,' it appears that everything depends on authorization: had the guarantor wished, he was not compelled to pay, for he could have said, 'Perhaps the borrower already paid you,' since we are dealing with an oral loan, as explained above. This somewhat conflicts with Rama's gloss in siman 129, seif 15, that an absolute guarantor cannot make that claim, although the Tur here also speaks of an absolute guarantor. However, seif 2 below indicates that the wording 'he authorized him to pay' refers to an ordinary guarantor and not to an absolute guarantor, for regarding an absolute guarantor it should have said, 'he did not authorize him to become an absolute guarantor for him.' See what I wrote in siman 129 on this matter.
After the creditor brought proof that he had not been paid. Baal HaTerumot concludes: and the borrower was found liable in judgment; see the Perishah.
The borrower must give him, etc. Meaning, even if the borrower claims that afterward he himself paid the creditor. One might have thought that even according to those who hold that one who pays another's debt is entitled to reimbursement from the debtor, except for the support of his wife, as Rabbeinu Tam, the Tur, and those with them hold, as explained in siman 128, they would agree here that the borrower is exempt, because the guarantor paid without his knowledge and the borrower then paid again. The ruling teaches that a guarantor is different, because his payment is considered as though made with the borrower's authorization, as concluded in seif 2.
