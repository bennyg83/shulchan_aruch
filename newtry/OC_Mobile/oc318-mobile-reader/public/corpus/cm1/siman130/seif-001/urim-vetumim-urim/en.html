Before witnesses. Meaning, without witnesses he would in any event not be believed, as stated in seif 3. 'Witnesses' is not literal, however. The same applies if the creditor wrote him a receipt, as in seifim 3 and 4, and likewise if the borrower admits to the guarantor that he paid. See Sema; this is straightforward.
If he paid before it became known, etc. The Sema writes that the Perishah explains this as an oral loan. If the creditor still held the note, that itself would establish that the debt had not been paid, as stated in siman 129, seif 8. The Tur's and Mechaber's concluding words, 'whether he became obligated by a note,' refer to the latter clause. The Bach disagrees and writes that the same law applies to a written loan if the borrower can prove that there was a receipt. The Shach cites this. The Taz, however, agrees with the Sema, because even if the borrower has a receipt, the guarantor would still have been obligated as long as the creditor held the note, and could not defer him by saying that perhaps a receipt exists, as the Beit Yosef writes at the end of siman 129. Therefore the borrower certainly must reimburse the guarantor. Although the wording can be pressed somewhat, their view is reasonable.
And he claims. Meaning, even if the borrower brings no proof that he paid, but merely makes that claim. See Sema; this is straightforward.
Anything. The Tur gives the reason that the borrower authorized the guarantor to pay only if the debt was still outstanding, not if it had already been paid. The Tur's wording, 'he did not authorize him,' implies that the matter depends on authorization, and that if the guarantor wished he was not compelled to pay because he could have said, 'Perhaps the borrower already paid you,' since we are dealing with an oral loan. This appears somewhat inconsistent with the Rama in siman 129, who rules that a kablan cannot make that claim, while the present discussion appears to include a kablan. The Sema therefore writes that seif 2 below proves this clause concerns only an ordinary guarantor, not a kablan. The Shach, seif katan 3, writes that the Sema's answer is insufficient. See Tumim, where I wrote that according to the Sema's own approach in siman 129, seif 15, that the kablan should have stipulated and caused his own loss by failing to do so, there is no difficulty. The Taz writes similarly. Nevertheless, I explained that the Sema's question was directed at the Tur's wording, and according to the Tur's approach the Sema's answer is sound. As practical law, however, I agreed with the Shach that the kablan is exempt from reimbursing the borrower, as explained above.
Proof. Meaning, he produced a note written for the loan, and we do not suspect that a receipt may have been issued. Although immediately below, with respect to an ordinary guarantor, we are concerned with the even less common possibility that the note fell from the creditor, there there is an adverse circumstance because the note is not in the creditor's possession and therefore provides no proof for the guarantor. See Taz.
For him. The Sema explains that this applies even if the borrower claims that he later paid the creditor himself. The novelty is that even according to the opinion in siman 128 that when one pays another person's debt the debtor must reimburse him, one might have thought the borrower is exempt here because the guarantor paid without the borrower's knowledge and the borrower then paid again. The law therefore teaches that a guarantor is different, for his payment is treated as though made with the borrower's authorization, as stated in seif 2 below. The Shach, seif katan 5, objects, asking what the borrower could have done, and adopts a strained interpretation. See Tumim, where I wrote that the Shach's objection is not strong, that his interpretation is unsupported by the Tur and Baal HaTerumot, and that one should not depart from the Sema, whose words are correct and clear.
