And the house will suffer loss. That is, the loss exceeds one fifth. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 12, questions this measure. Terumat HaDeshen derived the rule from the discussion of two claimants to an animal, where a division that destroys too much of the asset is avoided. Chatam Sofer argues that there is a difference. In the Gemara, ownership itself is uncertain, and the court already causes the true owner to lose half through the doubtful division; it should not additionally destroy a substantial portion of the half he receives. By contrast, heirs who inherit property that is intrinsically subject to division may be said to have inherited only their respective divisible shares, even if division reduces value. He nevertheless distinguishes partners. According to the overwhelming majority of authorities, partners may ultimately demand division or gud o agud because each may say that he originally thought he could tolerate the partnership but can no longer do so. Their original partnership therefore was not made on terms that one partner could later destroy a fifth of the other's value merely because coexistence became difficult. In such a case the substantial loss rule retains force. When both partners themselves desire to separate, however, there is no need for the special claim that one can no longer tolerate the arrangement; they may demand division or gud o agud by mutual desire. Chatam Sofer understands the earlier authorities accordingly.
