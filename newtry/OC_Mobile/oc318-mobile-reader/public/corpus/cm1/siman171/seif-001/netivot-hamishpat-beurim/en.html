They cannot retract. See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that even waiver without a kinyan can be effective. This cannot mean that a bare waiver suffices to prevent later division. A partner can ordinarily say, "Divide with me," even where there is no privacy damage, and waiver cannot transfer a proprietary interest in the body of land. Rashba, siman 907, writes that a kinyan works because we interpret it as a transfer of a right in the land itself, namely that the parties acquired from one another a continuing shared right in the property. Such a proprietary right cannot be created by mere waiver. Shach's proof from siman 157 is therefore directed only to privacy damage. Once the property has already been divided and a partition is required because of privacy, waiver can apply to the damage itself, like waiver of other neighbor damages. Rama's mention of a kinyan here is therefore precise: to bind the parties not to divide, a kinyan is required.
The law is with Reuven. He cannot compel the other party to divide the synagogue place by erecting a physical partition and giving each an area, as one can sometimes compel a courtyard partition, because synagogue seats are ordinarily sold by seating space rather than by ownership of a defined parcel. A partition would also make the place more cramped. Therefore Reuven's position prevails.
Likewise, many people sitting. See Sema, seif katan 3, who says the word "likewise" is imprecise. In my view it is a scribal error and should read, "However, many people sitting..." The meaning is that although one cannot object where another wishes to put a partition at the edge of his own seat, namely between one seat and the next, if the first person has a wide place and wishes to divide it by a partition in the middle so that it will visibly become two seats, the person sitting next to him may object because the partition changes the apparent ranking and arrangement of the seats. Without a partition, however, if the first person's place is broad enough and seating another person there does not crowd his neighbor, it appears that the neighbor cannot object. The Rosh's responsum discusses specifically making a partition. The Rosh also speaks of a case where it was known that the first seat had greater importance than the second. Where that is not known, we apply the principle that the place does not confer honor upon the person.
