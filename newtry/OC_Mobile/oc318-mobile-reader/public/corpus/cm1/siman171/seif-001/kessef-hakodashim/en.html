Seif 8, Rama. (And he may object.) In siman 171, a claim that a synagogue seat will be diminished in value can prevent the congregation from causing that diminution. Dagul MeRevavah distinguishes there based upon the wording of a responsum of the Rosh, and the matter requires study. At the least, however, it is possible that this protection applies primarily between one individual and another. If the congregation needs to extend or alter the synagogue boundaries, an individual who purchased a seat from the congregation may not necessarily prevent the public project merely because his seat will lose some value. In such a dispute the congregation may also have the stronger possessory status against an individual. There are various explanations of the legal nature of synagogue seats. Some later responsa are reported to treat them not as absolute ownership but as a form of security right, leasehold, or sale for a limited period. Although the ordinary understanding of the authorities appears to treat them more like an actual sale, these alternative analyses at least weaken the purchaser's ability to prevent necessary communal construction. One can also say that the original allocation and sale were implicitly subject to the congregation's continuing ability to add buildings and seats, since sacred communal use is expected to increase. The Sages even permitted public construction on Chol HaMoed because communal projects are prone to delay, and as the population grows an enlargement of the synagogue is often unavoidable. After a fire, in particular, buildings are commonly reconstructed on a larger plan. If the expansion diminishes or eliminates a particular seat, the owner should receive another seat among the newly built places corresponding to the value of his former right. Perhaps the valuation should measure the diminution caused by the new construction rather than assume that the original sale guaranteed the purchaser against every future reduction in value. It is plausible that the sale was always conditional upon the congregation's right to enlarge the building even though this might reduce the seat's market value. Entire cancellation of the seat is more difficult to justify merely on the basis of an implied condition. Even where the former roof was destroyed and the synagogue cannot be rebuilt without a changed plan, the fact that the property is destined for necessary communal construction means that the purchaser's interest has not simply disappeared. His share is intended to return in the reconstructed building. He must, however, contribute proportionately to the building expenses. It requires study whether those expenses are assessed entirely per capita or also according to the relative dignity and value of each seat. Even regular worshippers who do not own seats may be obligated at least per capita, because members of a community compel one another to build a synagogue and the mitzvah of communal prayer is not measured by the prestige of one's seat. Perhaps a fair assessment is partly per capita and partly according to the honor or ordinary value of each person's place, including the advantage of hearing the cantor and Torah reader. A person's financial ability may also be relevant in allocating general communal expenses. It may even be permissible to collect building expenses out of the value of the seat itself before satisfying certain other claims, because the seat is expressly subject to the obligation to maintain the building. Even if the roof over a particular seat did not itself burn, everyone who prays there benefits from rebuilding the roof over the cantor's and reader's area, so the expense can fall upon all seat holders and worshippers. All of this requires careful further study.
