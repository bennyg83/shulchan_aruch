If two brothers inherit two large houses, each independently divisible, and one says, 'Let us divide each house separately,' while the other says, 'No, let us allot one house against the other,' the proposal to allot house against house is accepted. Rama: All the more so, if neither house is independently divisible, the houses are allotted against one another because their uses are alike, as explained immediately above. The rule permitting house against house applies only where their uses are equivalent. Some say that most houses are not subject to such division, because not all houses and rooms are equal. A house is not allotted against an upper story or against a room whose use differs. Certainly, a single house is not divided where doing so requires closing and rebuilding windows or doors and incurring construction expenses. From the responsum of the Rosh, however, it appears that a house may be allotted against a shop or cellar. It appears to me that the same applies to a room or upper story, since these are considered comparable uses. Likewise, if beit din concludes that the necessary construction costs will not cause substantial harm, the property is divided, as explained above in seif 5 concerning depreciation of houses. With respect to buy or sell, however, there is no distinction between a house, a synagogue seat, or other property, and one partner may invoke buy or sell with respect to the whole. Some say that buy or sell applies only to property owned in its substance. It does not apply to a mortgage or similar right that will ultimately be collected in money; instead, the money is divided. It appears to me that if the partners presently use the mortgaged property, it resembles a lease, concerning which, as explained in seif 9, some disagree.
