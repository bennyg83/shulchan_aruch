Seif 14. (Two brothers who inherited.) A house inherited by two brothers stood on a slope. On the uphill side it had a window separated from the neighbor by four cubits. On the downhill side it had another window facing the neighbor, above and opposite the neighbor's sloped roof. After the house burned, the site was sold in two equal halves to two purchasers. The purchaser of the uphill half wished to build up to the neighbor's boundary without leaving a window. The other purchaser argued that the entire former house site and its surrounding boundary should first be divided equally, including the additional three or four cubits that would now be gained, as though no prior building had stood there and the raw site had been bought in halves. There is some basis for his claim. A divisible property should be divided into genuinely equal portions. Since the downhill part enjoyed window rights and open space toward its neighbor, one might say that the uphill part must receive corresponding window rights, thereby gaining the four cubits automatically. If the uphill owner later closes that side window and incorporates the four cubits into his own property, this could be treated as one party benefiting while the other loses nothing. However, the window facing the neighbor's sloped roof does not acquire a prescriptive right according to either of the opinions in siman 154. Therefore, even if the neighbor never objected to that window, it is not a property right that enters into the division. Moreover, if the four cubits in question were historically used as a public passage for pedestrians and wagons, they are likewise not subject to private division or acquisition merely because the public did not protest. The measurement of the private site must therefore be made outside that public way. A separate case concerned partners in a house who orally agreed that one would have only the liquor business and the other would have ordinary shop merchandise. They later reduced their arrangement to a signed writing stating that neither would sell the merchandise that the other "holds" for sale. The written language may appear to differ from the earlier oral formulation. This is not like the rules of testimony in simanim 29 and 30, where one strains to reconcile two statements because a witness cannot retract after testifying. Private parties can retract and make a new agreement, and therefore there are several reasons to follow the writing. First, the rule of following the first expression applies only where there was no later effective retraction. Here retraction and a new stipulation were possible. Second, when an oral statement conflicts with a written instrument, the written instrument is ordinarily primary, as Tur writes in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 177. Although Beit Yosef there cites language of Rambam that may point the other way, he ultimately leaves the matter requiring study, and we do not multiply disputes where the authorities can be reconciled. Beit Yosef himself offers a reading under which Rambam does not disagree with Tur. I have elsewhere used this principle to justify the common practice of orally using the terminology of a loan where the written document is expressly drafted as an iska. Even though certain oral formulations can be problematic, the written instrument is legally primary. Shach's discussion of an instrument written in another person's name does not prove otherwise, because there the parties expressly said that the writing was merely for appearance. So long as the parties did not expressly state that the document was only formal and not substantive, the writing remains primary. In addition, the holder of a document bears the disadvantage of ambiguous wording. Even if one could force the written language to mean that once one partner ever dealt in a product the other could never sell it, even at a time when the first partner no longer deals in it, that is not the necessary reading. Certainly the earlier oral language can be understood merely to allocate the income from ordinary shop merchandise while the other partner is actively dealing in that merchandise. When he is not selling it, preventing the other from doing so would be a case where one benefits and the other loses nothing. Why should he be barred from selling an item at a time when his fellow is not dealing in it? A signed private writing has the force of an instrument for this purpose, because a party's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses.
