And Shimon has only three, etc. See what I wrote above in siman 115, seif katan 23.
Reuven may say, etc. The Bach explains that because one partner has four cubits, he cannot be compelled through gud o igud. In his view, gud o igud applies only where neither partner has four cubits. Where one has four and the other has three, the owner of three cannot say gud o igud, because while "buy" is possible, "sell to me" is not feasible from his side. The owner of four can, however, say: I will designate for myself only three cubits, separate them by a partition, and use them myself, while leaving one cubit adjoining your three so that you will have a courtyard of four cubits. I will then be a partner with you in one fourth of your portion, and I can say gud o igud regarding that shared portion. The Bach further suggests that the Tur's attribution should read Maharam rather than Ramah, because these words appear in the Mordechai at the end of the first chapter of Bava Batra in the name of Maharam. In my view this is not correct. Why should the owner of three cubits be unable to tell the owner of four, "Buy my three cubits or sell me yours"? If the answer is that the latter can say, "I will sell you only enough to complete your minimum measure," then the same response could be given where each owns only three cubits. Ordinarily he does not say this because he would be left with less than the minimum measure. Even in the case of seven cubits the wording of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch is somewhat difficult, because the owner of four would likewise be left with less than the minimum. The Mordechai's case in the name of Maharam is clear, because there Reuven owns much more than the minimum measure, so after the suggested arrangement he still retains the minimum. I am inclined to emend the Tur to read: a courtyard of eight cubits, of which Reuven owns five portions. If we do not emend it, we must say the numbers are not exact and the ruling merely teaches that when one partner owns a minimum measure he cannot simply take it against the other's will. Rather, he may be told to divide jointly and give the other his share, and if he refuses, the other may join with him in one quarter of the relevant portion. In truth there is no distinction between a case where one owns four cubits and one where each owns only three. This is unlike the Bach. Examine and you will find it so.
