Regarding 'buy or sell,' it appears that a poor partner may obtain money from others in order to exercise the option to buy, even according to those who hold that one cannot force his partner with borrowed money. That limitation applies to forcing the other party. But one who is himself told 'buy or sell' may do whatever is reasonably possible to preserve his share. He could even sell his share to another, who would then have standing to make the same demand, as the Sema writes. All the more so here, where his own share remains his and he merely brings in another person to fund the option. Chazal's statement that a poor person is disadvantaged by the wealthy partner's 'buy or sell' demand refers only to one unable to obtain the funds. I also heard that 'buy or sell' may apply even when the other partner has the next year's use, with compensation paid for that year. This appears reasonable. If one partner had priority for a year and the other now says, 'I will take the priority first,' perhaps that is not a valid claim, because the established order already fixed alternating years.
