Dagul MeRevavah writes that Responsa Rosh, siman 7, appears to say the opposite, namely that this applies specifically where from the outset a board was placed at the head of the row, etc. See there for the reasoning. He notes that in Responsa Rosh, rule 3, siman 3, the beginning and end appear contradictory and require explanation. In any event, siman 7 expressly supports his reading. Perhaps on that basis siman 3 can also be reconciled: at the end of that responsum there was a board at the head of the row marking it as the first row. If a partition were now fixed there, the second person's place would no longer be in the first row. It is nevertheless explicit that where rows are not divided by such a board, one may add a seat so long as this does not reduce the open airspace belonging to the synagogue. If he seeks to appropriate otherwise open synagogue space for himself, the other can object, because all members have equal rights in the vacant area and no one may take it for himself, as the Rosh explains in siman 7. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 26, who mentions Dagul MeRevavah's observation that simanim 3 and 7 of the Rosh appear inconsistent and that siman 3 even appears internally inconsistent. Chatam Sofer writes that there is no true contradiction, only a minor textual correction is needed. In siman 3 two people sat side by side on a bench without a partition. Reuven wished to erect a partition within his own share. Shimon argued that the partition would make Reuven's seat superior and Shimon's inferior, thereby harming him. The Rashba, part 3, siman 155, was asked the same question and ruled simply that Shimon could not object. The Rosh explained that prayer itself is neither improved nor worsened by one location rather than another. If the issue is merely the honor associated with the seats, we find no rule preventing a person from building within his own boundary because his neighbor will thereby be less honored, where there is no injury to the neighbor's person or property. If, however, local custom assigns a higher monetary value to a superior seat, and the seats were originally purchased cheaply but Reuven's partition now raises the value of his seat and correspondingly lowers Shimon's, then Reuven causes Shimon monetary loss and may not erect the partition even within his own property. This, Chatam Sofer explains, is the intent of siman 3, though the text is somewhat defective. Siman 7 concerns a different case: many people sat on one bench enclosed by boards at its two ends, and the person at the upper end wished to remove the upper board and add another seat. The Rosh objected without making the ruling depend on monetary depreciation, because the closing board belonged jointly to all the partners. One partner cannot alter jointly owned property to increase his own honor while diminishing theirs, even if no monetary loss results. On this explanation, the rulings of the Rosh and Rama are correct.
