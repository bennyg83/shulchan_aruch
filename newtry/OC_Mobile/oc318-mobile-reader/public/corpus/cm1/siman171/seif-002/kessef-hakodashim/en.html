Seif 6. (Sell me your share.) Shach, seif katan 1, writes that several authorities hold that partners who voluntarily purchased property together do not have the right of "you buy me out or I will buy you out." My father in law also ruled in this manner, based upon the rule that one who seeks to remove property from another bears the burden of proof, so each partner can say that he relies upon the authorities who do not permit a forced buyout. Beit Yosef, however, cites Rashba that when one heir sells his share to an outsider, because the remaining heir can demand a buyout from the purchaser, the purchaser can likewise make the same demand against the heir. The purchaser is not barred by the claim that he knowingly entered the situation. Since no authority is cited as disagreeing with Rashba on this particular reciprocal rule, we should not unnecessarily multiply disputes. Likewise, Tur rules that if a new need arises after the original acquisition, one that did not exist when they became partners, a buyout demand can be made. Even though later glosses report that Ri HaLevi disagreed, one should not readily depart from Tur, whose ruling functions as a strong resolution of the matter. Maggid Mishneh, cited by Beit Yosef, explains that a partner can sometimes say: I did not stipulate for a buyout at the beginning because I feared that opposition would drive the price upward and make the acquisition more expensive. Similar reasonable explanations must always be considered. Mordechai, at the beginning of HaOmer in Kiddushin, cites the Yerushalmi that one who deliberately raises the price against another buyer can violate the obligation of kindness. I do not presently have the Yerushalmi and its commentaries before me, but in practice commercial custom must also be considered. Merchants commonly bid up prices, even against a non Jew, and perhaps where this has become the accepted method of trade it is not viewed as the same withholding of kindness, particularly when the seller benefits and the other purchaser can obtain the item elsewhere. The authorities likewise distinguish between commonly available and uncommon goods. According to the position attributed by Shach to Ri HaLevi, once one partner sells his share to another person, the same reciprocity should follow as in Rashba's case of an heir who sells. The partner who did not sell may demand a buyout from the new purchaser because he never agreed to become his partner. The purchaser should then have the corresponding right against him. The original lack of a buyout right between the first two partners did not arise from a deficiency in their ownership. It arose because by acquiring jointly without stipulation they are presumed to have accepted the need for mutual patience. The buyout rule itself appears to be an enactment for peaceful coexistence, though its precise Torah or rabbinic source requires study. When two people voluntarily buy together and omit a condition, they accepted mutual tolerance, and each must try to maintain peace. When a new purchaser enters, that assumption changes. Rashba permits the purchaser to say, in substance, that he expected the other side to exercise the buyout option if the partnership became intolerable, and therefore he had no reason to demand a special condition. It would be dangerous and inequitable if only one side possessed the option. The heir, for example, could tolerate the arrangement whenever it suited him and refuse to act, while the purchaser, when unable to tolerate it, would remain trapped. For this reason Rashba makes the right reciprocal. The same reasoning applies when one of two voluntary purchasers later sells his share. If only the nonselling partner had the option, the purchaser could be subjected to an intolerable arrangement without reciprocal relief. Because ordinary mutual tolerance is presumed to be equal, the buyer can say that he relied upon the assumption that if the arrangement became unworkable for him it would likewise be contrary to peace for the other partner, who would then exercise the option. The argument that the purchaser should simply have refrained from buying is no stronger here than in Rashba's case. Nor does the emotional attachment to a purchase materially distinguish the cases, for attachment may exist both when buying from an heir and when buying from a prior purchaser. Even an unlearned purchaser can consult a Torah scholar and discover that the other partner may demand a buyout. I ruled this way in practice together with competent dayanim in a case where the building was in danger of collapse if the parties remained in conflict and where their quarrels had become genuinely dangerous. In such circumstances the peace promoting purpose of the rule is especially strong. If one party claims that the purported sale was not genuine and was made only as a device, the court can impose an acceptance of cherem that no deception is involved, analogous to the laws of a neighboring purchaser.
