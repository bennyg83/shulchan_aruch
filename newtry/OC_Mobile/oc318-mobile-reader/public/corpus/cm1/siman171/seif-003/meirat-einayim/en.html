Enough must reach the partner with the smallest share, etc. If many people share a courtyard or field and one has only a small portion, all may prevent division if that one would receive less than the minimum usable measure.
Besides the four amot in front of the entrances, etc. At the beginning of siman 172 the law of the four amot allocated before each doorway is explained.
Any garden that does not contain enough for sowing half a kav, etc. This is stated in Maggid Mishneh and the Tur writes it below in siman 218. Here he omits it. In the Perishah there I explained that half a kav of sowing is less than a full day's work for a laborer, while the Tur here cites only the outside-Eretz-Yisrael measure, which the Gemara ties to one day's labor. See there.
Until there is enough for one day's plowing for each, etc. A worker is not ordinarily hired for less than a day. If a partner's share requires less than one day's labor, he must still pay a worker a full day and loses money. If his share requires a day and a half, however, they divide even though it is not two full days, because a worker can be hired for such a period. See the Perishah.
The two who have enough for division take their portions, etc. Maharam writes in a responsum that a small storage room can be divisible even if each would not receive four amot, because such a room serves only to store objects like a large chest. Mordechai in chapter HaMokher Peirot writes that a room ten by ten amot gives Reuven, who owns one quarter, a share large enough for division even according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who requires a house portion of four by six amot. The amot are measured by the Talmudic amah of twenty-four thumbbreadths, not by local vernacular measures. The reason the two larger shareholders may take their portions is that ordinarily the two smaller owners prefer not to take separate unusable shares. Jointly they can each use the remainder at scheduled times, or rent it and divide the rent. The owners who have divisible shares are not compelled to remain in partnership merely because the others do. But if one of the smaller owners also asks to take his own share and gives a reason, such as needing it to spread fruit or to attach it to another adjacent field he owns, we listen to him. He can say, 'Why should I be forced to remain in partnership more than you?' The parties must then compromise or all remain partners because the final owner would otherwise be left with less than the minimum measure. Therefore the Mechaber concludes that only where one asks to take his share specifically in order to prevent the others' division do we disregard him.
