(I further found.) I wrote elsewhere, based upon Rashba as cited by Beit Yosef in siman 171, that even though two people who voluntarily became partners may ordinarily lack a forced buyout right, once one sells his share the new purchaser and the remaining partner can each demand a reciprocal buyout, because the remaining partner can certainly make such a demand against the stranger. A case then arose in which one partner sold his share to another person, and the remaining partner himself acted as broker in the sale and even surrendered a small amount of his own benefit in order to facilitate it. At first sight, such conduct might be treated as an admission that he willingly accepts the new purchaser as partner, particularly since he openly demonstrated that he preferred the new purchaser to his former partner. If so, perhaps he could no longer demand a buyout, and consequently the purchaser could not demand one against him. This is not correct. The case is stronger than the law of a neighboring purchaser, where mere silence is not treated as waiver and even an express statement "buy it" together with a purported waiver of the neighbor's right may be ineffective. Here there is an additional reason: the remaining partner may have preferred the new purchaser precisely because the new relationship would create reciprocal buyout rights, whereas no such right existed against the original partner. He may initially have believed he could tolerate the first partnership and only later discovered that he could not, as Rashba explains. Rashba did not rely upon that thought alone to force a buyout against an original partner, especially in monetary law where one cannot easily overturn an acquired arrangement by saying, "I thought I could tolerate it." But it is certainly wrong to infer from a person's earlier consent that he will forever remain satisfied. A person can change his mind, and no one knows another's inner calculations. He may have chosen the second purchaser because the first partner was difficult, or specifically in order to obtain the buyout remedy. This is particularly plausible where the person is known to understand that a sale to a new purchaser creates reciprocal buyout rights, because he recently encountered that rule in another property dispute. In that situation, a buyout can itself restore peace. The reciprocal rule is not refuted by the case of a poor and a wealthy partner discussed in Beit Yosef. Even there, some authorities limit a buyout demand made merely to create a result that the poor party cannot practically match, and the precise missing line in the source does not undermine the principle. If partners own a house that burns and rebuilding requires very large expenses, while one partner is poor and unable to contribute, it appears that all authorities can recognize a buyout demand. Even according to the practice that voluntary partners generally cannot force a buyout, this is a new circumstance that arose after the original agreement. Tur expressly recognizes such newly arisen necessity. There was no reason to require the parties to anticipate and stipulate about a fire, since it is relatively uncommon and there were many uncertainties: perhaps the poor partner would later obtain the means to rebuild, or perhaps the other partner too would lack funds. The same reasoning applies to synagogue seats where, after a fire, substantial reconstruction is necessary and the property cannot sensibly be divided. A seat holder who refuses to contribute his proper share can be placed in a buyout type position. He cannot insist that the congregation build a smaller synagogue, because the partnership in the synagogue was always according to the needs of the holy community. In a time and place where it has become ordinary to build a substantial enclosing wall or other standard structure, even absent direct governmental compulsion, established building custom has halachic force, as the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Batra recognizes local custom regarding the materials of a partition. One cannot insist that public construction be postponed. Public buildings are especially subject to prompt action, as reflected by the permission to build them on Chol HaMoed because jointly owned projects are otherwise prone to delay. The value of synagogue seats should be assessed according to their ordinary market value in that place. Where seat values correspond broadly to a proportionate share of construction expense, the land itself may not require a separate valuation, particularly when the congregation did not originally purchase the land with private funds or when so many generations have passed that no reliable land valuation exists. Yet seats also differ in value by honor and proximity to the Aron Kodesh. A preferred location can be worth far more than its proportional construction cost and land value. That factor must therefore be considered. Perhaps, however, there is an element of "one benefits and the other loses nothing" when the land has no independent commercial value outside its synagogue use, which supports a fair compromise rather than allowing an individual to obstruct the public. Finally, if a partner has announced an intention to demand a buyout but it remains legally uncertain whether his claim will succeed, he may seek to prevent the other partner from beginning an activity whose interruption would itself cause substantial loss, such as producing liquor from flour that will spoil if the process is halted. The other partner may be permitted to proceed only if he accepts upon himself, in the most effective form, the loss that will result should the law later require him to vacate, and only where there is assurance that the activity will not itself create an artificial delay in enforcing the buyout.
