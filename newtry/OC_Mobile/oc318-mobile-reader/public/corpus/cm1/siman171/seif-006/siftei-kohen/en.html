One of the partners, etc. See Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, volume 3, siman 68, concerning the laws of gud o igud.
And the wealthy one says to the poor one, etc. The wealthy partner may say to the poor partner, "Buy my share or I will buy yours." But the poor partner cannot make this demand of the wealthy partner where he is unable to buy the other's share. If the poor partner wishes instead to sell his own share to a third party, the matter depends on the dispute cited below between Rambam and the Rosh. See Tur, seif 29, and Responsa Rabbi Eliyahu ibn Chaim, siman 16.
When he has no money. See Sema, seif katan 15. The Rama's statement that gud o igud is not said where he lacks money means that he cannot demand the arrangement while proposing that the entire property be sold to a third party and that the proceeds then be divided. Rather, the formula applies when one party can say, "Buy my share or sell me yours." This is primary, unlike the understanding in Imrei Binah, page 91b, which leaves the matter in difficulty. The Bach distinguishes the Rosh's responsum where a great loss would result, allowing purchase through others in that circumstance. The Sema's view appears more correct. I discuss this at length elsewhere. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 158.
