The rule "Sell me your share for such and such" is connected to the law of buy or sell. Bava Batra asks, according to the opinion recognizing buy or sell, why one who is half slave and half free must be fully emancipated rather than being resolved through that mechanism. The Gemara answers that there is a possibility of selling the slave portion but no corresponding possibility of buying the free portion. Rashi explains that a free Jew has no monetary price in this sense, since even a Jewish bondsman is sold only for a limited term. This raises a conceptual question under the original Mishnah, where the half slave works one day for the master and one day for himself. On the master's day, does the master have actual ownership of the whole person for that day, like a Canaanite slave, or merely a lien upon his labor? In an indivisible jointly owned house, Ran explains that Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov treats each partner as owning the entire body during his allotted time through bereirah, as though the property belonged to one and afterward to the other. One might therefore suggest that the master similarly has full body ownership on his day.
Upon analysis that cannot be correct for one who is half slave and half free. Gittin says that if an ox gores him on the master's day, damages belong to the master, and on his own day they belong to him. The Gemara asks whether he could likewise marry a maidservant on the master's day and a free Jewish woman on his own day, and answers that prohibitions cannot be divided the way monetary rights can. Rashi explains that he remains half free and half enslaved at all times. Accordingly, even on the master's day he retains his free half and may not marry a maidservant. A Jew can be owned in his body only through the Torah institution of a Jewish bondsman. So long as this person is not sold as such, the master's right over the free half cannot be body ownership. It must be a lien on labor. One cannot apply bereirah and say that on the master's day he is wholly a slave and on his own day wholly free. Emancipation, like bodily sanctity, cannot take effect for a time and then disappear. The Jerusalem Talmud says there is no model of an emancipated slave reverting to enslavement. Thus his legal status must remain half slave and half free continuously. The master's use of the free side is therefore only by way of labor lien, analogous to the Sages' view of partners who have liens in one another's shares.
This analysis raises a difficulty about vows between partners. Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov permits each partner to enter the jointly owned courtyard because bereirah treats the whole courtyard as belonging to the entering partner for his turn. Yet if a konam is bodily sanctity and once it takes effect does not simply fall away, how can the prohibition apply during the other partner's turn and then disappear when the property becomes his? Ran, citing Rabbi Yonah, distinguishes a general konam from a private konam. A general konam does not fall away automatically, but a private konam directed against one individual is weaker and can terminate when the relevant ownership period ends. The proof is from a vow prohibiting benefit only until a festival. Therefore, where partners prohibit benefit from one another, the prohibition is private. During the declarant's ownership period it applies, and when the other partner's period arrives it can end. One might object from the wife's vow, "What I produce is forbidden to you," where the Gemara says konam can displace the husband's lien. There too the prohibition is private. As I explained in siman 117, the reason there is not the rule that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. Rather, the husband has no ownership of the body of her hands, only a labor lien. The konam attaches to the body of her hands and thereby displaces the lien directly. The question of whether the prohibition later falls away is therefore distinct, and the sources can be reconciled.
A further difficulty remains. I wrote in siman 117 that a general konam, because it is redeemable, is not true bodily sanctity but monetary sanctity. Bar Padda's view in Nedarim that a konam does not lapse automatically must, according to Rabbi Yonah, refer specifically to a general konam, because he agrees that a private konam can lapse. Yet if a general konam is only monetary sanctity, Bar Padda elsewhere agrees that monetary sanctity can lapse automatically. This presently requires study. Returning to the half slave and half free person, the master's right on the master's day is therefore only a labor lien over the free half, not body ownership. Although emancipation ordinarily displaces a lien, as where a slave made an apotiki is freed, there the creditor had a lien only in the slave as the master's property, and once the master emancipates him the creditor has nothing. Here, by contrast, the master's lien is directly upon the person's free half itself for purposes of the alternate day arrangement. It therefore resembles the Sages' model of partners in an indivisible courtyard. A practical consequence is that even on the master's day the master may not impose slave labor upon him in the full sense of oppressive slave service. He remains half free at all times, and a Jew may not be worked with oppressive labor. This explains Rashba's comment at the beginning of Shabbat that the command, "You shall work them," does not prevent compelling full emancipation of a half slave, because his free side already removes the master's right to true slave labor. The alternate day arrangement preserves only a monetary labor lien, not the full institution of slavery.
