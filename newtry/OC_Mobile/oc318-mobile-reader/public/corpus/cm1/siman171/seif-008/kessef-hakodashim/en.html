(I further found.) Two partners owned a house with two rooms. They agreed that for three years one partner would have one room for his livelihood through the sale of beverages, while the other would use the second room to sell flour, and neither would sell the other's merchandise. After three years the use of the rooms would switch. Even though no penalty clause was written, neither party may depart from the agreed allocation by selling what the other was given the right to sell. The arrangement is analogous to a lease acquired by a valid kinyan, because each leased the relevant rights to the other only according to the stipulated details. Apart from the prohibition against causing the other loss of expected profit, each partner can object to unauthorized use of a place in which he has contractual rights. If one nevertheless sold merchandise that he had no right to sell, but did not actually take money or property belonging to the other, his conduct is essentially causing loss of expected profit, for which he is exempt in human courts unless the parties created an effective penalty clause. If no penalty was stipulated, and one partner violated the arrangement during his three year period, it requires study whether the other may then retaliate during the following three years by selling in the same manner. Even if the two periods are economically identical, the first violator may perhaps still prevent the second from deliberately violating the agreement going forward, despite being personally exempt from damages for his own past violation. This requires careful study. In editions of Tur printed with novellae and glosses, at the end of Choshen Mishpat 171, a difficulty is cited in the name of Perishah: one cannot invoke "you buy me out or I will buy you out" at a price below the property's value. In my view, the intended meaning may be only that he cannot set a price that is cheap relative to the substantially higher figure at which he could lawfully value the property. Shulchan Aruch permits the party making the demand to set a price materially above ordinary market value. The objection to a "cheap" figure can therefore mean cheap relative to that enhanced valuation, not necessarily below the ordinary market value itself.
