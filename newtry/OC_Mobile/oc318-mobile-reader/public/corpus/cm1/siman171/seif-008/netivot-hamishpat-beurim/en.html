If the property was intended for rental. According to many authorities cited by Sema, seif katan 12, this means that the father had designated it for rental. According to Ri's interpretation cited there, it means that the property is presently suitable and standing for rental. In either approach, if the father intended it for rental, or if it presently stands for rental according to Ri, even a poor heir can compel the wealthy heir to rent it out. The same applies to two wealthy heirs when they cannot conveniently use it together. Conversely, if it was not intended for rental, and according to Ri does not presently stand for rental, then with a house or shop that cannot be used jointly the parties compel one another to alternate periods of use, whether both are wealthy or one is poor. The wealthy party cannot tell the poor one, "Acquire merchandise and sit in the shop," because even if he does, he can still compel alternating use. A bathhouse differs. If it is not intended for rental and there is no privacy damage, one partner cannot compel the other to alternate days. This is true even when one is poor. The poor partner cannot compel the wealthy one by saying, "You have servants, so use it only on certain days," because even if the poor one had servants he still could not compel temporal division. Thus there is no fundamental distinction between two wealthy partners and one wealthy and one poor. Tur's statement about two wealthy people means merely that joint use is inconvenient for them, such as where one owns two thirds, or that both refuse to use it together. The novelty is that one can compel only alternating use where applicable, not rental to outsiders. Sema's contrary implication, that two wealthy people may compel rental even where the father did not make it rental property, is very difficult. It would allow the wealthy partner to do what the poor partner cannot, although the poor partner could reasonably say that even if he acquired the means to use the property he still could not compel rental. Moreover, if a property suitable for joint use is not intended for rental and one cannot compel even temporal division, all the more so one should not be able to compel complete rental that entirely excludes the other's possession. The correct distinction is therefore the one stated above.
Two thirds. See Sema, seif katan 19. Tur expressly gives the reason that otherwise the owner of two thirds loses. At first glance it is difficult to identify the case. If it concerns a bathhouse or olive press suitable for simultaneous use, temporal division is ordinarily unavailable. If it concerns a house that cannot be used jointly, then even equal owners divide by time. Why is the special rationale of loss to the two-thirds owner needed? It appears that where one has a larger share, even a bathhouse is treated as not conveniently usable together because the larger owner would suffer loss by equal daily use. This is also implied by the Gemara. Otherwise the Gemara's question concerning a firstborn and ordinary heir who inherited a slave or unclean animal would not extend to a bathhouse. Necessarily, where one has two thirds, even a bathhouse can be divided by days. The Rosh's responsum cited by Tur likewise says that there is no distinction between a house, bathhouse, or olive press. Any jointly owned item whose simultaneous use is inconvenient is divided by time. Mechaber's statement that in a bathhouse both use it every day refers to the beginning of the seif, where their shares are equal.
