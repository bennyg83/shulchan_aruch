He was dragging it out. The Gemara discusses one who steals a purse on Shabbat. Monetary liability and the Shabbat prohibition must arise simultaneously for the thief to avoid payment by the rule that the more severe liability controls. Rav Ashi establishes the case where the thief joins his hand below three handbreadths and receives the purse into it. Rashi explains that just as the hand is treated as a significant place for Shabbat, it can serve as a place of acquisition. This is especially important according to Rashi's view that ordinary lifting requires three handbreadths. A hand lower than three is not an independent monetary acquisition except through the same legal significance that makes it a place for Shabbat. Therefore the theft acquisition does not precede the Shabbat violation. This helps resolve the separate sugya concerning whether a container's remaining attachment to the original domain prevents the Shabbat transfer. If the thief acquires through the hand below three only because of its Shabbat significance, monetary acquisition cannot occur before the Shabbat act itself. It also explains why Rav Ashi did not establish the hand above three. At that height, ordinary lifting would already acquire whatever entered the hand, and the theft could precede the completed Shabbat transfer. Accordingly, many of the additional technical conditions discussed in the Shabbat passage, such as whether the purse had straps, are needed chiefly under the alternative explanation of acquisition by pulling. Under Rav Ashi's hand-based explanation, the monetary and Shabbat acts are already synchronized.

Tosafot says the novelty is that even if he broke them afterward, he can remain exempt. Baal HaMaor explains that the burglar legally acquired the objects at the time of the burglary for their monetary value, because at that moment the capital liability prevented a monetary obligation from arising. The later rabbinic requirement to return the actual object while it remains intact is only a stringency. Once the object is destroyed, the law returns to its original status and no new monetary debt arises. Ramban disagrees and holds that while the object remains intact it was never actually acquired. Therefore if the burglar later breaks it, a new ordinary damage liability can arise after the capital-liability event has passed. The same dispute applies to theft on Shabbat. The Shach and Derishah suggest that because the Shabbat capital liability remains conceptually hanging over the thief, later destruction is still exempt. That cannot be correct. A person who violated Shabbat and later damages unrelated property certainly pays. The exemption applies only when the monetary liability arises at the same moment as the more severe offense. Thus, under Baal HaMaor's theory, later destruction remains exempt because the object was already acquired in value at the initial protected moment. Under Ramban, later destruction is a new act and is liable. This is the true point of dispute, not the continuing existence of the earlier capital offense.

Even where the thief did not acquire the object while it remained intact, he may still have no Torah duty of restitution arising from the original theft if the original taking occurred at a moment when monetary liability was blocked by the more severe punishment. This distinction helps explain Rashi concerning a gentile who steals less than a perutah. The object itself remains its owner's wherever it is, but the gentile is not necessarily under a separate monetary duty of restitution. Among Jews, Scripture expressly imposed the command to return stolen property. A gentile's prohibition of theft does not necessarily contain that same distinct restitution obligation. Likewise, one who stole on Shabbat may not have acquired the object, but he also may not have incurred the ordinary restitution obligation at the moment of taking because the monetary law was blocked. If the object remains intact, the owner can take his property because it is still his. The burden of physically returning it need not be a separate debt upon the thief. This resolves Tosafot's difficulty with Rashi. Ownership remaining with the original owner does not prove that the wrongdoer is under an affirmative legal duty of restitution. The two questions are distinct.
