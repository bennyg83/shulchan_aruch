I later repurchased it. The Rambam omits Rav Yehudah's statement that if the claimant says, "I later repurchased it from him," he is believed. The Beit Yosef wonders why the Rambam omitted this law. The Lechem Mishneh answers that the rule was too obvious to require mention, because according to the Rif's interpretation the case is one where the orphans themselves held the field for three years and their predecessor held it for one day. In such a case it is self-evident that a claim of purchase is believed. But according to the Rif's reading, if the case already involves three years of possession, what does Rav Yehudah teach by saying that if he claims, "I bought it," he is believed? Did we not already know throughout the entire chapter Chazakat HaBatim that one who possesses land for three years and claims purchase is believed? The statement must teach something additional. The case must therefore be that Reuven came and challenged Shimon, saying that Shimon occupied a field stolen from him, and Reuven brought witnesses that Shimon had stolen it. Beit din removed Shimon and restored the field to Reuven. Reuven then possessed it for three years. Afterward Shimon produced the earlier document in which Reuven had signed as a witness identifying the boundary as Shimon's field, which amounts to an admission of Shimon's ownership. Reuven is nevertheless believed to say that after making that boundary identification he later purchased the field from Shimon. The Ran explains similarly. At first glance this is difficult. Reuven initially came with a claim of robbery. One who says, "you stole this field from me," necessarily says that he did not purchase it from the alleged robber. Once the signed boundary document establishes that Reuven had admitted Shimon's title, how can Reuven later change his story and say, "I then bought it from you"? Has he not already been established as contradicting himself by having claimed that the field was in Shimon's possession by robbery? Rav Yehudah therefore teaches a significant point. Reuven's earlier statement that the field was stolen concerned the field as a whole. He was not required to distinguish one narrow strip within it. That strip may indeed have belonged to Shimon at the time Reuven identified it as Shimon's boundary, and Reuven later purchased that strip from him. Reuven's later explanation is not considered retracting and asserting a new claim, because his original accusation of robbery addressed the field generally and did not necessarily deny Shimon's ownership of that particular strip. This is a substantial novelty and explains the Ran's statement that the challenger had earlier recovered the field, meaning necessarily on a robbery claim. Accordingly, the question regarding the Rambam returns. If Rav Yehudah's ruling is not obvious, why did the Rambam omit it? Tosafot on 30a likewise say that the claimant had possessed the property and that the novelty is that we might have thought he is not believed because he challenged the other person's ownership before the three years of chazakah, and people do not ordinarily purchase from someone whose ownership they have just disputed. This accords with what was explained. It appears that the Rambam understands the case differently. The challenger had already been in possession of the land from earlier times. Then the other person produced the boundary deed on which the challenger had signed as a witness, creating an admission that the strip belonged to the other. The challenger answers that after that admission he later repurchased the strip and then again possessed the field for three years. According to Rashbam's explanation on 39b regarding the requirement to renew a protest every three years, Rashbam rejects the view that the reason is lest the possessor later say, "after your protest I bought it from you." Rashbam holds that once the possessor initially entered without a lawful purchase, he is not later believed merely by continued possession to say that he subsequently acquired it. Applied here, the challenger's possession before he made the boundary identification was effectively an admission that his earlier entry as to the disputed strip had not been lawful. One might therefore think that he could no longer claim that the strip later came into his hands by purchase, because before the boundary deed he had occupied it without valid title. Rav Yehudah teaches that since the field as a whole had genuinely been his from earlier times, his continued possession after the admission can support the claim that he later repurchased this particular strip. This may explain why the Rambam regarded the ruling as subsumed within broader principles and did not state it separately. From this point onward, unfortunately, material is missing from the manuscript until siman 149, seif katan 8, and some sections of siman 150 are likewise missing. Many valuable discussions were contained there. It is a great loss.
