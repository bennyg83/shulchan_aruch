This is how Raavad, cited by the Rosh, explains the incident in Ketubot 109 concerning one who designated another's field as a boundary and then protested. In my view, the case must specifically be one where he made it a boundary marker after he had already protested and recovered the land through beit din. The Gemara's language "he made it a boundary marker and protested" can mean that he had previously protested. He is then believed to say that he repurchased it after identifying it as the other's boundary, once he later establishes even minimal chazakah. But if the sequence were that he first identified it as the other's boundary, then protested and recovered it through beit din only because the possessor and beit din did not know of the document containing that boundary description, and afterward held it for three years, then in my view he could not claim that he later repurchased it. The possessor could say, "I did not know of that document until now." This resembles Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 146, concerning a gift deed deposited with a third party, where later possession is no chazakah because the recipient can say, "I did not know of the deed." Here too the claimant can say, "I did not know of your earlier admission in the boundary description, so why should I have protested after beit din had already awarded the field to me?" Moreover, once it becomes clear that beit din removed the field from the possessor in error, the claimant's later entry is treated as known wrongful possession. Even where the land originally came to a person as a mortgage or rental there is a dispute whether later chazakah helps, as stated in siman 150, seif 3. In this stronger case everyone should agree that it does not.

The underlying incident in which one designated another's property as a boundary has troubled the earlier authorities, particularly why he can later say, "I repurchased it," and why the guardian first needed to claim, "Only one furrow," before saying, "He later repurchased it." It appears to me that the Mishnah concerning one who protests ownership of a field teaches that if he later identifies it as another's boundary, he loses his right. The novelty must be that this is so even if he loudly denies that he meant to admit title and gives an explanation for using the other's name as the boundary. Otherwise there would be little novelty in saying that an express admission is binding. The incident can therefore be explained as follows. The claimant had identified the field as the other's boundary, everyone knew of that identification, and the field thereafter remained with the claimant for a substantial period. The present possessor then took it from him by force. The claimant came to beit din, produced witnesses of the taking, and argued that his earlier boundary description had never been an admission, but had been written for some other reason. He died before judgment. The guardian came before Abaye. Beit din ruled that the deceased's lifetime claim that the boundary description was not an admission was ineffective, because the Mishnah says that one who identifies it as another's boundary loses his right. The guardian could not immediately claim, "He later repurchased it," because the deceased had already asserted in his lifetime that the boundary description was no admission at all and that the field had always been his. Saying that it was always his is equivalent to admitting that he did not later purchase it, just as one who says, "I never borrowed," thereby admits that he never repaid. If he says, "I never sold it," why would he later pay money to buy what he insists he never sold? Therefore the guardian first claimed, "I made only one furrow a boundary." Since the document itself proves an admission only as to the area actually described, even if the deceased became an established denier concerning the larger field, he was not thereby an established denier concerning different property. There is no basis to deprive the heirs of more than the area proven by the document. After he succeeded on that point and the deceased had not ordered more than one furrow given, the guardian could then say that perhaps the deceased later repurchased that furrow, and perhaps even possessed a purchase deed for it. The reason the deceased had been forced in his lifetime to deny that the boundary description was an admission at all is that he knew that the possessor's demand concerned more than the single furrow and was based on the boundary description as applying to the entire field. A claim limited to one furrow was weak, and many poskim reject it when the other party holds the whole field. The deceased therefore denied the admission generally. Tumim likewise writes that the guardian's later claim was not considered a retraction, because the deceased's earlier response addressed the whole field and he had no need to be exact regarding the single furrow. The case should be understood as one in which the claimant possessed the property for three years after making the boundary designation, before the possessor seized it, as Ritva writes.
