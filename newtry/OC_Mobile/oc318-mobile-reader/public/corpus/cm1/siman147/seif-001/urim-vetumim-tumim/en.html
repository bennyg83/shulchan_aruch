We do not listen to him and do not consider the proof he brings, etc. I do not understand. If he brings proof that Reuven stole the field from him, why should it matter that he signed as a witness on a deed by which Reuven sold it to Shimon? Even if he expressly admitted before witnesses and in a deed that the field belonged to Reuven, we would say that the admission was made under duress. Had he not admitted, the robber might have harmed him further. We say an even stronger rule: where a robber sold the field and the victim afterward signed or even made a sale to the purchaser, such conduct is ineffective because it is all done under coercion. The Mishnah states that if one purchased from a violent usurper and then purchased again from the true owner, the latter purchase is void because the owner acted under duress. Many authorities hold this even if the owner accepted warranty. Why then should signing merely as a witness on the robber's sale be stronger than an actual sale deed from the victim to the purchaser? Under coercion one may do whatever is necessary. Even an individualized deed of sale to the purchaser could be void, all the more so merely signing as a witness. One cannot answer that the rule of coercion applies only where the person was already publicly established as a robber regarding this field, whereas here the claimant merely later brings witnesses that Reuven had robbed it from him and therefore the earlier signature is still an effective admission. If so, why did the Rosh labor to distinguish the case where a claimant came and consulted another person, who told him that the second party was more favorable to him, from the ordinary principle that an admission to a robber is not an admission? The Rosh answers that in that case the claimant could have spoken privately to the person rather than publicly. Yet there too the other party had not been publicly established as a robber beforehand; the claimant later proved by witnesses that the land had been robbed from him. Evidently, once witnesses establish that the other person was a robber, he is treated as an oppressor and admissions made under that pressure are ineffective. The present ruling therefore requires study.
