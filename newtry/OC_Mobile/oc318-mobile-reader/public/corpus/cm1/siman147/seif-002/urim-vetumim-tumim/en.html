Guardians or beit din who sold property of orphans in such a case do not lose their rights in the field, etc. See Sema, seif katan 5, who explains at length that the Rama derived this law from Rivash, responsum 244, where the case concerns a debt. Thus the guardians or beit din do not lose the debt-related right, just as any person would not, as explained above in siman 111. But if their claim concerns ownership of the land itself, the Sema says they would indeed lose the right. He therefore struggles to explain the Rama's phrase "in such a case," which sounds as though it refers to the immediately preceding situation. The Sema is correct regarding the content of Rivash responsum 244. Nevertheless, the Rama may have intended something else entirely. Suppose Reuven stole a field from Shimon, and Shimon owned an adjoining field. Shimon died, and his guardians or beit din sold the adjoining field to another person, identifying the stolen field in the boundary description as belonging to Reuven. The orphans do not thereby lose their right to the stolen field, because an admission by their guardians or by beit din does not bind the orphans so as to destroy their property rights. Guardians were appointed to improve the orphans' affairs, not to harm them. In this interpretation, "their rights" means the rights of the orphans. Alternatively, the Rama may rely on the rule that the claimant loses by identifying the field as another's only when he makes the identification in a transaction with a third party, but not where the transaction is with the claimant himself, because had he not acted that way the other party would not have sold to him. Tosafot have the reading "sold to him." Accordingly, if a guardian sells orphan property to a robber who had taken an adjoining field from the orphans, and in the deed the guardian describes the boundary as the field belonging to that robber, the guardian does not thereby destroy the orphans' claim. Just as a person dealing with his own property can say, "had I not made that concession he would not have sold to me," the same applies to a guardian selling for the orphans, because he must take every reasonable step for their benefit. This latter approach is significantly novel, because the guardian is not selling his own property but the orphans'. Yet we may still say the same principle. There is some support from Rivash responsum 252, where a communal collector who made an admission can say, "had I not admitted, he would not have paid what he owed," and the situation is treated like one who acted for his own acquisition. If so, there is no essential difference between a communal collector and a guardian. Thus the Rama's words can be understood in either of these two ways, and need not have been derived from Rivash responsum 244 at all. Indeed, the Darkei Moshe does not cite responsum 244 here. It therefore appears that the Rama wrote the rule from his own reasoning, intending one of the two explanations stated above. Consider this carefully.
