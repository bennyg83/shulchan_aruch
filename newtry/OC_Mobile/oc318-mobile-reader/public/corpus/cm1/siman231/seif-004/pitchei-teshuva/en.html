To make enactments. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 44, concerning a city where three men, Reuven, Shimon, and Levi, were selected as brokers for wine sales pursuant to a governmental regulation that every city appoint brokers by official authorization and oath so that there should be no fraud. The regulation further required the brokers to maintain a protocol recording how many barrels were sold, and that brokerage fees be divided equally among them, even where a merchant dealt with only one broker and that broker personally went to the cellar. Reuven later claimed approximately one hundred fifty gold coins from the vintage season because Shimon and Levi had not given him his share, arguing that they themselves had transacted the business and had seized the portion to which he was entitled under the law of the kingdom. Shimon replied that most towns had never accepted this regulation. Chatam Sofer discusses at length the principle of dina d'malchuta dina and the early authorities, and concludes that the governmental regulation requiring officially selected brokers does not conflict with Torah law. On the contrary, if the matter came before a Jewish court, the court would likewise enact that the number of brokers not exceed what the size of the city and its commerce can sustain, so that each can earn a livelihood and they do not destroy one another's livelihood. This is also the practice throughout the countries, where numbers are fixed for various trades. All the more so with brokers, such regulation is found in many large towns. There is a parallel in the first chapter of Bava Batra regarding the butchers, and in Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 231, and likewise communal enactments intended to prevent one person from cutting off another's livelihood. Maharik, cited also by Rashdam, siman 407, writes that although by strict law anyone subject to the tax of the kingdom cannot be barred by a city from conducting commerce there, if the townspeople see that outsiders are greatly destroying their livelihood they may seek governmental assistance to restrict them even though strict law would not otherwise provide that result. See what I wrote above, siman 156, seif 7, seif katan 17. All the more so here. The regulation that all brokerage fees be divided equally is also proper. Regarding the butchers in Bava Batra and the bakers in the Tosefta, we find no exact Torah precedent for one worker being forced to work on his assigned day while another sits idle and risks hunger. Nevertheless, once they join together, they may impose such arrangements with the approval of the communal scholar. The law of the kingdom is at least as strong for this purpose. Certainly they may establish that all craftsmen function as partners and all profit goes into a common fund. A parallel exists in Torah law, where regarding the priestly service the verse states, "the priest who offers it, it shall be his," yet Chazal received that this means every priest qualified to serve in that watch shares. King David likewise enacted that the share of one who goes down to battle equals the share of one who remains with the baggage. In the present case, moreover, Shimon and Levi themselves accepted Reuven among them on this condition. According to one view in Rama, siman 176, seif 3, even a formal kinyan is unnecessary, and here the governmental rule also applies, concerning which all agree no kinyan is required. It is therefore clear that Shimon and Levi must give Reuven his share of what they already earned and of what they will earn so long as he is not disqualified by the community and judges. See there:
