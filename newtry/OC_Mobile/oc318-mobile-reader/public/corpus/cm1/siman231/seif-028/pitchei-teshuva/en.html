To enact regulations. See Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 44, concerning another city in which three men, Reuven, Shimon, and Levi, were selected to act as brokers in the wine trade pursuant to a governmental order requiring each city to appoint brokers through the local authorities and under oath that they would act without fraud. The brokers were also required to maintain a protocol recording the number of barrels sold, and their brokerage fees were to be divided equally, even where a merchant dealt with only one broker and that broker personally went to the cellar. Reuven later claimed 150 gold coins as his share from the vintage season, which the others had withheld on the ground that they themselves had handled the transactions. Reuven argued that they were taking the share to which he was entitled under dina de-malchuta dina, while Shimon replied that most towns had not accepted this ordinance. Chatam Sofer discusses at length the scope of dina de-malchuta dina and the views of the Rishonim. He concludes that the governmental regulation limiting brokerage to appointed brokers does not conflict with Torah law. On the contrary, if the matter came before a beit din, it would be proper to establish a similar regulation so that the number of brokers not exceed what the city and its commerce can support, thereby allowing each to earn a livelihood without destroying the livelihood of another. This is common practice in many lands, where the number of craftsmen in each trade is regulated, and all the more so with brokers. A similar concept appears in Bava Batra regarding the butchers, and in Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 231 concerning trade regulations intended to prevent one from cutting off another's livelihood. Maharik, cited also by Maharashdam, siman 407, writes that although in strict law any person subject to the taxes of the kingdom may trade in the city, if the townspeople see that outsiders are seriously damaging their livelihood they may petition the ruler to restrict them, even beyond the strict rule of law. Certainly that applies here. The further regulation that all brokerage fees be divided equally is likewise valid. With the butchers in Bava Batra and the bakers in the Tosefta, Torah law does not otherwise require one to work on one day while another must sit idle, yet once they associate on common terms they may enforce their arrangement with the approval of the communal authority. Dina de-malchuta is at least as strong for this purpose. Likewise, a rule that all craftsmen act as partners and that all profit fall into a common fund has an analogue in Torah law: regarding the priestly watch, all kohanim fit to serve in that watch share in the portions, and David HaMelech enacted that the share of one who goes down to battle is like the share of one who remains with the baggage. Here, moreover, Shimon and Levi themselves accepted Reuven into their partnership on this condition. According to one opinion in Rama, siman 176 seif 3, no formal kinyan is required, and where the law of the kingdom also governs, all agree that no kinyan is required. It is therefore clear that Shimon and Levi must give Reuven his share of what they already earned and of future earnings, so long as he has not been disqualified by the community and its judges.

Of all the townspeople. Baer Heitev cites the view that the Sema did not rule this way in the name of Rabbi Moshe Alashkar, siman 49. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, end of siman 117, writes that Rabbi Moshe Alashkar's position is also that of Rivash, siman 399, namely that members of a particular trade require the consent of the communal scholar, whereas the townspeople as a whole do not. Rivash himself anticipated the Shach's objection and answered it. Chatam Sofer appears to rule like Rivash and Rabbi Moshe Alashkar, and in siman 116 he likewise writes that although the Shach disagrees, the Shach's objection can readily be rejected. However, Chut HaMeshulash, responsum 15 at the end of Tashbetz, cites Rivash and disagrees, adducing Rashba to show expressly that even the townspeople may not enact a regulation, where a Torah scholar is appointed over the community, without his consent. VeShav HaKohen, siman 92, likewise challenges Rivash and Rabbi Moshe Alashkar. He notes that their attribution to Rambam and Ramban is not established from Rambam, while Ramban's novellae indicate the opposite; Rashba, responsum 1206, also writes in Ramban's name that the townspeople may not regulate without the local sage. Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot states the same, as do many Rishonim. VeShav HaKohen concludes that since Rav Hai Gaon, Ramban, Rashba, Semag, Rosh, Tur, Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot in the names of Raavan and Rabbi Baruch, all make no distinction between craftsmen and townspeople, Rambam should also be understood that way. Thus Rama and Levush, who speak also of all the townspeople, are correct in halachah and practice. Baer Heitev further writes that even according to the view that a majority can compel the minority, this applies only when all were present for the deliberation. Beit Yosef, siman 13, cites Rashba that one follows the majority only when the majority emerged from the discussion of the entire body. A majority is legally significant only when it arises from the whole and from deliberation with the whole; otherwise, or when some were absent, the act is ineffective. Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 228, similarly cites Rashba regarding a communal tax agreement: the agreement follows what all agreed upon, not what only some agreed upon, and even where the majority controls, that majority must have been formed in the presence of all, like ordinary adjudication. Get Pashut, in his general rules, rule 1, discusses this at length. Kenesset HaGedolah, siman 13, records several distinctions. If the first deliberation did not occur together, even if all later reconvene, the first group that already resolved the matter is considered interested in maintaining its original position, because it is embarrassing to retract and admit that the others corrected its error. Therefore its later numerical majority does not establish a valid majority. This is explained by Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 79, and by other authorities. Maharit writes that even Mahari Beirav agrees to this; his disagreement in the controversy over semichah in Tzfat concerned a matter that required no adjudicatory inquiry, only communal selection of one sage for semichah according to Rambam. Mahara di Boton, however, understood Mahari Beirav as disagreeing with Ralnach. Birkei Yosef concludes that Maharit's reading is primary. Where all subsequently agree, Kehunat Olam records a dispute among later authorities. Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 406, was asked about a community that normally assessed taxes every three years but made an early assessment against one individual while he was absent and greatly increased his assessment. He ruled that an ordinary individual could not object and the communal enactment stood, but if the absent person was one of the appointed officers or communal leaders, he could object, because Rashba requires a majority emerging from the whole. Birkei Yosef notes that Masa Melech challenged this as inconsistent with Maharashdam, siman 398, where a man who had been excluded from the synagogue was later assessed while absent, and Maharashdam ruled in his favor because all taxpayers normally participated in the assessment and he had not been summoned. He could say that had he been present he might have persuaded them to select different assessors. Penei Moshe reconciles the two responsa by distinguishing an individual who was in town but was not summoned, in which case he may object, from one who was out of town, in which case the required majority is the majority of those present, unless he is a communal officer whose absence always prevents action. Birkei Yosef prefers another reconciliation: the rule requiring a majority from the whole applies to a defined body of officers and leaders. In siman 406 the absent person was only an ordinary resident, so he was bound by the leaders' decision, while in siman 398 that community's custom was for all taxpayers to constitute the deliberative body. Thus every taxpayer there had the status of an appointed member, and omission of one was like omission of a communal officer elsewhere. See also what was written above, siman 163 seif 1.
