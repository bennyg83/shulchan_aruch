The city. The Rosh writes this in the chapter HaShutafin, explaining that craftsmen are considered the residents of the city with respect to matters of their trade. See there. It appears that he needed to write this, and the ruling is correct and compelled by the Gemara. The Gemara challenges Rava from the teaching that the residents of a city may establish their measures and prices, and answers that the case lacks an important person. This shows that even the residents of a city may not enact such regulations without him. Moreover, if city residents had greater authority, the Gemara's challenge would have no force. It therefore appears correct to rule as the Rama writes. The Sema cites Responsa R. Moshe Alashkar, siman 49, differently, but in my view the Rama's ruling is compelling. Bnei Chayya discusses at great length the laws governing communal ordinances and enactments of a beit din made to safeguard the law or for the benefit of the town. He gathers many responsa and rulings of early and later authorities. Since they are numerous, I record only several points. Even according to the opinion that a majority may compel the minority, this applies only when all were present together, as ruled in Mishpat Tzedek, part 2, simanim 4, 35, and 66; see also Masa Melech, part 5, gate 1, judgment 1, law 1, and Responsa R. Betzalel Ashkenazi, siman 25. Once the residents of a city have already upheld and accepted an ordinance, they can no longer protest it, as the Rama rules in Responsa, siman 73. If the matter is intended to remove danger or prevent loss, the majority may compel the minority according to all opinions, as Mahari ibn Lev, part 1, siman 149, rules, and this also appears from Mabit, part 1, siman 237. Even according to the opinion that the majority may compel the minority where one group profits and another loses, those who were absent from the city at the time of the agreement are not included, according to Mahari ibn Lev, part 1, page 149. Mishpat Tzedek, part 2, simanim 4 and 35, however, writes that even minority members who were absent must observe what the majority agreed, and such is the custom. To alter an established custom, even a single individual may protest, as stated in Masa Melech, part 2, siman 2, and part 5, judgment 2. A case of gain to one and loss to another means that at the present time one gains and the other loses, even though it is possible that later the situation will reverse. Mabit, part 1, siman 307, and part 2, siman 77, so rule; Responsa D.R., siman 63, disputes this. Whatever members of a trade agreed upon and stipulated is binding even without a kinyan, and none of them may retract. So rule Maharik, shoresh 182, and Rashdam, siman 259. See also Rashach, part 1, siman 155, and what is written in siman 176 in the Beit Yosef gloss, where several rabbis concur. The rule that a majority can prevail over a minority applies specifically where all were assembled together, debated the matter, and reached a decision to exempt or obligate. If they were not all gathered in one assembly, but the attendant went to each person separately, a majority does not prevail over the minority. Responsa Rashach, part 2, siman 109.
