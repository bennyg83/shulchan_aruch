With goods necessary for human sustenance, etc. In the Perishah and Derishah I wrote and proved that according to Rambam and the Tur this law has three categories. With primary food staples analogous to wine, oil, and fine flour, a merchant may profit up to one sixth and no more. With items having no food function at all, such as costus and frankincense, he may profit as much as he wishes, even many times the principal. With items used in preparing food, such as cumin, spices, and the like, he may profit up to double but no more. This is unlike Beit Yosef's explanation. See there.
His labor and all his expenses, etc. Meaning, all these expenses are added to the principal, and he may earn one sixth on the entire resulting amount in addition to recovering his principal. This yields more than merely earning one sixth on the original purchase price. It also appears that specifically one who sells in small quantities may include separate compensation for his labor in addition to the permitted profit. Other merchants do not add labor in the same way, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 227 at the end of seif 28 regarding trustworthy cost-plus commerce. See the Perishah.
This one alone is not obligated, etc. Meaning, where there is no beit din capable of protesting and the other sellers pay no attention to the Sages' rule that profit on staples should not exceed one sixth, even one who fears the word of Hashem need not alone observe that ceiling. The Tur explains: once his own produce is sold out, the others will sell at the higher price and he alone will have suffered the loss. We do not cause him alone an active loss. There is no need to explain that the other sellers are non-Jews. See the end of siman 156, where the Tur and Rama write a similar principle concerning giving priority to the merchandise of a Torah scholar.
