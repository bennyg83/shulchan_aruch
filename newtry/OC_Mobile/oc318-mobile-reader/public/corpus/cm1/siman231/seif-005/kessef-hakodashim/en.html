Seif 27. The townspeople may fix prices and impose penalties. I previously wrote concerning the rule at the end of siman 231 that townspeople may enforce their agreed commercial regulations, and whether a majority is equivalent to the whole community. My recollection is that I was shown responsa supporting the rule that the majority is sufficient. Reason likewise suggests that communal enactments fall under 'follow the majority.' Maharlbach, in his discussion of semichah, may have denied that a mere majority suffices there because true semichah was understood as a continuation of the spirit transmitted from Moshe Rabbeinu, a unique institution for which the Torah supplied no alternative mechanism. The Rambam's view that agreement of the sages of Eretz Yisrael can renew semichah may rest on the special status of the collective Torah authority of Eretz Yisrael, derived from verses concerning ascent to the chosen place and 'from Zion shall Torah go forth.' Ordinary communal regulation is different. The power of hefker beit din hefker and the Torah's rule of majority allow the residents of a city, functioning with communal judicial authority, to enforce commercial rules, which is why the Sages required association with a chaver ir. Standard guild regulations and communal enactments could never function if every single person had to be physically present and consenting. The precise geographic boundary of 'the city' requires study: whether it follows contiguous settlement, Shabbat boundaries, seventy cubits and a fraction, the ordinary municipal name, or even named suburbs commonly treated as part of the city. Similar questions may arise in the priority of the poor of one's city and in the rule that there is no yichud concern when a woman's husband is 'in the city.' For yichud, however, practical likelihood that the husband might suddenly arrive may be the real criterion, so even a nearby house may not count if he is known to be occupied there for an extended time, while a very large city with separately named districts may present the opposite problem. Where townspeople cannot all assemble, they may issue a public notice that anyone who fails to appear by a fixed date or authorize a representative forfeits his right to object, so that the enactment can proceed as if he consented. The precedent in Ezra includes stronger confiscatory measures requiring beit din authority, but simple loss of the right to obstruct may follow naturally once proper notice has been given; otherwise communal regulation would often be impossible. According to the Shach, even unanimous agreement may require a chaver ir, but the presence of communal benefit is enough once the residents properly act through that authority. A fixed levy attached to ritual slaughter, as practiced in some places, may be easier than direct confiscation because no one is forced to buy meat. Still, since it can indirectly cause financial loss, it should be enacted with communal authority. A levy that falls according to family size rather than wealth may burden poor large families and should not be imposed without the consent of a majority together with the chaver ir. Torah scholars whose Torah is their occupation should not simply be included in ordinary communal burdens where halachah exempts them. If the residents cannot agree on one plan, majority rule may decide. If counting all views is burdensome, they may use a ballot to choose representatives who will formulate a compromise, similar to relying on three expert appraisers. With the community's authorization this also carries the force of beit din. Residents, acting with communal authority, may regulate visiting merchants who attend the local market and require a fixed contribution for the city's poor, even if this might reduce what the visitors give to the poor of their own towns. Since local residents could in some circumstances restrict outside merchants from bringing goods at all except where they benefit local consumers, they can attach reasonable communal conditions for the poor. However, where a proposed communal regulation creates a real risk of danger, Heaven forbid, the majority has no authority to impose it, and even one person may be entitled to object. The same may be true where the enactment creates a serious spiritual stumbling block or an improper test of temptation. The chaver ir must therefore exercise careful judgment to structure communal regulations so as to avoid both physical and spiritual harm.
