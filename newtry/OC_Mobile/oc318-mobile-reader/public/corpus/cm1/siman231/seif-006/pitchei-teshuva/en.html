See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, at the end of siman 117, who writes that Responsa Rivash, siman 399, says the same as Maharam Alshakar, and Kesef Mishneh cites it: specifically members of a trade require the consent of the city's scholar, but the townspeople as a whole do not. Rivash himself already anticipated Shach's objection and answered it well. Chatam Sofer appears to rule like Rivash and Maharam Alshakar. At the end of siman 116 he likewise writes that although Shach disagrees, his objection can readily be answered. However, in Responsa Chut HaMeshulash appended to Tashbetz, question 15, I saw the words of Rivash cited and rejected. He brings a responsum of Rashba explicitly ruling otherwise, namely that even the townspeople cannot make an enactment where there is a Torah scholar appointed over the community unless that scholar agrees. See there. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 92, likewise discusses the matter at length and questions Rivash and Maharam Alshakar. Their attribution to Rambam and Ramban is unconvincing: Rambam proves nothing, while Ramban in his novellae appears explicitly to hold the opposite. Responsa Rashba, siman 1206, likewise cites Ramban as holding that even townspeople cannot enact regulations without the city's scholar. He also cites Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot, gate 60, who rules likewise, and shows that many early authorities agree. He concludes that since Rav Hai Gaon, Ramban, Rashba, Semag, Rosh, Tur, Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of Raavan, and Rabbi Baruch all hold that there is no distinction between members of a craft and the townspeople, we should certainly understand Rambam the same way. Otherwise, how could none of these authorities have noted Rambam as disagreeing? It is therefore beyond doubt that this is the practical halachah, and the wording of Rama and Levush, "or all the townspeople," is clear and correct in practice. See there. Baer Heitev further writes that even according to the view that the majority can compel the minority, this is only when everyone was present, and similarly that majority rule over the minority applies only when all were present at one meeting. This is explicit in Beit Yosef above, siman 13, citing Responsa Rashba, who writes that we follow a majority only where the majority emerged from discussion among all. A majority everywhere means a majority arising from the entire body after discussion with the entire body. Otherwise, or in the absence of some, their action has no effect. Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, siman 228, likewise cites Responsa Rashba, part 3, siman 304, concerning a communal agreement about taxation, that the agreement follows what all agreed upon and not what only some agreed upon. Even where majority rule applies, the majority's agreement is effective only if reached in the presence of all, like ordinary judicial decisions. See there. See Get Pashut at the end, general rule 1, on this subject. See also Knesset HaGedolah, siman 13, for several distinctions. In Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 24, he writes that if the discussion was not held together, then even if afterward all reconvene and discuss the matter, while those who originally agreed outside the others' presence still maintain their earlier view, their majority does not establish a valid decision. They are interested parties, because they are embarrassed to retract and appear to have been corrected by the others, and will seek every argument to preserve their first position. This is clear from Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 79. See what I wrote above, siman 17, seif 5, seif katan 10, and siman 33, seif 2, seif katan 5. Ralbach, in the ruling concerning semichah, page 278, column 4, Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 32, Mishpat Tzedek, part 2, siman 4, and Mahara Sasson, siman 71, all write similarly. Maharit, siman 79, writes that even Mahari Beirav agrees with this. He disagreed only in his particular case, concerning the semichah in which the rabbis of Tzefat agreed to ordain one sage according to Rambam, because that matter was not a halachic ruling requiring discussion and investigation but merely a collective choice of whom to ordain. Mahara Di Boton, in manuscript responsum siman 84, however, writes that Mahari Beirav disagreed with Ralbach. Birkei Yosef there, letter 5, writes that Maharit is the principal view and that Mahari Beirav himself clearly agrees, as appears from his responsa. Where all later agree, however, Responsa Kehunat Olam, at the end of siman 12, says this remains a dispute among later authorities. See what I wrote above, siman 163, seif 1, seif katan 1. See Responsa Rashdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 406, concerning a community whose custom was to make a tax assessment every three years. On one occasion they assessed an individual early, substantially increasing his assessment while he was absent from the city, and he later objected. Rashdam ruled that the individual cannot protest and the communal action stands, unless the objecting person was one of the appointed officials or community leaders. In that case he could object, based on Rashba's rule that the majority must emerge from all and that what the community did without one of its appointed members is ineffective. But if he was merely an ordinary individual, the community's act binds him. Birkei Yosef above, siman 13, letter 7, cites Masa Melech as questioning this because Rashdam himself appears to rule otherwise in siman 398. There Reuven had been opposed by many members of his community, who barred him from entering the synagogue, and they later wished to assess him for taxes. He argued, "What do I have to do with you, when you have rejected me?" Rashdam ruled in Reuven's favor and wrote, among other reasons, that Reuven was one of the taxpayers and all taxpayers had gathered to make the assessment, but he had not been summoned. He could say that had he been present, the assessors selected might have been different because he could have advanced reasons everyone would accept. Rashdam cites the principle that majority means a majority drawn from all, as taught by the Rosh, Rashba, and Ralbach. Thus in siman 398 he relied on that rule for a single absent individual, while in siman 406 he limited it to an official or community leader. Responsa Pnei Moshe, part 2, siman 110, reconciles the two by saying that even an ordinary individual may object when an act was done in his absence because there was no majority drawn from all, but this applies only where the person was in the city and was not invited, as in siman 398. If he was outside the city, majority is measured from those present. Siman 406 concerns such an absent person, and therefore Rashdam distinguishes only an official or community leader, whose absence can still invalidate the act. Birkei Yosef does not find this reconciliation persuasive from the wording of Rashdam in siman 406. He instead suggests that Rashdam always understood the requirement of a majority drawn from all to apply only to selected representatives, such as officials and community leaders, which is the simple sense of siman 406 and also fits his rulings in Yoreh Deah, simanim 68 and 82. Therefore, in siman 406, if the objector is merely an ordinary individual, even if he was in the city, he must obey whatever the community leaders agreed. This assumes the common communal structure in which only heads of the community participate in such decisions. Siman 398, by contrast, concerned a community whose custom was that all taxpayers attend and are counted, as is explicit in Rashdam's words that "all gathered to make the assessment and he was not called." Since by local custom all taxpayers were members of the deciding body, every taxpayer was effectively an appointed participant. The absent man's position was therefore like that of an absent official elsewhere, and Rashdam ruled in his favor. See there, and see what I wrote further above, siman 163, seif 1, seif katan 1:
