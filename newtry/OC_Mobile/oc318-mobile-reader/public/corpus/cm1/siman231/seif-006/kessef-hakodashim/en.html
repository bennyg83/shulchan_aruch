Artisans are permitted to enact regulations. It is customary for members of a trade to form an association under the supervision of the city authority, and such regulations have force as explained in Choshen Mishpat siman 231. It requires study whether their agreement is effective only against artisans of their own city, or also against artisans from outside the city who come there to practice that trade. There are situations, as explained in siman 156, in which the townspeople may not prevent an outsider from practicing there, for example where he performs better work or charges less. Perhaps an agreement among the local artisans cannot deprive the other townspeople of their right to employ such outsiders. Although the Shach, based on the Rosh, writes that members of a trade are like all the townspeople, this may mean only that their regulation is binding upon members of that trade just as a communal enactment is binding upon the townspeople generally. It does not necessarily mean that the artisans may bind townspeople who are not members of the trade. Likewise, although the Beit Yosef indicates that an artisans' enactment may stand even when it indirectly causes loss to the entire town, that may be only where the artisans regulate their own conduct, such as assigning each member his own day to work. There the substance of the regulation concerns their own labor. We have not found that they may, without the consent of the townspeople, forbid townspeople to give work to an outsider. It is possible that the Torah's expression concerning the open land of the cities of refuge, "for all their living needs," alludes to the authority of a city's inhabitants over arrangements affecting livelihood. Yet if so, the city boundary and its livelihood concern all the townspeople. The artisans could therefore bind everyone only if they themselves constituted a majority of the city, according to the view that a majority is treated as the whole for enactments of the kind discussed in siman 231. Where the nonartisan townspeople are the majority, from where would the artisans derive authority to override them concerning something that is not the artisans' own act? We find that the poor of one's city have priority over the poor of another city, but we do not find that townspeople are forbidden to give needed work to artisans from elsewhere. Siman 156 implies the contrary, for a slight saving or improvement in workmanship can justify employing an outsider. One could argue in the opposite direction. If a trade itself is the livelihood of those who practice it, as Chazal say, "you have cut off his livelihood," then with respect to that trade the artisans themselves may constitute the relevant community, even if the service is one necessary to human life such as milling or baking. Others can obtain the service elsewhere, whereas for those who earn from the trade it is their livelihood. This would explain why members of a trade may enact regulations even against the wishes of most townspeople. Still, this does not prove that they may prevent townspeople who are not members of the trade from choosing outsiders to perform the work. The enactment governs the artisans' livelihood, not the conduct of persons whose livelihood does not consist of that trade. This requires further study. Perhaps, however, any person who actually practices the trade within the city falls under the local enactment, even if he comes from elsewhere, because while he practices there the trade is his livelihood within the place governed by the enactment. Just as persons born after an enactment was adopted are bound by it, perhaps visitors who later come to practice the trade are also included. Yet one may distinguish an outsider who did not come to settle permanently, since he has no fixed share in the local livelihood. A case came before me concerning an artisan who fled from a country where there was conscription for war. The local artisans would not permit him to work. I mediated that he could work until a specified time, but afterward they complained that he continued. No penalty or kinyan sudar had been made at the compromise. At first I assumed that their original enactment bound him as well, but I now have doubts and this requires further study. The same uncertainty affects whether visiting artisans count toward the majority needed to enact a regulation. If visitors are not included in the enactment, they certainly do not count. Even if they count for a temporary enactment applying only while they remain in the city, it is doubtful that they can decide a permanent enactment extending long after they leave. Although an enactment validly adopted by a majority remains binding even if later the permanent artisans who oppose it become the majority, that does not prove that temporary visitors can be counted toward the original majority for a long term regulation, since from the outset it is known that they will depart. It also requires study whether artisans residing in villages near the city are treated as city artisans, either so that the regulation binds them or so that they count toward the majority. If the authority derived from "for all their living needs," perhaps those within the Shabbat boundary would count as townspeople. If the authority instead rests on enactments of Chazal under "you shall not turn aside," perhaps only those within the city's inhabited extension count, or perhaps villagers who are attached to the city for communal needs, such as the High Holy Days, are treated as townspeople for these regulations as well. A further question concerns overlapping trades. Where tailors and furriers or leather workers both perform certain kinds of sewing, perhaps they remain distinct trades and even a majority of tailors cannot regulate the furriers. Or perhaps, with respect to work actually performed by both groups, they constitute one trade and the majority governs. Thus, if they enact that no one encroach upon another's sphere, the tailors might be able to prohibit the furriers from sewing garments upon hides. It may be necessary to examine whether leather work alone provides the furriers with a sufficient livelihood, while the tailors can support themselves without sewing garments upon hides. Where the latter is so and the former is not, the city authority should compromise between them in a manner that properly preserves everyone's livelihood. This requires further study.
