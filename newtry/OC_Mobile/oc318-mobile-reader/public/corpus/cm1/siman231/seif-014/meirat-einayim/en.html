In a place where the custom is to tip the scale, etc. The Tur likewise places this after the preceding laws governing the scale. In the Perishah I wrote that it does not refer to all the weights discussed above. It is an independent Mishnah dealing specifically with a shopkeeper selling food and similar goods. There it is also explicit that the shopkeeper's scale must tip by one tefach. The Levush writes: "he must tip it one tefach, which is a little more than the ordinary tipping." I do not understand what he means. Why call it "a little more"? One tefach is substantially more. In a place where they do not tip one tefach, they weigh exactly balanced and then add an additional amount, as the Tur and Mechaber explain immediately below. If his point is that exact balance cannot be mathematically perfect, that cuts both ways; sometimes the seller gives a little less just as sometimes a little more.
He must tip it one tefach. Rashbam writes that in his opinion for less than one litra a full tefach of tipping is not required, because a tefach would be an excessive addition for a light item. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this.
And he must add, etc. Meaning, in a place where the custom is to weigh with the pans level, the seller must add an amount corresponding to the tipping given in other places.
And eight parts out of four hundred for dry goods. With liquids, some of the sold substance clings to the seller's measuring vessel and remains behind. Therefore the Sages required a larger addition for liquids. Chazal derive all this from the apparently superfluous word "just" in "You shall have a full and just measure": make it just from your own property and give the purchaser extra.
And one tipping for all of them. In the Perishah I quoted Rashbam, who writes that even if a single tipping of one tefach on the entire combined weight and separate tippings on each smaller weighing might appear proportionally similar, in practice the amount is not equal. The heavier the total load, the more substance is needed to tip the scale a full tefach. Nevertheless, because exact measurement is impossible, weighing ten separate times generally produces more cumulative excess than weighing the entire amount once. My meaning there is that one can make one measurement more exact than ten separate measurements, because it is impossible to achieve the identical degree of precision on all ten occasions.
And he gives one quarter litra together with the meat. In the Perishah I quoted Rashbam that this applies specifically where the local custom is not to tip the scale. Where tipping is customary, one should not do this because it would harm the seller. If a quarter-litra weight is placed on the meat side of the scale, the seller would have to provide tipping not only against the one-litra weight but also against the extra quarter-litra placed opposite it. The tipping itself is entirely meat, not part of the added metal weight, so the meat would need to outweigh the entire litra placed on the other pan plus the additional quarter.
Lest he drop it. Meaning, the seller might deliberately drop it deceptively. This reason is Rambam's wording in chapter 8 of Hilchot Geneivah. See there.
