The townspeople are permitted. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 81, who mentions the dispute among the great early authorities whether this means specifically all the townspeople together or whether the majority can compel the minority. See Baer Heitev, seif katan 27, concerning this. He writes that the dispute can be reconciled. With enactments that do not conflict at all with Torah law, such as household management, conduct of spouses, customs of meals and matchmaking, and all the more so enactments that serve as a safeguard for Torah and mitzvot, an individual or a few individuals cannot prevent an enactment made by the majority of the community or by officials appointed by the majority. But where the enactment runs against ordinary legal rules, such as imposing taxes on property outside the city when the law would otherwise exempt it, or fixing a maximum tax even for one possessing many tens of thousands when ordinary law would require more, then even if the public purpose is proper and the reasoning sound, because the enactment departs from the law an individual may object. He concludes that this appears to reconcile the words of the sages, and even if they did not intend such a distinction and disagreed in all cases, logic supports it and it appears consistent with the later poskim. He further writes that even where an individual can initially object to the majority, that applies when the enactment is first proposed or if he protests immediately upon its promulgation. If he remained silent at the outset and the community already followed the practice two or three times without objection, and only afterward individuals protested, their initial silence is treated as consent.
