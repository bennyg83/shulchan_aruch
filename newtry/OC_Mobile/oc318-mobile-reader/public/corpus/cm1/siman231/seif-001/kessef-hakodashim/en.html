One who measures or weighs short. I have already warned and publicized here with the greatest force the obligation of accurate measures and weights. The local official recently ordered them to use small quarter measures, one fifth of the local quart. It is obvious that one should not rely on such an order to be lenient merely because an official issued it. As I have explained elsewhere, dina d'malchusa applies to regulations issued by the sovereign whose coin circulates in the land, or by an agent expressly empowered over such details, not every local official. The Sema, seif katan 1, explains the severity of false measures and weights because people rely on the measurer's honesty. This differs from ordinary commercial mistakes, where one can sometimes say that the mistaken party failed to protect himself. With ordinary mistakes, the person who gave misleading information may not have caused the damage directly, and one who voluntarily relies without proper caution may be considered to have misled himself. Ona'ah itself is prohibited only in the forms and measures fixed by Torah law, because ordinary bargaining inevitably includes each side pressing for its own advantage. Measures and weights are different. The entire functioning of commerce requires buyers to rely upon the honesty of the person measuring and weighing. Exact verification demands specialized tools, repeated comparisons, accurate calibration of pans, cords, beams, and balances, and corresponding expertise with dry and liquid measures. It is impossible for every person to carry calibrated scales and measuring devices in every transaction. The Torah therefore placed the prohibition directly upon corrupt measurement and weighing, because deceit here destroys the foundation of commercial society and, once discovered, can also cause chillul Hashem. Ordinary pricing errors do not have the same character. Regarding the local quarter measures, I spoke with the townspeople and, with God's help, the matter will be corrected so that only honest measures bearing their customary names are used. Glass vessels may be sold by price rather than by pretending that a particular vessel constitutes a fixed measure. I later heard that there are local differences in the size of another customary measure because this town lies between regions using larger and smaller standards. If the seller specifies that the sale is according to the measure used in a particular place, this can justify the practice, because the parties openly acknowledge the variation and choose that standard. Where no single custom is fixed, the stated name may function like selling by approximate measure, more or less, rather than falsely representing a precise fixed quantity. This provides room to justify practices in many Jewish communities where the parties know and expressly or implicitly accept the local variation. Still, the laws of measures and weights are fundamentally monetary law. They should not be confused with the incomparably more severe realm of forbidden relationships, which damages the soul itself. The severity of false measures stems from the needs of orderly society and from reliance between people. In ordinary ona'ah, matters of unexpressed intention do not become legally effective merely because one party internally expected more.
