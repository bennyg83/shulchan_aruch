Seif 2. Beit din are obligated. Choshen Mishpat siman 231 states that beit din must appoint officials to ensure honest weights and measures. In our present condition, where Jewish communal authorities no longer possess coercive power to enforce judgments and our practical authority is largely limited to instruction when people ask how to conduct themselves in matters of prohibition and permission, this obligation cannot be implemented in its former manner. We presently lack a stable communal structure capable of appointing inspectors with genuine authority over the administration of a city. Even where communities enact agreements and local regulations, experience shows that such arrangements are often unstable because persons unwilling to accept discipline appeal to outside governmental authorities and use influence or bribery to undermine communal enforcement. Thus those who have tried to enact durable communal regulations have repeatedly found that they cannot impose corrective measures against people who resist them. For this reason it is presently impossible in many places to appoint effective inspectors over weights, measures, price gouging, and commercial conduct as contemplated in siman 231. If a proper communal order were reestablished among Jewish leaders and scholars, with recognized power to enact regulations suited to the generation and to enforce them gradually and lawfully, even ultimately through confiscatory powers like those described in Ezra, the communal system could again be repaired in a proper manner. The governmental authorities themselves have seen how disorder in Jewish communal leadership can produce wider disorder. One source of such disruption is the appointment of chief rabbis through bribery or political influence, whereas Torah law forbids appointing even a person learned in many disciplines if he is not genuinely qualified in Torah law and halachic analysis. Such appointments can generate severe conflict and financial loss. Chavot Yair and Urim VeTumim discuss the separate question of Jews giving money to non-Jewish judges. Since the descendants of Noach are commanded to maintain systems of justice, and bribery corrupts judgment, giving a bribe may raise lifnei iver concerns. Chavot Yair records that a wise king asked a great rabbi this question and several answers were offered. One is that because a person does not see his own liability and naturally regards his own position as just, he may view the payment not as inducing injustice but as counteracting the judge's preexisting bias so that the result will accord with what he sincerely believes to be the truth. For Bnei Noach, whose judicial obligations follow basic rational justice, this reasoning may bear on the giver's own perception. None of this justifies bribery given merely to secure an improper rabbinic appointment against Torah law and against qualified scholars. Hashem should forgive such conduct. In one practical case, a written agreement required use of an imperial standard measure. The purchaser brought a sealed measure from the court in his own city, and the parties measured the goods with it. After the goods had been loaded, the sellers produced another official measure bearing the imperial seal from Lemberg, under which an additional specified quantity was due to them. If the purchaser's measure lacked the imperial seal and bore only a local court seal, while the contract expressly required the imperial standard, the sellers may clearly insist on the later official measure. Even if both measures bore imperial seals but differed and one could not determine which was correct, the initial measurement does not necessarily bind the sellers. They had relied on the purchaser's representation and on the presumption that the sovereign maintains uniform measures throughout the realm. Once the discrepancy was discovered, the initial measurement can be treated as a mistake, which is always reversible. If the ambiguity existed from the outset, the holder of the document has the weaker hand and the sellers may have grounds to use the smaller local measure or otherwise resist extraction. At the least, one should not prevent them from retaining the excess indicated by the larger measure. I later heard that after the written contract the parties renegotiated the price because the quality of the liquor had not been fully checked, and this later agreement occurred after they had already accepted measurement by the larger standard. Even if they did not know there was a legal issue at that time, the purchaser can argue that he bought only on the assumption that the larger measure would govern. Since the purchaser is now absent and only his agent is present, I instructed the local communal leader to prevent the sellers from taking additional liquor until the dispute can be adjudicated.
