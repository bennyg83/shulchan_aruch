The townspeople are authorized. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 81, discusses the dispute among early authorities whether communal enactments require unanimity or whether a majority may compel a minority. He proposes reconciling the views. For regulations that are not themselves determinations of Torah law, such as household administration, communal conduct at meals and celebrations, and certainly measures serving as safeguards for Torah and mitzvot, an individual or small group cannot obstruct an enactment adopted by the majority or by representatives appointed by the majority. By contrast, where the proposed regulation contradicts substantive Torah law, such as a tax rule that would allocate liability contrary to the halachic rules or impose an arbitrary cap regardless of wealth, even a well-intentioned majority cannot deprive an individual of his Torah-law right, and an individual may object. Chavot Yair writes that this distinction best reconciles the earlier authorities and accords with later poskim. He adds that even where an individual could have objected, the objection must be made when the enactment is adopted or promptly when it becomes known. If people remained silent and the community followed the practice repeatedly without protest, their initial silence is treated as consent and they cannot later undo the established regulation.
