Seif 2. The heirs do not inherit until they bring proof that the person from whom they inherit has died; but if they merely heard that he died, etc. Note: I was told in the name of the author of Sha'agat Aryeh, and the same appears in Rivash and in Noda BiYehuda, Mahadura Kama, Even HaEzer, that this rule is explicit in Ketubot 107. The Gemara explains that the case is where they heard through one witness that the husband died. An abandoned wife may remarry on the testimony of one witness and therefore receives maintenance, but sons and daughters may not descend to the estate on the basis of one witness and therefore do not receive maintenance from it. This seems to state explicitly that heirs do not take possession of the estate on the basis of a single witness. In my view, however, this is not a proof. The passage there answers Shmuel, who holds that maintenance is not awarded to a married woman whose husband is absent. According to Rav, who does award such maintenance, it may well be that heirs can descend to the estate on the testimony of one witness. The matter can be explained more fully. Rav and Shmuel may follow their general approaches. The concern that the husband gave her bundles of money in advance is uncommon. Rav, who holds that in monetary matters we follow the majority, does not fear such an uncommon possibility; Shmuel, who does not follow the majority in monetary law, does. Although we generally rule like Shmuel that we do not follow a majority to extract money, we nevertheless rule that maintenance is awarded to an absent man's wife. This necessarily means that giving her money in advance is so uncommon that even in monetary law we follow the overwhelming probability and disregard it. So too, when one witness says the husband died, the wife carefully investigates before remarrying, and because she does so we treat his death as established. Shmuel, who is willing to consider even an uncommon monetary possibility, can still say that one witness is insufficient for heirs to enter the estate. But according to our ruling that an extremely uncommon possibility is disregarded even in monetary law, one witness together with the presumption that the wife carefully investigates should suffice for the heirs as well, since the possibility that he is still alive is then exceedingly remote. This appears correct to me, with God's help.
