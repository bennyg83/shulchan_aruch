All heirs inherit through chazakah. Beit Yosef cites Rivash, who holds that if people heard from the decedent that someone was his relative, such as his brother, at a time when the decedent still had children, and those children later died, the statement does not establish the relative as heir. Tashbetz disagrees. He argues that otherwise a statement has effect only when made specifically for inheritance purposes, while regarding a firstborn the father is believed even in ordinary conversation. Ketzot HaChoshen supports Rivash and distinguishes firstborn recognition, which rests on the special verse "he shall recognize," from ordinary relatives, where credibility rests only on migo. He therefore requires intent to establish inheritance. In my view this distinction is unsound. If casual conversational statements were treated as unreliable because a person may speak inaccurately about matters not presently relevant, then even the firstborn verse should not help. One would first have to know the father actually meant his words. Necessarily, when a person says in ordinary conversation, "This is my relative," we do not dismiss it as careless speech. We treat it as deliberate and truthful absent contrary reason. The same should therefore apply to ordinary kinship. The speaker can be believed through the relevant migo even though the statement was made casually rather than in a formal inheritance context.
