Rivash's discussion of reciprocal testamentary instruments similarly should be understood through ordinary conditional law. Each party gives only if he dies first. Since the conditions are mutually exclusive but each transfer is independently valid, there is no defect of simultaneous impossibility. The transaction is unlike a case where one donor simultaneously grants half to a legally capable person and half to an incapable recipient. Here there is no shared single grant whose invalidity on one side can infect the other. Accordingly, if one party dies while the other lives and no effective retraction occurred, the survivor can acquire under the fulfilled condition. If both remain alive, either can still exercise whatever contractual power of retraction the instrument preserved. The validity does not depend on an abstract later clarification of which one was always the recipient, but on fulfillment of the stated condition.
A relative who was not previously known. The central dispute here concerns whether informal statements of kinship suffice to establish inheritance rights. Rivash limited the deceased's credibility where, at the time he called someone his relative, that person was not then in line to inherit because closer relatives were alive. A casual remark made without inheritance consequences in mind can be unreliable. Tashbetz objected from cases where a person is established as a firstborn through the way people call him, and from general presumptions of family relationship formed through ordinary social conduct. Those cases, however, involve a true public presumption. Where a person is publicly known for thirty days as another's spouse, for example, halachah relies on that status even for severe consequences. A public presumption differs from a single private statement. Likewise, a father identifying his firstborn has special Torah credibility under the law of recognition. He is believed even against an established contrary presumption, though not against actual witnesses. Other kinship claims do not enjoy that special Torah rule. They rest only on a migo, since the estate owner could have given his property to the claimant. A migo does not defeat an established contrary presumption. Therefore, where the alleged kinship was never publicly established and the only evidence is that the deceased once called the claimant a relative in casual conversation, Rivash's hesitation is well founded. If the deceased did not intend his words to establish inheritance, the migo that he could have given the estate has little force, since such a gift never entered his mind. By contrast, where the relationship became a public established status, casual speech is enough because the legal force comes from the public presumption, not from the deceased's private statement alone.
The first authority objected to Rivash's reference to casual conversation. The objection from others calling a person firstborn proves little. If two witnesses merely say that they once heard people call him firstborn, that is hearsay unless the matter rose to the level of a public established status. But where the entire city has long treated him as firstborn, that is a halachic presumption, comparable to a man and woman who live publicly as husband and wife for thirty days. The same explains the cases where everyone went to a certain healer because the father called him "my firstborn." The cumulative public conduct established the status. In addition, firstborn recognition is unique because the Torah gives the father direct credibility under the law of recognition, even without an inheritance motive at the moment of speech. Rivash's case concerned a relative whose relationship had not become publicly known. The only basis was the deceased's statement. For ordinary relatives such a statement is believed only through the migo that the deceased could have given the claimant his estate. That migo does not work against an established contrary presumption and is weakened where the statement was casual and the deceased never contemplated inheritance consequences. Tashbetz's broader argument that any kinship statement is enough because Heaven itself grants the inheritance once relationship is established overlooks this distinction. If relationship is independently known, no proprietary act by the deceased is needed. But where the relationship itself depends entirely on his statement, his credibility must come either from the special recognition verse, from a valid public presumption, or from migo. Ordinary casual speech without any of those foundations is insufficient. Rivash also wondered whether failure of the relationship to become known in a place where one would expect it to be public creates a contrary presumption. That is a legitimate concern. Baal HaMaor holds that a migo can fail where there were observers whose knowledge would ordinarily expose the truth. Ramban disputes that limitation, but Rivash was entitled to take the issue seriously. Thus the various proofs from firstborn status, public family presumptions, and casual kinship statements do not contradict Rivash. Firstborn status rests on a special Torah credibility; public status rests on legal presumption; and an isolated statement about an ordinary relative rests only on migo, which is weaker and sensitive to contrary presumptions and context.
