To restore the walls, etc. See the new Responsa Beit Ephraim concerning Reuven, whose property fronted a large public square for a width of ten amot. Long ago he built a house so that for the first five amot from one corner the building stood directly against the public domain, while along the second five amot he recessed his building five amot or more into his own land. He built a projecting upper structure over that recessed area near Shimon's courtyard and also constructed an upper story above the projection. Reuven now wished to build a stone structure and arch in the recessed space, bringing that portion too to the edge of the public domain and aligning it with the original corner building. Shimon objected that this would lengthen his route, because previously he could walk diagonally from the entrance of his courtyard beneath the projection, whereas the new stone structure would force him to go around its entire length. Beit Ephraim ruled for Reuven. He explains that if the projection had extended fully across the frontage, like the common covered overhangs in Polish towns beneath which townspeople actually traveled, no resident could obstruct that public passage by extending his building to the edge of the square. The rule is that a boundary strip in which the public established rights may not be damaged. Although the Gemara in HaMokher Peirot and Rashbam imply that Rav Yehudah's rule concerns a place the public actually leveled and prepared for passage, and that mere walking does not ordinarily acquire a boundary strip, these covered passages are like vineyard paths, which are made solely for walking and are therefore acquired by walking itself. Thus even one who initially recessed into his own property may not later restore his wall where the public acquired such a passage, even if the public domain is otherwise broad. In the actual case, however, the projection covered only half the width of the property and there was no destruction of a public thoroughfare, because public traffic never passed underneath this particular projection. Reuven may therefore restore his wall. Tosafot in Lo Yachpor go even further and state that Rav Yehudah's prohibition applies only within sixteen amot of the public road, a distinction also ruled by Hagahot Ashri at the end of Chezkas HaBatim. Here more than sixty amot of open space remained, so even a genuine obstruction would arguably not be prohibited. Beit Ephraim does not rely on that point alone because the other poskim do not mention it. Rather, the decisive point is that no public path actually passed beneath the projection. Although people sometimes gathered there, sat on benches under it, stood beneath it to escape rain, or walked there when the street was muddy, this does not constitute acquisition of a public road. Rabbeinu Yerucham, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 417, similarly limits the rule to a place the public actually treads as a route. All the more so here, where the corner building prevented people from using the area beneath the projection as a shortcut. Nor is this like a vineyard path, since the public's occasional use was not ordinary passage but temporary shelter, standing, sitting, or detouring around mud. Since they made no improvement there and had only intermittent use, they acquired no public-road right. Reuven may therefore extend the arch to the edge of the public domain, aligned with the corner building. See also what I wrote above in siman 377, seif katan 1.
