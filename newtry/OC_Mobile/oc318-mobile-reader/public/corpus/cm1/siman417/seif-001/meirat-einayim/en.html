Under the public domain, but not pits or trenches, etc. This is likewise the wording of Rambam and Tur. They write "and not pits," with a conjunction, implying two distinct matters: hollow spaces beneath the public domain and pits. It is possible that the pits are open and visible so that people in the public domain can avoid them, yet creating them is nevertheless prohibited. In the Mishnah, however, the wording is "not pits," without the conjunction. Rashbam explains that it is one matter: the Mishnah says one may not excavate a hollow space and then explains that this means pits, trenches, etc. This also appears from its conclusion, "even though a wagon can travel above them," which shows that they are covered. See Perishah.
Lest it collapse underneath without his knowledge. It is not enough that he accept liability for any damage to people passing over it. People prefer their own property not to be damaged rather than to suffer damage and then receive compensation or replacement. They also do not want to have to enter litigation and adjudication, as explained above.
And everyone waives this. Because today or tomorrow he or his children may also need to do the same.
Belonging to the rulers of the city. Meaning, to the local authority. Rashba writes that it is well known that roads, although given over for public use, belong to the rulers of the land. They have authority to close roads and open them elsewhere, because the roads are theirs. This resembles one who brings his wall inward into his own property with the intention of later extending projections and excavations when he wishes. See Darkei Moshe.
