Belonging to the rulers of the city. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 22. After explaining in the case before him that it is prohibited to build beneath the projecting overhang extending from the roof toward the street, he writes that one cannot permit it on the basis of the Rama's gloss here that the public domain belongs to the city's rulers, a rule drawn from Rashba. Rashba himself concludes that the arrangement is comparable to one who recessed his building into his own property on the understanding that he might later extend projections or excavations. Yet with respect to projections, seif 2 expressly states that although one who recessed his wall may later project outward, he may not restore the wall itself to its original line because a boundary strip in which the public acquired rights may not be damaged. Rashba's analogy therefore applies only to underground excavations, which do not impair public passage but merely create a cavity beneath the public domain. In such a case, the fact that the public domain belongs to the rulers is relevant because from the outset it was given subject to their right to permit projections and excavations, just as one who recessed into his own property may later make such projections. It does not permit restoration of a wall where even the original owner who recessed it could not lawfully restore it. Beit Ephraim repeats this in siman 23 with further explanation. He notes that Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef in siman 162 and Rama there, concerning placement of doors in an alley, might appear contrary because Rashba relied upon the governmental laws of the kings, implying that dina de-malkhuta dina can apply even where the public acquired rights in the strip. Beit Ephraim answers that Rashba did not rely on that consideration alone, and that the case there was different because explicit governmental permission had been granted to place the doors. He gives three further distinctions. First, Rashba and Rama spoke where established custom itself shows that the public area was granted from the outset subject to a right to make projections and excavations. In the present case there is no basis to assume that the street was granted subject to a private person's right to appropriate public space for his own overhang. Second, even if the ruler granted the roadway subject to such a right, this can operate against the public at large because the roads belong to the sovereign and he may redirect them. It cannot operate against an individual whose access to his own house is impaired by the new building. The ruler governs the entire city and cannot be presumed to have granted a right to benefit one person specifically at another's expense. Even a new permission granted now would therefore not constitute valid dina de-malkhuta for this purpose, as the poskim explain. Third, Beit Ephraim suggests that the underlying reason in Rashba is that the prohibition against damaging a boundary strip used by the public applies when the road was granted permanently. If the owner explicitly granted it only temporarily, later public use after the agreed period is use without permission. Since the roads belong to the rulers, who retain authority to build and close them, the public receives them from the outset subject to the condition that the rulers may reclaim them whenever they wish. They may therefore permit excavations and projections, or order doors to be installed, because the public's access to the alley was conditional from the beginning. By contrast, in Beit Ephraim's case the private owner himself built inward and opened the entrance to his cellar toward the street, shifting his boundary inward. His conduct indicated an intention to dedicate the strip permanently to the public. The rule that he may not restore the wall therefore applies. See there further, and what I wrote above in siman 377.
