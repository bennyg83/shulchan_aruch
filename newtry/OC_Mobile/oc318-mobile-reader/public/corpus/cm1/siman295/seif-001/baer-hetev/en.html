I hereby. The Rosh writes that even after the custodian pays, he must swear that the item is not in his possession. We do not say that the depositor waived the oath by accepting payment, for it is more reasonable to say that he accepted the payment lest the custodian retract and force him to litigate again. The Bach's question, that the custodian cannot retract once he accepted in beit din to pay, is not difficult. The meaning is that the depositor fears he will attempt to retract and require renewed litigation, and therefore he accepts the money. Ma'adanei Melech writes likewise, and this is primary. The Rif, Rambam, and other authorities do not disagree with the Rosh. Their wording, "I hereby pay," merely describes the ordinary sequence, that he is first required to swear and then pays, because perhaps the oath will cause him to withdraw his claim and return the actual item. But if he paid first, he nevertheless must swear that it is not in his possession, contrary to the Bach. Shach.
Available. Two conditions are stated: first, the same type of item must be available for purchase in the market; second, the available item must be equivalent to this one. If either is lacking, we are concerned that he set his eyes upon this particular item. Even if another of the same type is equivalent, if it is not readily available for purchase, there is concern. And if it is available but not equivalent in its particular design, there is likewise concern that he desired this specific design. So writes the Sema. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 29.
Pays. One may ask: since in any event he must take a stringent oath, why should he pay? The answer is that if he does not pay, he must include two additional matters in his oath, that he guarded it properly and that he derived no benefit through unauthorized use. The custodian may fear that perhaps at some point he derived slight benefit or did not guard it properly. Sema.
