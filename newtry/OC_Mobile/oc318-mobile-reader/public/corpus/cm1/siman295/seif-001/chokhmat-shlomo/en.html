Seif 2. Thus, every custodian who swore, etc. Note: See the Shach regarding a case where witnesses establish the event, so that the custodian is exempt, but he nevertheless pays. The Shach writes that the owner does not thereby transfer to him the right to the thief's double payment, because when the deposit was first entrusted the owner did not anticipate that the custodian would pay despite available witnesses. The owner cannot newly transfer the double payment now, because it is a future right not yet in existence. The Shach adds, however, that if the deposited item later appreciates, the custodian can acquire the appreciation by an ordinary acquisition rather than merely through payment. His wording might imply that even in the very case where he does not acquire the double payment, namely theft, he can acquire the appreciation. That cannot be correct. In a case of theft the owner did not transfer anything initially because he did not anticipate payment, and when the custodian later pays the item is already stolen. A person cannot sell property that is not in his possession; in the case of stolen property before owner despair, neither the owner nor the thief can consecrate or sell it. How, then, can a new transfer now take effect? The Shach must be speaking of separate cases. His statement about appreciation concerns an item that was lost and still legally in the owner's possession for purposes of sale, not one that was stolen. His reference to acquisition by meshichah is also difficult, because the custodian does not know where the object is. Meshichah requires the seller to say, 'Pull it and acquire.' If only the custodian knows where it is and pulls it without the depositor's knowledge, that clearly does not work. If the depositor also knows where it is and tells him to pull it, of course it works like any sale of an animal. Perhaps the Shach means that at the time of payment the depositor tells him, 'When you discover it, pull it and acquire it,' though that too is straightforward. Possibly he meant acquisition by chalipin, where the depositor takes the object given by the custodian and thereby the lost animal is acquired by the custodian wherever it is, as in the laws of sale. Regarding the Shach's further question whether the body of the returned object itself becomes the custodian's, I think there is some support that it does from the Gemara's case of jewels, where Rav Nachman said that the jewels return to their owner and the mansion returns to the purchaser. If the body of the returned object always belonged to the depositor even when the custodian paid voluntarily, Rav Nachman's statement that the jewels return to their owner would contain little novelty. The only consequence would be that the depositor need not reimburse the custodian for their appreciation, yet Rav Nachman would have omitted the core distinction and relied on an inference from silence. According to the Shach, however, Rav Nachman's formulation states the essential law directly: had the custodian paid voluntarily, the jewels themselves would have belonged to him and the mansion would have remained with the depositor; here, by contrast, the jewels return to their original owner. This fits well.
