He paid and did not wish to swear. See Shach, seif katan 3, who rules that a declaration of willingness to pay is sufficient, and the Sema likewise so rules. At first glance it is very surprising that Rama omitted the entire major distinction in the Gemara between an ordinary custodian and a borrower. For custodians, merely saying that he will pay is sufficient, whereas for a borrower actual payment is required. Where the custodian already swore, actual payment is required according to all opinions, subject to the dispute between the Rosh and the other authorities cited by the Shach. It appears to me that the omission can be explained as follows. The Shach writes in seif katan 9 that an increase arising from the body of the property is not acquired by the custodian even if it arose after the theft. His language implies that an increase arising after payment does belong to the custodian, for otherwise he should have stated the stronger novelty that even such an increase remains the owner's. Ritva writes similarly in HaMafkid regarding jewels that rose in value, explaining that appreciation in value is an increase arising from the body. The statement that bodily increase was not transferred to the custodian refers to increase arising before payment, but appreciation after payment, once the acquisition has been completed, belongs to him. Even authorities who classify market appreciation as an external increase can agree with Ritva that actual bodily increase after payment belongs to the custodian; their dispute is only whether appreciation is categorized as external and can therefore be acquired even if it arose before payment. According to this, Rama may follow Ritva that appreciation is bodily increase and is acquired only if it occurs after payment. The Tur discusses the laws of double payment and therefore sets out all the distinctions stated in the Gemara. The Shulchan Aruch, however, is not here dealing with double payment but only with whether appreciation before payment belongs to the custodian. He therefore states a case of actual payment to teach a greater novelty: even after payment, bodily increase is not acquired retroactively for the period before payment. Rama likewise addresses only increase that arose before payment. Increase arising after payment is certainly acquired in every case, even if the custodian did not take the oath that the item is not in his possession, even according to the Tur, and even if he had already sworn and then paid, or if witnesses established his negligence. Rama therefore had no need to present those distinctions.
And did not wish to swear. See Shach, seif katan 7, through his statement that perhaps he was negligent only now. Ketzos HaChoshen asks that if the owner himself claimed, "Perhaps you were negligent only now," demanding payment at the current higher value, while the custodian claimed, "I was negligent earlier and therefore owe only the earlier lower value," the custodian would be believed even without an oath. There is no difficulty. In that case the owner would be demanding from the custodian, and the truth may indeed be as the custodian says. Here, by contrast, the custodian is demanding from the owner. He claims that he acquired the appreciation because his negligence occurred when the animal was worth less and the later appreciation occurred while the animal was considered in his domain. The owner answers that perhaps the negligence occurred only now, when the value was higher. If so, since the custodian paid only the earlier lower value rather than the amount actually due at the time of negligence, he never acquired the appreciation. The Shach understands the condition "when you wish and pay me" to mean payment of whatever the law truly requires, not merely whatever beit din compelled him to pay because of uncertainty. Thus, even after he pays the amount beit din awarded on account of doubt, the owner may still say, "Perhaps your negligence occurred when the value was high, and you therefore did not pay me everything actually due." The owner prevails because the burden of proof rests on the claimant, and the animal remains in the possession of its original owner. Indeed, the custodian would not even have acquired the body of the animal in full, just as one who paid only half did not acquire the unpaid half. Ketzos HaChoshen is nevertheless correct that the primary view of Ra'avad follows Rashba, that market appreciation is bodily increase and is acquired only as of the time of payment. It follows that where fleece or offspring came into existence after payment in a case of loss, the custodian acquires them.
Any increase that comes from outside. See Shach, seif katan 7, through his question: if so, why does the Gemara ask that the fleece and offspring should likewise belong to the custodian? His meaning is that the owner did not transfer the animal until the condition was fulfilled, and the condition was, "when you wish and pay me," meaning whatever amount the custodian truly owes by law. If the owner sued him regarding the cow before the transfer, beit din would require him to pay for the animal together with its fleece and offspring even if they remained extant. Only afterward, when the custodian fulfills the condition, does the cow become his. It would therefore seem that he must return the fleece and offspring as part of fulfilling the condition even if they themselves had become his. The Gemara's question is therefore that the primary insistence of the condition concerned only payment of monetary value, as indicated by the phrase "and pay me." The bodies of the fleece and offspring should consequently remain his. Ketzos HaChoshen did not understand the Shach this way. Know also that the custodian acquires the double payment only if the thief is discovered after the custodian has said, "I will pay." If the thief is discovered first and is poor, and only afterward the custodian says, "I will pay," the owner did not transfer the right to double payment to him. This is evident from Shitah Mekubetzes. See further Shach, seif katan 8, through his statement that where some acquisition was made, such as meshichah. It appears that the word meshichah is a textual error, for how can the custodian perform meshichah after the object has been stolen? The text should read "such as kinyan sudar." It must also refer to a case where the object was lost but remains in the custodian's domain, because where it was stolen the owner cannot transfer it at the time of payment, since when an item was stolen and the owners have not despaired of recovery, neither the thief nor the owner can consecrate it. The Shach further seeks to prove Ramban's view from the Gemara's statement that the custodian acquires double payment only because he could have exempted himself by claiming theft. In my view there is no proof. The Gemara uses that reasoning only where the custodian merely said, "I will pay." Where he actually paid, it is evident that even if he could not have exempted himself he may acquire the double payment. According to the first version concerning Rav Pappa, a borrower cannot exempt himself by claiming that the animal died through work because that occurrence is uncommon, yet according to Rashi's reading he nevertheless acquires double payment upon actual payment. Tosafos, however, read the passage differently and understand it as a conclusive refutation, so according to their reading he indeed does not acquire even upon payment. The dispute between Rashba and Ramban therefore depends on the variant readings of Rashi and Tosafos.
Bodily increase. See Shach, seif katan 11, through his statement that the animal returns together with its fleece. In my view Rambam's position can be defended. Tosafos in Bava Metzia, HaMafkid, s.v. "such as the produce of a palm tree," ask why one cannot transfer the cow for purposes of acquiring the future double payment, as in Gittin concerning one who sells his slave with respect to a future penalty. They answer that the Gemara's argument assumes the side of the unresolved question that a master cannot sell a slave solely with respect to the penalty. Shitah Mekubetzes explains similarly. Since the sale of a slave for the future penalty remains an unresolved question, if we assume such a sale is effective the entire argument of the Gemara falls away, because the owner could transfer only the right to double payment without transferring the body of the cow at all. Since the question was never resolved, the custodian cannot establish acquisition of the cow's body. We therefore leave the animal in the possession of its original owner, and the burden of proof rests upon one seeking to remove it. Rambam's ruling that the custodian acquires market appreciation can be understood as referring to appreciation occurring after actual payment, following Ritva's view cited above.
