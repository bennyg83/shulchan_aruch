He paid, etc. This is not literal. Once he says, 'I will pay,' he is treated as though he paid. According to the Rosh, however, where he has not yet sworn that the item is not in his possession, his statement alone may be insufficient until payment is actually made, and later authorities follow this distinction. The acquisition of the prospective double payment rests upon the owner's presumed transfer to one who accepts liability, but the owner does not intend to benefit one who can still avoid payment or who retains the property. Accordingly, the precise effect of the declaration depends upon the accompanying oath and the custodian's legal ability to retract. See the Rosh, Tur, and later authorities.
And he did not wish, etc. This applies only where he paid voluntarily. If the judge compelled him to pay, such as where he admitted negligence and refused payment, or said that he did not know where he placed the deposit, which constitutes negligence, and the court then collected from him, he does not acquire the double payment. The owner's transfer is presumed only in favor of one who voluntarily undertakes payment, not one compelled by judgment.
He wished to swear. Meaning, he did not wish to persist in relying upon his oath. The same law applies if he first took the custodial oath and later paid. He acquires all increase that comes from outside, as Rava states in Perek HaGozel Kama, and so rule the authorities.
And afterward the thief was identified. The same applies if the item itself was later found and the thief was not identified. The custodian acquires automatic appreciation, such as an increase in market value, as is evident from the incident of the ornaments and from the authorities. The text mentions identification of the thief because that is the ordinary case involving double payment.
All increase that comes from outside. This means increase arising after payment. The Raavad so writes in chapter 8 of the Laws of Borrowing. The Sema writes broadly that if the animal increased in value after it was deposited, the custodian need pay only its original value. That formulation is too broad. Before payment or a binding undertaking to pay, the owner retains the increase. The owner's implied transfer concerns future external appreciation granted to the custodian in exchange for his assumption of payment. One must therefore distinguish the time of the increase and the time at which the custodian became bound. See the Gemara, Rosh, and later authorities.
That comes from outside. This includes double payment and market appreciation. The Mordechai adds that where a depositor paid a custodian for a gentile's collateral, the depositor acquires the gentile's interest payments as well. The Maggid Mishna discusses whether fourfold or fivefold payment is likewise transferred. The controlling principle is that the owner transfers external benefits that arise from recovery of the deposited property, but not bodily increase inherent in the animal or object itself.
But not bodily increase. This implies that even bodily increase arising after the theft is not acquired by the custodian. The Maggid Mishna so understands the Rambam, and this appears to be the view of the Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham, who follow Rava's first version. Under that version the owner is treated as though he said, 'I transfer the item except for its bodily increase.' Rabbi Zeira also appears to follow this version. Although some commentators seem to disagree in resolving Rabbi Zeira's question, after the Gemara's conclusion they too may agree that bodily increase is excluded even when it arises after the theft.

Bodily increase. The Rambam writes that if the custodian said, 'I will pay,' and the thief slaughtered or sold the animal, when the animal itself returns, it returns together with its shearings and offspring. The students of the Rashba bring a proof, though it is not conclusive. Tosafot imply that the body of the cow is transferred to the custodian, but bodily increase is expressly excluded, as though the owner said, 'except for bodily increase.' It is possible that the Rambam does not mean that the owner can insist upon the very animal in every case. He discusses one who merely said, 'I will pay,' but had not yet paid, and therefore uses the ordinary expression that the animal and its bodily increase return. The Rosh, Tur, and Rabbeinu Yerucham rule like Rava's first version that the custodian acquires external increase even if the animal stood in a marsh at the time of theft. Although the Rosh's proof is not decisive, this appears correct because Rabbi Zeira follows that version, and the Maggid Mishna understands the Rambam likewise.
