He acquires every increase that comes from outside. Sema writes that if the deposited animal rose in value after the deposit, the custodian who chooses to pay need pay only its value at the time of the original deposit. Shach objects that, at minimum, the custodian acquires the deposit retroactively from near the time of the theft, and under another version from the time of deposit. Therefore intervening appreciation should belong to him. Raavad distinguishes. The custodian acquires increases only from the time he actually pays or declares, "I will pay." Maggid Mishneh disagrees and says that if later payment reveals a retroactive acquisition, the appreciation that occurred before payment should also belong to the custodian. In my view the amount the custodian must pay is fixed by ordinary custodial liability, namely the value at the time of the breach that created payment liability, not by the earlier fictional acquisition date used to allocate the double payment. If the owner had sued him at that time, beit din would have required the amount then due. The retroactive transfer should therefore be read as conditioned upon his paying whatever the law required at the obligating event. Shach argues that if the custodian cannot prove when negligence occurred, he might be forced to pay the value at the later time of suit. That is not generally correct. The principal custodial oath concerns possession and the claimed exempting event, not the market value. If the custodian admits liability and says the object was worth a particular amount at the time of the breach, ordinary evidentiary rules govern the valuation. Where the object is common in the market, there may be no special oath fixing a higher value. The deeper dispute between Raavad and Maggid Mishneh concerns appreciation that occurred before payment. Raavad and Ritva treat market appreciation as an increase of the body's own value. The owner does not ordinarily transfer bodily appreciation until the custodian has actually completed payment or at least declared his readiness to pay. This is different from external benefits such as the thief's double payment. The Gemara's challenge, "If so, even shearings and offspring should be his," is answered by the principle that a person does not transfer bodily increase. The same conceptual answer can include market appreciation. Ritva explicitly distinguishes the later price rise that occurs after payment, which clearly belongs to the custodian, from appreciation before payment, which remains with the owner. Accordingly, Raavad's position is coherent: although the legal root of the custodian's right to the future double payment may be related back to the earlier time, bodily appreciation remains the owner's until the custodian actually pays or unequivocally undertakes payment. This is the stronger reading of the sugya.
The previous discussion also clarifies the large dispute over the timing of the custodian's acquisition. One cannot simply say that because the custodian acquires retroactively from the time of deposit or shortly before the theft, he must receive every increase from that time. The Gemara itself distinguishes the body of the deposit from different classes of improvement. The owner's implied transfer is limited. External gains, such as the thief's double payment, are transferred because the owner is willing to reward the custodian who pays rather than insists on his oath. By contrast, bodily increase, including offspring, shearings, and according to Raavad significant market appreciation, is not normally given away before payment. If the custodial object itself remains intact and merely appreciates, the owner retains that increase until the custodian's payment crystallizes the arrangement. Once payment has been made, later appreciation belongs to the custodian because the acquisition has been completed. This also explains why the custodian's own payment need not always equal the later appreciated value merely because the owner now sues after appreciation. The amount owed follows the ordinary law of custodial damages at the time of the obligating event. The separate law giving the custodian later benefit from the object and from the thief's double payment does not rewrite the underlying measure of custodial liability. Thus the correct conceptual structure contains two timelines: the time that fixes the owner's claim against the custodian, and the time or retroactive theory that allocates the reward for payment. They need not be identical for every form of appreciation.
But not appreciation arising from the animal itself. The Shach understands that even appreciation occurring after the theft remains with the owner. I wrote above, however, that according to Raavad, an increase in market value is treated as appreciation of the body itself. On that approach, once the custodian has paid, subsequent shearings and offspring belong to the custodian. The owner retained the body only for its bodily appreciation until payment. After payment, the custodian's retroactive acquisition of the animal takes effect from the earlier time when it was still in the owner's domain, comparable to a transfer of the body today with fruits after thirty days. Even if the animal was already stolen before the thirty days ended, the body had been acquired while it was still transferable. Shitah Mekubetzet records an opinion distinguishing theft from loss. In theft, the custodian is deemed to acquire shortly before the theft and payment. In loss, since the object remains somewhere in the world and can theoretically be acquired when payment is made, he acquires only at payment. I find this difficult, because payment itself is money and money does not acquire movables. The Gemara's discussion also indicates that an acquisition act is needed. The distinction therefore requires study. See also simanim 61 and 189.
Arising from the body itself. At the beginning of Perek HaMafkid the Gemara asks how the custodian can acquire the future double payment when a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence. Tosafot explains that the initial assumption was that at the time of meshichah the owner transferred a proprietary interest in the cow for purposes of the future double payment. The difficulty was that the fine was too remote and uncertain to be transferable. Rashba asks why this should not work as an ordinary partnership in the body of the slave or animal. If the owner can presently make another person a partner in the body for purposes of a future fine, why is this a transfer of future property? The answer can be drawn from the Gemara concerning sale of a slave for his future fine. Tosafot says that even a complete sale of a slave that has no present value except the fine does not work, because there is presently no monetary value in the transaction. Accordingly, even saying that the buyer receives a present proprietary interest only for the future fine fails if that interest has no current monetary content. But once the owner gives the custodian a present share in some real external appreciation, such as market appreciation under Rambam's theory, the custodian becomes a true partner in the animal to that extent. Once he owns a real monetary share in the body, the future fine follows his ownership automatically. It is then no longer a direct sale of the fine itself. This explains Rambam. He rules that if the stolen animal itself later returns, the animal, its shearings, and offspring return to the owner, because the custodian does not acquire bodily appreciation. What he acquires is external appreciation. Once that genuine monetary share is enough to create present partnership, the later double payment can attach to it. The solution therefore does not require transferring the entire body of the animal to the custodian, only a real present proprietary share that has monetary substance.
