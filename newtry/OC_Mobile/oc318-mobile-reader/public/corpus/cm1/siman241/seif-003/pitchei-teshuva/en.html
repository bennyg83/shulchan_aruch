Every mistaken waiver may be retracted. Baer Heitev cites the general rule. Zichron Yosef, siman 4, addresses a case in which one argued that a waiver was mistaken because an orphan girl later died. One might answer, as Nachalat Shivah does, that where both parties were equally unaware of the future event and knowingly entered a doubtful compromise, there is no mistake. Zichron Yosef rejects that general proposition, citing Bava Batra where Rav Nachman told Rav Anan that a waiver was mistaken even though the waiving party himself had not known the true facts, and Bava Metzia regarding asmachta and onaah. Nevertheless, the case before him was not a mistaken waiver. A mistaken waiver concerns a fact that already existed at the time of the waiver but was then unknown to the waiving party or to both sides. A later event does not retroactively transform a deliberate waiver into a mistake. If a creditor waives a debt because he believes the debtor is poor, and the debtor later inherits wealth, no one would say the waiver is retroactively void. This principle is supported in many places, including Rama at the end of siman 246. Zichron Yosef further writes that where the circumstances do not themselves demonstrate that the waiver was based on a mistake, we do not presume such a mistake.
