One who waives a claim against his fellow; see Shach, seif katan 5. The Shach offers two explanations of the responsum of the Rashba. It appears that this creates a halachic uncertainty in each of the factual forms covered by his two explanations. In a case of mechilah, the debtor might characterize himself as one who says, "I do not know whether I paid you." Yet where the claimant likewise does not know whether he was paid, the ordinary rule that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one does not apply, and even in a case of "I do not know whether I paid you" the defendant can be exempt where both sides are uncertain. If, however, the claimant denies that any language of mechilah was ever spoken while the defendant asserts that it was, the defendant's position can resemble "I do not know whether I paid you" with a definite claim against him. Although a possessor may generally say kim li even against many authorities, perhaps that principle does not operate in the same manner where the underlying posture is "I do not know whether I paid you." Moreover, here the Shach himself was not certain which of his two explanations is correct. Ordinary kim li rests upon reliance on an authority who clearly rules in one direction. When an authority himself offers two possible explanations, there is no equivalent definite ruling to rely upon. This is especially understandable according to the Urim VeTumim's explanation of kim li. On the other hand, the fact that the matter is one of possession still makes extraction difficult, and it is possible that we should not multiply disputes. Perhaps everyone agrees to both legal possibilities suggested by the Shach, while the Sema merely found it forced to read the Rashba's unqualified language as limited to one of those cases and therefore interpreted the case as one of gift rather than mechilah. The Taz explains that unrestricted money deposited may be treated like a loan, making language of mechilah effective. There is a printing error in the citation to the Sema in siman 73, for the Sema elsewhere appears to treat unrestricted money like other deposited movables. In any event, it may be that all authorities agree that the two factual cases formulated by the Shach are legally valid, even if they disagree which one was the Rashba's case. This requires further study. There is also reason to question how much authority to assign to the material printed in the Shach's second volume on Choshen Mishpat, said to have been written in his youth. Perhaps he never intended those remarks as final halachah and did not himself submit them for publication. A dream calling for their publication cannot determine halachah, for Torah is not in Heaven. Still, a youthful work can reveal an interpretive direction, particularly where it expressly disputes earlier authorities, and the fact that the Shach's learned son in law brought the work to print may furnish some support that he found it fit for publication. Yet perhaps he merely transmitted what his father in law had written without undertaking to endorse every conclusion. This too requires study. As to the Shach's second explanation, it seems limited to a case where the amount taken will never ordinarily become independently ascertainable, even through one witness, so that the taker could simply deny taking anything. Once he has taken, a personal monetary claim arises against him, and mechilah can apply because the amount is not objectively identified. Where the amount is expected to become ascertainable, however, even if neither party knew the amount at the time of the statement or at the time of taking, mechilah may not work. Perhaps what can be established by one witness is not fully identified, but what stands to be established by two witnesses is already sufficiently determinate that it is not merely an unliquidated claim. Maharik's proof from an eved Ivri gives some support, since the value of the servant is not fixed and fluctuates with future labor value, yet his person is still a defined subject of ownership. The proof is not conclusive because the identity of the servant himself is fully known even if his value is not. If part of the amount is definitely known while another part is not, language of mechilah may work only for the uncertain excess and not for the minimum definitely established. If at the time of the statement the amount appeared fully ascertainable but later circumstances made it uncertain, the speaker's halachic knowledge may matter. If he knew that mechilah cannot apply to a determinate amount, his language may have been deliberately meaningless. If he did not know that rule, then because the legal effect is not needed until after the money is taken, there is room to say the mechilah takes effect at that later moment when the amount is no longer determinate. This differs from an attempted acquisition of something not yet in existence, because here the underlying right can be transferred through a valid formulation such as gift, and even mechilah coupled with a kinyan can be effective according to the Taz. This entire matter requires further study. The Shach's first explanation also appears limited to a case where, when the statement was made, the speaker knew that the other person would take and spend the property and that only a personal claim would remain. If at that time he did not contemplate the taking at all, the statement may not be effective. This requires further study.
