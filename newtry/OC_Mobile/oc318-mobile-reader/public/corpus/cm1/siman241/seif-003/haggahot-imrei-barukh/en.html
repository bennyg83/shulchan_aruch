(Sema, seif katan 16) And this is an error, for what was written there, etc. Note: It may be that his intention is to answer why the Mechaber writes here that some disagree, while above in siman 209 he cites no dissenting view. The Sema therefore explains that the case there is worse because even the type of property is unknown. In my humble opinion, however, this requires study as a matter of law. One can say that the case in siman 209, where a person says, "I sell you everything in this house," is not included in selling an unspecified item. After all, he specified that everything in the house is sold. The reason the acquisition fails is not lack of specification, but because the buyer does not know whether the contents are straw or gold and therefore does not rely upon the transaction; it resembles gambling. This reason is stated explicitly above in Tur and Shulchan Aruch and derives from the Rambam. Thus, although the Tur disagrees here with the Rambam concerning transfer of an unspecified item, he nevertheless rules like the Rambam in siman 209 for the separate reason stated above. There is proof from Maggid Mishneh, Hilchot Zechiyah 3, who writes that the Rambam derived the present rule from Gittin 8b. Yet regarding the Rambam's rule in Hilchot Mechirah 21, "I sell you everything in this house," the Maggid Mishneh writes that the rule is reasonable but that he found no Gemara from which it could be learned. If the latter were merely another case of an unspecified sale, and indeed a worse one because even the type is unknown, the Maggid Mishneh could have grounded it in the same source. Rather, as stated, it fails because it resembles gambling. Indeed it is worse in one respect, because unlike ordinary gambling, where both parties accept a possibility of gain or loss, here neither seller nor buyer knows what is being transferred. Therefore the Tur accepted this rule even while disagreeing with the Rambam concerning gambling. If so, perhaps the defect applies only to a sale. In a gift, if the donor says, "I give you everything in this house," perhaps the recipient does acquire it. This may explain why Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch state this rule only in the laws of sale and not in the laws of gifts.
