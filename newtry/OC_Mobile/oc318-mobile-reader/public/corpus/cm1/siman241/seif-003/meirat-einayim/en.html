But after he took what he took, he keeps it, etc. It has already been explained that the term "waiver" properly applies only to something presently in another's possession and no longer in the waiving party's hand. We must therefore say that here the giver transferred it through a valid language of waiver or gift. The present ruling is not focused on the technical wording of the transfer, but on when he may retract. See Responsa Rashba cited by Beit Yosef, who explains that even if this was a mistaken waiver, the waiver remains effective. His intent must be limited to a mistake of this type, where the giver initially told the recipient to take, similar to the Tur's discussion at the end of siman 246 concerning one who gives all his property to another because he mistakenly thought his son had died, and the other examples listed there. Alternatively, it is like what the Tur writes at the end of siman 154, seif 38: a mistaken waiver of this kind, where the circumstances do not clearly demonstrate that the waiver was based on mistake, remains a valid waiver. See there.
What he writes, "but land does not," etc. The reason is that ordinary possession does not apply to land in the same way, because land remains in the presumptive possession of its owner.
What he writes, "the maneh I have in your possession, give it to him," etc. This applies when all three parties were present, under the law of ma'amad sheloshtan, as explained in siman 126.
