When does this apply? When this condition was made in accordance with the laws of conditions. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the condition was not formulated in accordance with the laws of conditions, for example if it was not doubled, he must nevertheless pay what was stipulated, comparable to Yevamot 106 regarding a condition attached to an act that cannot be performed through an agent, where the Rosh and Rashba write that he must pay by virtue of the employment obligation. In my humble opinion, this certainly is not so. No halachic authority has ever written such a rule. Rather, whenever a condition was not made in accordance with the laws governing conditions, we regard it as mere words intended to induce the other party. I discussed this at length in my work Torat Gittin, siman 143, seif katan 1; see there.
According to all the requirements of conditions. I will record here several general principles concerning conditions. One who says "on condition that" is treated as one who says "from now." If, however, he says "on the condition that," the Beit Yosef, siman 207, seif 14, writes that this is not treated as saying "from now." See what I wrote in Torat Gittin, where in my humble opinion it appears that every condition not stated with the word "if" takes effect from now. As to whether a doubled condition is required when one says "from now," see Even HaEzer. Likewise, as to whether a doubled condition is required in monetary matters, see Even HaEzer, the Beit Shmuel there, and the Bach. Other later authorities write that the custom is to require a doubled condition for land, but not for movable property; see there. Concerning whether cancellation or waiver of a condition causes the gift to take effect retroactively, see Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer, siman 35, and the Ran in the chapter HaMadir in Ketubot. The Rashba and Ran disagree. The Rashba maintains that waiver is effective only for a monetary condition, for then it is equivalent to saying, "I regard myself as though I have received it." This does not apply to other conditions, even a condition requiring passive inaction. The Ran maintains that there is no distinction. Even with conditions concerning defects, vows, and other matters, and even a condition requiring passive inaction, waiver is effective, for it is as though he had said when imposing the condition, "if I am particular about it." Therefore, if after learning the facts he is not particular, the kiddushin take effect retroactively. The Ran challenges this from the Mishnah concerning one who stipulated, "on condition that I am a kohen," and was found to be a Levi, where even if she says that in her heart she had wanted the kiddushin, she is not betrothed. Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchot Zechiyah, rule 14, answers that with defects some people are particular and some are not, so the matter remains dependent upon whether, after discovering the defect, he objects. By contrast, where he said "on condition that I am a kohen," once she was particular at the time of kiddushin and expressly stipulated it, and the representation proved false, the kiddushin are void. At first glance this is puzzling, for if so, what was the Ran's question? Upon reflection, however, the Ran's question is sound. According to that approach, known defects and vows that were expressly specified as conditions could never be waived, yet the Gemara speaks without qualification. The Ran therefore required his own answer: although she says that in her heart she had wished to proceed and was not particular, since she nevertheless expressly stipulated, those unspoken intentions are disregarded and we follow her statement. If he objected upon hearing of the matter and later waived it, then where the condition concerned a nonmonetary matter and the act was already nullified, later waiver is ineffective. For a monetary matter, however, fulfillment of the condition remains possible. Thus writes the Beit Shmuel there. Ketzot HaChoshen, siman 243, seif katan 2, writes that this is not compelling, for even in monetary matters, once he objected and had the right to retract, later waiver by one party does not prevent the other from retracting. He compares this to ona'ah in siman 227; see there. It may be answered that the Beit Shmuel is speaking where he objected during the period in which the condition could still be fulfilled, for example where one said, "on condition, from now, that you give a zuz after thirty days," and during those thirty days the giver said that he did not want the condition fulfilled and that he did not want the gift if the zuz was not given. Since the recipient could not yet fulfill the condition until the appointed time, against the giver's will, the giver's subsequent waiver is effective when the time arrives, so that the gift takes effect retroactively. But with a condition concerning a past fact, even if monetary, once the truth becomes known and he says that he does not desire the transaction, the kiddushin are void and his later waiver is ineffective. See Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 6 of Hilchot Ishut and chapter 2 of Hilchot Zechiyah, who writes that a condition concerning a past fact need not be stated in all the formal detail of a condition, and raises a difficulty from Kiddushin, which derives rules from sotah although sotah involves a condition concerning the past. See my Torat Gittin, where I explain that according to Rabbi Meir we do not infer the negative from the affirmative even in a condition concerning a past fact, but the other formal laws of conditions are not required for a condition concerning the past.
