Rambam, Ri, and Rabbeinu Tam require doubled conditions and the other technical rules of conditions even in monetary matters, as in kiddushin. Kiddushin discusses one who says, "Here is a maneh on condition that you return it to me." Rava rules that in a sale the transaction can acquire, while in kiddushin it does not because it resembles barter. The Gemara compares this with a gift on condition of return, such as an etrog given on condition that it be returned. Tosafot asks why failure to return invalidates the etrog use if the donor did not double the condition. They answer that some conditions do not require doubling, especially where the donor's purpose was clearly to perform the mitzvah with his own etrog. Rosh and other Tosafot passages, however, hold that even the etrog case requires a properly doubled condition. In Even HaEzer, the Shulchan Aruch says that if a man gives a woman a maneh on condition that she return it, she is not betrothed because it resembles barter. The commentators there establish this where the condition was properly doubled. If it was not, the condition would be void, she would keep the maneh, and the transaction would no longer resemble barter. Yet Rosh, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch do not consistently mention the technical laws of conditions in the ordinary gift on condition of return. In my view those technical laws are not needed to create the recipient's obligation to return. As I explained above, a gift on condition of return can be understood as temporary bodily ownership with a retained reversion. The donor never gave perpetual ownership. The return requirement is therefore a reservation in the body of the gift, not merely an external condition. Ritva similarly writes in another context that no technical condition formula is needed where the language functions as a reservation in the gift itself. This explains Tosafot's precise question. They do not ask at the beginning of the sugya how Rava can even call the arrangement a gift on condition of return without a doubled condition. The return obligation exists regardless. Their question arises only from the statement, "If he did not return it, he did not fulfill the mitzvah." If the recipient had full ownership at the moment of the mitzvah and only later failed to return, that later failure would ordinarily make him a wrongful holder from then onward, not retroactively. To make failure of return undo the original gift retroactively, the return must operate as a true formal condition, and for that the technical rules of conditions can be necessary. Even according to Rosh and Ritva, who understand a gift on condition of return as a complete gift followed by a required new transfer back, the recipient can still become personally obligated to return the object even if the condition was not formulated with all the technical rules. Once he pulled the etrog or accepted the money on the stated terms, he became obligated to provide the promised return, just as one who receives an object on terms that he will provide a kor of wheat becomes liable for that agreed consideration. Therefore, failure to formulate a technical doubled condition does not necessarily eliminate the duty to return. It affects whether failure of return retroactively cancels the original act. The law of chalitzah proves the distinction. A woman agreed to pay two hundred zuz on condition that the unsuitable brother perform chalitzah. Formal condition law does not operate in chalitzah because chalitzah cannot be performed through an agent. Nevertheless Abaye initially required her to pay. Ramban, Rashba, and Rosh explain that where the underlying act cannot be undone by failure of the condition, the promised payment can still be enforced like a wage, unless special grounds such as "I was merely playing with you" apply. This yields a general rule. If a condition is made according to the full laws of the condition of the tribes of Gad and Reuven, then the promisor has a choice. He can refuse performance, and the underlying act is thereby undone. If the condition was not technically valid, the underlying act remains effective regardless, but the recipient can still be personally obligated to perform what he promised in exchange for receiving the benefit. Thus, if one gives an etrog or money "on condition that you return it," even without doubling the condition, the recipient must return the etrog or money because that was the agreed consideration for his receipt. If he does not return it, the original gift is not necessarily retroactively void, but he remains liable to fulfill the return obligation. This resolves a comment of Hagahot Maimoniyot requiring care with all technical conditions in an etrog gift. On a simple reading the comment is puzzling: if the condition is technically defective, the condition should fall away and the gift become stronger, so the recipient should certainly fulfill the mitzvah. According to the present analysis, however, a defective condition leaves the gift standing but creates an enforceable duty to return the etrog. Since for an etrog return of money does not suffice and the donor can ultimately recover the actual etrog, the donor's later recovery may be treated retroactively in a manner that jeopardizes the original status of "yours." A properly formulated condition avoids this by making the legal structure depend cleanly upon fulfillment. This is the proper resolution.
