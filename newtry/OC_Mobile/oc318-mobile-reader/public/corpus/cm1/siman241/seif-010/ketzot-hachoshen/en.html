On condition that you not do something, the donor bears the burden of proof. Maharit asks why there should be a distinction between a positive condition and a negative condition for purposes of proof. In divorce there is reason to distinguish. If the condition requires an act, she should not remarry until the act is proven, because perhaps an unavoidable circumstance will prevent performance. If the condition is merely that she refrain from something, she can guard herself from violating it. But regarding evidentiary credibility after the fact, why should positive and negative conditions differ? One cannot simply say that positive acts are harder to perform, because sometimes abstention can be harder, such as a condition not to drink wine for someone who cannot live without it, while a trivial positive act such as dancing once can be easier. Maharit therefore suggests a practical evidentiary reason. With a negative condition, the recipient cannot realistically bring witnesses proving that he never acted. Witnesses are not hanging from the rafters observing him continuously. Therefore the one who alleges violation must prove that the forbidden act occurred. With a positive condition, the recipient can ordinarily bring witnesses that the required act was performed, so the burden is placed on him. Maharit remains uncertain where the recipient dies. The person who could have clarified performance is gone, and the heirs cannot prove what he did. Perhaps the case then resembles a negative condition where the opposing party should bear the burden. In my view the better basis is presumption. Where a condition requires a positive act, we leave matters in their prior status of nonperformance until evidence establishes that the act occurred. This resembles Tosafot's explanation concerning a nazir who drinks wine. Although he could later annul his vow retroactively, we treat him according to his present status and presume the vow remains unless and until annulment is shown. So too here. Even if failure of the condition would undo the transaction retroactively, so long as the condition is a positive act we presume nonperformance until the recipient proves performance. The evidentiary burden follows the prior status, not merely practical ability to produce witnesses.
