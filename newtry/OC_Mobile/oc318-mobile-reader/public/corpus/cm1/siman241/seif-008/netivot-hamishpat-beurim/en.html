He is exempt from payment. See Sema, seif katan 21, who says that even for theft or loss he is exempt because he is only an unpaid custodian. At first glance this is difficult. If, once the condition fails, we retroactively treat him as having held the object as a custodian, then where he used the gift he must retroactively pay for that use, as Mishneh LaMelech writes concerning fruit consumed. Why then is he not a renter and therefore a paid custodian? True, he is not a person who made unauthorized use of a deposit because the use was initially permitted. But once he must pay for the benefit, why not give him the status of renter? If, by contrast, we do not treat him as having accepted any custody at all, why is he even an unpaid custodian? Perhaps the answer is that because he keeps the object in his house for his own benefit, he has basic custodial responsibility. Yet he does not become a renter retroactively merely because, after failure of the condition, he owes payment for the benefit previously received. The use was originally based on a gift, not a rental. If one gives an item on condition that it be returned so the recipient can use it for kiddushin, and it is stolen or lost from the woman, the recipient is certainly liable for its value. This is obvious where he originally borrowed the ring for kiddushin. Even if we interpret the lender as meaning a gift on condition to return the ring or its value, the language of borrowing remains. The structure can be: if he returns the ring or value, it is a gift for purposes of kiddushin; if not, he is a borrower and liable even for ones. But even without express borrowing language, where it was simply given as a gift on condition to return, he can be liable. If he negligently guarded it, even if he told the donor in advance that he would be negligent, he is not thereby exempt unless the donor expressly said, "and you shall be exempt." The donor can simply remain silent because the condition already protects him: if the recipient returns the item or value, the gift stands; if not, the gift fails and ordinary custodial liability follows. When the recipient then gives the ring to the woman as an absolute gift for kiddushin, there is no greater negligence. Even according to Rava, who holds that one custodian who transfers to another can remain exempt because he delivered to a competent custodian, here the woman received it as her own gift and undertook no custody obligation to the donor. If the original recipient later violates the return condition and the gift is retroactively void, he is liable for that negligence. This resembles one who tells another, "I need this object to burn or tear," and the owner says, "I give it to you on condition that..." If the recipient destroys it and then violates the condition, the gift is retroactively void and he is liable as a damager. He pays its value at the time of damage, not necessarily its value at the time of the original gift. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 28 writes that even if the husband does not return the value, the kiddushin may remain doubtful because Rosh's first answer can be read as saying the lender may have intended an absolute gift. In my view Rosh did not mean that this was definitely an absolute gift. His point was only that the kiddushin should not fail merely because a gift on condition to return were theoretically ineffective for kiddushin. Since the borrower expressly asked for a ring to use for kiddushin, if such a conditional gift could not work, the owner must have intended whatever form of gift was sufficient. But according to our actual ruling that a gift on condition to return value can effect kiddushin, there is no basis to assume the owner intended to give more than necessary. We therefore interpret the transaction as the limited conditional gift.
