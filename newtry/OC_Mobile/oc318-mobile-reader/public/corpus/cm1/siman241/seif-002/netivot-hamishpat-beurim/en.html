According to what I wrote in siman 66, seif katan 12, every pledge is acquired d'oraisa at least to the extent that the creditor need not return it until his money is repaid, even where the special acquisition of R. Yitzchak does not apply. That limited right can itself be transferred d'oraisa. It is no worse than a debt instrument: although sale of the underlying debt may be rabbinic, the purchaser can have a d'oraisa right to hold the document itself. Only the creditor's right to collect the actual debt from the pledged property depends on the personal debt and on the property's lien as guarantor. Where the pledge was taken not at the time of the loan, R. Yitzchak can give the creditor a stronger proprietary right at collection. But with an ordinary pledge, the creditor's right to satisfy the debt from the pledge derives from shibud nechasim, which itself depends on shibud haguf. Since the personal debt cannot be transferred d'oraisa in the ordinary manner, the collection lien cannot be transferred d'oraisa either. This explains Shach in siman 72. When the creditor seeks actual collection from the pledge, he may need an oath and migo because he is extracting payment. But if he merely seeks to retain the pledge until payment, he is defending possession of the object itself. For that limited d'oraisa right, neither migo nor oath is required in the same way. Likewise, heirs holding a pledge can retain it in a case of legal doubt, as I explained in simanim 64, 66, and 72. Accordingly, waiver of the debt is effective to prevent collection from the pledge because waiver certainly removes the personal debt. Once shibud haguf disappears, the guarantor-like lien on property also falls, as seen in Bekhorot 48. But waiver does not necessarily require return of the physical pledge itself. The creditor's limited d'oraisa possessory right to hold the pledge until payment does not arise from shibud haguf in the same way. To surrender that proprietary right requires an actual kinyan rather than mere waiver. The same logic applies to a debt instrument according to the view that waiver alone does not force return of the document. He can no longer sue for the debt because shibud haguf has been waived, but may still retain the physical instrument. This also answers the question from an eved Ivri, where Kiddushin says the master can say before two witnesses, "Go free." Although the slave can be viewed as a kind of pledge, his body is not something the master may physically imprison. The master's right is the personal service obligation. Once that obligation is waived, nothing remains to hold.

The language of waiver applies only to money. Although the ruling itself is correct, Maharik's proof from the fact that waiver language does not free an eved Ivri is difficult. As Ketzot HaChoshen notes, even gift language does not suffice for an eved Ivri. Ketzot's explanation that the slave is literally in his master's domain is also unclear. The simple reason is this. When an object is already in the recipient's courtyard or hand, effective gift language can transfer it through that possession. If the object is not in his domain, mere words do not transfer it at all. An eved Ivri is different. His body is legally acquired to the master, and release requires a recognized acquisition mode, such as money or deed. If his body were not acquired but only personally obligated in service, then waiver of that service obligation would be enough, just like waiver of any personal lien. The slave is personally present and the obligation rests upon him. But once we conclude that his body is acquired, his body is more like land than movable property for this purpose. It is not acquired by mere courtyard language. It requires the specific Torah modes of money or deed for release. Although meshichah can acquire a Canaanite slave rabbinically, that is a special commercial rule for acquisition by another. Chazal did not institute ordinary meshichah for a slave to acquire himself from his master. His self-acquisition therefore requires the d'oraisa modes.
