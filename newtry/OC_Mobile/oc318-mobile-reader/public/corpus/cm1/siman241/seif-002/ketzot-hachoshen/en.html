In Kiddushin he explains that a loan secured by another person's collateral has continuing substance in the lender's hand: it is not converted into ordinary movables for the lender's heirs, it is not cancelled by Shemittah, and the borrower is not released merely by the lender waiving the debt until the collateral is returned. Accordingly, if the lender transfers or sells the secured claim, the recipient can rely on it because the lender cannot simply destroy it afterward by waiver. Rashba writes similarly. Since the lender has a proprietary interest in the collateral, he cannot simply waive the debt after transferring the secured right. Although Rashba discusses transfer to another person, his reasoning is not limited to a completed sale of the collateral itself. Ritva and Rashba follow one conceptual approach: while the collateral remains in the lender's hand, waiver of the debt alone does not release the borrower. Mordechai proves the opposite from the Hebrew servant. The Gemara asks why a document is needed to free him and why the master cannot simply say before two witnesses, "Go." It answers that a Hebrew servant's body is acquired. Mordechai infers that if his body were not acquired, then even though he resembles collateral, waiver would suffice. This proof can be answered. If the servant's body were not legally owned by the master, then the "collateral" would effectively be in the borrower's own possession, because the servant himself possesses his body. In that case the master could waive the debt, and the servant would thereby acquire the security interest resting on his own body. That is different from tangible collateral physically retained by the lender. Where the collateral remains in the lender's hand, waiver does not suffice under Ritva and Rashba. Since the lender is presently in possession of the collateral, it is difficult to remove it from him against these authorities.
The language of waiver applies only to money. Maharik writes that it is elementary that the language of waiver does not transfer existing movables. Waiver applies to a debt, where the creditor has only an intangible claim. If the property itself exists, one must use language of gift. He proves this from the Hebrew servant. The Gemara asks why a document is needed and why the master cannot simply say before two witnesses, "Go." Rava answers that the servant's body is acquired. Maharik understands this to mean that because the servant's body is existing property, waiver is ineffective. Only where the master has merely an intangible claim could waiver suffice. Maharik further argues that the fact the servant is physically in his own possession does not change this. Tosafot says that even a Canaanite slave can be considered in possession of himself for evidentiary purposes. Yet waiver still does not free him when his body is owned by the master. Rambam and Tur likewise distinguish between forgiving a debt and giving a deposit, implying that existing deposited property requires language of gift even when the property is already in the recipient's hands. The basic law is correct, but I do not understand Maharik's proof from the Hebrew servant. Even if waiver does not work on existing property, the master should be able to use language of gift, such as, "You are hereby your own," or the oral equivalent of the language written in an emancipation document. Tosafot in Gittin says that the Gemara's question, "Let him say before two witnesses, Go," means that he could orally say the same substantive words used in the document. The true reason a Hebrew servant cannot be freed merely orally is that his body is actually under the master's proprietary authority and does not leave that authority except through the Torah's prescribed mode. Even a document works through the special principle that his document and his hand arrive together. The question is therefore not merely that "waiver" is the wrong wording. It is that the master's bodily ownership cannot be extinguished by ordinary oral transfer. Maharik's conclusion nevertheless remains correct independently: an existing object is not transferred through mere waiver. A debt can be waived because only the personal claim is being extinguished. Existing property must be given through a valid transfer.
