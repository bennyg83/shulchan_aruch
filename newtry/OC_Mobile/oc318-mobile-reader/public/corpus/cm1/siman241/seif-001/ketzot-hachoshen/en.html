If both admit, because witnesses were created only to prevent falsehood. A major difficulty arises. In monetary law, an act can be effective without witnesses when both parties admit the truth. Yet kiddushin and divorce require two witnesses to the very act, even if both parties admit it, and this is derived by comparing the word "matter" in family law with the word "matter" in monetary law. If the monetary act itself stands without witnesses, how can the comparison generate an absolute witness requirement for kiddushin and divorce? Rashba asks this in Kiddushin and answers that the comparison is specifically to the monetary verse requiring two witnesses in a case of contradiction. Just as monetary fact is established by two witnesses when contested, family status requires two witnesses to the act even when the parties later agree. Shitah Mekubetzet gives a similar formulation. I still find this incomplete. Why assume that the word "matter" in family law refers to witnesses at the act itself rather than merely to a requirement of two witnesses when the parties later disagree, especially since one witness is generally believed in ritual prohibitions? The Gemara itself sharpens the problem. Rav Kahana says kiddushin before one witness is ineffective even if both admit it. Rav Ashi challenges him: if you derive "matter" from monetary law, then just as a party's admission is like one hundred witnesses in monetary law, perhaps the parties' admission should establish kiddushin too. Rav Kahana answers that monetary admission does not impose consequences on others, while kiddushin affects others by prohibiting relatives. If the entire source were merely a comparison to contested monetary testimony, why would the Gemara entertain the admission analogy at all? The answer follows what I wrote in siman 34. A party's admission is not merely a voluntary gift, as Mahari ibn Lev suggested. The Torah itself gives every person credibility concerning his own legal status and obligations, like one hundred witnesses. This is the same principle underlying "he rendered the object forbidden upon himself." Even people disqualified as witnesses against others, such as slaves or relatives, are believed about themselves. Rashi derives the rule from the verse concerning partial admission, "when he shall say, This is it," where the Torah relies on the defendant's own admission. This proves the force is evidentiary, not merely donative. Accordingly, the verse "by the mouth of two witnesses a matter shall stand" means that no legal matter is established without testimony. In monetary law the parties themselves can supply that testimony, because each is believed concerning himself like one hundred witnesses. Therefore ordinary monetary transactions need no outside witnesses when both parties agree. If later one denies, the other's own self testimony establishes enough to require the ordinary oath structure. This is analogous to a man who says, "I betrothed you before so and so, and they went overseas." His own admission can prohibit him concerning her relatives, because he is believed as to himself. Ran's discussion of signatures in divorce supports the broader concept that legal knowledge can function as testimony even without direct visual observation. If a signed get emerges from the woman's possession, the signature witnesses make the delivery legally known as though they themselves witnessed it. In kiddushin and divorce, however, the parties' admissions cannot function as the needed testimony because the act creates consequences for other people. Kiddushin prohibits relatives, and divorce likewise affects third party status. Therefore the parties themselves are not accepted as witnesses to establish the matter for the world. Two qualified witnesses are needed at the act so that the status becomes publicly knowable. This is why the Gemara's question is exact: perhaps, as in money, the parties' admission should count as one hundred witnesses and establish the act. Rav Kahana answers that in money the admission concerns only the parties themselves, while here it imposes consequences on others. Since their admission lacks that public evidentiary force, the act was never established as a "matter" under the Torah's witness requirement. Rashi therefore specifically explains in this sugya that admission is like one hundred witnesses from the verse "This is it," even though he often cites the rule without explanation elsewhere. The Gemara is not treating admission as gift or vow. It is testing whether the parties themselves can satisfy the Torah's witness requirement. The answer is that they can in money, but not in kiddushin and divorce because those statuses bind others. This resolves the comparison.
