One who gives a gift on condition that it be returned. Rosh writes concerning an etrog that it must be given as a complete gift on condition that it be returned, and after the recipient fulfills the mitzvah he must give it back through a fresh act of transfer. If the donor merely says, "It shall be yours until you fulfill the mitzvah and afterward automatically be mine as before," Rosh says the recipient does not fulfill his obligation because that resembles borrowing. Ritva likewise writes that a gift on condition of return is complete ownership in the recipient, and when he returns it a new acquisition is required, just as in any other transfer. He even says that if the recipient subjected the object to a creditor while it was his, the lien can attach and remain relevant after the return.
Were it not for Rosh and Ritva, I would understand a gift on condition of return more literally. The donor gives the recipient full ownership of the body only for the limited period needed to use it. The recipient owns it completely during that time, but the ownership itself is temporary and automatically reverts afterward. Rosh's concern that such a gift resembles borrowing is difficult. In Bava Batra the Gemara discusses, "This etrog is given to you as a gift and after you to so and so." It says that according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel the first recipient certainly fulfills the mitzvah. Since the body belongs to him during his period, the fact that another person will own it afterward does not make it borrowed property. If full bodily ownership for a limited period is enough when the next owner is another person, why should it be different when the later owner is the original donor? The distinction between borrowing and temporary ownership is clear. A borrower has only a right of use while the body remains the lender's. One who receives the body itself for a fixed period owns the body during that period. Accordingly, the phrase, "afterward it returns to me," can mean that the recipient owns the body until that time and then the donor's ownership resumes. A condition of return is still necessary. If the donor merely says, "afterward it is mine," under the ordinary law of successive gifts the first recipient might consume, sell, or otherwise exhaust the object, leaving nothing for the later owner. The condition of return limits that power by making the validity of the gift depend on preserving something to return. The gift is therefore full bodily ownership for the period, subject to the requirement that the object remain available afterward. This is also consistent with Rosh's discussion of a gift "after you" to another. Under Rabbi, if the first recipient destroys or sells the property, the condition favoring the second is violated. The second receives by force of the original donor, not through a new transfer from the first. A gift on condition of return can be structured in exactly that way: the original donor gives the body for a time, retaining the later interest in himself, with a condition that the recipient preserve the object for that later reversion. The fact that Ran in Nedarim describes temporary marriage as only a fruit like ownership does not undermine this. Marriage uniquely requires a perpetual marital bond. Monetary ownership need not be perpetual. Even a person who has the body for only an hour can be the full owner for that hour. There is a clear proof from communal etrogim. Beit Yosef cites Rashba that partners can fulfill the mitzvah with a jointly owned etrog through bereirah. Ran explains at length that each partner is treated as having ownership of the body during his period of use, even though after that hour the other partner owns it. If temporary bodily ownership suffices for the Torah requirement of "yours," then a gift on condition of return can likewise be full bodily ownership for the required time, notwithstanding that it later returns. Tosafot in Arakhin also writes that something destined to return to its owner, such as a loan, rental, or gift on condition of return, is not acquired through barter. If a gift on condition of return were perpetual ownership in the recipient followed by an entirely new gift back, it would be no different from any absolute gift and should be acquired through barter. Tosafot's classification therefore supports that the recipient's ownership is intrinsically temporary. I later found an early authority stating precisely this. Rabbi Avigdor Kohen Tzedek, cited in Rosh's responsa, writes that for the first day of Sukkot mere permission to use another's lulav is insufficient because Scripture requires full bodily ownership. A gift on condition of return nevertheless suffices because it gives bodily ownership for a limited time. It is called a fruit like acquisition only in the sense that the recipient may not consume the object, but during the relevant moment he possesses bodily ownership. The Torah requires that it be his at the time of taking, and that condition is satisfied. This greatly strengthens the present analysis. The Gemara's warning not to give a lulav to a minor who can acquire but cannot transfer must then concern an ordinary unrestricted gift where the adult relies on the minor later transferring it back. If the adult expressly retained a later reversion in himself and gave only temporary bodily ownership, the return could occur automatically and would not depend on the minor's capacity to transfer. The ordinary case is simply not structured that way.
