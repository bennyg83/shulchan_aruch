He consecrated it and returned it. I am troubled by this rule. Rosh in the chapter Lulav HaGazul writes that after using an item given as a gift on condition that it be returned, the recipient must actually transfer it back as a gift. He proves this from the rule not to give a lulav to a minor on the first day, because a minor can acquire but cannot transfer back. Rashba and Ritva say similarly. A gift on condition to return is a complete acquisition, and return itself requires a full reacquisition by the original giver. If so, once the recipient consecrates the object, how can he later transfer it back? A person cannot transfer property that no longer belongs to him. The condition has not been fulfilled. Why does the source mention specifically consecration? It should equally say that he sold or gifted it to another. In my view the case concerns consecration as a korban shelamim. We follow R. Yehudah that kodashim kalim can be the owner's property for certain monetary purposes, and a person can sell his shelamim in the limited sense that the purchaser receives the right of offering and the meat and hide. Since the item remains capable of a legal transfer, the recipient can make a complete reacquisition back to the original donor and thereby satisfy the condition. But with bedek habayit consecration or charity consecration, where the item no longer belongs to the recipient, return should not work. Nimukei Yosef, however, implies that even if he sold the object and later returned it, the condition can be fulfilled. That is very difficult, because he no longer owns the item when he purports to return it. Nimukei Yosef may follow Ran's reasoning in Gittin concerning a get given on condition that the woman not marry a certain man. Ran explains that where the condition can invalidate the get only at the moment the prohibited marriage takes effect, the legal structure can be understood as though the get temporarily ceases to operate only to the extent necessary for the marriage to take hold, then resumes. Although ordinarily "today you are not my wife and tomorrow you are my wife" does not work because once severed it remains severed, a condition can structure the legal effect differently. So too here. The donor gave the object on condition of return. The recipient never had power to transfer a greater right than he himself possessed. If he consecrates it, the consecration itself is therefore subject to the original return condition. At the moment necessary to transfer the object back, the later consecration or sale can be viewed as temporarily yielding so that the original condition can be fulfilled; afterward it can resume according to its own terms. Sale is even easier because parties can expressly create temporary interruption and later revival. Ketzot HaChoshen concludes that no new kinyan is needed when the recipient returns the object, because a temporary body-acquisition automatically expires. He argues Ran's statement in Nedarim that nonpermanent ownership is only fruit ownership applies only to marriage-like rights. In my view this is incorrect. Ran proves his principle from sale of land during the Jubilee system and the law of bikkurim. If the buyer had full body ownership for those years, he should be able to say, "the land that You gave me." Evidently, even in ordinary property law, ownership that is inherently temporary can have the legal character of fruit ownership. Ketzot also asks from Bava Batra 137, where one gives an etrog "to you, and after you to so-and-so," and the first person can fulfill the mitzvah. Rosh says it is effectively like a gift on condition that he give it to the next person afterward. Ketzot argues that "after you" means after death, when no acquisition can occur. There is no difficulty. An etrog has no ordinary consumptive fruit-use beyond fulfilling the mitzvah. The first person was therefore plainly given it only for the period needed to perform the mitzvah, as Rashba explains. "After you" means after your beneficial use has ended, not necessarily after death. Sema in siman 248, seif katan 16, likewise explains that "after you" can mean after the time when the first recipient no longer needs it. The same applies to a communal etrog. The communal arrangement can be treated as though, from the outset, each person receives it in turn as a gift on condition to return. Rashba expressly writes this. Therefore the primary ruling follows Rosh, Ritva, and the other poskim that a full kinyan is required when returning a gift on condition to return. According to what I wrote, there is no material difference between saying "return it to me" and merely "return it," except in a special case such as an animal consecrated as kodashim kalim. If the recipient merely sold or gifted it away before the return, that generally does not constitute return. If he merely encumbered it to a creditor, however, he can still transfer it back; the creditor may later collect because a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward.
To me and not to my heirs. Beit Yosef in siman 207 implies that if the donor dies and the condition required return specifically "to me," the original gift is void because the condition can no longer be fulfilled. Even according to authorities in Even HaEzer siman 143 who hold that where a condition was only for the other party's benefit, an external impediment can preserve the act, that does not help here. The recipient could have returned the object before the donor died and failed to do so. The inability to fulfill the condition is therefore treated as arising through his own delay. See Sema, seif katan 18, citing Rashba: if one says, "This field is given to you on condition that you give me five gold pieces each year," and the donor dies, sums already due during the donor's lifetime but unpaid are paid to the heirs. This is difficult, because Rama says that when the donor dies the gift itself is void, since "to me" excludes heirs. The case must therefore be one where the donor had converted the accrued sums into a debt before death, or one where the annual payments were not framed as a condition governing validity of the gift but as a separate personal obligation undertaken by the recipient. In that arrangement, the donor intended the annual obligation only for his lifetime, while amounts already accrued became ordinary debts payable to heirs. Nimukei Yosef implies the same in the analogous case, "on condition that the first tithe is mine," where the sale does not necessarily depend on the ongoing condition in the same manner.
To give him the value of the object. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 311, rules that he may substitute money only when the object is no longer extant, such as where it was stolen. If the object remains in existence, he must return the very object, because the condition concerned the object and not its value. Only in the case of two people who exchanged all their possessions did Terumat HaDeshen rule that the one with the surplus value can pay the difference in money and need not return the actual items. There it is evident that the parties' primary purpose was economic gain rather than attachment to the specific objects. Rama quotes this ruling in siman 203, seif 10, together with the reason. This shows that outside such circumstances the specific object must be returned. If the parties intended only value, they could have conditioned on a sum rather than on the object itself. Gittin likewise says that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel permits value only where the object is no longer present. Ketzot HaChoshen correctly adds that if the recipient sold or consecrated the object and thereby removed it from his control, this is treated as no longer extant and he can fulfill through value according to the relevant opinion. But if he merely vowed the object to charity, he must still return the object itself because charity has not yet acquired the body of the object; it remains legally present. Rosh further writes that in a gift on condition to return, the return must be voluntary. If beit din must compel the recipient to return it, the original gift is retroactively void.
