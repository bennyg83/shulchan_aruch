His sons give it to him. Beit Yosef, siman 207, cites Rashba that where a gift was made on condition that the recipient “give” something to the donor and the recipient dies, his sons may perform the condition. Rashba explains that the word “to me” may indicate personal insistence because the donor needs the thing himself, but the bare expression “that you give” does not ordinarily imply insistence on the original recipient personally so long as the required thing is given. Immediately afterward Beit Yosef cites Ritva in the name of Ramah concerning the rule “to me and not to my heirs.” From the case of a loan secured by land conditioned on “if you do not bring me the money within three years, the field is mine,” Ramah infers that both terms matter: “to me” excludes the lender's heirs and “you” excludes the borrower's heirs. At first glance this conflicts with Rashba. The sources may be reconciled by distinguishing “you shall give,” where the word “you” is superfluous and therefore shows personal insistence, from the ordinary phrase “that you give,” where there is no extra word and no reason to infer that only the original recipient may perform. On this reading, Ritva and Rabbeinu Yerucham agree with Rashba: a condition phrased simply as “that you give” may be performed by the recipient's heirs, but an emphatic “you shall give” may not. This yields a practical qualification to Rama's statement here that the sons give it to the donor. It applies to the ordinary phrase, not necessarily to an emphatic personal formulation. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer siman 38, independently raises the apparent contradiction in Beit Yosef and suggests this distinction. Chavot Yair, siman 91, also considered it, although he later thought the language of Rashba and the sugya did not support the reconciliation and understood Ritva actually to disagree with Rashba. If so, Rama's unqualified ruling here might be limited to a case where the recipient had already taken possession of the field, so that even if the issue is a doubt of law, the field cannot be removed from him. Chavot Yair further writes that the rule that the sons may perform applies where they inherited money or movable value from the recipient. If he left only the very house or field that is the subject of the transaction, they may be treated like outsiders with respect to performance. He also formulates two broader principles. First, the inferences “to me and not to my heirs” or “you and not your heirs” are characteristic of formal conditions, where parties are presumed to be precise in the wording of the condition. They do not ordinarily apply to an unconditional sale in which the purchase price simply remains a debt due immediately or later. Second, one makes such restrictive inferences from the words of the condition itself, not necessarily from the descriptive language of the underlying gift or sale. Finally, these inferences are generally employed for the benefit of the heirs or successors of the person who imposed the condition. They are not ordinarily used against him where he himself formulated the condition in a way that would defeat his own rights.
