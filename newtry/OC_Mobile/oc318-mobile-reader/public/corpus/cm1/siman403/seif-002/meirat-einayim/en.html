This applies specifically where the injured party became aware, etc. Meaning, once the injured party knew of the carcass, he should have attended to selling it before it spoiled and declined in value. Even if it fell into a pit, where the damager must pay the expense of raising it and delivering it to the injured party at its assessed value, as the Tur and Mechaber write immediately below in seif 3, the damager is obligated only to bear the expense paid to those who raise it from the pit. The effort to arrange its removal falls upon the injured party, who then recovers the expense from the damager. The Tur and Beit Yosef write that this is the consensus of the authorities.
If its market value declined. Meaning, the carcass itself did not physically deteriorate from its condition at death; rather, the market price of meat fell.
Even if he did not know. Knowledge makes no difference regarding a market decline. Only where the carcass deteriorates through spoilage, which ordinarily results from delay, do we say that had he known he certainly would have attended to selling it. A market decline, however, is not something he would necessarily have anticipated.
If it was a tam, he pays one quarter. We always calculate the gain based on the original valuation. Initially the animal was worth two hundred, and in that two hundred the damager effectively has a one-quarter interest in the injured ox, namely fifty, since a tam pays only half damages. Therefore he receives only one quarter of the later increase in value.
This is the meaning of "and they shall also divide the dead animal." Meaning, they divide the increase in value of the carcass, as Rambam states explicitly there. We derive this from the extra word "also," since the verse could have said simply, "and they shall divide the dead animal." Although the damager bears only the depreciation, as explained above, he shares in the increase, because the Torah had compassion on the damager, since he himself did not cause the damage but his ox did.
We do not say that the damager takes half the appreciation and profits. See Perishah and Derishah, where I explained and proved that although in such a case he does not receive a share in the appreciation, he is nevertheless also exempt from paying the injured party for the original damage caused by his ox. For example, an ox worth five sela'im gored an ox worth five sela'im; when the owner learned of the damage, the injured ox was worth only three sela'im, but afterward it recovered in value until it was worth thirty zuz, namely six sela'im. Since after the appreciation the injured party has suffered no present loss, the damager need not pay. This differs from the rule at the beginning of the following siman, where an ox gored another and diminished its value, yet the animal remained alive and later appreciated to twice its original value. There the damager still pays according to the damage at the time of the goring, because the injured party can say, "Had your ox not gored it, this later increase in flesh and size would have accrued to me in even greater measure," since live animals naturally appreciate through growth and fattening, and an uninjured animal normally appreciates more than an injured and sick one. Here, by contrast, once the animal is dead, a rise in the market price of meat is unusual and depends on fortune. One cannot say that had it not been killed, its value would necessarily have doubled.
