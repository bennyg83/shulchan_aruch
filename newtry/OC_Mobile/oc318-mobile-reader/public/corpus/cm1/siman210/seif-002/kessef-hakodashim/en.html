Seif 3. You and this animal. The law of 'acquire, you and a donkey' requires further study. Rashi in Arakhin 29b writes that if the principal sale is ineffective, movables cannot be acquired incidentally through it. Perhaps Rashi and Tosafot said this only according to the view that where one transfers something not yet in existence together with something already in existence, the entire transfer fails, or according to the view that the two cannot be separated through the principle of 'since one is acquired.' Even the view that the existing item is acquired may concede regarding Yovel that the principle cannot preserve the transfer of the other component. The Gemara's kal vachomer that property already sold must go out in Yovel may reveal that the Torah intended the sale to terminate entirely in all cases, or perhaps that there is also an independent prohibition. This requires study. Tosafot there, on 30a, write that chalipin works only for a complete acquisition, not for an interest destined to be returned. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 195 cites this and asks that Boaz's kinyan sudar also involved land destined to return at Yovel. Perhaps Tosafot mean only where the limitation is intrinsic to the grantor's own conveyance. By contrast, at Yovel the seller conveys absolutely, and the Torah itself commands that the land return. Even if acquisition of fruits is not acquisition of the body and the buyer cannot read the declaration of bikkurim when Yovel is approaching, the seller's own conveyance contains no defect. It is also possible that attached produce requiring the land was included and was fully transferable. Yet that does not fully answer why chalipin should work on the land itself. Perhaps Tosafot hold that a sale for a fixed term is still a true sale and may be acquired by chalipin, while a loan for use or a rental is not a full sale, even though 'rental for its day is a sale' for some rules. A rented courtyard, for example, does not necessarily acquire ownerless property for the tenant, as the Shach writes in siman 313. Thus, for several laws rental is not a complete sale and resembles the acquisition of a mere benefit. If Tosafot's discussion there applies also to a fixed-term sale, then according to that view even a sale for a limited period would not be acquired by chalipin, and this requires study. The statement at the beginning of the chapter HaKotev that they made a kinyan from the husband, although if the wife dies he later inherits her and he may sometimes retain rights in fruits of fruits, does not necessarily conflict. Inheritance is a separate legal event, and fruits of fruits are a newly arising object; in the original conveyance he did not reserve them for himself. Ketzot in siman 195 adduces proofs that sale itself may constitute a benefit. Perhaps Tosafot speak where the buyer had already made clear that he wanted the transaction and the kinyan sudar was performed in his absence. The same would apply to standard acquisition documents and instruments reciting 'and we made a kinyan from him,' where there is ordinarily prior disclosure of intent by both parties. This requires study.
