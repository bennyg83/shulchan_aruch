If the beneficiary was his son, he acquired, etc. See the Rosh in the chapter Almanah LeKohen Gadol, who cites the Raavad's objection to the Rif. The Rif rules like the Sages that an inheritance which comes automatically is not acquired by a fetus, even though we rule in the chapter Mi Shemet that one who grants property to his own unborn child effects the grant. The Raavad argues that if a gift to one's unborn son is effective, certainly an inheritance that comes automatically should be effective. The Ramban answers for the Rif that the rule that a person's mind is especially close to his son was stated only regarding a person on his deathbed. His answer does not appear correct to me. The Rif's position is clear. At the beginning of the sugya, when the Gemara sought to explain the Mishna on the basis that a fetus can acquire, it stated that one who grants to a fetus generally does not effect acquisition because a fetus has no legal capacity to acquire, while an inheritance coming automatically does vest through the law of inheritance. At the conclusion, however, the Gemara holds that one who grants to a fetus does not effect acquisition and explains our Mishna by saying that a person's mind is especially close to his son. Thus a fetus itself has no independent capacity to acquire, and an inheritance coming automatically likewise does not vest, in accordance with Rava, who disputes Abaye there. The Mishna is different because a person's mind is close to his son, and we are certain that he fully resolved to give to him through a legally effective method. It is as though he declared that he had transferred it to his son in an effective manner. Even according to the view that a direct grant to a fetus is ineffective, there is certainly a lawful method of transferring property for a fetus's benefit, such as acquisition on condition that it later be transferred to him, as stated at the end of Nedarim 48b. Nimukei Yosef cites the Ritva accordingly, and the Rosh in Nedarim cites the Ramban. Even according to the Rosh's ruling like Rav Ashi, the Ran explains there that if the grantor says, 'Acquire from now on condition that you transfer it,' Rav Ashi agrees. Since a person's mind is close to his son, his declaration is treated as though he acknowledged that he had transferred to his son through such an effective arrangement. Even if no particular formal method were available, the closeness of his mind to his son causes the law to treat the non-acquiring fetus as though it acquired. Outside this special case, however, neither a grant to a fetus nor an inheritance coming automatically is effective. This is clearly the Rif's view and his ruling is primary. The great authorities raised needless objections, for his words are sound. The Ramban further writes that the Rif places the Mishna concerning a deathly ill person next to the rule that a person's mind is close to his son, implying that the rule applies only to a person on his deathbed. This does not appear correct. Were that the Rif's intent, he should have placed the statement that the Mishna concerns a deathly ill person after the rule about closeness to one's son. Rather, the Rif intended to explain the Mishna's wording, 'One who says,' meaning that a mere oral declaration suffices for one on his deathbed, whereas a healthy person requires barter or another effective acquisition. Accordingly, the Tur's statement below in siman 253:42 in the Rif's name is also incorrect. The Rif's statement that the Mishna concerns a deathly ill person excludes only a healthy person's bare oral declaration, not a healthy person's transfer through an acquisition. This appears entirely clear in the Rif. See also what I wrote below in siman 253:27. The Rambam's view is likewise clear. In chapter 22 of Hilchot Mechirah he writes without qualification that one who grants to a fetus does not effect acquisition, but if it is his son, because a person's mind is close to his son, the son acquires. He does not limit this to a person on his deathbed. Yet in chapter 8 of Hilchot Terumot he appears to rule like the first Tanna that an inheritance coming automatically does not vest in a fetus. The Raavad objects there according to his approach against the Rif, arguing that if a fetus may receive a gift from his father because the father's mind is close to him, certainly he should inherit automatically. But there is no difficulty, for the Mishna's special rule does not rest upon the fetus's own capacity to acquire. Nor can the Ramban's limitation to a person on his deathbed explain the Rambam, since the Rambam states the law without qualification. The Maggid Mishneh explicitly explains that the Rambam rules like Rabbi Yochanan and notes that some limit the law to a person on his deathbed while others do not; he states that the Rambam follows the latter view and that this is primary because the Gemara states the rule without qualification. Although the Yerushalmi in Yevamot distinguishes between a person on his deathbed and a healthy person, and between movable property and land, we do not follow that Yerushalmi because the Bavli in the chapter Mi Shemet makes no such distinction. Indeed, no Bavli source or accepted posek distinguishes between movable property and land here. Moreover, throughout the Bavli's initial discussion the Mishna was understood to apply even to a healthy person, and although the Gemara ultimately rests the rule upon the closeness of a person's mind to his son, it never retracts the assumption that the Mishna includes a healthy person. The Semag likewise states without qualification that if the fetus is his son, the father's closeness of mind causes him to acquire, and the other poskim also do not distinguish. Thus the law applies even to a healthy person. See Even HaEzer 41:1 and Responsa Rabbi Shmuel Kohen, part 3, siman 94.
