If he said, "When he is born." Rabbeinu Tam, Rashbam, and Rosh rule like Rav Nachman that one who transfers to a fetus does not effect present acquisition, but if he says, "When the child is born, he shall acquire," it can work. Rashbam explains that although ordinary future fruit cannot be transferred even by saying, "When it comes into existence," a fetus is already partially in the world. The defect is mainly that it presently lacks legal capacity to acquire. Accordingly, if no fetus exists at all, a transfer to "the child who will later be born" does not work even if the donor says, "When he is born." This is no better than selling future palm fruits with language postponing the acquisition until they appear. This explains the Gemara's question concerning a three party arrangement. If the mechanism is treated as though the obligor had said, "I obligate myself to you and to whoever comes through you," why can it transfer even to people not yet born? The answer cannot simply be that the transfer is deemed effective when they are born, because a person not yet conceived is wholly nonexistent and ordinary future acquisition language is ineffective. The three party arrangement is therefore a special enactment without an ordinary legal rationale. Rav Hai Gaon, cited by Rashba, distinguishes sharply between the acquired object and the recipient. He holds that the object being transferred must exist fully, but a fetus as recipient can be enough because the recipient is already partially in existence. This is why he can rule that a recognizable fetus receiving later at birth can acquire, while still ruling that a fetus cannot be betrothed as future property in the same way. Thus even according to Rav Nachman, the recipient is only stronger than the future object when the recipient already exists partially, such as a recognizable fetus. A person not conceived at all remains outside the rule. This resolves the apparent contradiction with Rosh's statement that a three party arrangement is a halachah without an ordinary rationale.
If he is his son. Maharam of Rothenburg writes that although the halachah follows Rabbi Yochanan that one may transfer to his own fetus because a person's intention is especially close to his son, this does not mean the fetus already acquires immediately and can transmit property while still in the womb. Ramban explains that the special rule for a deathly ill father is that the child acquires only when born, in a manner resembling inheritance, in order not to distress the deathly ill person. Since the fetus does not acquire until birth, there is no case of an unborn fetus transmitting the gift onward while still in the womb.
