He said, "when it is born." See Sema, seif katan 1, who writes that the authorities who disagree here also disagree concerning property not yet in existence. The Taz already questioned this. However, the Taz's statement that they regard a fetus itself as something not yet in existence is difficult, because the Rosh in Kiddushin 63 writes that with respect to sale of a fetus, even once the pregnancy is recognizable it is still treated as property not yet in existence. The principal reason is stated explicitly by Tosafot and the Rosh: according to Rav Nachman, conveying to a recipient who does not yet exist is a greater defect than conveying property that does not yet exist. Even though saying "when the property comes into existence" does not help for future property, saying "when he is born" can help where the recipient himself does not yet exist. There is, however, a difficulty with Maharit, Choshen Mishpat responsum 23, who writes that although Nazir 12 says one cannot presently appoint an agent for an act one cannot presently perform, if the principal expressly says that the agency should take effect when the matter becomes possible, the agency is valid. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 9 of Hilchot Ishut, law 6, cites this. If so, here, where one grants property to a fetus through another person and the donor does not retract, why should the fetus not acquire immediately upon birth? Acquisition on behalf of another operates through agency, and the Ran in several places, including the Mishnah of one who sees people running, writes that in monetary matters agency of the owner of the property is required. Since the owner expressly appointed the person as his agent to acquire for the fetus once born, why would the fetus not acquire at birth? One must say that this opinion holds that zechiyah is not based on agency. Since the recipient cannot presently acquire, the act of zechiyah cannot be suspended and revive later.
And if it was his son. See Shach, seif katan 1, who explains that it is as though the father said, "I acknowledge that I conveyed this to my son by a present kinyan on condition that you later transfer it to him." This is difficult. The plain meaning of the Gemara and authorities is that the very zechiyah now held by the intermediary becomes effective for the fetus when it is born, and it also appears necessary that the donor still be alive and not have retracted before the birth. According to the Shach's explanation, the intermediary acquires the property for himself immediately, on condition that he later convey it to the fetus. A condition is a separate matter, while the principal acquisition would be to the intermediary. When the intermediary later conveys to the child, he should need a new kinyan, such as kinyan sudar or zechiyah through a third party, because a person cannot acquire his own property for another merely by holding it. Moreover, even if the donor dies or retracts before the child is born, the intermediary could still convey it, since he already acquired it subject only to performing the condition. If he refuses to make a new kinyan, the child would not acquire and the gift would return to the donor. If the original transfer to the intermediary was through kinyan sudar, it should still work because the first acquisition was to the intermediary himself. And if the gift remains in the intermediary's hands, the child should need to prove that after birth the intermediary actually conveyed it, because with an affirmative condition the recipient bears the burden to show performance. The Shach must therefore understand that in "acquire on condition to convey" no new kinyan is required, but the wording itself does not naturally imply this, for "on condition to convey" sounds as though the intermediary himself must later perform a conveyance. His further statement that the Sages treated one who cannot acquire as though he can merely because a person's mind is close to his son is also difficult. The Raavad's proof appears correct. A fetus must possess some capacity to acquire, but ordinarily another person's fetus does not acquire because the donor does not fully resolve to convey to it. Toward his own child, where his mind is close to him, he does fully resolve to convey. If a fetus had no capacity to acquire at all, even full intent would not help because there would be no recipient in existence. Since it has acquisition capacity, all the more so it can have the status of an heir. Beit Yosef asks what practical difference there is whether a fetus can inherit, since Niddah 46 discusses inheritance and answers that sometimes the mother dies first, leaving uncertainty, and the rule would be that the claimant bears the burden. This is very difficult, for siman 280 states that where a house falls upon a person and his mother, the property is presumed to belong to the mother's heirs. More fundamentally, there are many practical differences. If the fetus is an heir, the other heirs do not acquire its share until after the fetus dies. If one of them betroths a woman with the fetus's share before the fetus dies, the kiddushin is ineffective. If the fetus is not an heir, the other heirs own the property immediately, and if one betroths a woman with it and the fetus later dies, the kiddushin was effective retroactively.
