We compel the borrower. The Sema, citing Maharik, limits this to where the borrower is not already going to that location for another reason. The Shach says there is no rationale for the limitation. It can be explained from Mordechai in the name of Riva. Other claimants, such as a robbed or injured party, can also sometimes compel the defendant to litigate in the claimant's location, even though the verse, "the borrower is servant to the lender," does not apply. The reason is that the claimant is not expected to spend money uselessly in pursuing his claim when, even if he wins, the defendant need not reimburse litigation expenses. The defendant, by contrast, is not heard when he chooses to spend money merely to make litigation more difficult. Accordingly, where the claimant is already traveling there and incurs no special expense for the litigation, that rationale disappears. In an ordinary lender and borrower case, however, the Gemara's reason that the borrower is servant to the lender applies even where the lender incurs no added expense, such as where he is traveling there anyway. This also resolves Maharik's question against the Geonim. If all claimants can compel a defendant, why does the Gemara need the special reason concerning borrower and lender? The answer is that other claimants can compel only because they are actually spending money to pursue the claim. A lender can compel even without that factor. Therefore, as a practical resolution, in lender and borrower cases the law follows Bach and Shach against Sema, while for other claimants the Sema's limitation following Maharik is correct.
