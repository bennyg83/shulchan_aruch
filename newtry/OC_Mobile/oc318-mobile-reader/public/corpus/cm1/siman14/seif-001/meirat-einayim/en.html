Let us go up to the great beit din. The Mechaber copies Rambam, who does not distinguish between the great beit din and a regional gathering place of scholars. Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur distinguish: concerning a regional court, the claimant may compel transfer where there is substance to his claim, but the defendant may not, even if he offers to pay the expenses. Concerning the great beit din, however, even the defendant may sometimes demand transfer. Rama alludes to this later when he says that regarding a claim to go to the great beit din there is no distinction between claimant and defendant. He abbreviates because nowadays such compulsory transfer is generally not practiced.
For what reason did you judge me, etc. See below, seif 4, where Rama explains that they need not write all reasons and proofs, only the claims and ruling, with further distinctions. Here the case is one in which he was compelled to litigate in his city. In that circumstance all agree he is entitled to a written record sufficient for review, so Rama does not present a dispute here.
Afterward they extract, etc. Meaning they collect from him immediately. If he later brings a written ruling from the great beit din showing that the local court erred, the money is returned to him, as explained below and in Darkei Moshe.
If they needed to ask, etc. Even if the local judges do not know the answer and must send the question elsewhere, the litigant cannot say, 'Since they themselves do not know and we must spend money on messengers, let us go ourselves to the great beit din.' The local court remains the court of judgment.
They judge them in their city according to, etc. See above, siman 13, seif 6. The great beit din may answer the legal question, but the actual judgment is rendered by the local judges, because the litigants must stand before the judges who decide their case.
This one claims and that one claims. Rambam's wording distinguishes ordinary disputes, where both sides' claims are of equal procedural standing, from a lender against a borrower and analogous cases. In a loan, because 'the borrower is servant to the lender,' the lender's claim is given greater procedural weight. The same applies to an injured person or robbed person whose allegation is supported by evidence as described later. In ordinary disputes such as partners suing one another, rent claims, or other reciprocal monetary claims, neither party can force the other to go to the great beit din. Thus 'in ordinary cases this one claims and that one claims' means that where the claims are procedurally equal, neither can compel transfer. By contrast, in a loan or comparable injury or robbery claim, the lender or injured claimant may have that right. Rama later cites Rabbeinu Tam, the Rosh, and the Tur, who distinguish instead between a great beit din and a regional gathering place. According to them a defendant may also demand the great beit din, while only a claimant can compel travel to a regional court, because one should not spend a maneh to litigate over a maneh. Rama's statement that an important court in each generation may count as the great beit din is written as a preliminary clarification for this latter view. Likewise, when he says that the regional court must be in the same country, this limitation concerns the ordinary gathering place, not the great beit din of the generation.
The correct reading is. 'Or when the lender says: let us litigate,' etc.
And the borrower says. 'Let us go,' etc. If the lender is in any event going there, the borrower may compel the case to be heard there, as Ramban writes.
They compel the borrower, etc. This applies where the lender is not otherwise traveling there, as Maharik writes.
If the claimant has witnesses or proof and it appears to beit din, etc. This combines Rambam's wording with that of the Rosh and Tur. Rambam writes concerning the great beit din that if there are witnesses or proof, and from this we infer the same principle for present-day practice. The Rosh and Tur write that if the claimant lays out his claim before the local court and the judges see substance in it, they may allow transfer. Rama's wording therefore means either that he has concrete witnesses or proof, or that the local court finds real substance in his presented claims.
So long as there is a beit din in the city, etc. Mahari writes that this follows prevailing practice. He also writes that anyone affiliated with the same cemetery is regarded as belonging to that beit din and that these questions depend greatly on what the judges see fit in the circumstances.
Until the third day. This applies where the person offers some plausible reason for delay. Then he is given until the third day, corresponding to the Talmudic allowance for people traveling to and from villages. If he is continuously present in the city with no excuse, he may be placed under ban on the first day, as explained at the beginning of siman 11.
And they immediately select judges. The source says the parties choose the judges at once, on the first day, so that the third day cannot become an excuse for additional delay.
Even if the defendant had money, etc. The cited responsum concerned a defendant who had money on deposit in another city. If the law permits attachment there, he must litigate in the place where the money is attached. The correct text therefore is that even if the defendant had money on deposit in the claimant's city or another city.
Unless he can attach his money, etc. Meaning unless the law allows pre-judgment attachment because the claimant knows that otherwise the defendant will dissipate his assets, leave, or become unavailable for judgment. Once the money has been lawfully attached there, if the defendant wants it released he must litigate where it is attached.
He must notify, etc. Meaning the claimant must pay the messenger's expense. Beit Yosef cites Maharik; see also Darkei Moshe to siman 106.
Then he must litigate where his money is located. Maharik writes that the practice developed to attach a defendant's funds wherever found, which might imply that litigation also occurs there. Rama's later wording in siman 73, however, does not clearly support that conclusion.
