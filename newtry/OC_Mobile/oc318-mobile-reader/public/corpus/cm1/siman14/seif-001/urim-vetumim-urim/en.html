They compel him, and he is judged in his own city. The Mechaber follows Rambam, who treats a central gathering of sages and the Great Beit Din as one category. He therefore follows Sanhedrin, which says that the defendant cannot insist, "Let us go to the gathering of sages," while the plaintiff may make such a demand, as the Shulchan Aruch states explicitly. Tosafos and Rosh distinguish between the two. A defendant may not force the plaintiff to travel to a general gathering of sages, but he may demand adjudication by the Great Beit Din. Rama alludes to this later when he writes that some say there is no difference between plaintiff and defendant when the claim is, "Let us go to the Great Beit Din." Sema. See Tumim, where I reconcile Rambam with the Gemara in Bava Kamma and conclude that if the plaintiff has already opened his case before the Great Beit Din, all agree that the defendant may insist that the litigation continue there.
And afterward they collect from him. Meaning immediately after the judgment and its reasoning have been written. If he later proves that the judges erred, the judgment is reversed, as explained above in siman 25. Sema.
If it was necessary to ask, etc. We do not say that because the local judges must in any event send the question to the Great Beit Din, the plaintiff and defendant should themselves be sent there for final adjudication. The ruling teaches otherwise. Sema. His intent is to explain why this point is repeated after it was already stated above in siman 13, seif 6.
When this one claims and that one claims, or when the lender says, "Let us be judged here," etc. Sema and Perishah reject the simple explanation that "this one claims and that one claims" means each asserts that the other owes him, because even in such a case there may still be a lender and borrower relationship. For example, one says, "I lent you," and the other says, "You hold collateral of mine worth more." Sema therefore explains that the first clause concerns other types of claims, such as unpaid rent, or the price of a house or merchandise, where one says, "You owe me," and the other says, "I paid." In such cases we do not compel the defendant to travel to the Great Beit Din. But where the relationship is clearly that of lender and borrower, injured party and tortfeasor, or robbed party and robber, the borrower, tortfeasor, or robber may be compelled to follow the claimant to the Great Beit Din. This interpretation is strained and requires study. A more plausible explanation is that where the defendant entirely denies that he was ever indebted, saying, for example, "You never lent me anything," or "I held even more of your property than you claim," he never entered the status of "the borrower is servant to the lender," and therefore cannot be compelled to travel. But if he admits that at one point he borrowed, robbed, or caused damage, and only afterward claims, "I paid you," or "I had property of yours worth more," then because he admits that he once was the claimant's debtor, the presumption of "the borrower is servant to the lender" applies and he must follow him to the Great Beit Din. This fits Rambam and the Mechaber more naturally, though it requires study.
And the borrower says, "Let us go," etc. The lender is not compelled because of the burden and expense. But if the lender is traveling there anyway and therefore incurs no additional burden or expense, the borrower's request to be judged there is heard. Responsa Ramban, cited by Sema.
They compel the borrower, etc. Maharik and Sema write that if the lender is traveling there anyway and asks the borrower to accompany him, the borrower is not compelled. Bach, Bnei Shmuel, and Shach object that Maharik's proof from Ramban's responsum is no proof. They are correct about the proof, but Maharik's conclusion may nevertheless stand on independent reasoning. When the lender would otherwise have no reason to travel, we say that since he himself is willing to bear his own burden and expense, why should we spare the borrower, who is subordinate to the lender? But when the lender is already going there and bears no extra burden at all, perhaps the principle does not apply. It may be unreasonable that because the lender happens to travel there he can impose an otherwise unnecessary journey on the borrower.
Some say that an important beit din in every generation, etc. The Mechaber's wording, following Rambam, already says that even today, when there is no Great Beit Din, great sages publicly recognized as unmatched in the locality of the lender and borrower have the status of the Great Beit Din. Sema therefore explains that Rama's statement is tied to the later opinion of Tosafos distinguishing a general gathering of sages from the Great Beit Din. According to Rambam, who equates them, Rama adds nothing. This is strained. It appears more likely that Rambam means that even today one may demand adjudication before great sages within the same country, as Rama later states in the name of Maharik, but not in another country. A true Great Beit Din may be demanded even across countries. Bach writes this explicitly. Rama therefore adds that according to some authorities an outstanding beit din of the generation has the status of the Great Beit Din even today, so one may demand travel from one country to another.
And it appears to beit din, etc. The connecting word divides the alternatives. Either the claimant actually clarifies his basis, or beit din independently sees substance in his argument even without formal proof and witnesses. Sema.
Some say that regarding the claim, "Let us go to the Great Beit Din," etc. This is Tosafos and Rosh, who distinguish a general gathering of sages from the Great Beit Din. Only regarding the former is there a difference between plaintiff and defendant. Regarding the Great Beit Din of the generation, even the defendant may demand that the case be heard there.
So long as there is a beit din, etc. Persons belonging to the cemetery maintained by the townspeople are likewise subject to the beit din of that town. Everything depends on the judge's assessment. Piskei Mahari and Sema.
Until the third day, etc. Meaning after three summonses. See what I wrote above in siman 11 explaining this custom according to the Rosh's text.
And they shall immediately select judges. Meaning at the first summons they should already select the judges, so that no further delay occurs when the third summons arrives. Sema.
He must notify him. The messenger's fee and similar expenses are borne by the plaintiff. Sema.
A father who has, etc. The same applies to a student and his teacher. This presumably means his primary teacher. See Yoreh Deah, laws of Torah study.
A wealthy person established as forceful, etc. At first glance this sounds limited to a person known to be violent. However, Tashbetz, and Knesset HaGedolah citing several responsa, write that "forceful" is not literal. If in his own city the litigant is a person of great wisdom and stature, or even without exceptional scholarship most townspeople defer to him, the opposing party may demand adjudication in another city. Shev Yaakov likewise rules this way, and it is correct. Rama in responsum 73 writes that although the custom is generally that the plaintiff follows the defendant even to a beit din less distinguished than that of the plaintiff's city, if the plaintiff happens to find the defendant in the plaintiff's own city he may compel him to be judged there.
