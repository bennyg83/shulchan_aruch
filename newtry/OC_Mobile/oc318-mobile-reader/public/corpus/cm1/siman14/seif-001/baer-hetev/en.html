They judged me. Here the case concerns one who was compelled to litigate in his own city. All agree that the judges must write for him the grounds on which they ruled. Rama therefore did not record a dispute here, unlike seif 4, which does not concern a case adjudicated by compulsion. Sema.
In their city. He cannot say: since these judges do not know how to decide the case and we must incur the expense of sending inquiries, let us instead go ourselves before the Great Beit Din. The law teaches that he cannot demand this. See above, siman 13, seif 6. Sema.
They compel. The Sema writes that this applies where the lender is not otherwise traveling there, citing Maharik. The Shach disagrees, writing that there is no reason for such a distinction. The rule belongs to the preceding case: if the borrower says, "Let us go to the Great Beit Din," he cannot compel the lender. If, however, the lender is already traveling there, then the borrower may compel him to litigate there as well. The Bach explains similarly and rules that the lender may compel the borrower to go to the Great Beit Din even if that location lies on the lender's route but not on the borrower's. The Sema questions why claims of lender and borrower should be treated differently from other claims. He explains that only in a lender-borrower case is the lender's claim given greater weight because "the borrower is servant to the lender." The same applies to a victim of damage or robbery, whose claim is given weight because, according to his allegation, and where there is also testimony or other proof supporting him, as the conclusion indicates, the opposing party directly damaged or robbed him. Therefore his request to go to the Great Beit Din is heard. The same applies to comparable substantial claims. But in ordinary disputes, such as between partners, the defendant's claim has equal standing to the claimant's, and neither can force the other to go with him to the Great Beit Din. This is what the Mechaber means by saying that "in other cases, this one claims and that one claims," meaning that their respective positions are equal, unlike lender and borrower, and similarly unlike a robbery or damage claim. Thus the Sema.
And it appears. The Sema writes that this phrase should be understood as "or it appears," with the vav functioning disjunctively. See there.
To delay him. In these matters there is no fixed rule beyond what the judge perceives from the circumstances. Sema.
A third. This applies only where he offers some plausible excuse for the delay. If he is continuously present in the city and simply refuses, he is placed under nidui on the first day. Sema.
Immediately. Meaning on the first day, so that the matter will not be postponed until the third day. Sema.
To restrain. Maharik writes that the custom developed to attach a defendant's money wherever it may be found. According to this, it would appear that the case should then be adjudicated in the place where the money was attached. However, Rama's words below in siman 73, seif 10, do not imply this. See there.
