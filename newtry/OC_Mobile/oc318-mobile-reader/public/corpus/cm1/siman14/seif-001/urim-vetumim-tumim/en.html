We will go up to the Great Beit Din, etc. Tosafot ask that in the chapter Zeh Borer the Gemara says that when a litigant demands to go to the beit vaad, the defendant is not heard, whereas in Bava Kamma, in the chapter HaGozel, even the defendant can say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." Tosafot therefore distinguish between a beit vaad and the Great Beit Din, as explained in Urim. Rambam, however, does not distinguish between the two. His position therefore requires explanation. The Gemara says that where proceedings have not yet been opened, testimony is not accepted outside the litigant's presence because he can say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." It then asks: once proceedings have been opened, why can he not still say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din"? The Gemara answers that the case involves a letter from the Great Beit Din. Rashi explains that the Great Beit Din sent a written directive to the local court authorizing it to compel the defendant to litigate there. Rambam evidently understands that the letter cannot have said explicitly, "You must litigate only in this city." If it had, then even before proceedings began the defendant could not say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din," because the Great Beit Din itself had ordered him to litigate locally. He could not disobey their instruction. Rather, the letter must have said in substance: see that the matter is adjudicated according to Torah law. Either adjudicate it locally, or if he chooses to appear before the Great Beit Din, see that he goes there without delaying the case. Accordingly, when the Gemara initially asks that even after proceedings begin he can still say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din," it could have asked more fundamentally that a defendant ordinarily has no right to compel the plaintiff to go there. Instead it asks a stronger question based on either possibility. If the defendant has such a right, why should commencement of proceedings remove it? And if he has no such right, then even before proceedings begin why should his statement "I will go to the Great Beit Din" prevent the local court from acting? The answer is that the plaintiff had already initiated the matter before the Great Beit Din and obtained their letter. Before local proceedings begin, the defendant can say: "Since you began before the superior court and they authorized either forum, I will go there. You cannot begin with the greater court and then force me to remain before the lesser." But once he has already accepted the Great Beit Din's instruction by beginning proceedings in the local court, he can no longer reverse course and demand the superior court. Thus in an ordinary case where the plaintiff did not first bring a directive from the Great Beit Din, everyone agrees that a defendant cannot compel the plaintiff to go to the Great Beit Din. This reconciles Rambam without distinguishing between a beit vaad and the Great Beit Din.
