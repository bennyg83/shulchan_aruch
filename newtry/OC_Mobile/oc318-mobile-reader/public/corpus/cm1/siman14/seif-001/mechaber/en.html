When One Litigant Says, 'Let Us Go to the Great Beit Din,' and the Law of One Who Asks, 'For What Reason Did You Judge Me?' Eight seifim. If two litigants are engaged in a dispute and one says, 'Let us litigate here,' while the other says, 'Let us ascend to the Great Beit Din,' the latter is compelled to litigate in his own city. If he says, 'Write and give me the reasons for your judgment, lest you have erred,' they write them for him, but first enforce payment from him. If the local judges must ask the Great Beit Din a question, they write and send the question, and then adjudicate in their city according to the Great Beit Din's written reply. Both litigants pay the messenger's fee. {Rama: If one litigant or the judges wish to send far away while a nearby sage is fit to rule, they send to the nearby sage even if the distant one is wiser, because one party may not cause the other a loss by increasing the messenger's fee. This is certainly so if another sage in their own city is fit to rule. If two sages are equally near, they consult the greater one. The foregoing applies to ordinary disputes, or where the lender says, 'Let us litigate here,' and the borrower seeks the Great Beit Din. But if the lender seeks the Great Beit Din, the borrower is compelled to go with him. The same applies when a claimant alleges that the other damaged or robbed him. This is so only when the claimant has witnesses or other evidence. An empty allegation does not require the defendant to leave at all; he takes an oath where he is and is exempt. This is also the law nowadays, when there is no Great Beit Din, though some define the leading beit din of each generation as the Great Beit Din.} If some places in that country have eminent sages recognized by the public while others have lesser students, and the lender names a particular eminent sage in a particular place, the borrower is compelled to go with him. {Rama: This applies if the claimant has witnesses or evidence and the local beit din sees substance in his claims; otherwise, he is not compelled to go. Some say that regarding a demand to go to the Great Beit Din there is no difference between claimant and defendant, and either can compel the other to go. Strict law aside, the present custom is that whenever a city has a beit din, neither party can compel the other to go to another beit din, because we now have neither a Great Beit Din nor a central academy. A party may postpone litigation until the third day, but the judges must be selected immediately. A visitor claiming against residents of the city, or one visitor claiming against another, must be heard immediately and cannot be delayed. Likewise, wherever a resident finds a visitor whom he is suing, if a beit din is there, he may compel the visitor to litigate there. If their city has no beit din, either party may compel the other to travel to a beit din. The claimant ordinarily follows the defendant to the defendant's city even when the beit din in the claimant's city is greater. This is so even if the defendant has money on deposit in the claimant's city or another city, unless the claimant can restrain the money in his own city; he must then notify the defendant, and the case is heard where the money is located (Terumat HaDeshen, siman 305, and Piskei Terumat HaDeshen, siman 62). See siman 73 regarding when money may be restrained. A son against whom his father has a claim must go to his father even though the son is the defendant, as explained in Yoreh De'ah, siman 240. A wealthy person who is established and violent in his own city is removed to litigate in another city even if the beit din in his city is greater (Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 418). See also siman 65.}
