He is obligated to reimburse. Maharshal discusses this at length and concludes that only expenses caused by the defendant's refusal to obey beit din are reimbursed. Other expenses incurred in collecting a debt, such as traveling to another place or hiring an agent to bring the money, are not reimbursed. Even if the debtor wrote in his own handwriting that he would pay all collection expenses, the undertaking is asmachta unless made through a valid kinyan before an eminent beit din. Likewise, the fee paid to an agent merely to summon the debtor is ordinarily not reimbursed. Rosh, however, writes that where the creditor already holds a due debt instrument and the debtor has assets, but beit din will not descend to the assets in the debtor's absence and therefore sends a messenger to notify him, the debtor reimburses the messenger's expense. Rosh compares this to the case in Arakhin where one who consecrated assets subject to a ketubah and creditor adds a dinar and redeems the property so that consecrated property should not appear to leave without redemption. The wording "adds" implies that the creditor adds the dinar to his debt and later collects it together with the rest. The same applies to the messenger's expense, which is incurred for the debtor's benefit because, strictly, the court could otherwise have descended to the debtor's assets immediately in order not to lock the door before lenders. The principal proof can be understood differently. One who damages another person's lien can be liable. Thus, a debtor who consecrates encumbered property actively harms the creditor's lien, much like heirs who sell property subject to a creditor. The cost of redemption would then be damages. A debtor who merely traveled away, by contrast, did not actively damage anything. Even after the due date, his absence is only passive nonpayment. Perhaps he should not reimburse the messenger. Yet Rosh himself holds that pushing another person's coin into the sea, where a diver can recover it, is merely indirect damage and exempt because the object itself remains intact, even though recovery costs money. If so, consecrating property that can be redeemed for four zuz should likewise be only indirect damage. Nevertheless Rosh requires the additional dinar. This suggests that because the expenditure was for the debtor's benefit, he must reimburse it even without ordinary tort liability, and the messenger sent for his benefit should be comparable. According to Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch in siman 381, however, one who pushes a coin into the sea is liable under direct causation even though a diver can recover it. Accordingly, the consecration case can be explained as ordinary damages, and no proof remains for messenger expenses. Even according to Rosh, one could still say that a debtor whose property must be appraised by beit din and who then consecrates it must cover the redemption cost as part of satisfying the debt. The matter requires study.
