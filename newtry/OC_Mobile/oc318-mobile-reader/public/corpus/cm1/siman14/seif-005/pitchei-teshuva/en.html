A wealthy person established as intimidating. Urim VeTumim writes that the Rama’s wording might imply that the rule applies only where the wealthy litigant is known to be violent or intimidating. Tashbetz, however, and Knesset HaGedolah in the name of many responsa, indicate that “intimidating” is not literal. If a person is prominent in his city because of wisdom and stature, or even without special scholarship most townspeople defer to him, the opposing litigant may demand to adjudicate in another city. The same applies whether the powerful party is plaintiff or defendant. Shav Yaakov agrees, as do Netivot HaMishpat and Shevut Yaakov, part III, siman 141. Shevut Yaakov interprets the Rama’s phrase “wealthy and established as intimidating” disjunctively, while Shav Yaakov, siman 2, rejects that grammar but explains that “intimidating” means influential and feared, not necessarily wicked or aggressive; even a righteous and upright person can fall under the rule if his stature creates fear. He further writes that an intimidating person need not be wealthy, provided he is not poor, for a pauper generally inspires no such fear. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit cites Tashbetz: communal leaders and officials who controlled all city affairs summoned a man to litigation. He protested that their authority intimidated the judges and would inhibit his own arguments. Tashbetz advised both judges and litigants to go to a nearby place. He recounts precedents in which earlier sages allowed litigants not to adjudicate in the city of a powerful local leader because everyone deferred to him. The Bach cites the same material from the long Mordechai and notes that Maharik, shoresh 1, also cites it briefly. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 2, applies this principle to a tax dispute among districts and explains that the Rama is not inconsistent with these authorities. The Rama speaks of one who is merely wealthy but has no particular wisdom or public stature. Wealth alone does not create a presumption that judges will favor him or that the opponent’s arguments will be stifled, so in that case he must also be known as intimidating. A recognized leader or communal official is different: the opposing litigant naturally suspects that the judges fear his stature and therefore may be unable to argue freely. Chacham Tzvi, siman 14, cites the Tashbetz material in the name of Maharam and interprets it as good advice rather than an enforceable rule when an individual litigates against a community. He infers this from the phrase “it is good for the judges and litigants to go.” He distinguishes an individual powerful litigant, against whom the opponent may demand another city as a matter of law, from a community, where relocation is only advisable. He gives two reasons: a public body is less suspect of wrongful intimidation, and requiring an entire community to travel elsewhere for every municipal dispute would be impracticable. He concludes in his own case that where qualified scholars were available locally who were not under the community’s influence, individuals were obligated to litigate locally before departing. According to Chacham Tzvi’s reasons, the same leniency might extend to a dispute between two groups. Noda BiYehudah, however, does not appear to accept that distinction. Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 1, also disagrees and observes that Chacham Tzvi did not state his distinction decisively. Responsa She’erit Yosef, siman 40, expressly rules that even an individual litigating against a group may require them to go elsewhere if among them is someone whose authority intimidates the judges. He rejects Chacham Tzvi’s proofs and rules that an individual may disqualify the city judges from hearing a dispute over a will where the leaders of the local chevrah kadisha are also the principal communal leaders and control municipal affairs, so that their influence extends over the city and certainly over its judges. He distinguishes Chacham Tzvi’s case, where Altona and Hamburg contained independent scholars not subject to the authority of the Sephardic community in Hamburg. In a single unified community no such distinction exists. The case should therefore be heard in a nearby city, though the litigants cannot demand a distant venue. Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 47, likewise indicates that even where the defendants are numerous, plaintiffs may as a matter of law object to adjudicating in the defendants’ own locale, and the defendants must appear either at a neutral place through selected judges or before a nearby beit din. This is contrary to Chacham Tzvi’s view that with numerous defendants relocation is only good advice. Avodat HaGershuni adds that a demand to go to a nearby beit din is preferable to zeh borer lo echad, particularly in generations where people misunderstand Rashi’s description of that procedure. Shav Yaakov, siman 1, discusses two parties who had agreed to zeh borer lo echad with the local av beit din serving as the third judge. The plaintiff later became concerned because the defendant was wealthy and powerful in his city and sought to remove the av beit din. Shav Yaakov rules that he cannot do so. Tashbetz and Rama deal with judges whom the parties had not previously accepted. Here, the entire province had accepted this av beit din as their rav, expressly including his role as the third judge in disputes, without distinguishing an ordinary resident from a communal officer. Since both litigants belong to that community, each has already knowingly accepted him. Even in ordinary zeh borer lo echad, once the parties have formally written their selections they cannot retract; here the rav’s letter of appointment, signed by communal householders on behalf of the community and maintaining an ancient practice that the rav serves as third judge, is stronger still. Shav Yaakov finds direct support in She’erit Yosef, siman 40, concerning a Moravian ordinance that all disputes among local residents be adjudicated within the province. A later litigant argued that local powerful men intimidated the judges. She’erit Yosef ruled that if this was his only claim it failed, because a communal enactment or established custom accepting local judges, even for tax matters, has the force of accepting a relative or otherwise disqualified judge. In a prior case involving Brud, an outsider had not been a resident when the ordinance was enacted and therefore was not bound by it. Shav Yaakov explains that this distinction also resolves his own earlier concurrence with a Hamburg ruling requiring a wealthy and powerful local litigant to go elsewhere: in that earlier case the plaintiff came from another jurisdiction, whereas here both parties belong to the community and accepted the rav in advance as third judge for every dispute.
