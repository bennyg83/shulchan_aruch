And all the more so if he says, “Write for me,” etc. The Sema finds the Rama’s phrase “all the more so” difficult, because the two cases are not fully comparable. Tumim explains that although a judge need not necessarily write the reasoning in the written ruling, he must tell the litigant the reason orally. The “all the more so” is therefore clear: if the litigant explicitly asks, the judge certainly must tell him the entire reasoning orally. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 12, explains why the Sema and Tumim did not simply interpret Rama as speaking of a case where the litigant both suspects the judges and asks them to write the reasons. Such a reading would not properly be called “all the more so,” because the later case would already be contained in the former, and since the Mechaber says that even without a request the judges must explain why they ruled as they did, it would be obvious that they must do so when asked. Chatam Sofer nevertheless ultimately defends that interpretation. Nimukei Yosef writes that explanation is required only where there is some basis for suspicion. Where there is none, a litigant who challenges the judges is merely scoffing and may be placed under ban. The Sema and Be’er HaGolah cite this. Even when both litigants are scholars who voluntarily selected judges, each side naturally believes, according to his own legal analysis, that he is correct, for we do not assume wickedness. When the judge rules for one side, or sometimes contrary to both sides’ expectations, the losing litigant might think he has grounds for suspicion because the decision differs from his own analysis. Nevertheless, if he casts suspicion on the judge merely for that reason, he deserves censure, because a person does not readily see his own liability. In the case involving non-Jews in Bava Metzia, however, Rav Pappa himself created grounds for suspicion by issuing apparently inconsistent rulings, and he therefore had to clear himself. One might still have thought that although it is proper for the judge voluntarily to explain himself where genuine suspicion exists, the litigant has no right to demand an explanation in accusatory language. Perhaps if the litigant brazenly says that he suspects the judge, the judge should not answer him at all. On that understanding, the Mechaber’s statement “even if he did not ask” refers to a party compelled to litigate before the court, while where genuine suspicion exists and the case was voluntarily submitted, the judge should explain himself even without being asked. One might infer that if the litigant does ask in an insulting manner the judge should refuse. Rama therefore teaches that where there is genuine ground for suspicion, even if the litigant asks and behaves improperly by voicing suspicion, the judge still provides the reason. This parallels the case where the non-Jew brazenly accused Rav Pappa of following his own prior ruling, yet Rav Pappa did not penalize him and explained the reason. Rama then adds that the primary rule is that the reasons need not be written but may be stated orally, because the purpose is only to clear the judge of suspicion. Why should he write a document that can be shown to others and publicize that he was suspected? Since the party voluntarily accepted the court, he has no unilateral right to seek appellate advice elsewhere. Oral explanation suffices. Where, however, the litigant was compelled to appear and wishes to go to the great beit din, the claims must in any event be written. If there is also genuine ground for suspicion and the litigant asks for the rationale, the reasons should be included in writing. Perhaps once the litigant understands the reasoning and sees that his suspicion was unfounded, he will no longer pursue review by the greater court. Chatam Sofer concludes that this is the proper understanding of Rama.
