Sema already questions why Rama omits Tur's view that where the two courts are equal, the defendant cannot evade one court in favor of another. According to Tur, the defendant may insist only on going to the Great Beit Din. Rashba likewise appears to hold this. Tur's statement above in siman 3 that where the defendant does not want the three judges selected by the plaintiff, each side chooses one judge, must therefore concern a case where the defendant wants a court more distinguished than the one chosen by the plaintiff. It is difficult to explain Tur as speaking of two equal courts where the defendant merely says, "I prefer to choose one judge myself so that he will seek my merit more effectively." The language of Tosafot and Rosh at the beginning of Sanhedrin cannot readily bear that interpretation. The root of the dispute is the Gemara's explanation at the beginning of Zeh Borer. The Mishnah says that each party selects one judge. The Gemara initially understands this as meaning each party selects an entire court, and gives the example of the beit din of Rav Huna and the beit din of Rav Chisda, where one litigant says to the other, "What have I deprived you of?" Semag understands that although Rav Huna was greater than Rav Chisda, if the plaintiff selected Rav Huna's court the defendant could say, "I will go to Rav Chisda's court," even though it was the lesser court. The plaintiff cannot compel him to the greater one. Instead, the parties must agree upon a third court acceptable to both. From this Semag derives his rule. Later authorities generally disagree. They explain that the plaintiff wanted Rav Chisda's lesser court and the defendant wanted Rav Huna's greater court. In that direction the defendant may indeed object and insist upon the greater court, but not the reverse. I believe Semag and Tur disagree over the implication of Tosafot's explanation that Rav Chisda lived more than three parsangs away from Rav Huna. Within three parsangs, Rav Chisda would not sit in judgment before Rav Huna, his teacher. Semag holds that traveling more than three parsangs constitutes meaningful effort and burden. Once the distance exceeds the Torah's recognized territorial measure, it is treated as a journey involving real inconvenience. Now examine the Gemara's example. If both plaintiff and defendant are located in Rav Huna's city, the defendant wishes to litigate before Rav Huna, and the plaintiff demands that he travel more than three parsangs to Rav Chisda, the defendant obviously can refuse. Why would the Gemara need the reason "What have I deprived you of"? The defendant can simply say: why should I undertake a substantial journey to a lesser court when the greater court is here? The principle "a borrower is servant to the lender" does not mean that a debtor must travel far to a lesser court at the creditor's whim. Conversely, if the plaintiff wants Rav Huna's court while they are in Rav Huna's city, the defendant certainly cannot evade the local greater court and demand the lesser court located more than three parsangs away. Therefore Semag concludes that the case must be where both parties are in Rav Chisda's city. If the defendant wishes to travel to Rav Huna, ordinarily he has no right to compel the plaintiff to travel to the Great Beit Din, especially where the travel itself is significant. Thus the case must be that the plaintiff demands Rav Huna's greater court while the defendant wants to remain locally before Rav Chisda. Why then does the Gemara accept the defendant's argument "What have I deprived you of"? We normally rule that a creditor can compel the debtor to go to the Great Beit Din even over a long distance. Semag answers that this applies where the greater court is genuinely separate and distant. Where the greater and lesser courts are close enough to one another to function within one judicial region, the defendant can say, "Litigate before the local lesser court." If a difficult question arises, that court can consult the greater one. The plaintiff has not materially lost anything. That is Bach's explanation of Semag's reasoning, and on this reading Semag's position is compelling. This also answers another question. One might say that the Gemara's identification of Rav Huna and Rav Chisda was only part of the initial interpretation that "each selects one" means each selects an entire court. Once the Gemara concludes that it means each selects one individual judge, perhaps the entire Rav Huna versus Rav Chisda construction falls away. But according to the analysis above, the Gemara could easily have illustrated its initial reading with two equal courts in the same city. If the plaintiff says one court and the defendant says another equal court, it would be obvious that the plaintiff cannot force his choice. The fact that the Gemara deliberately chose Rav Huna and Rav Chisda, courts of unequal stature, teaches an additional substantive point: even though one court is lesser, the plaintiff cannot necessarily force the greater court. That implication would be pointless if it were merely a discarded preliminary assumption. Tur disagrees because he does not treat three parsangs as a significant burden in this context. According to him, both parties can be in Rav Chisda's place, the plaintiff wishes to litigate before Rav Chisda, and the defendant wishes to go to Rav Huna, whose court is greater. Because the distance is only three parsangs, the travel is not treated as substantial. The defendant may therefore say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." But where the two courts are equal, and certainly where the defendant's preferred court is lesser, the plaintiff's choice prevails. According to Rosh and Tur, who distinguish a beit vaad from the Great Beit Din, one must further say that Rav Huna's superiority over Rav Chisda was in the category of a beit vaad rather than the unique status of the Great Beit Din. The entire passage in Zeh Borer concerns a beit vaad. Were Rav Huna's court truly equivalent to the Great Beit Din, the defendant could compel travel there even at substantial inconvenience, and the phrase "What have I deprived you of?" would not resolve the issue. This also clarifies Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 3, part 8, ruling 6. He rules that if there is another court in the same city equal to the court selected by the plaintiff, the defendant may say, "I will litigate before the other court." All the more so if the second court is greater, based on the Gemara in HaGozel that a party can say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." This initially appears difficult under either reading. If he follows Semag, Semag permits the defendant even to choose the lesser court, whereas Baal HaTerumot speaks specifically of an equal court or a greater one. If he follows Tur, where the defendant asks for Rav Huna's greater court, why does the Gemara need such a case? According to Baal HaTerumot the defendant can already choose another equal court. The Mishnah could have been established with two equal courts. The answer is that the Gemara teaches an additional point about distance. Where the courts are separated by three parsangs but not more, the defendant can still demand the greater court. If the two courts are equal, even this limited travel need not be imposed because there is no legal advantage in changing courts. They should litigate locally. Baal HaTerumot treats beit vaad and Great Beit Din as one category, as appears at the end of that ruling. He understands the HaGozel passage as speaking of courts within the same city. Outside the city, a debtor generally cannot compel the creditor to travel even to a superior court, except where the distance is limited as described. This also answers the question of Gidulei Terumah and Beit Shmuel. Baal HaTerumot brings proof from "I will go to the Great Beit Din," yet how does that prove a right to choose an equal court? His understanding of "Great Beit Din" there is not necessarily a formally supreme tribunal. It can mean a court that the defendant regards as superior in wisdom and trustworthiness. He says, in effect, "I trust that court more and believe they will clarify the matter better." This reading is necessary because Rav Ukva explains Shmuel's statement that testimony can sometimes be accepted outside a litigant's presence by establishing the case where proceedings have already been opened. Before that, he says, the litigant can say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." If "Great Beit Din" meant a unique superior tribunal, why must Rav Ukva limit Shmuel's statement? He could simply say that Shmuel speaks of the superior court itself, which in an urgent matter may accept testimony without the litigant. Rather, Shmuel's statement is general. In most cities there are several courts of comparable stature. Before proceedings begin, the defendant can evade one by selecting another comparable court that he considers better. Once proceedings have opened, he can no longer do so in the same manner. Thus Baal HaTerumot learns that a defendant may choose another equal court in the same city. For practical halachah, I therefore incline to Baal HaTerumot's mediating view. Where two equal courts are in the same city, the defendant may say, "I will litigate before this other court," even though the plaintiff prefers the first. But where one court is clearly lesser and the plaintiff wants the greater court, the defendant cannot insist on the lesser court. Where the alternative court is three parsangs away, even an equal court generally cannot be demanded merely to change forums. A greater court may be demanded within that limited distance, as Ramban indicates in responsum. This seems the proper practical resolution among the opinions.
