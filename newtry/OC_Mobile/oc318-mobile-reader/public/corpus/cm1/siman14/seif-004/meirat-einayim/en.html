The litigant suspects him, etc. Only where there is a reasonable basis for suspicion must the judge explain the ruling. Where there is no basis and the litigant merely mocks the court, he is placed under ban. So writes Nimukei Yosef.
All the more so if he says, 'Write for me the reason you judged me.' At first glance 'all the more so' is difficult because the two cases differ. Where the litigant suspects the judge, the judge must disclose the reasoning to remove suspicion. Where the litigant asks for a written explanation so that he can consult a higher court, the judges need not write all legal reasons and proofs, only the claims and ruling, as Rama concludes. The point is that one who asks for writing is not necessarily asking the judges to justify themselves to him. He may already know their reason but dispute it and wish to seek review. If even one who says nothing must sometimes be given enough explanation to satisfy Heaven and human suspicion, all the more so one who asks for the record needed to seek review must be given what is appropriate for that purpose.
But otherwise they do not write it for him. Since he accepted them simply as judges, he cannot reopen the whole proceeding at will. If he merely asks orally for the reason, however, they certainly tell him, for even one whom they perceive as suspicious without asking must sometimes be told the reason.
They need not write the reasons and proofs. Any competent beit din that hears the claims can determine the halacha, for the Torah is one for all of us.
And he must pay immediately. The Mechaber already wrote at the beginning of the siman, following Rambam, that the money is extracted. One might understand that beit din merely holds the funds until a higher court reviews the matter, lest the claimant spend them and the judges later be personally liable if reversed. Rama therefore clarifies that the defendant pays immediately into the claimant's hands. If the judgment is later overturned, beit din compels the claimant to return the money.
