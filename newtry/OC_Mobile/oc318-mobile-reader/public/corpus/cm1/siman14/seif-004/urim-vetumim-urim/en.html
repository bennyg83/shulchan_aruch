If the judge sees that a litigant suspects him. This applies only where there is a genuine basis for suspicion. If there is none and the litigant merely mocks the decision, he may be excommunicated. Nimukei Yosef and Sema. An example of genuine suspicion is the case in Bava Metzia 69 where Rav Pappa upheld a division of money by one partner without the other's knowledge, yet later invalidated division of wine in a similar manner. Since the same person seemed repeatedly to lose, suspicion could naturally arise. Rav Pappa therefore explained the substantive distinction between money and wine.
All the more so if he says, "Write for me." Sema asks: if in a case of suspicion the judges must explain the reason orally, how is it all the more obvious that in an ordinary case they must write the claims, especially when Rama says that the written decision need not state the legal reasoning? Sema answers that the *a fortiori* concerns writing itself: if suspicion justifies disclosure of reasoning, surely a request merely for a written record can be granted. See Tumim, where I explain that although the written decision need not contain the detailed legal reasoning, the judges should state their reasoning orally. That makes the *a fortiori* straightforward.
But otherwise they do not write. Since the litigant voluntarily accepted these judges, he cannot simply reject their ruling and demand another forum. If he asks for the reason of the judgment, however, they should certainly explain it, since even when he did not ask, the court must explain where his conduct gives rise to reasonable suspicion. Sema. The first rationale is difficult. The purpose of a written decision is not to enable a litigant to reject a judgment at will, but to allow a superior court to determine whether the judges erred. That concern can exist even where he accepted them voluntarily. The better reason is evidentiary: if he freely chose these judges despite knowing their level of scholarship, there is no reason after losing to suspect that they erred. If he had genuine concern about their competence, he should not have selected them. This also clarifies the two resolutions given by the early authorities to the tension between the Sanhedrin passage requiring a written decision and the Bava Metzia passage requiring explanation only in cases of suspicion. One answer distinguishes coerced adjudication from voluntary acceptance. Another, Rosh's answer, distinguishes a situation where the court itself sees suspicion from an ordinary case where a party must affirmatively request the reasoning. The approaches should not be casually combined. Under the first, voluntary acceptance can mean that even a request for detailed reasoning need not be honored beyond what fairness requires. Under the second, a request itself is sufficient. The sources must be read carefully.
