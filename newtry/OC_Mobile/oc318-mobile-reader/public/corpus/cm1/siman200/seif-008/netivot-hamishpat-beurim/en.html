He intended only to measure. This is initially difficult from Rashba in Gittin 20. Regarding an elderly man who lent to everyone in his village, the Gemara asks that a deed of transfer is required and answers that the elderly man knew how to transfer. Rashba asks that the borrowers did not know the old man had transferred to them, resembling one who hoes property of a convert thinking it is his own, who does not acquire. Rashba answers that where another person consciously transfers, the rule is different. Nimukei Yosef similarly writes concerning Rav Anan, who redirected water onto land, that if the owner had known and intended to grant the land, Rav Anan would acquire even without knowing of the gift, because another person consciously transfers. Machaneh Efrayim therefore rules that with another transferring mind, the recipient can acquire even if he did not intend the act as acquisition at the moment. Ketzot HaChoshen raises precisely this question here: why does it matter that the buyer intended only measurement? He suggests there are two different versions of Raavad and uses this to challenge authorities who rule, concerning avoiding firstborn status, that a non Jew's meshichah does not work where he had no acquisition intent. In my view Raavad, Rashba, and Nimukei Yosef mean only that if, at the moment the recipient performs the otherwise valid acquisition act without his own acquisition intent, the transferor himself simultaneously intends that act to transfer, the acquisition is effective. One of the two parties has present acquisition intent, and they hold it does not matter whether that intent is on the transferor's side or recipient's side. Thus in Gittin the elderly owner himself knew and intended to transfer at that moment. In Rav Anan's case, if the landowner had seen Rav Anan's act and consciously intended to give him the land, Rav Anan would acquire. Here, by contrast, seller and buyer both agreed only on price and contemplated an eventual sale. When the buyer measures merely to ascertain quantity, if the buyer does not intend the measurement as acquisition, we likewise presume the seller did not intend that measurement as transfer. They stand equally in the transaction and both view it as measurement only. There is also a broader distinction between gift and sale. In a gift, if the recipient knew of the transfer he would certainly wish to acquire, so another person's transfer intent supplies a compelling presumption of consent. In a sale, the buyer can say that he does not yet wish to become obligated in the price and intentionally postponed acquisition so that he could still retract. Therefore acquisition intent is required.
If the buyer measured, he acquires. Mishneh LaMelech questions this from Yoreh Deah, Tur siman 132, where if a non Jew lifted the wine initially to acquire it he acquires, but Beit Yosef says that if he lifted it only as part of measuring, he does not acquire because he had no acquisition intent. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that "another person consciously transfers" operates through zekhiyah and does not apply to a non Jew. Even without that, the cases can be reconciled. MahariT writes that a person's domain acquires without his awareness, whereas one who performs a specific kinyan act without intent, such as hoeing a convert's property thinking it is his own, does not acquire. Here the buyer's hagbahah works because his hand is itself his domain, and his hand is no worse than his courtyard. A person's domain can acquire for him without conscious intent. Although a sleeping person's courtyard can be better than his hand, because a guarded courtyard acquires even while he sleeps whereas an unguarded hand while sleeping is not considered guarded, when he is awake his hand is certainly no worse than his courtyard and can acquire without specific acquisition awareness. Once this hagbahah itself places the item in his acquiring hand in the seller's presence, it is as though he performed meshichah before the seller and acquires. Bava Metzia 11 explains that a courtyard's acquisition without one's awareness ultimately works through agency, because courtyard is no worse than an agent. The same is true of one's hand when the acquisition is without awareness, because the courtyard concept was included from hand. Machaneh Efrayim likewise says that this operates through agency. A non Jew has no ordinary agency. Therefore a non Jew's guarded courtyard does not acquire for him without his awareness, even if when he consciously uses it it can function as hand. Likewise, his physical hand does not acquire an item without his awareness through this agency extension. This resolves Yoreh Deah siman 132. Acquisition of ownerless property without awareness will be explained elsewhere.
