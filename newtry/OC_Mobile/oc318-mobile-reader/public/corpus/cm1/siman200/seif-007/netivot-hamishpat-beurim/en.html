The produce was in a side alley. See Sema, seif katan 15, who writes that even if it later enters the buyer's vessel, either side can retract until the entire kor has been measured. At first glance this contradicts his later seif katan 21, where he writes that if the goods are in the buyer's vessel in a side alley the buyer acquires just as in his own domain. The reconciliation is that if the entire kor that was sold is already present in the buyer's vessel in the side alley, so that the kinyan of the whole quantity has been completed and only measurement remains, the buyer acquires even before measurement. The buyer's vessel acquires in a side alley without measurement according to Rif. Here, however, Sema means that the produce initially lay on the ground of the side alley, where there was no acquisition, and it was then measured gradually into the buyer's vessel. Because the parties used the formulation "a kor for thirty," they showed that no acquisition should take effect until the act is completed for the entire kor. Until the last se'ah is placed into the vessel, even the buyer's vessel does not complete the transaction. Regarding the seller's vessel in the buyer's domain, the rule is even clearer. According to Mechaber, acquisition there depends on measurement itself, so when the parties say "a kor for thirty" the entire kor must be measured before acquisition occurs. But if the seller places the goods into the buyer's vessel located in the buyer's own domain, the kinyan is stronger because both vessel and domain favor the buyer. It resembles meshichah performed before the seller, which can acquire the quantities successively even before the entire kor is finished. Mechaber's language further implies that once measurement of the entire kor is completed, the buyer acquires even if each measured se'ah was afterward poured from the vessel onto the ground of the side alley. Completion of the entire measuring process reveals retroactively that each se'ah was acquired when measured, like Rosh's opinion cited by Tur, rather than Beit Yosef's view requiring the whole kor to remain together in the buyer's vessel at the final moment.

Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Batra writes that according to this opinion the same applies in siman 203 to the exchange of a cow and lamb for a kor of wheat: even though the wheat is divisible, partial acquisition does not occur where the parties revealed that the entire unit is one transaction. Rama did not gloss there. See what I wrote there distinguishing the cases. Darkei Moshe writes that even if the buyer paid separately for each se'ah, he does not acquire until measurement of the entire kor is complete. This was not brought in Shulchan Aruch. As I wrote above in siman 182, the primary rule follows Rashba and Ritva: when separate payment is made for each se'ah, each is acquired successively. Proof appears from the sale of ten fields in ten provinces, where without payment of the whole price one nevertheless acquires corresponding to the amount paid, and the seller cannot say, "I needed the entire price."
To accept responsibility. See Sema, seif katan 24. The barrel is appraised according to its value at the time he opened it, in accordance with the appraisal Sema describes. Even if it deteriorated further before beit din, he need pay only the loss at the time of the damaging act, because subsequent depreciation of the damaged object is borne by the injured party. This differs from Bava Kamma 34, where an animal weakened from the blow is valued at the time of litigation because the damaging force remains embedded in it. Shitah Mekubetzet explains that a living animal is ordinarily retained in the hope it will recover. Here, however, the defendant is only a causative damager and is liable because the harm is treated as sufficiently certain. It resembles depreciation of a carcass. Shitah explains that where deterioration from delay is known, the injured party should have sold immediately. The same applies here because the further deterioration is predictable. This also answers Taz's question from a custodian who makes unauthorized use and the wine later turns sour. Such a custodian becomes a robber of the entire item, and a robber is not given the ordinary appraisal defense. He therefore bears accidents and even later souring. A stranger who caused only the initial damage is liable only for the diminution at the moment he acted; later souring is not his liability. One can derive from here that if a person tells another, "Bring me a particular commodity with a fixed market price and I will buy it," and the other incurs expenses bringing it, but the first then refuses to purchase, he must reimburse those expenses. This resembles our case and also the rule in siman 12 where one says, "Go, and I will come after you," and becomes liable for causative loss.
