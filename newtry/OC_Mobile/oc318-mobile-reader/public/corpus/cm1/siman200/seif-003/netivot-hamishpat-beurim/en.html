A person's utensil. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 9, in the name of Rashba and other authorities, writes that a person's utensil acquires through the law of courtyard. As I wrote above, a utensil is treated as a guarded courtyard and does not require the owner to stand beside it. Accordingly, wherever a guarded courtyard cannot acquire, such as for a female minor according to siman 243, her utensil likewise does not acquire.

Go and acquire. See Shach, seif katan 7, who writes that the same law applies if the seller merely gave the buyer permission to place his vessel there, disagreeing with Ramah and with Bedek HaBayit. In my view Ramah is primary. Tosafot and Rosh explain the Gemara's uncertainty concerning the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain and the seller's vessel in the buyer's domain as depending on whether the vessel or the underlying domain predominates. Tosafot add that although R. Yochanan says a person's vessel acquires wherever he has permission to place it, perhaps that statement excludes only the public domain, while a seller may be presumed to lend the buyer the place specifically so that the buyer can acquire the item being sold. This cannot mean that the Gemara's entire uncertainty is simply whether the seller lends the place. If the buyer's vessel does not acquire in the seller's domain merely because the seller did not lend the place, that would not imply that the seller's vessel acquires in the buyer's domain. One could say the domain and vessel mutually prevent one another. The poskim themselves formulate the Gemara's question as whether the domain is subordinate to the vessel or the vessel to the domain, not merely whether a place was lent. The correct reading is that the Gemara assumes the seller ordinarily permits the buyer to place his vessel there. The remaining uncertainty concerns the legal status of the airspace occupied by the vessel. This can be clarified from Bava Metzia 102. The Gemara asks why produce caught from the air belongs to the catcher when the airspace is the courtyard owner's. It answers that the air in question is air not destined to come to rest, because a vessel intervenes. The Gemara then asks that Rava himself was uncertain whether air not destined to come to rest acquires, and answers that interruption by a vessel makes the case different. If so, why is there any uncertainty regarding the seller's vessel in the buyer's courtyard? The buyer should not acquire because the object rests in air not destined to reach the actual ground, with the seller's vessel intervening. In ownerless property such a configuration would not acquire. One can distinguish that in Bava Metzia the case involves a rented courtyard under Rambam's reading, or at least a situation where the landlord cannot presently remove the intervening vessel. The vessel therefore constitutes a real interruption preventing the item from being treated as destined to rest on the courtyard floor. Here, however, the buyer has not granted the seller a fixed-term right to leave his vessel there. Even if he permits it temporarily, he may remove the vessel at any time. Such an intervening vessel may not be a sufficient interruption. The Gemara's uncertainty is therefore whether the air within the vessel is legally treated as the vessel's own air, or whether it remains the air of the underlying courtyard. This is what the poskim mean when they ask whether the domain is subordinate to the vessel or the vessel to the domain. Even if the air is considered courtyard air, the case still depends on whether air not destined to rest is legally like rest. If such air is considered at rest, then in the buyer's vessel inside the seller's domain both buyer and seller have a form of possession: the buyer through his vessel as hand, and the seller through the courtyard air as his hand. Neither alone acquires. If air not destined to rest is not legally at rest, the seller's air supplies no acquisition, and the buyer's vessel can acquire. Conversely, if the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain does acquire, that indicates either that the vessel's air belongs to the vessel, or that air not destined to rest does not acquire. In either case, the seller's vessel in the buyer's domain will prevent the buyer's courtyard from acquiring. If the vessel's air belongs to the vessel, the buyer's courtyard has no air acquisition over the object. If such air does not count as rest, the buyer likewise cannot acquire through courtyard air. If, however, the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain does not acquire because the vessel's air is treated as the seller's courtyard air and air not destined to rest is treated as rest, then the seller's vessel in the buyer's domain can allow the buyer to acquire. The seller's vessel itself does not acquire there because the seller lacks permission to place it as an acquiring vessel, while the buyer's courtyard air is treated as present possession. This also explains why "go and acquire" helps for the buyer's vessel in the seller's domain. The seller's instruction means he is not retaining his own courtyard acquisition against the buyer and is lending even the relevant vessel air in the manner needed for acquisition. Where circumstances show the same intent, such as the husband selling baskets in the gittin cases, it can be treated similarly. But mere permission to place the vessel is not enough. Ordinary permission to place the vessel was already assumed by the Gemara; otherwise there would have been no meaningful uncertainty. "Go and acquire" is different because it grants the place and air for acquisition itself. Therefore Ramah's requirement of explicit acquisition permission is correct, and it is reasonable that if mere permission sufficed, the Gemara would not have specifically said "go and acquire."
