Seif 11: One who takes an article from an artisan's premises in order to inspect it, etc. Note: The Shulchan Aruch follows the view that in every case the price must be fixed, even where the merchandise is especially easy to sell. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia, however, cites Ritva's distinction: with especially marketable merchandise, a fixed price is unnecessary; with ordinary merchandise, the price must be fixed. Ritva distinguishes the sugya there from the sugya in Bava Batra. His reasoning appears to be that the liability can arise either from the law of a borrower or from the law of a purchaser. With especially marketable goods, the entire benefit is the recipient's, so he has the status of a borrower; therefore the lack of a fixed price is irrelevant. With ordinary merchandise, the benefit is not exclusively his, so borrower liability does not apply. His liability is only as a purchaser, and a purchaser requires complete reliance of mind, which in turn requires a fixed price. This explanation, however, creates serious difficulties in the Gemara. What proof can be brought to Shmuel from the baraita about one who takes an article to send to his father-in-law? Perhaps there the price was fixed and liability arose as a purchaser, while Shmuel's case, according to Ritva, concerns no fixed price and borrower liability. Likewise, why does the Gemara challenge Shmuel from the Mishna of one who vows that Israelites may not benefit from him? Perhaps Shmuel speaks where the price was fixed and liability is that of a purchaser, while the benefit is shared. From where does the Gemara know that Shmuel addresses a case with no fixed price? After writing this I saw Netivot HaMishpat, whose approach might resolve part of the difficulty, but his assertion that Shmuel implies liability even after return is itself difficult. The Gemara could just as well distinguish stages and limit Shmuel to the period before return. Rashbam indeed rules that way, but it is not compelled by Shmuel. Netivot's further objection that merchandise cannot be called especially marketable where the price is unfixed because the seller may demand an excessive price is also not decisive. Tosafot themselves discuss a case where the intermediary wished to sell high, yet the goods were still called especially marketable. In any event, where the seller has not demanded an excessive amount and has not even stated a price, the recipient may reasonably assume that a normal price will be asked, and the benefit of the opportunity remains substantial. Examine this carefully.
