(Seif 1, in the gloss: however, if the seller, etc.) This gloss requires study. Hagahot Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia writes: if a person transfers produce in his house to another and rents him the place where the produce is located, while there is no separation between that produce and the owner's own produce, the purchaser does not acquire, because the courtyard is not guarded for him. In the case of a found object, an unguarded courtyard does not acquire. However, concerning a gift, Rav Pappa concludes that the recipient acquires because another person's intent transfers it to him, and a guarded courtyard is unnecessary even when he is not standing beside it. These words themselves require explanation. It appears that the statement regarding a gift retracts the opening statement. At first the author equated a sale and a found object, and naturally a gift would have been the same. He later distinguishes a sale or gift where another person intentionally transfers ownership from a found object, thereby retracting the initial equation. In any event, we see that he rules like Rav Pappa that where another person's intent transfers ownership, acquisition works with a gift and a sale even where the produce is mixed, because the area is guarded according to the seller's awareness. The seller rented only the specific place of the produce. Although the Rama says "the courtyard itself," his intention must be the same, because this is the source from which he derived the law. If the entire courtyard had been rented, it would be guarded for the purchaser. Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia 11 explicitly explains that the case concerns a courtyard guarded according to the seller. Moreover, the Rama himself rules that in a sale we apply the principle that another person's intent transfers ownership. Why, then, does he write here that when the produce is mixed the purchaser does not acquire?
