Introduction
Behold there are many opinions concerning why a person's courtyard acquires for him. Rif, at the beginning of Bava Metzia, holds that a man's courtyard was included through the law of agency, while a woman's courtyard in gittin was included through the law of her hand. His words initially imply that the courtyard included in the law of theft, whether guarded or whether the owner stands beside it, always works through agency, since Rif uses this to explain why a minor has no courtyard because a minor has no agency. If standing beside it worked through the law of hand, then a minor should at least acquire through a courtyard while standing beside it. Ran, by contrast, holds that even a guarded courtyard where the owner does not stand beside it works through the law of hand. His reason is that a person ordinarily places anything beneficial to him in his guarded courtyard, so the courtyard is an extension of his hand. Ran proves this because a courtyard acquires a found object although ordinary agency does not acquire a found object for another in the relevant setting. It follows from the same proof that an unguarded courtyard where the owner stands beside it also works through the law of hand. Rosh holds that when one stands beside the courtyard, so that the courtyard resembles one's hand by proximity, it works through the law of hand, whereas a guarded courtyard works through agency. Several difficulties arise. According to Rif: first, Bava Metzia 11 says expressly concerning monetary acquisition that a courtyard was included as one's hand and is no worse than agency, implying that monetary courtyard acquisition includes both hand and agency. Second, at the beginning of HaZorek the Gemara explains why Scripture had to teach courtyard both regarding theft and regarding gittin. If a get courtyard works only as hand while a theft courtyard works only as agency, one could not learn gittin from theft in any event because agency is not available to impose a detriment on a person without his consent. Third, Rosh himself cites Rif's statement that a minor lacks a courtyard because a man's courtyard derives from agency, which suggests agreement with Rif, yet in the sugya of one who sees people running after an object Rosh writes that standing beside the courtyard works as hand. Fourth, if a man's courtyard always works only through agency, a non Jew, who has no ordinary agency, should not acquire through a courtyard. Yet Avodah Zarah 71 clearly assumes a non Jew has courtyard acquisition when it asks that his utensils should acquire for him, and utensils acquire as courtyard. Ketzot HaChoshen attempts to distinguish that Rashi explains the exclusion of a non Jew from Jewish agency because an agent must resemble the principal. Since the idea of courtyard itself is not limited to members of the covenant, perhaps that exclusion does not apply. But this is difficult. Rashi also explains concerning a minor that the principal source of agency is written in gittin and therefore speaks only of an adult. The same reasoning would exclude a non Jew as well. Ran's view also appears difficult: if courtyard is always hand, then a minor, who has a hand, should also have a courtyard. Rosh's view raises the same question at least where the minor stands beside it. Therefore it appears that for a male minor a courtyard is not treated as his hand. Ran's explanation of a guarded courtyard depends on the fact that an adult normally places beneficial property into a place he guards. That logic does not apply to a minor. Property placed in a minor's possession is often treated as knowingly exposed to loss, as is evident in Bava Batra 87. Therefore his courtyard is not his hand on that basis. Likewise, even where standing beside an unguarded courtyard ordinarily makes it resemble one's hand because of proximity, a minor is different. Ran in Gittin, on the Mishnah of "Take this debt instrument," shows that when a person is sleeping, placement in his hand does not work for divorce because the item is not guarded by him. Similarly, even a guarded courtyard does not function for divorce merely because the woman sleeps beside it where the law requires standing beside the courtyard. Thus, for an unguarded courtyard, mere physical proximity is insufficient. The owner must be present with awareness and guard it. A sleeping person is not considered standing beside it in the relevant sense. A minor, who lacks the requisite awareness to guard, likewise is not. There is an additional reason. The principal verse for courtyard in theft speaks in a context applicable to an adult who can acquire lost or stolen property. Since there is no other mind transferring the property to him, a minor does not acquire those things ordinarily. Thus the standing-minor courtyard is not automatically classified as a hand. A female minor is different because Scripture specifically decreed in gittin that her courtyard, while she stands beside it, is treated as her hand even though she lacks mature capacity to guard. We do not derive a male minor from a female minor. Why then does the Gemara connect a male minor's lack of courtyard to his lack of agency rather than simply saying that neither a guarded courtyard nor standing beside it counts as his hand? The answer is that the distinction matters for a gift. With a gift, because there is another person's conscious intent to transfer, one can sometimes acquire even without standing beside the courtyard and without the courtyard being independently guarded. In that kind of gift transaction the courtyard clearly operates through agency, as Ran explains in Shitah Mekubetzet. The courtyard acts both as the recipient's agent and, because there is another mind transferring, within the transferor's disposition. A minor has no agency, and therefore even in that favorable gift case the courtyard does not acquire for him. The opinion that does allow a minor a courtyard must derive him from a female minor and treat it as hand by special law. When Rif and Rosh write that a man's courtyard is included through agency, even though standing beside it can work as hand, their main reference is to a guarded courtyard where the person does not stand beside it. That is comparable to the minor case where mere standing is legally irrelevant. In that setting, they properly say that a man's courtyard works through agency, and a minor, who lacks agency, lacks that type of courtyard. A female minor is an exceptional scriptural decree that her nearby courtyard counts as hand. According to this, for a non Jew, according to Ran, a courtyard can acquire both when guarded and when unguarded with the owner standing beside it, because both ordinarily work through hand. The exception is a gift case where a Jew can acquire through an unguarded courtyard while not standing beside it due to another person's transferring intent. That works through agency, and a non Jew, who lacks agency, cannot acquire that way. According to Rif and Rosh, a non Jew acquires through a courtyard only when he stands beside it, because that works through hand. A guarded courtyard where he does not stand beside it works only through agency, and therefore does not acquire for a non Jew. This resolves the difficulties above, because all the approaches recognize both hand and agency within the law of courtyard in their respective settings. Raavad, cited by Shitah Mekubetzet, also implies that there are two scriptural inclusions, one corresponding to hand and one to agency. This explains why the Gemara in Gittin still needs a special inclusion of hand even though theft also contains the concept of hand. One might ask that gittin needs a special inclusion because of a female minor, which is not relevant to theft. That is not so. The reason we know a female minor's courtyard can function in gittin is precisely that "he shall place it in her hand" cannot mean only the physical hand once Scripture broadens the verse. Rashi explains that "her hand" means her domain. Since the verse speaks about all women capable of divorce, including minors, once "hand" is read as domain it includes the minor as well. The Gemara can therefore ask that once theft already revealed that "hand" can mean domain, perhaps gittin could be learned from there. And that which the Gemara says that a found object can be learned from gittin, even though at the beginning of HaZorek the Gemara says the laws are not simply learned from gittin, can be explained as follows. Had courtyard not been written at all regarding theft and we sought to derive it only by a general comparison to gittin, one could object that divorce is different because it can operate against the recipient's will. But once the theft verse contains a general-and-particular structure that includes things resembling the particular, and a female minor's courtyard is treated as hand in gittin, that kind of courtyard is now "like the particular" because it has the legal quality of hand. A male minor, whose courtyard was never called hand, is not learned from the female minor. Although Raavad speaks of two inclusions even though the text does not contain two explicit extra words, the explanation may be that Rashi views the inclusion as revealing that "his hand" means his domain. Shitah asks why a general-and-particular derivation is then necessary. According to what I have explained, the general-and-particular structure is needed to include the agency-type courtyard even where it does not resemble physical hand. This also answers Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia 10 that gittin lacks such a general-and-particular structure. In gittin only the hand aspect is needed, and therefore one inclusion suffices. According to this one can also answer a learned person's question on the beginning of HaZorek. The Gemara says we cannot derive theft from divorce because divorce can operate against the woman's will. Why not first derive kiddushin from divorce through the juxtaposition "she leaves and becomes another man's," and then derive theft from kiddushin, as Tosafot discuss? According to what I wrote, kiddushin with another person's transferring intent can work even in an unguarded courtyard while the woman does not stand beside it, through agency according to Rav Pappa. Such kiddushin therefore cannot teach the hand aspect needed for theft, namely standing beside an unguarded courtyard, because ordinary agency does not work in an unguarded courtyard that fails to resemble a guarded agent. The presence of another transferring mind changes the situation. For the authorities who do not accept Rav Pappa, the question can be answered through Rashba's principle in Kiddushin 60 that the divorce-kiddushin juxtaposition is not applied in every detail. This also supports Rashi, Tosafot, and Ran, cited by Shach in siman 243, seif katan 10, who hold that a female minor does not acquire monetary property through a guarded courtyard when she does not stand beside it. Such a courtyard does not resemble her hand by proximity and works only through agency, which a minor lacks. Although Ran himself says that an adult's guarded courtyard can work as hand, his rationale depends on adult control and guarding, which does not apply to a minor. The female minor's special inclusion in gittin therefore extends only to a courtyard that sufficiently resembles her hand, namely where she stands beside it. Regarding whether a courtyard acquires while the owner is asleep, it follows from what was explained that a guarded courtyard, which does not require the owner to stand beside it, acquires even while he sleeps. But an unguarded courtyard requires the owner to stand beside it in the active guarding sense. A sleeping person does not count as standing beside it, as Ran explains in Gittin. Concerning whether hekdesh has courtyard acquisition, Magen Avraham in Orach Chaim 154, seif katan 3, cites Agudah: one who finds an object in a synagogue acquires it himself, and we do not say that the synagogue courtyard acquires it for hekdesh, because hekdesh has no "hand." Agudah derives this from the rule that if one consecrated a cistern and it later filled with water, there is no me'ilah in the subsequently entered water, because hekdesh has no hand. Ramban in Bava Batra 79, however, writes that perhaps hekdesh does acquire the water, except that me'ilah does not apply to property that came to hekdesh automatically. Me'ilah requires property consecrated through a person's act, as indicated by the verse speaking of the sacred things of Israel. Even according to Tosafot, who hold that me'ilah can apply to consecrations of non Jews in certain settings, property that became hekdesh without any human consecration may remain outside me'ilah. Ramban therefore appears to hold that hekdesh does have hand. Tosafot Yom Tov asks that even if hekdesh has no hand, it surely has agency, for kohanim are agents of Heaven. Since courtyard can work through agency, why should a hekdesh courtyard not acquire? See Ketzot HaChoshen. The answer appears from Nimukei Yosef on the Mishnah of seeing people run after a found object. A found object cannot be acquired through ordinary agency, because in monetary acquisition the effective agency must ultimately derive from the owner of the property, and in a found object there is no owner's appointment. In addition, acquiring a found object for one person can prejudice other potential finders. Therefore courtyard acquisition of a found object works only through hand. If hekdesh has no hand, its courtyard cannot acquire a found object. Even if Ran in Shitah Mekubetzet says that a courtyard may acquire a found object through rabbinic agency, that enactment may have been made only for private persons, like the four-cubit acquisition instituted to prevent quarrels. That policy does not apply to hekdesh. One may object that hekdesh does appear to have a hand. Shach in siman 255 writes that once consecrated property reaches the treasurer, the donor can no longer annul his consecration because the treasurer's meshichah acquires it. This implies that the treasurer is the hand of hekdesh. Why then should the treasurer or hekdesh courtyard not acquire a found object? It appears that Agudah is still correct, and there need not be a dispute with Ramban. A found object cannot be acquired through the treasurer's hand because the treasurer himself functions as an agent of Heaven. For a found object, ordinary agency is ineffective both because it prejudices others and because there is no owner appointing an agent. Just as Hashem did not make one person an agent to acquire a found object for another at others' expense, the treasurer was not made an agent to acquire a found object for hekdesh at others' expense. Ramban can agree to that. Ran in Nedarim 34 writes that one can cause ownerless bread lying in his own courtyard to become hekdesh through his courtyard. That is different. A private person is using his own courtyard to acquire for hekdesh, and because he could acquire for himself, he can also acquire for hekdesh. By contrast, a courtyard already belonging to hekdesh does not acquire a found object for the reason stated. Ramban's case of a consecrated cistern filling with water, or a dovecote filling with doves, is also different because there is no prejudice to others. Without the consecrated cistern or dovecote, the particular water or doves would not have come there. The hekdesh property itself caused them to arrive. In addition, ownerless property has no present owner, so there is no requirement of agency from an owner. In such a case the treasurer can function as the hand of hekdesh and agent of Heaven. This explains the Mishnah in Shekalim concerning money found on the Temple Mount, which remains ordinary money rather than being acquired by hekdesh. This does not contradict Ramban. A lost sum of money remains tied to its former owner until someone acquires it, so the special barriers to acquisition by agency remain. But if a person gives a gift to hekdesh, the gift certainly can be acquired by a hekdesh courtyard when delivered there before the treasurer, because there is agency from the owner and no prejudice to others. Ketzot asks that a synagogue is a jointly owned courtyard of the congregation. Nevertheless Agudah needed the reason that hekdesh has no hand because once the congregation dedicated the building to sacred synagogue use, it is no worse than a courtyard rented to hekdesh. A rented courtyard can acquire for the renter. The treasurer is the hand of hekdesh and seeks to acquire for the sacred purpose, and the dedicated character of the property supplies the relevant intent. Agudah therefore had to explain why a lost object found in a synagogue still belongs to the finder. Ketzot also asks from Shevuot 24, where the Gemara says consecrated property can sometimes be subject to annulment. Why does entry into the Temple courtyard not immediately make it acquired by hekdesh in a way that eliminates annulment? This is not a proof that hekdesh has no courtyard. Acquisition that would defeat the donor's later annulment is like seizing for a creditor where others are prejudiced. It cannot be accomplished by agency without the owner's relevant instruction. In any event, there is an even simpler answer. Shach in siman 255 says that once it reaches the treasurer there is no annulment because the treasurer's meshichah is like acquisition. If the animal was delivered to kohanim, agents of Heaven, slaughtered and its blood thrown, how could annulment remain possible? Tosafot in Keritot 13 indeed write that after slaughter and blood service there is no annulment. One source in Beit HaZevach questions this from Shevuot, but there is no difficulty. Shevuot merely says the Mishnah does not count something that can ever be annulled; hekdesh can at least be annulled before slaughter, so it belongs to that category. In Keritot, however, the discussion concerns whether annulment remains possible at the present stage, and after slaughter it does not. The sugyot therefore speak from different perspectives. Regarding a rented courtyard, siman 313 states that a courtyard rented to another may nevertheless acquire for the landlord, even though it is in the tenant's possession. Sema asks from siman 260, seif 4, where if landlord and tenant live together and a disabled deer enters, both can acquire it. In my view the distinction is as follows. Although a person's courtyard can acquire for him without his awareness, a rented courtyard acquires for the tenant only when the tenant consciously wants the courtyard to acquire, such as where the transferor intends to transfer through the rented courtyard. But when a lost object enters without the tenant's awareness, it belongs to the owner of the courtyard. A rented courtyard is certainly also the tenant's hand. Ran explains that a courtyard is like a hand because a person ordinarily places everything beneficial to him there. The same is true of a rented courtyard. But the underlying corpus remains the landlord's, and it is also his hand in a basic sense. The tenant has rented the owner's "hand" for the purposes of the rental. This resembles a hired worker. Siman 270 says a worker's own found object belongs to him because the employer did not hire him for that purpose. Likewise, a minor daughter's ordinary found object belongs to her father because her hand is treated as his. Even if another person hired the daughter for work, an unrelated found object can still belong according to the underlying family rule because she was not hired for finding. So too with a rented courtyard: when a lost object enters without the tenant's awareness, the rental was not made for that acquisition, and the landlord remains the one whose courtyard receives the found object. But siman 260 speaks of a disabled deer or fledglings that cannot fly, where Sema in siman 268, seif katan 15, requires the claimant to say, "My field shall acquire for me." Siman 270 says that if one hires a worker for all tasks and then specifically points out found objects for him to gather, the finds belong to the employer. The worker's hand is then being used for the hired purpose. Similarly, if the tenant sees the found object entering and says, "My field shall acquire it for me," he is using the rented courtyard for the very purpose covered by his possession, like directing a worker to gather found objects. The find therefore belongs to the tenant. This also answers Raavad's question on Rambam from the incident in Bava Metzia where a place was rented in order to acquire produce. It also explains our ruling in siman 198 that if the seller's house is rented to the buyer and the object is there, the buyer acquires through courtyard even though siman 313 says a rented courtyard can acquire for the landlord. When the tenant-buyer expressly wants the rented space to acquire the purchased object, the rental is being used for that purpose and it is his courtyard for that object. Conversely, if the buyer's courtyard is rented to the seller and the seller's merchandise remains there, the buyer-landlord does not acquire merely because the goods rest in the corpus of his land. As against ordinary goods the rented space is the tenant-seller's domain. Only with an unrelated found object, which the tenant never intended the rental to cover, do we revert to the landlord's residual courtyard right. MahariT, responsum 65, writes that the landlord acquires even merchandise resting in a courtyard rented to the seller, treating it as the landlord's courtyard. In my view the ruling in siman 195 points the other way, because when the buyer's courtyard is rented to the seller, acquisition there is treated as dependent on money rather than as courtyard acquisition. I later saw Machaneh Efrayim, who likewise disagrees with MahariT, though for a different reason. He says every acquisition requires movement from one domain to another. When the seller has rented the place, the object has not yet left his domain; when the seller rents the location of the goods to the buyer, the site itself has partially passed to the buyer and the goods therefore move with it. My explanation above remains, in my view, the primary rationale. And a courtyard that is borrowed rather than rented is the same. Borrowing and renting both create possessory use rights. Eruvin 30, Tosafot, implies that a borrowed courtyard can acquire for the borrower. A worker is different: to acquire the legal status of a hired servant one ordinarily needs payment, because an unpaid worker can withdraw even from work involving potential loss, as explained in siman 333. A courtyard, however, is not a servant; the borrower receives a property-use right. The tenant can also acquire for a third person through his rented courtyard, because for covered uses the courtyard is fully his. Only with a found object arriving without his awareness does the landlord acquire, because that was not part of the rental purpose. Concerning a deposited object that the depositor later transfers to the custodian, Taz in siman 189 writes that the custodian does not acquire through his own courtyard because Rashbam says the custodian's premises where the deposit rests are treated as the depositor's domain. MahariT rejects this for several reasons. First, by what kinyan did the depositor acquire the custodian's space? Renting land ordinarily requires money or chazakah. Second, if no specific place was designated, no defined area was ever transferred to the depositor. Third, when the depositor now transfers the deposit to the custodian, the custodian's courtyard and the gift can become effective together. MahariT is correct. Ketzot HaChoshen attempts to reject the proofs, but in my view his objections do not stand. Permission for use alone does not create the kind of land right needed to make the place an acquiring courtyard, as I wrote in siman 192. Even if the depositor had permission to place the object wherever convenient, the custodian remains entitled to move it from place to place, so no particular spot ceases to be the custodian's courtyard. And the depositor can certainly waive any residual claim against the custodian and allow the custodian to hold the object in his own house merely by speech; no additional proof is needed. Ketzot himself cites a clear proof for MahariT from Kiddushin 47: if a man betroths a woman with a deposit of his that is already in her house, she is betrothed. That is decisive. For partners acquiring from one another through a shared courtyard, see what I wrote at the beginning of siman 176. Practical rules emerging from the introduction
A person's courtyard that is unguarded, while he stands beside it, works according to everyone through the law of hand because it resembles one's nearby hand. This applies to an adult. A male minor does not have that type of hand, because an adult sleeping beside the courtyard also fails where awareness and guarding are required. A minor, who lacks that awareness, is like a sleeping person. A female minor is different because Scripture specifically treats her courtyard beside her as hand for gittin, and that rule can extend to monetary law. Because an adult's nearby courtyard works as hand, a non Jew likewise acquires through an unguarded courtyard when standing beside it. A non Jew has a hand and has awareness to guard; he is unlike a minor. A guarded courtyard where the owner does not stand beside it works, according to Rif and Rosh, through agency. Therefore a male minor, female minor, and non Jew do not acquire in that manner because they lack ordinary agency. Shach in siman 243 disagrees and says a female minor can acquire monetary property through such a guarded courtyard without standing beside it, but the primary ruling follows the majority of authorities. According to Ran, an adult's guarded courtyard can work as hand even without standing beside it, because anything beneficial to him is normally placed there and the courtyard is like an extended hand. According to Ran, a non Jew can therefore acquire through a guarded courtyard without standing beside it as well. However, Ran's reasoning does not apply to male or female minors, because they are not people who legally maintain guarded courtyards in that sense. Property in their hands can itself be treated as knowingly exposed to loss, and they also lack agency. Thus even Ran agrees that minors do not acquire through a guarded courtyard unless they stand beside it where the special female-minor rule can operate. A guarded courtyard acquires for an adult even while he sleeps, because the courtyard guards itself. An unguarded courtyard, which requires standing beside it, does not acquire while he sleeps. An unguarded courtyard where the person does not stand beside it does not acquire at all according to Rif and Rambam, even for a gift, because they do not rule like Rav Pappa in Bava Metzia 11. Rosh and Ran, who do rule like Rav Pappa, hold that a gift can be acquired there through agency when another person consciously transfers it. Therefore minors and non Jews, who lack agency, do not acquire even a gift in that manner. The general rule that a courtyard can acquire without the owner's awareness operates in such cases only through agency; minors and non Jews therefore do not receive that advantage. A courtyard of hekdesh does not acquire an ordinary lost sum of money even according to Ramban, who recognizes a form of hand for hekdesh, because the treasurer's status itself is agency of Heaven and ordinary agency cannot acquire a found object where others are prejudiced and there is no appointment by the property's owner. But if one consecrates a cistern and it fills with water, or a dovecote and it fills with doves, the courtyard can acquire because no one else is prejudiced by property that would not have arisen there absent the hekdesh structure, and true ownerless property has no owner from whom agency is required. Likewise, if a person gives a gift to hekdesh and places it in a hekdesh courtyard, hekdesh acquires because there is another person's conscious transfer and no prejudice to others. A private person may also acquire ownerless property for hekdesh through his own courtyard where he could have acquired it for himself. Sacred property delivered to the treasurer can no longer be annulled, because delivery to the treasurer functions like meshichah. A courtyard rented or borrowed through a valid kinyan is the courtyard and hand of the tenant or borrower. It resembles hiring the owner's hand for the agreed use. An unrelated found object entering without the tenant's awareness ordinarily belongs to the landlord because the courtyard was not rented for that purpose. But if the tenant sees the find and says that his rented field should acquire it, it belongs to the tenant, like a worker expressly directed to gather finds. All the more so, a gift placed there for the tenant is acquired by the tenant, even if the landlord himself is the donor. The tenant may also acquire for another through the rented courtyard. Conversely, where the tenant gives a gift to the landlord while it remains in the rented courtyard, the landlord does not acquire merely by virtue of ownership of the underlying land, because for ordinary uses the courtyard is presently the tenant's domain. The general principle is that a rented or borrowed courtyard functions like the hand of the tenant or borrower for the purposes included within his possessory use. Concerning the opinion that a courtyard guarded by the seller is sufficient, Shach, seif katan 1, asks from Kiddushin 27. There the Gemara explains Rabban Gamliel's transfer as acquisition by agav. If Rav Pappa's rule of another transferring mind were accepted, why not say the acquisition occurred through courtyard? In my view, the Gemara needed agav because of the poor man's tithe transferred through R. Akiva for the poor. Even if the tithe given directly to R. Yehoshua could be acquired through the rented courtyard, R. Akiva could not acquire for the poor merely through his courtyard unless after the donor's statement R. Akiva himself expressly caused his courtyard to acquire for them. The Mishnah does not record such a statement. Therefore that transfer must have worked through agav. The Gemara's proof in Kiddushin is thus from the poor man's tithe, not necessarily from the tithe delivered personally to R. Yehoshua. The latter could work through courtyard even though agav would be difficult because the donor's discretionary benefit in choosing the recipient is not treated as property. The grain in the incident was still tevel, as Shitah Mekubetzet explains, and Rabban Gamliel transferred whatever tithe he would later separate. Regarding terumat ma'aser, the remaining interest is only the discretionary benefit of choosing the recipient, and that is not property sufficient for agav. Nor can we treat the tevel as ordinary property because use involving destruction is prohibited while it remains tevel for terumah and terumat ma'aser. Poor man's tithe differs. Destructive benefit from produce that is tevel only to poor man's tithe is permitted; the special terumah prohibition does not apply. Such produce therefore has ordinary monetary value through fuel and similar uses, and can be transferred through agav. This is the source of the Gemara's proof. R. Yehoshua's terumat ma'aser, by contrast, could require Rav Pappa's courtyard rationale.
Nevertheless, if the seller... See Shach, seif katan 2, who questions this clause. According to the opinion that another person's conscious transfer allows acquisition through a courtyard guarded by the seller, the buyer should acquire even here, as the sugya in Bava Metzia implies. Taz also asks why the text says "the courtyard itself" and what that phrase excludes. In my view the words "Nevertheless, if the seller..." do not belong here. They should appear at the beginning of the gloss, referring back to Mechaber's statement that a guarded courtyard acquires. Rama means that if the seller rented to the buyer the very courtyard that is itself guarded by the seller, while the seller's other goods remain mixed in it, and the buyer merely rented the place occupied by the goods he bought, the place is treated as unguarded relative to the buyer and does not acquire for him. This also explains the phrase "the courtyard itself." It is specifically where the seller rented the same courtyard to the buyer while the seller's own goods remained intermixed. But if the seller's goods were located in the buyer's own courtyard and the seller sold only some of them, the courtyard remains guarded for the buyer even though the seller retains a right of entry to reach his remaining goods. The seller is then like a guest whose access does not destroy the buyer's guarding control. Accordingly, when the transferor himself guards the goods and the buyer has a genuine rented possessory space, the opinion that another transferring mind suffices can still apply. But where the goods are entirely in a truly unguarded courtyard of the buyer, even sale or gift does not help merely because the donor is present. The reason that sale can help in some unguarded settings is that the transferor's guarding is treated as the recipient's guarding. Yet even the recipient's own guarding from outside an unguarded courtyard is insufficient unless he stands in the required position so that the courtyard resembles his hand. The transferor's guarding cannot be stronger than the recipient's own guarding. This also refutes Shitah Mekubetzet's suggestion that a gift is always considered to be in a guarded courtyard because no one else is permitted to take it. Our ruling requires the courtyard to resemble the recipient's hand or agent in the legally prescribed manner; mere legal protection from strangers is not enough. Even though Beit Yosef writes that an unguarded courtyard, guarded neither by seller nor buyer, does not acquire, there is a further application. Suppose Reuven gave movable property to Shimon and rented him the place where the goods lay, while the goods remained mixed with Reuven's property. According to the opinion that guarding by the transferor is sufficient, Shimon acquires. Later Shimon transfers those same goods to Levi and also assigns Levi the possessory right he had in Reuven's courtyard for keeping them. Levi can acquire even though the courtyard is not guarded by Shimon, his transferor, or by Levi himself. It is sufficient that it remains guarded by Reuven, the transferor from Shimon's own transferor. Proof appears from the Mishnah where Rabban Gamliel gave tithe to R. Yehoshua and rented him its place. R. Yehoshua then stood and gave terumat ma'aser to R. Elazar ben Azaryah and rented its place to him. The courtyard was not guarded by R. Yehoshua or R. Elazar ben Azaryah; it remained guarded by Rabban Gamliel, the previous transferor. Yet the acquisition could work according to the same theory. Thus guarding by the transferor's transferor can suffice where the chain of possessory rights is established.

A person's four cubits. See Shach, seif katan 5, who discusses why the Sages still needed to institute meshichah in a side alley if four cubits already acquire there. In my view the answer is simple. Meshichah is needed where the seller is also standing there. In that case the buyer certainly does not acquire automatically through four cubits. Meshichah acquires in a side alley even if the seller arrived first and initially occupied those four cubits. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that according to Ran, who distinguishes whether the vessel came first, the place occupied by a vessel in a side alley can become associated with the vessel's owner. He then suggests that kiddushin differs because four cubits were instituted even in the public domain. Therefore, if the husband threw kiddushin into the public domain and afterward the woman entered the surrounding four cubits, she could become betrothed, because the place where the kiddushin rested never became the husband's acquiring domain. In my view this cannot be correct. If so, even if the husband himself first entered those four cubits in the public domain and the woman entered afterward, he should still be able to throw her get or kiddushin there, because the husband has no ordinary acquisition in public-domain four cubits. Yet Gittin 78 says we must examine which one entered the four cubits first. Necessarily the Sages gave the public domain, for purposes of gittin and kiddushin, the same functional law as a side alley to both husband and wife. Just as the husband's prior presence can defeat the woman's four-cubit acquisition there, his vessels or money already resting there can also prevent the place from becoming hers.
