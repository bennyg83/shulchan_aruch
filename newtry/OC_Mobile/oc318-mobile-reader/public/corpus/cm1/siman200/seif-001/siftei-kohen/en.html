And so it appears proper to rule, etc. It appears that the Rama followed the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. The Rosh writes that the Rif omitted Rav Pappa's distinction between a found object and a gift, implying that he treats them alike and follows the first resolution, namely that Rabban Gamliel transferred the movable property by agav. The Rosh nevertheless preferred Rav Pappa because he was later and because Rav Ashi answered the challenge from divorce to gift. In my opinion, however, the Rif and Rambam are primary. The entire anonymous discussion in Kiddushin 27a supports them. The Gemara seeks to prove from Rabban Gamliel's act that movable property transferred by agav must be piled upon the land, and rejects the proof only because he may have acted so as not to burden the recipients. It later asks whether land may be sold while movable property is given as a gift, and proves it from Rabban Gamliel's declaration that the tithe he would measure was given to Rabbi Yehoshua and its place rented to him. If Rav Pappa's explanation were accepted, this would prove nothing, because the acquisition could have been through the recipient's courtyard rather than agav. It is also difficult why Rabban Gamliel needed agav at all if Rav Pappa's courtyard principle itself acquired for the recipient. Although strained answers can be offered, the plain sugya excludes Rav Pappa's resolution. The Maggid Mishneh states that some rule like Rav Pappa, but the Ramban inferred from the Rif that gift is equated with finding and that the end of the first chapter of Kiddushin proves this, which is also the Rambam's view. The Ritva likewise explains that although some follow Rav Pappa because he is later, the Rif requires the recipient to stand beside the field even for a gift. The Gemara in Kiddushin treats Rabban Gamliel's act as agav, not as Rav Pappa's courtyard acquisition, and therefore Rav Pappa's resolution is not accepted. Tosafot Rid, the Semag, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and apparently the Ran agree. The Rosh's point that Rav Ashi answers for Rav Pappa is not decisive, because even if Rav Ashi held that view, the anonymous Gemara in Kiddushin does not. Moreover, the Ran explains that Rav Ashi's answer was needed even according to the accepted law, so that divorce should not present a difficulty, and the Ritva says the same. Nevertheless, since the Baal HaItur, Rabbi Yehuda bar Reuven of Barcelona, the Rosh, Raavan, the Hagahot Mordechai, and Hagahot Maimoniyot support Rav Pappa, I am not fit to decide conclusively and the matter remains a monetary doubt. But to instruct initially as the Rama does is certainly difficult, for the majority of poskim follow the Rif and Rambam, and that view appears primary.
However, if the seller, etc. The Rama's words are surprising. He derived them from the Beit Yosef in the name of Hagahot Mordechai, yet Hagahot Mordechai itself states in the name of Ri that this applies only according to the Gemara's first answer, which requires a protected courtyard even for sale or gift. Where the buyer's produce is intermixed with the owner's and no separation exists, the courtyard is unprotected. That is the Mechaber's view. According to the Rama, however, who rules like Rav Pappa that another person's intent to transfer is different, the buyer acquires even when the produce is mixed. Hagahot Mordechai states this explicitly, as do Hagahot Maimoniyot and Tosafot at the end of Bava Metzia 11a. Thus the Rama's ruling is inconsistent with his own position.

For a person's four cubits acquire for him, etc. The Beit Yosef cites the Ran and students of the Rashba: if so, why did the Sages institute pulling or delivery in an alley, since four cubits should acquire? They answer that four cubits were said only where the recipient is standing there and the object is placed within his four cubits. If the utensil was there first, the rule does not apply, because the utensil's place is already acquired to its owner. In my view, this distinction applies to gift and sale, but not to a found object, as is evident in Bava Metzia 10a. A gift is certainly no weaker than a found object when it is placed within his four cubits. The Beit Yosef further writes that the Rosh appears not to distinguish whether the person or utensil came first, and the Darkei Moshe says the same of Rabbeinu Yerucham. The Sema infers from the Rama's unqualified wording that he likewise makes no distinction. According to them, pulling was required in an alley only outside the buyer's four cubits, such as when he draws the object by a rope, or because pulling is a Torah acquisition whereas four cubits acquire only by rabbinic enactment.
