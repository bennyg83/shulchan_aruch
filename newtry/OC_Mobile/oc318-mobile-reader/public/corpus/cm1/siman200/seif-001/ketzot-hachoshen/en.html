In the purchaser's domain. Sefer Agudah says that one who finds an object in a synagogue acquires it and we do not say the synagogue courtyard acquires it for consecrated property, because courtyard acquisition derives from one's hand and consecrated property has no hand. The source is the Mishnah concerning a consecrated pit, refuse heap, or dovecote. If they were full when consecrated, misuse applies to what was inside; if they filled only afterward, misuse does not apply to the later contents. Rashbam and Tosafot explain that the Temple treasury's domain does not acquire like a private person's courtyard, because courtyard derives from hand and consecrated property has no hand. Tosafot Yom Tov asks why courtyard should not work through agency. Bava Metzia says that even if courtyard derives from hand, it is no worse than agency for beneficial acquisitions. Why should there be no agency for Heaven? Kohanim are described as agents of Heaven. The answer is that courtyard agency works only for a courtyard that remains sufficiently analogous to a hand. Tosafot in Gittin explains that the agency model does not rescue a moving courtyard, because Scripture first defined the institution through the hand analogy. An unguarded courtyard can still qualify because the defect is only in guarding, not in the body of the courtyard. A moving courtyard differs fundamentally from a hand and cannot acquire even as an agent. Consequently, where no concept of "hand" exists at all, as with consecrated property, the special agency of courtyard never arises. Even if Heaven can have agents in other contexts, the statutory courtyard mechanism is absent because there is no hand model to which it can attach. This answers Tosafot Yom Tov. Ramban records another approach. Perhaps the Temple treasury does acquire through its courtyard, but property acquired automatically by the courtyard is not subject to misuse because the sacred status did not arise through a human act of consecration. This possibility creates concern even for a found object in a synagogue. Yet the Temple Mount poses a difficulty. The Mishnah says money found there is ordinary money in the relevant places, and the Jerusalem Talmud explains we do not assume it came from the Temple collection. If the Temple courtyard acquired all found money automatically, it should become sacred. One answer follows Rosh and Nimukei Yosef regarding a lost object entering a courtyard before the owner has despaired. A courtyard is no better than the owner's hand. If the object entered before despair, later despair does not let the courtyard acquire it. Money dropped on the Temple Mount likewise enters before the loser becomes aware and despairs. Other authorities explain the old wall case differently, namely that a courtyard does not acquire unusual finds without the owner's awareness. That explanation is harder for consecrated property because "Heaven's awareness" is conceptually always present. Those authorities therefore likely must follow Rashbam and Tosafot that the Temple treasury has no courtyard acquisition at all. The synagogue itself should not be classified as Temple consecration for this purpose. A synagogue is communal private property, like a jointly owned courtyard. Rivash says a person's four cubits can acquire there, and the first chapter of Bava Metzia expressly says money found in synagogues and study halls belongs to the finder under the relevant circumstances. Thus Sefer Agudah's result is correct, but not because a synagogue has no "hand." The better reason is that communal courtyard acquisition is subject to the ordinary rules of prior despair and unusual finds. Ramban's alternative theory also encounters a difficulty from vows. If a person has an ownerless loaf before him and says it should be consecrated, Ran explains that his four cubits can acquire it for consecrated property. Misuse then applies. This shows that property acquired for consecration through the courtyard mechanism can indeed become subject to misuse. It therefore strongly supports Rashbam and Tosafot that a courtyard belonging to consecrated property itself does not acquire, while a private person's courtyard can acquire on behalf of consecration. The distinction is clear. The Temple treasury has no statutory courtyard of its own. But a private person can use his own courtyard, or four cubits operating as his courtyard, to acquire property for the Temple treasury just as one can acquire for another person. One remaining issue concerns future property entering a retained courtyard. Tosafot in Bava Batra says that if one consecrates a pit or dovecote and reserves in himself the power to acquire future water or birds for consecration, the mechanism still depends on whether one can act through an agent for something not yet in existence. Since ordinary agency cannot be appointed for a future subject, the retained courtyard cannot acquire those future items unless one follows Rabbi Meir, who recognizes transfer of future property. This completes the structure.
If it is guarded according to the seller's intent. The simple wording implies that if the courtyard is not guarded at all, even by the seller, it cannot acquire. The usual explanation of another person's transfer is that the courtyard is at least guarded by the giver. Shitah Mekubetzet, however, gives a stronger theory. Where another person is transferring the gift, no guarding is needed at all. The property is effectively guarded for the recipient because no third person can acquire it against the transferor's intent. By contrast, a found object is ownerless and anyone can acquire it, so an unguarded courtyard gives its owner no preference. This explanation is elegant and is supported by the version of Hagahot Maimoniyot cited by Beit Yosef. There, even an awake slave who cannot walk can serve as a courtyard for a gift, although the property is not guarded in the ordinary physical sense. The decisive factor is that another person is transferring it and no outsider can acquire without that person's consent. Beit Yosef nevertheless writes that no authority allows a completely unguarded courtyard even in a gift. I find that difficult in light of the sources just cited. The Gemara concerning an owner who publicly announced that any find entering his courtyard that day should be his also supports the principle. Although the courtyard was ordinarily unguarded, once people withdrew because of the public claim it became effectively guarded. All the more in a gift, where the transferor's will itself blocks outsiders. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Gittin concerning the airspace of a vessel. Rashi says the air of a vessel is not made for guarding because vessels are intended to hold objects in their interior. In divorce, the woman's courtyard must be guarded like her hand, so mere airspace is insufficient. In a sale or gift, however, if another person is transferring the object, physical guarding is unnecessary under the Shitah Mekubetzet approach. The airspace can therefore acquire. Rosh's phrase that the giver's guarding counts as the recipient's guarding can be understood in the same conceptual way: because no one can acquire without the giver's intent, the gift is effectively secure for the recipient.

A person's four cubits. Sema and Shach cite the students of Rashba that the rule applies only if the person entered his four cubits before the seller's vessel was placed there. If the seller's vessel was there first, the place became the seller's domain and the buyer's later four cubits do not acquire. Rabbeinu Yerucham and Rosh appear not to distinguish. The reason for the distinction follows Tosafot in Kiddushin. Meshichah works in an alley, while mesirah ordinarily works in the public domain. Tosafot asks why one acquisition should exclude the other merely because of location and answers that an alley is like a private domain in which whoever first occupies the relevant space acquires the use of it. Ordinarily the seller brought his animal there first, so the area of the animal is treated as the seller's courtyard. Mesirah cannot acquire in the seller's domain. Meshichah works because it removes the animal from its original place. The same explains the four cubit rule. Four cubits acquire only in a side street or similar area, not the ordinary public domain. In that side street whoever first occupies the space has the better temporary right. If the seller's vessel preceded the buyer, the space is already treated as the seller's domain and the buyer's later four cubits cannot acquire there. With ownerless property there is no seller and no prior owner. Therefore the four cubit acquisition works even if the found object was there first. For divorce and kiddushin, the Sages extended four cubit acquisition even into the public domain for practical marital reasons. There the seller domain concern is absent. Accordingly, in kiddushin, if the man threw money into the public domain and the woman afterward entered its four cubits, her four cubits can acquire even though the money was there first. In divorce, by contrast, a get already lying on the ground creates the separate problem of "take your get from the ground," so the practical application differs.
