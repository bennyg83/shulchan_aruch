Guarded according to his awareness. Meaning according to the purchaser's awareness. If it is guarded only according to the seller's awareness, it is ineffective, because the purchaser's courtyard must resemble his hand, which is guarded according to his own awareness. The dissenting view holds that when another person intentionally transfers property to him, the transferor's guarding is treated like the recipient's guarding. Sema. See the Beurim, seif katan 3, that if the area is enclosed by walls, even if it is not locked and not roofed, it is considered a guarded courtyard:
And it appears proper to rule this way. The Shach writes that the principal ruling follows the Mechaber, because the plain conclusion of the Gemara in Kiddushin 26 is that Rabban Gamliel transferred property to Rabbi Yehoshua through the latter's courtyard as an agav acquisition. This indicates that an unguarded courtyard does not acquire even where another person intentionally transfers the property. Nevertheless, the Shach did not wish to rule against the Rama and therefore writes that it remains a doubt of law. See the Beurim for a resolution of the Shach's difficulty:
Mixed together. This is not literal. Whenever there is no partition separating the respective property, the courtyard is considered unguarded. Sema. The Shach and Taz question the Rama: what difference does it make that the courtyard is called unguarded, when according to this view it acquires even through an unguarded courtyard where another person intentionally transfers the property? See the Beurim. The clause beginning "however" does not belong here. It should appear before "and some disagree" and refers to the Mechaber. Therefore the Rama speaks of a courtyard that the transferor himself rented to the purchaser, teaching that specifically where the body of the courtyard still belongs to the transferor, who merely rented the purchaser a place for his produce, and there is no partition between the purchaser's acquired goods and the seller's remaining goods, it is considered unguarded and does not acquire. But where the courtyard itself belongs to the purchaser and the seller's produce is lying there, and the seller sells him part of that produce without a partition between the purchased produce and the seller's remaining produce, the courtyard is considered guarded and acquires even according to the Mechaber. A seller's goods in another person's courtyard do not nullify the purchaser's guarding, because anyone whose body of the courtyard is not his is like a guest and does not defeat the resident's control:
And some disagree. According to this view he acquires even if the goods are mixed together in the transferor's own courtyard. See the Beurim. The dissent applies specifically in this type of case. But where the recipient's courtyard is itself completely unguarded, such that he must stand beside it, the transferor's standing there does not help. See further in the Beurim: if the courtyard is fundamentally guarded but the purchased produce is mixed with other produce, then according to the dissenting view, if this purchaser later transfers the produce to a third person by means of the original transferor's courtyard, that third person acquires, because the courtyard is guarded according to the awareness of the current giver, who is the owner of the produce being transferred:
Beside that domain. See the Beurim. He must stand literally beside it, even according to the Mechaber, because we require his hand, as it were, to be adjacent to the courtyard. Standing at some distance is ineffective even if he can watch it. Conversely, even if he stands beside it or within it, he must also be capable of guarding it. Therefore, if a large field contains a found object at one end and he stands at the other end, he does not acquire it because he cannot effectively guard the object from there:
Four cubits. The Ran distinguishes that the recipient acquires through his four cubits only if he entered those four cubits before the utensil. If the utensil entered first, the space is acquired for the owner of the utensil and the recipient cannot afterward acquire through the same four cubits. Many authorities make no such distinction and hold that even if the utensil arrived first, he acquires. Sema. The reason the Sages nevertheless had to enact meshichah is that one may be standing outside the relevant four cubits and then pull the item, or because meshichah may itself be a Torah acquisition. See the Beurim for another practical difference: if the transferor himself entered the four cubits first, all agree the recipient does not acquire through those four cubits and needs meshichah. See further in the Beurim. Although four cubits can effect kiddushin even in the public domain, if the kiddushin money entered first and the woman only afterward entered those four cubits, she is not betrothed.
