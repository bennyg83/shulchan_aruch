That it contains a forgery, etc. Nimukei Yosef in chapter Get Pashut cites Ritva and Rashba that if a litigant demands a copy of a document being used against him, all agree the document holder can say, "You seek to devise false stratagems against my document so as to cause me loss." That is a substantial claim. He therefore shows the original to the judges and they read it, but no copy is given. See also Responsa Rashba, siman 585. The Mechaber holds that a specific allegation of forgery is different, and Beit Yosef and Rama explicitly make this distinction in siman 60. They cite Rambam and the Rosh, who writes in abbreviated form: there is concern that the document is forged, and if a copy is placed in the defendant's hands he may think carefully about how the document was fabricated and clarify the matter. If it is genuine, he cannot invalidate it merely through the copy. Beit Yosef and Rama infer that Rosh allowed a copy only where forgery is claimed. In my humble opinion, the responsum indicates the opposite. The questioner said that their practice was to provide a copy to every litigant who requested one, but recently they had seen Rashba's responsum forbidding it. The Rosh answered that specifically writing the opposing party's claims for him is objectionable. A clever and scheming person who constantly sees the other's claims may devise answers to distort known facts, undermine true statements, and dress falsehood in persuasive form. A copy of the document is different. Therefore, he said, maintain your custom, for it is proper and just. At the beginning of the responsum he also writes that the ordinary practice among Ashkenazic judges is to give the defendant a copy of the document so that perhaps he will detect forgery. The questioner had stated that they gave copies to every defendant, and the Rosh answered that this was indeed proper Ashkenazic practice. He did not limit the ruling to an explicit allegation of forgery. Moreover, he appears to reject Rashba's general rule, not merely to distinguish cases. We therefore hear that according to the Rosh and Ashkenazic practice there is no distinction: whenever a defendant requests a copy in order to find a possible defense within the document, one provides it. Nimukei Yosef's statement is no proof otherwise, because he cites Rashba, whose view the Rosh rejected. It is also possible the Rosh mentions forgery as an especially strong example. Even though an erasure or scraping that constitutes forgery may not be visible in the copy itself, the copy may help the defendant realize where in the original to direct the judges' attention. All the more so with other defenses that may be visible directly in the copy. This explains why Rosh distinguishes only between transcribing the parties' claims, where a clever person can fashion new arguments, and copying the fixed language of a document, which is difficult to add to or subtract from.
