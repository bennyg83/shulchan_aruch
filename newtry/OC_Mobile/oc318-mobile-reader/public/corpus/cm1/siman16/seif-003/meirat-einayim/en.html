The Tur concludes in the name of the Rosh: all the more so where the other party himself holds a document establishing his opponent's right and is suing unlawfully, how can we allow him to consume prohibited money and collect what is not his? This requires study because the Rosh writes in Responsa, rule 6, siman 20, that when a general ban is imposed after testimony, the ban does not apply to relatives and they need not testify. Rama cites this below in siman 28:2 and rules that it is primary, yet here the Mechaber says the opposing party must disclose the information and Rama is silent. One can answer that a general, unspecified ban on those who know testimony does not include relatives because they are not legally competent witnesses. But where the court explicitly includes relatives, and even the litigant himself, requiring everyone to acknowledge the truth and describe what occurred, the ban applies to them as well, and if beit din requests this they must do so. Although I write below in siman 28 that Or Zarua disagrees even where the ban explicitly includes relatives, perhaps Rama ruled here like Mordechai. More likely, however, the Tur and Mechaber's statement that the right is "in the opponent's hand" means that the opponent actually holds a written document recording the other party's right, or knows who the witnesses are who can establish it. The ban then requires him to produce the document or identify the witnesses. This differs from a general ban upon anyone who knows oral testimony. In the latter setting, relatives and the opposing litigant are excluded because "testimony" applies only to one legally fit to testify. We do not say that the litigant is obligated to confess under the ban and that his admission would equal one hundred witnesses. If that were so, anyone who seized property without witnesses would likewise be compelled under the ban to disclose it, which would make his seizure equivalent to one made before beit din and destroy his credibility to assert a counterclaim up to the value of the seizure. Rather, even if he admits the seizure, he remains believed to say, "you owe me," by a migo that he could have denied the seizure, as the Gemara says regarding one who denies a loan, perhaps he has another loan against him.
