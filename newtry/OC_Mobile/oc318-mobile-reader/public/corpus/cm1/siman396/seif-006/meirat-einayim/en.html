Because the ox commonly breaks the tie. The Rambam uses similar language. It should not be understood literally as meaning that the ox unties the knot by itself, as Rashi explains in the first chapter of Bava Kamma. If so, why would entrusting it to a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor count as the owner's negligence? The ox would have undone the tie even without them. Moreover, the word "untie" is awkward when applied to the animal itself. One might think the printed text should read that these incompetent custodians commonly untie it, but it is difficult to emend the standard books, and then the Rambam should not have said, "the ox and the like." The better explanation is that the Rambam follows the Gemara's phrase that an ox commonly breaks free. Tosafot explains there that this occurs through the handling of these custodians. The Maggid Mishneh likewise explains the Rambam: the animal may be tied properly, yet the owner is liable because such custodians commonly play with the animal and loosen the tie. This makes the beginning negligent, so even if the end is unavoidable, such as the animal tunneling out, liability can remain.
Even if they guarded it with superior guarding and it tunneled out, etc. The meaning appears to be as follows. When the owner first entrusted the animal to a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, he tied it strongly but left it with them without closing a door, relying upon them and upon the tie. Later it was found that the animal had become loose and caused damage. Even if those custodians also provided superior guarding, such as closing a door capable of standing securely, but the animal nevertheless tunneled out and caused damage, the owners remain liable. Although tunneling is an unavoidable accident, the owners were initially negligent by relying on the tie because such custodians commonly loosen it. We therefore assume they did so here, so the animal was effectively left without proper guarding. Ordinarily initial negligence creates liability only when the ultimate accident results from the same negligence, as explained in siman 291. Here the very danger expected from the initial negligence, namely that the animal would escape and damage, is what ultimately occurred. Therefore the owner remains liable even though the final mode of escape was unavoidable. Compare the law below in siman 410 concerning a pit covered with a cover unable to withstand camels.
