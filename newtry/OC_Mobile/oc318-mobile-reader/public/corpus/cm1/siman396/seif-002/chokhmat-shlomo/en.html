Seif 8. "He delivered it to one of the four types of custodians, etc." Note: Bava Kamma 40, as codified by the Rambam in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon 4:8, states: If one borrowed an ox believing it to be innocuous and it was found to be forewarned, then if the borrower knew that it had a habit of goring, the owners pay half damages because wherever the animal goes its owners' ownership remains upon it, and the borrower pays half damages because even had it been innocuous he knowingly accepted the risk of an animal that gores. If he did not know it had a habit of goring, the borrower pays nothing and the owners pay full damages, for if an innocuous ox became forewarned while in the borrower's house and was then returned to the owners, it would return to innocuous status because the change of possession alters its status. This is the Rambam's language from the Gemara. See my work on Orach Chaim, written in 5604 on the twentieth of Sivan, in the laws of Pesach, siman Tam, where I discussed this. It is surprising that the Shulchan Aruch here says nothing about this Rambam. One possibility is that the Talmudic statement that returning the animal to its owners restores its innocuous status follows the view that a rental acquisition effects a proprietary change. On that view, when the animal was lent it entered another possession, and a later return is another change of possession. According to our ruling that rental does not acquire, however, the animal remains legally the owner's even while in the borrower's house, so there is no change of possession. One could also say that the Rambam follows the view of the Hagahot Ashri cited by the Magen Avraham in Hilchot Shabbat that rental creates an acquisition at least stringently. That would create a monetary doubt as well, allowing the lender to defend himself by saying that perhaps rental did acquire and the change of possession altered the animal's status. The Mechaber, however, rules unequivocally that rental does not acquire and therefore would not recognize a change of possession; even after the animal is returned it would retain forewarned status. As for the omission of the distinction between whether the borrower knew the animal had a habit of goring, the Raavad challenges the Rambam and says that the Rambam's ruling contradicts the Gemara. The Maggid Mishneh answers, but the Lechem Mishneh objects that his answer works only according to the view that half damages are compensatory, whereas we rule that half damages are a fine. Under our ruling the borrower can say, "Had I admitted, I would have been exempt," and the Lechem Mishneh leaves the matter unresolved. In my view there is no difficulty. When the Gemara says that he can argue, "Had I admitted I would have been exempt," it must be speaking of a case where witnesses eventually testified that the animal gored, for otherwise there would be no actual payment. If witnesses existed, what would an earlier admission accomplish, since the witnesses would later come? The Gemara must follow the view that one who admits a fine and afterward witnesses come remains exempt. Yet there is another view, cited later in siman 75, that this exemption applies only where the admission obligates him in at least some amount, whereas if by admitting he would obligate himself in nothing, later witnesses do impose the fine. Since half damages for an innocuous animal are a fine, an admission would obligate him in nothing. According to that view, later witnesses would create liability and his hypothetical admission would not help. The Gemara must therefore follow the opposing view, that even an admission creating no monetary liability exempts him from the fine despite later witnesses. According to our practical ruling that when the admission obligates him in nothing later witnesses do create liability, the borrower cannot say, "Had I admitted I would have been exempt," because witnesses exist. The Rambam and Maggid Mishneh are therefore understandable. Conversely, the Mechaber may hold that even an admission creating no monetary liability exempts from the fine despite later witnesses. If so, a borrower who did not know that the ox was forewarned is exempt in every case, and the owners pay full damages. If he knew that it was already forewarned, that is the case covered by the Mechaber's general statement here that custodians enter in place of the owners. This appears to me for now, though the matter still requires study. Perhaps Hashem will merit me to clarify it further elsewhere.
