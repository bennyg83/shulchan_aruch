Someone then took that second book from him, claiming with certainty that whoever left it must have taken the missing one. Later the owner of the found prayer book appears and says that he never took any other book. The found prayer book must plainly be returned to its owner, even after a long time. If that owner lives in another city and has rarely been present since the loss, there is no basis to presume despair. With sacred books, despair is especially difficult to presume, all the more so in a synagogue. Even if the finder lacked a distinctive identifying mark in the found book, he can recover it from the person to whom he previously gave it. The fact that he remained silent for a long time does not establish a gift or abandonment. He gave it only because several people told him that the circumstances strongly indicated an exchange. If he had known from the beginning that there was no exchange, he plainly would not have surrendered the book. Once new information removes that assumption, he may withdraw from what he did beyond the letter of the law. Indeed, where he does not know that he may do so, beit din should inform him, under the principle of opening one's mouth for one who does not know how to state his claim. Ketzot HaChoshen challenges the Taz, who writes in a related case that after many days one may presume the owner despaired and the item need not be returned. Ketzot argues that when an object came into one's possession before despair in a manner already subject to the duty of return, later despair does not acquire it. There is room, however, to defend the Taz. The rule of "it came into his hand in prohibition" means that the duty of returning the lost object had already attached when he received it. In the case discussed by the Taz, the householder may never initially have had any mitzvah of returning a lost object at all. The item was brought into his house by a non Jewish worker in the course of work, and his only claim was against the worker for his own item or its value. If the worker later takes the substitute item, there may have been no initial duty upon the householder to litigate ownership of that item on behalf of an unknown third person. If it is known with certainty that the substitute item belongs to the complaining owner, the analysis changes. Once ownership is clearly known, returning it to the non Jewish worker would be improper, and the ordinary duty to return it to the Jewish owner can attach. In such a case later despair should not ordinarily help. One could try to explain the Taz as addressing a situation where for many days everyone believed the item belonged to the householder and only later discovered the mistake. Yet it is difficult to say that a mistaken belief eliminates the objective duty of return where the property in truth belonged to another from the beginning. A finder who does not know whose object he found does not thereby escape the duty to preserve it merely because he was mistaken about the owner. A more plausible defense is that, at the moment the non Jewish worker left the item, the Jewish owner of the item may himself have preferred that it be returned first to the worker, because she remained responsible for returning what she had taken. Thus the householder's domain was not initially holding it under a duty of lost property return to that owner. If the worker forgot where she placed it and paid the owner its value, the householder would not necessarily have had any duty to hand the object directly to the original owner. On this reasoning, until the owner later demands that the worker no longer be trusted, the item is legally closer to property still held through the worker. Another possible explanation concerns the legal character of the place where the item rests. One reason despair after receipt does not help may be that the finder, being obligated to return the object, holds his domain in a limited sense for the owner, much as a custodian's domain can be regarded as available to the depositor for certain legal purposes. If so, an object delivered through a non Jew may differ because a non Jew lacks the ordinary law of agency and acquisition through a courtyard. The precise effect of this distinction requires further study. There is also room to connect the Taz's ruling to the fact that the non Jewish worker may already have compensated the original owner. One explanation of why despair after receipt is ineffective is that even if the owner despairs of the object itself, he does not necessarily despair of the monetary value owed by the finder or custodian if the object is lost through negligence. Where the worker has already paid the owner and no monetary liability remains connected to the object, later despair over the physical item may be more effective. These explanations require further study.
