Seif 2. (He shall not use them.) If a person knows that his fellow took his tallit and tefillin as security and uses them every day, and later that fellow's tallit and tefillin come into his possession, he may not simply use them in return. The law concerning exchanged articles at a craftsman's shop or bathhouse in siman 136 does not permit retaliatory use. Nor may he assume with certainty that the other owner waives objection. Even if people sometimes tolerate use of another's mitzvah objects because they are pleased that a mitzvah be performed with their property, the first taker may have relied on some claimed justification, such as an old debt, an intention to obtain later consent, or another rationale. One may not therefore answer suspected wrongdoing with another unauthorized taking. Shoes are somewhat different because people often show less concern about temporary use, particularly in a bathhouse, where mistaken exchanges are common and the shoes may already be dirty. Many people temporarily wear the shoes left in place of their own and return them quickly, especially before Shabbat when even a short delay in dressing can matter. Yet there is no firm assumption that the owner of the remaining shoes necessarily took his shoes, even where only two pairs were present. Where the owners can later be identified, each should exchange and recover his own shoes, even if the pairs are identical in quality and condition. Otherwise each person is using property not his own without a clear arrangement for return, unless they subsequently agree that each acquires the pair in his possession. If the owner of the remaining shoes cannot be identified, one should ordinarily not take them home, because once he wears them away there may be no practical way to return them. The person who mistakenly took his shoes may not realize the mistake and therefore may never report it. Similar looking shoes make this concern greater. In a case of substantial distress, however, there may be room for leniency through multiple doubts. Perhaps the person who took his shoes will recognize the mistake and report it to the bathhouse, permitting an exchange. Perhaps he willingly transfers ownership of his own shoes to the person whose shoes he took. If the pairs are of equal value and people ordinarily do not object in such circumstances, the combined doubts may be significant. There may also be an analogy to reciprocal acquisition arising from mutual benefit. When property is standing for sale, a person can sometimes acquire it in the owner's absence by depositing the full value through another who acquires the money for the owner. Here, the mistaken taker already possesses the other person's shoes, and there is no greater benefit to him than being saved from the prohibition of retaining another's property through a reciprocal transfer. If the legal conditions for beneficial acquisition and acquisition by one's hand or domain are met, each pair might become exchanged for the other. This reasoning cannot justify taking shoes of materially different value without intent to return them. Still, observers may not always be obligated to intervene where it is doubtful whether the person will listen and several additional doubts exist, such as whether the original taker knowingly authorized an exchange, whether he intends to return, or whether the bathhouse attendants know the identity of the owner and can arrange the exchange.
