One whose items were exchanged, etc. See Sema and Shach, who cite the Tur that if the owner of a garment comes and claims, I never instructed the craftsman to sell it, then if he has witnesses that the garment was his or can identify it by a distinguishing mark, it must be returned to him. The Shach writes that this is so even where there are no witnesses who saw the garment in the craftsman's hands, despite the rule that a craftsman ordinarily has a migo that he returned the object. This requires explanation. If the craftsman is before us and admits that the garment belongs to this claimant, why is any distinguishing mark needed? If we fear collusion between them, then even a distinguishing mark should not help, because the craftsman became familiar with the item while it was in his possession and could tell the claimant its distinguishing features. If the craftsman is not present, how does a distinguishing mark help? We should plead for the present possessor, It never belonged to you; perhaps it always belonged to the craftsman, as above siman 134 seif 3. We must therefore say that such a scenario is uncommon, and as below siman 297, where property may be recovered from heirs through a distinguishing mark because we do not plead an uncommon event for heirs, the same applies here. One who comes through a craftsman does not receive the benefit of an improbable plea. Although below that rule generally requires that the deceased was not a person of means, while if he was wealthy we do plead that he owned the item unless the deposit was made before witnesses, perhaps a craftsman's status is worse. Property ordinarily found in a craftsman's possession is presumed to belong to others, effectively paralleling a deposit made before witnesses. Terumat HaDeshen similarly rules regarding a laundress that property can be recovered through a distinguishing mark alone even though the laundress denied the claimant's ownership. The reason is that a non Jewish possessor's denial is not treated as a reliable plea, and we ourselves do not invent an uncommon defense. I saw in responsa MaharaSh, siman 109, a discussion of Maharshal's view that if there are no witnesses who saw the item and the craftsman could have claimed that he bought it, we should make the same plea for the present possessor. But according to the Rashba's view above in siman 72 seif 18, cited by the Rama, the bailee himself stands in place of the requirement that the item be seen, and the same is true here: the current possessor stands in that evidentiary position, so the craftsman has no migo. See what I wrote above in siman 134 seif 4. I am surprised that they did not address this point. Otherwise, what force would a distinguishing mark have against the plea that the item was never the claimant's?
