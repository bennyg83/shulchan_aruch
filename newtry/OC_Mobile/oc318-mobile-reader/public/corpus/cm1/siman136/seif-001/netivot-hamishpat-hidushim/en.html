He may use it. The reason is that when the artisan says merely, "Here is a garment," we assume that the owner of this garment asked the artisan to sell it, the artisan mistakenly exchanged it with the claimant's garment, sold the claimant's garment, and gave the money to the owner whose garment he now hands over. Since we presume that seller is using the money, the Sages permitted the claimant likewise to use this garment. But if the artisan says, "Here is your garment," we see that he himself made an error and no such presumption applies. The same is true where the exchange was made by the artisan's wife or children, because they presumably did not know the details of his business. Likewise, if garments were exchanged at a wedding feast or house of mourning, people do not ordinarily tell the host to sell their garment. If the true owner then comes and says, "I never instructed him to sell it," he is believed because he asserts certainty. This is so even if the item had not been seen in the artisan's possession, so that the artisan himself would have been believed to say, "I purchased it," through a migo that he could have claimed return. Shach. If the true owner admits that he instructed the artisan to sell his garment but says the garment now in the other person's hands is worth more than his own, the recipient has the choice either to pay the difference or to return the garment and receive the value of his own garment that was sold. Sema. See Beurim, where this is the primary law. If he learns that his own garment was lost, he must immediately return the other garment. Shach.
