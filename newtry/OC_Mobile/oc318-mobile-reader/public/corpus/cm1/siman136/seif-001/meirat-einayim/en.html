He may use it, etc. The Tur explains the reason. The craftsman gave him a cloak and said simply, "Here is a cloak," rather than, "Here is your cloak." As the Mechaber concludes, this indicates that the craftsman himself knew he was intentionally giving him another person's cloak in place of his own. He presumably did this only because the owner of that other cloak had instructed the craftsman to sell it. The craftsman intended to sell that cloak, but mistakenly sold or delivered the present cloak instead. The craftsman already received the buyer's money and gave it to the owner of the cloak. Since that owner is already using the money received from the craftsman, the Sages permitted the recipient to use this cloak as well. We are not concerned that the buyer will later return and say, "I paid for a different garment," or discover a defect and rescind the sale, leaving the recipient to have used a cloak that was not his. Since the seller is already using the money, he does not object to the recipient's corresponding use of his cloak. This explains why use was permitted only when the craftsman said simply, "Here is a cloak," and not, "Here is your cloak." It also explains why the rule does not apply when the craftsman's wife or children delivered it, even if they said simply, "Here is a cloak," because they presumably did not know about the sale arrangement and used imprecise wording without intending anything. This also explains the difference between a cloak exchanged in a craftsman's shop and one exchanged at a banquet or house of mourning. In the latter settings there is no ordinary assumption that people authorize the host or mourner to sell their cloaks.
And even the craftsman himself, etc. The Mechaber abbreviates and does not state all the distinctions that appear in the Tur. The Tur writes: If the substituted cloak is worth more than his own, when its owner comes for it the recipient must either pay him the excess or return the cloak, in which case the owner gives the recipient the value of the recipient's cloak that had been sold. The choice lies with the person to whom the cloak was delivered, because the owner had instructed the craftsman to sell it and had not insisted on keeping that specific cloak. If the owner later comes and says, "I never instructed the craftsman to sell my cloak and I received no money from him," he is believed to take his cloak from the recipient if he has witnesses that the cloak was his, or if he can identify it by a distinguishing sign. Although we presumed while the matter was uncertain that he had instructed the craftsman to sell it, once the owner arrives and makes a definite claim to the contrary, he is believed. The meaning is that he takes his own cloak from the recipient, and the recipient must then pursue the craftsman. Even if the recipient's own cloak was lost in the craftsman's possession, the owner still takes his property. This is also the point of Rama's gloss in the following seif, which applies here as well. In the case of objects exchanged at a house of mourning or banquet, however, whenever the owner arrives the object must be returned to him because there is no ordinary presumption that he authorized the mourner or host to sell it. Therefore Rama states that case without qualification. Here, by contrast, the owner takes it back only when he specifically claims that he never authorized its sale.
The correct reading is: specifically if he said simply, "Here is a cloak," but if he said, "Here is your cloak," etc.
