And it was found that they are hired for more. See Sema, seif katan 11. His words imply that if the employer expressly misled workers by saying he was hiring them for four because most workers receive four, and it is later found that most workers receive more, he must pay according to the majority rate rather than the lowest minority rate. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in his novellae at the end of Bava Metzia, disagrees. He explains that the case of workers who mislead an employer is not symmetrical with an employer who misleads workers. Where workers tell the employer that most workers receive ten and he agrees to ten, but it is later found that most receive nine and a minority eight, the employer demonstrated willingness to pay the majority rate, so he pays nine. But where the employer tells workers that most receive five, he expressed willingness to pay only five. Even if his statement about the majority proves false, this is essentially like a hiring without a valid wage stipulation, and the workers can be compelled to accept the lowest established rate. This distinction is straightforward and correct. See there further.
