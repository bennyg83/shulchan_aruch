Workers for three. Meaning, for three dinars.
And he loses one from his own pocket. This is Rambam's wording and the substance of Rif and Rosh, whose words I cited in the Derishah. There I proved, however, that Rif and Rosh also hold that if all workers are hired for four, or for three and a half, or if these workers performed labor worth four, the agent may recover from the employer the amount the employer would have had to pay for workers or the value of the labor. This is so even though the employer told the agent to hire for only three and thereby revealed that he did not wish to pay more. Rif and Rosh prove their law from one who hires a worker to work on his own property and then directs him to another person's property. The hirer must pay the worker and may recover from the property owner the amount of benefit conferred. Everyone agrees there that if the work was worth four or all workers were hired for four, the hirer recovers four from the owner of the field. Rambam himself later writes that where the agent tells the workers, "Your wage is upon the employer," the employer must pay according to the prevailing local wage, even though this employer instructed the agent not to hire for more than three. This accords with the view Rama cites here as "some say." Rambam's statement that the agent loses one from his own pocket therefore refers to a case where workers are ordinarily hired for three, which is the case of the baraita from which the law is derived. For that reason the baraita simply says that the hirer recovers what he benefited the owner and does not add that he may recover the amount he stipulated with the workers. Rama's presentation of the second view as though it disputes Rambam follows the Beit Yosef's explanation that according to Rif, Rosh, and Rambam the agent loses the extra dinar even where all workers are hired for four. In the Derishah I explained why that reading is not compelling, and Darkei Moshe likewise says that the Beit Yosef's words are not conclusive. See there. The Beit Yosef also struggled to explain the Tur's citation of Ramah that the agent can recover only up to four, but not more than four even if the labor was worth more, because he may not conduct business with another person's property. In the Ir Shushan another explanation was offered, followed by the statement that it appears more correct than the Beit Yosef. In my opinion neither explanation is satisfactory. Ramah's words are clear according to my approach. The employer told the agent to hire a worker for three. The agent hired him for four and told him, "Your wage is upon me." The agent therefore must pay the worker four, and he may recover from the employer the value of the benefit, namely the prevailing wage or the value of the work. Ramah teaches that in any event he may not recover more than four, even if this particular worker's labor was worth more than four. The agent cannot say: had the labor been worth less than four, you would have paid me only what you benefited and I would have had to absorb the difference; now that the labor is worth more, I should profit and receive the full value of your benefit. The ruling teaches otherwise. Since the agent himself paid the worker only four, he may not make a profit by trading with the employer's property. Ramah uses the expression "conduct business with another's cow" from the Mishnah in chapter HaMafkid, where one rents a cow and then lends it to another and it dies by accident in the borrower's possession. Rabbi Yose says that although the renter is exempt from accidents, once the borrower pays him for the cow, the renter must turn that money over to his own owner. How can he conduct business with another's cow? This is recorded by the Tur and Mechaber in siman 307, seif 5. I discussed this at length in the Derishah to clarify the matter, unlike the explanations of the two great luminaries mentioned above. Darkei Moshe's own words follow the approach I have written.
