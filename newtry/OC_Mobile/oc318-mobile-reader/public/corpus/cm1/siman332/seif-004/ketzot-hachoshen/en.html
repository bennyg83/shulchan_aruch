They receive only three. The Gemara asks about an employer who authorized four, the agent offered three, and the workers said, "As the employer said." Did they rely on the agent's representation or on the employer's actual terms? The question remains unresolved and is ruled leniently for the employer. Although the worker possesses his own labor, once he voluntarily performed the work under the unresolved arrangement, money remains with the employer. This is analogous to the teacher who already taught during an intercalated month. If the worker himself knows that the employer actually authorized four and refuses to continue unless he receives four, he need not complete the work for three, because he remains in possession of his own labor. A parallel applies to rental. If an agent was authorized to rent Shimon's house for three gold coins but offered two, and Shimon said, "As the tenant said," then after Reuven occupies the house the unresolved amount is governed by the presumption of land in Shimon's possession. Reuven must therefore pay three, analogous to the case of twelve gold coins per year versus one gold coin per month.
Do work with me for four, as your colleagues did. Beit Yosef cites the students of Rashba that if the employer said, "Come work for me for five, as your colleagues did," and the workers answered, "As they did," and it was later discovered that the colleagues received ten, the workers receive ten. Their intent was to rely upon the comparison to the colleagues and not to accept less than those colleagues received. This parallels the case where the employer told an agent to hire at three, the agent offered four, and the workers said, "As the employer said." Their words are understood as preserving the higher amount actually authorized by the employer. So too here, once the employer himself added, "as your colleagues did," and the workers accepted on that basis, the comparison governs. By contrast, if the employer authorized four, the agent offered three, and the workers merely said, "As the employer said," without hearing from the agent that the employer had quoted a higher number, the Gemara remains uncertain whether they intended to rely on the employer's actual words or merely trusted the agent's representation. But if the agent himself says, "I hire you for three, as the employer said," and the workers answer, "As the employer said," then they clearly rely on what the employer actually said and cannot be reduced below it. The Shach's proposed textual correction is therefore unnecessary.
He told him that he would give him an object as his wages. Rabbeinu Tam was uncertain whether an employer who promised a particular object as wages can later substitute its value after the work is completed. He ultimately inferred from Avodah Zarah that the worker does not acquire the specific object merely through the labor. The object itself still requires the ordinary act of acquisition, such as meshichah. Nevertheless, the employer certainly owes the value of the stipulated object. Employment obligations are created by verbal agreement, even for a large amount. The worker did not acquire the object itself, but he did acquire a monetary wage measured by that object. If the employer wishes to give the actual object, the worker cannot ordinarily insist on cash instead. The employment was fixed in that object, and because the labor cannot be returned, the worker cannot undo the agreement as an ordinary buyer could after money but before meshichah. If the object was destroyed before delivery, the employer cannot say, "Your object was lost," because the worker never acquired it. The very reason the object remained unacquired was the absence of meshichah. Therefore the risk remained with the employer, and he still owes the monetary value of the wage. There is also a question whether an employer who retracts from delivering the object is subject to the curse of "Who exacted payment." If the final part of the labor had itself the value of a perutah, that labor can function like present money, so the situation more closely resembles a monetary purchase without meshichah. But where the entire wage is already merely a loan owed for past labor, it has the law of a loan, and according to Rama there is no "Who exacted payment" on a purchase made only with a loan. The responsum of Panim Meirot raised a difficulty from a different Talmudic discussion concerning a condition to return an object. That comparison is not decisive. A condition can require the actual object because the act is made dependent on fulfillment. In employment, once the labor has already been done, failure to deliver the object cannot retroactively erase the labor. The worker therefore has a monetary claim even though he did not acquire the object itself.
He may afterward give him its monetary value. Sema distinguishes a debt instrument from an ordinary object, citing Terumat HaDeshen. The Shach understands that if the promised wage was a share in a debt instrument, the employer can instead pay the present market value of that share. In my view Terumat HaDeshen says otherwise. The case involved one who labored to collect a debt and was promised a fraction of whatever would ultimately be collected. At first Terumat HaDeshen considered saying that because the worker never acquired the debt instrument through writing and delivery, he should receive only the value of that fraction of the instrument when he began his work. He ultimately rejects that conclusion. The reason is that the employer did not promise a fraction of the instrument as it then stood. He obligated himself to pay a fraction of the amount actually collected. If the worker received only the low market value of the instrument before collection, the agreement would not be fulfilled. Since a person can obligate himself even regarding something not yet in existence, the employer must pay the stipulated fraction of the actual proceeds. Accordingly, one does not appraise the instrument at its earlier discounted value. The worker receives the agreed percentage of what was ultimately collected. This is explicit in Terumat HaDeshen and is contrary to the Shach's reading.
Since he did not perform meshichah on the object. The same principle applies if the promised wage was something not yet in existence, such as future dates of a palm tree. Although the worker does not acquire the future fruits themselves through the employment agreement, the employer becomes monetarily obligated to provide the agreed wage. Therefore, once the fruits later grow, the employer must either give those fruits or their value at that later time. One does not appraise the immature future fruits at their low speculative value when the work was first undertaken. This follows from the law of an ordinary object promised as wages. The worker does not acquire the object itself, yet the employer owes its value. So too with something not yet in existence, the personal wage obligation is effective even though a proprietary acquisition of the future object is not. This also explains an employer's promise to provide maintenance. Even according to Rambam, who generally questions obligations of an unascertained amount, wages are acquired by the employment agreement and the employer can become obligated in such future support. The arrangement between Yaakov and Lavan concerning the future spotted and speckled offspring can likewise be understood through employment law. Yaakov's wage became binding by their verbal employment agreement even though the particular offspring did not yet exist. There are authorities who instead explain that Lavan transferred the mothers for their future offspring, or that before the giving of the Torah future property could be acquired. The employment mechanism provides an independent explanation.
