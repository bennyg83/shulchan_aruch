He must pay these workers like their companions. See Shach, seif katan 15, who asks: since the employer agreed only to four, and the workers agreed only to receive what their companions received, perhaps the parties never reached an agreement at all and the workers should receive only the lowest ordinary wage. Rashba's reasoning is that the workers said, "like our companions." In the analogous Talmudic question where workers say, "as the employer said," the Gemara leaves unresolved whether they mean to accept whatever the employer actually authorized or merely that they trust the agent's report. If it were certain that they intended the higher authorization, then here the employer would unquestionably owe what the companions received, even though he also mentioned a lower number. The workers themselves never consented to less than the companions' rate, and the employer accepted their labor on that basis. The reason an unresolved case ordinarily gives only three is that the workers performed the labor themselves and thus their possession of their own labor functions like permitted possession under Rashba's rule concerning a teacher. Here, however, the employer himself misled them concerning the market value of the work. In ordinary transactions, deception about value can constitute ona'ah, which is included within the Torah's prohibition of theft. A contractor can certainly be subject to ona'ah in the proper measure. Where the employer himself created the value-deception and the workers performed only because of it, their labor was effectively taken through unauthorized deceptive conduct. The workers are therefore in the stronger position and can demand the rate received by their companions. This differs from an agent who misquoted the employer. The employer himself did not deceive them. The workers relied on the agent, and perhaps they would have worked for the lower amount even had they known the employer's actual instruction. Here people ordinarily do not agree to work for less than their peers, and the employer's false representation is what induced them. That is Rashba's reason.
Like the lowest of the workers. See Shach, seif katan 16, who questions Rama. It is difficult to say that when a seller sells an item worth four for five and falsely says that five is its value or that everyone sells at five, the buyer can recover only according to ordinary market-value rules and that the lie itself has no further significance. Most ona'ah cases presumably involve representations of this kind. Likewise, siman 227 concerning a person who says, "I planted it with the proper amount of seed," treats a false statement about value-related facts within ona'ah law. In my view there are two different cases. The Yerushalmi's case says, "Come work with me as your companions did." The workers ask, "How much?" and he answers four. The basic hiring was already framed by the companions' terms. The later false statement about the number does not erase that first benchmark. At minimum the workers can say they relied on the ability to clarify later what the companions actually received. Similarly, Rama's wording, "for four, like other workers are hired," can be read as two inconsistent price formulations in the very act of hiring. Since the workers were deceived about the market rate, and ona'ah is a form of wrongful taking, we construe the ambiguity in their favor, much like the bathhouse case where possession was obtained under uncertainty. The same applies in an ordinary sale. If the seller says, "I sell to you for four, according to the market rate," both expressions define the price. If the market is lower, the buyer can rely on the market expression. If the seller says, "I sell according to the market rate," and only afterward, when asked what that rate is, falsely says four, the sale was already concluded on the true market rate. But if at the time of agreement he says only, "four," and afterward merely tries to persuade the buyer by saying that this is also the market price or that others charge the same, then the contract price is four. The later false statement is governed only by ordinary ona'ah law, including the threshold of one sixth and the special rules applicable to workers. Thus Rama and Shach can both be correct. Rama speaks where the representation about other workers' wages was part of the price formulation itself. Shach speaks where the price was fixed independently at three or four and only afterward, as persuasion, the employer falsely said that other workers receive the same. In that case the laborer has only ordinary ona'ah law, and a hired worker is not generally subject to ona'ah in the same manner as a sale.
To give him an object as his wage. See Ketzot HaChoshen and what I wrote above in siman 203. In my view such an undertaking has the law of an explicit apoteiki. Although the employer may discharge the obligation with money while the designated object exists, if that object is destroyed he need not provide other money beyond the legal structure of the undertaking. Shach writes that if the promised wage is a debt instrument, the employer can give the worker the value of that instrument. Ketzot questions this. If the promise was, "For your work I will give you whatever I eventually collect on this debt instrument," then certainly one cannot pay only the present discounted value of the note before collection. No one would understand the promise that way. Shach must mean a case where the worker's own task relating to the note has already been completed but the note itself still requires later collection, such as where he was hired to arrange its validation. At completion of his labor, the employer can satisfy the wage by giving the note's then-current market value. The general rule is that when wages are promised in the form of an object, the relevant amount is the object's value at the time the labor is completed, because that is when the wage becomes payable. For the same reason, if the promised object is a davar shelo ba la'olam, I do not agree with Ketzot that the worker should receive the value the fruits will have only when fully grown. He receives their market value as of the completion of the labor, meaning what the future fruits could then be sold for.
