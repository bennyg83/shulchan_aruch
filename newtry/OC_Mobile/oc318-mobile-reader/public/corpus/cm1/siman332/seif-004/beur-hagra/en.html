An employer who said, etc.; an employer who misled, etc. The Yerushalmi there asks regarding workers who misled one another: what is the law? "Come and work with me. How do you work?" Meaning, he asked the workers at what rate workers usually hire. They answered five, etc., but it was found that workers usually hire for ten, etc. Regarding an employer who misled them, what is the law? He asked how they work with their fellow workers, and they answered ten, but it was found that they work for five. The text is reversed, as Nimukei Yosef writes. In the first case the workers told the employer that most workers hire for ten, but it was found that they hire for five; in the latter case the reverse occurred. It is possible, however, to preserve the Yerushalmi's reading by explaining that he asked how many workers are hired for that sum per day, and they answered five, etc. Likewise in the latter clause, "how do you work for ten days, etc.," meaning that he hired them to perform a defined task, such as weaving an entire garment or harvesting an entire field, with a known daily wage, and asked how many days they would need to complete it. This is the meaning there, "for the work, do you work with me? How, etc.?" See there. Rashba and Nimukei Yosef write that our Gemara does not accept this Yerushalmi rule, as is evident from its need to establish the case as workers misleading one another. The reason is that where the agreed wage was based on the usual majority rate, the hiring was mistaken and is void. But where workers misled one another, either they genuinely resolved to accept the ordinary wage, in which case they receive it, or the hiring is void and it is as though they worked without a fixed wage, in which case a person's intent follows the lower market rate, as Rif and Rosh write, because the agent deviated, etc. This is the meaning of "an employer who misled, etc." The first clause, "an employer who said to workers, etc.," corresponds to the latter clause of the Yerushalmi, concerning how many days the task should take. There it does not say "most workers," but merely what the other workers were hired for. Rashba's proof that our Gemara rejects the Yerushalmi is likewise as above.
And he gives them, etc. Rabbeinu Yerucham, as Nimukei Yosef writes. Since they misled one another, the hiring was mistaken and it is as though they worked without a fixed wage. The Gemara's rationale is that some hire for, etc. But in the Yerushalmi's case he must pay according to the majority market rate, because the Gemara's rationale that a person's intent follows the lower rate does not apply. Rabbeinu Yerucham and the Rama write a case where they stated the ordinary rate without saying "the majority." See Shach.
