They receive only three, etc. This is an unresolved question in the Gemara, and the authorities rule leniently for the party in possession. The Semag states that since the question was not resolved, possession controls. Accordingly, if the workers seized four, it is not removed from them, according to the Rambam, Semag, and those who generally hold that seizure is effective in an unresolved question. This may apply even where their work is not worth four, because the Rif, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef understand the Gemara’s question to include a case where the employer said four and the workers answered, ‘As the employer said,’ although their work was not worth four. It may even apply where all workers ordinarily accept three, since the employer himself offered four and they relied upon his words.
The employer must give these workers the same as their companions, etc. This ruling requires study. Although they said, ‘As our companions were hired,’ and it was later discovered that the companions received more, the employer expressly offered these workers four. He therefore never agreed to pay more than four. Even if he intended to mislead them and that intention failed, he can still say, ‘I did not agree to pay what the others received; I stated only four.’ It would seem that the parties are then treated as though no definite wage was fixed, and the workers receive the lowest prevailing wage. The text cited by the Beit Yosef from the students of the Rashba appears corrupt. It should read: the employer said, ‘Come work for me for ten, as your companions came,’ and it was found that he had given the companions five; the workers receive ten, because their reliance was upon the higher amount. This correction is supported by the reason given, that their intent was to obtain the better rate. The same concept appears in the Gemara where the employer instructed the agent to offer three, the agent offered four, and the workers said, ‘As the employer said.’ Their intent was to rely upon the employer’s higher offer. By contrast, where the employer or agent expressly offered less, there is no basis to award more. Moreover, where the employer told the agent to offer four, the agent offered three, and the workers said, ‘As the employer said,’ they still receive only three, because the actual hiring was at three. The Sema’s distinction, that here the employer falsely represented that other workers had been hired for four, is not persuasive. In the other case the agent also misled the workers by representing that the employer had said three. Nevertheless, because they accepted three they receive only three. The Yerushalmi likewise rules that if an employer said that most workers are hired for five and the workers accepted on that basis, but it was later discovered that most are hired for ten, they receive only five. Even if one follows the Rama that a mistaken hiring is treated as though no wage was fixed, the result would be the lowest prevailing wage, not the higher wage. In my view they receive only the amount expressly agreed upon.
An employer who misled workers, etc. The Rama writes that it is as though no hiring occurred. In my view, the Rashba did not reject the Yerushalmi’s rule that when the employer misleads the workers, they receive the amount actually agreed upon. He rejected only the Yerushalmi’s application where the workers misled the employer, because the Bavli did not explain the case that way and because workers who misled the employer cannot extract more than the lower wage upon which he relied. Nimukei Yosef indicates this expressly. Where the employer misled the workers, however, they heard and accepted the lower wage and then performed the work. The employer may say that had they not accepted his offer he would not have hired them. Even where workers misled the employer, if he already paid them and only later discovered that the market wage was lower, it appears that he cannot recover the payment. The Bavli’s unresolved question, where the employer said four, the agent said three, and the workers answered, ‘As the employer said,’ further shows that such a mistake does not automatically void the agreement. Once they heard and accepted three, they may have intended to waive the fourth dinar even if it later became known that the employer had authorized four. The same reasoning applies here: after accepting the lower wage and working, they intended to receive only that amount even if the market later proved higher. It also appears that Rabbeinu Yerucham’s text in the name of the Rashba contains a scribal error. Nimukei Yosef attributes the rule of an unpriced hiring only to a case where workers misled one another. In any event, the Raavad was uncertain whether the Yerushalmi should be followed in all its details, and there is no sound basis to reject the Yerushalmi in the present case, especially since the Bavli is consistent with it.
He gives them the lowest prevailing workers’ wage. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that he gives them five. His intent appears to be that where the parties initially stipulated payment according to what most workers receive, the majority rate of five controls rather than the absolute lowest rate. But if they merely agreed to pay ‘as workers are hired,’ and the hiring was mistaken, then the lowest prevailing rate applies. The Rama did not need to explain that an express stipulation to follow the majority is governed by the majority, for that is self-evident. The Sema’s criticism of the Ateret Zekenim is not compelling. The latter agrees that an employer may expressly condition upon the minority rate, or later claim that this was his intent. He merely explains that the Rashba’s ordinary case concerns a stipulation according to the majority wage, as stated by Nimukei Yosef and the Yerushalmi.
