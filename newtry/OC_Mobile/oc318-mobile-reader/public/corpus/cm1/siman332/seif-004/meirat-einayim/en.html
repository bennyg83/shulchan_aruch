Like their fellow workers. When they returned and said, "as our fellows did," they did not mean that they believed the agent's statement that the others received only four and knowingly accepted that amount. Rather, they intended to improve their position: if the others received more, they too would receive more. Since the others did in fact receive more, they are entitled to the higher amount, even though some workers are hired for less and their particular labor may not itself be worth that much. This differs from the preceding case where the employer told the agent, "Hire for four," the agent offered the workers three, and they said, "as the employer said." There they receive only three, even if their work is worth more, because the agent did not tie their hiring to the wage of other workers; he simply offered three. Here, by contrast, he misled them by saying that he hired the others for four. This is straightforward.
And it was found that workers are hired for more. Meaning, all workers are hired for more, though the higher amounts differ: some receive five, some six, some seven, some eight, etc. In that case too, the stated agreement for four is void because everyone earns more, and he gives them the lowest actual market wage, namely five. But if even a minority of workers are hired for four, he need give only four, because people ordinarily contract at the cheaper available rate. The same principle applies when Rama concludes, "or the workers misled the employer in such a case." According to his unqualified wording, the workers simply told the employer, "Give us ten, for other workers also receive ten," and it is then discovered that all workers receive less, although the lower wages vary, some nine, some eight, seven, six, and so forth. He gives them the lowest wage because the mistaken wage agreement is void and the ordinary presumption is that a person intends the lowest market rate. Rabbeinu Yeruham in Rashba's name, however, writes the latter case differently: if the workers misled the employer and told him that most workers receive ten, and it is discovered that most receive five, the hiring was mistaken and they receive only five. The reason appears to be that workers ordinarily would not foolishly demand ten while knowing that most workers receive less. Rather, the realistic case is that they told the employer that most workers are hired for ten. Workers can plausibly say, "Pay us what the majority receives," because the minority willing to work for less is not readily available. The employer agreed on that premise. When it is later discovered that the majority actually receives five, the mistaken agreement is void and they receive five, the majority rate, even though a minority works for less. Since he agreed to pay the majority rate in order not to trouble himself to seek the cheaper minority, we no longer follow the minority. In the first case, by contrast, where the employer misled the workers, Rashba does not mention a majority, because it is ordinary for an employer to say, "Take four; I have some workers available for four," referring even to a minority. So it appears to me, unlike the Ir Shushan, who inserts "majority" into the first case as well. Examine carefully.
