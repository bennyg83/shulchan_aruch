One who forcibly takes another person's slave. The Gemara asks whether a slave is like land or movables. Even if slaves are treated as movables for theft, taking another's slave and preventing him from working can still create liability for lost labor because damage to a person includes the category of lost work. This explains Rif. Although he treats slaves as movables for theft, he distinguishes whether the slave was taken during the master's work time. When the master was actually deprived of labor, the wrongdoer is liable for that lost labor as direct human damage. The rule that appreciation of stolen movables belongs to the thief does not eliminate the master's separate damage claim for daily lost work. When the master was not using the slave, there may be no lost-labor damage. The wrongdoer's own benefit from using the slave can then be treated within the ordinary benefit-without-loss framework, and the slave's status as movable property can matter. Thus Rif's ruling is coherent even while treating slaves as movables: during work time, liability arises from direct lost labor; outside work time, that damage category is absent.
