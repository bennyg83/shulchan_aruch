One who forcibly takes another person’s slave, etc. It is evident from this seif and the following seif that movable property which was stolen, if the robber uses it for labor without diminishing its value, is exempt even if it is ordinarily rented out. Once it has been stolen, it stands in the robber’s domain, and when he uses it he is using what is legally in his own possession. Examine this carefully. The same is implied below in siman 371, seif 2. And if he prevented him from other work, etc. See Sema, seif katan 11. He writes, citing the Perishah, that even according to Rif, who disagrees concerning stolen slaves and holds that they have the law of movable property, where the slave was taken not for the purpose of robbery but merely to perform work, Rif agrees that the taker must pay for the loss caused by preventing the slave from performing other work. This requires study, because the Gemara does not appear to imply this. Rather, it seems that once Rif retracted and ruled that slaves have the law of movable property, he also retracted from the ruling cited afterward concerning one who forcibly employs another’s slave. Shiltei HaGibborim states this explicitly at the end of Perek HaGozel. Maadanei Yom Tov and Lechem Mishneh also discuss the issue at length. There is no need to elaborate, since for practical halacha all the other authorities disagree with Rif and hold that slaves have the law of land. Ramban as well, in Milchamot, writes in Perek HaGozel that Rif’s earlier position appears more correct, and that is the primary ruling.
