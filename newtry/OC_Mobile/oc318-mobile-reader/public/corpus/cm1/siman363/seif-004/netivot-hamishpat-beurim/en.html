See Sema, seif katan 11, who writes that here the taker did not intend theft but only to use the slave, and therefore even Rif, who treats slaves like movables for some purposes, can agree that lost labor must be paid. Shach leaves the point requiring study. Perishah cites Tosafot in Bava Metzia. Maharsha explains Tosafot as speaking where the user entered intending to pay rent. In such a case liability is straightforward: just as one who uses another's ship intending to pay owes rent, so too one who uses the slave. In my view Tosafot's point is different. With movables, one who uses another's item without permission and without intending rent can be treated as an unauthorized borrower or robber. With a slave, however, the owner may actually prefer that the slave remain occupied rather than idle, so that he not become undisciplined. Therefore, when someone takes the slave away from the master and does not let him work for the master, but instead has him perform work for the taker, the master's concern that the slave remain occupied means the actual use by the taker is not necessarily treated as theft of the slave's body. The wrong consists in preventing the slave from working for the master. That is why the Gemara's real reason is precisely that the master prefers his slave not to remain idle. The user is not a thief of the slave's body and does not thereby acquire the slave's appreciation as stolen property. His liability is for the interference with the master's labor right. This distinction matters, for example, where the slave's own body improves during the period. If the user were a robber in the full movable-property sense, he might claim the improvement. But because the use itself was tolerated insofar as it kept the slave occupied, that consequence does not follow.
