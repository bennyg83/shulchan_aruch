The Tur cites the Ramah that although the robber took the property as a robber, if it is ordinarily rented, one who rents it from the robber must pay the rent to the owner. The robber acquired only sufficiently to bear liability for accidents, not complete title, so when he takes rent from the lessee he is taking the owner's money. The Beit Yosef objects that all robbers pay according to the time of robbery, and asks why renting it to another should differ from using it himself. Indeed, rental to another should be more effective because rental for a day resembles a sale and involves change of possession. My father-in-law cites the Mordechai that property not acquired by the holder remains in the owner's domain even after passing through several hands. That source, however, concerns return of the principal object, not the increment or rent generated by it. Rent received by the robber is like other increase from stolen property and would ordinarily fall under the enactment encouraging repentance. The Ramah's reasoning appears to be that when the robber actually receives rent, he commits a new act of robbery at that moment, because the money truly belongs to the owner. Nevertheless, if the lessee already paid the robber, he need not pay the owner a second time. This is included in the market enactment, for there is no difference between purchasing from a robber and renting from him. This differs from the ruling in seif 10 that rent paid to Reuven must be paid again to Shimon, because that case concerns land, which cannot be stolen at all. Siman 373 similarly allows the original owner to seize produce still attached to the land, but not produce already consumed.
