Where he told him, "Leave, etc." In such a case, under all circumstances, even if the courtyard is not ordinarily rented and the occupant is a person who ordinarily would not rent, he must pay the full rent because the owner expressly revealed that he did not consent to his presence. We do not apply the rule that one may be compelled not to act with the character of Sodom where he expressly objects, as explained nearby.
He need not pay rent. Although here the reason stated above in seif 4 regarding a slave does not apply, namely that a person prefers his slave not to learn idleness, another reason applies. The Gemara says that an inhabited house remains in good condition, while an empty one deteriorates. For this reason too, the owner is ordinarily pleased that someone live there. See the Perishah.
Even though he removed the owner, etc. Meaning, the person who entered the house displaced the owner who had been living there and himself lived there in his place. Nevertheless, this is only indirect causation of the owner's loss. Mordechai and Darkei Moshe write this.
For he caused him a monetary loss. This is not classified as indirect causation, because this occupant actually lived there and consumed the value of the property. Although he did not personally benefit in the relevant sense, he nevertheless consumed the owner's value. This differs from one who prevented the owner from using the house but did not himself live there. He neither consumed nor benefited from the use, and that is only indirect damage, for which he is exempt, as Rama writes immediately afterward: "However, if he did not live there, etc."
