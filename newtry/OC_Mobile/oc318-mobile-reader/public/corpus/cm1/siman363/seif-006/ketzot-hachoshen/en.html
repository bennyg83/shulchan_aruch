For he diminished his money. Tur cites Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia that even one who merely locks another's rentable house and does not live there can be liable because he prevented its rental. Rosh disagrees, treating it as indirect damage. Bach tried to distinguish this from confining a person in a room, where lost labor is compensable, by saying the latter damage begins immediately. The better distinction is categorical. The Torah imposes special liability for lost labor in human injury. It does not impose an equivalent "lost use" category for injury to property. Therefore, locking another person's shop or animal so that it cannot produce income is generally only indirect prevention of profit unless there is actual physical damage. By contrast, confining a person is direct human injury and the Torah's category of lost labor applies even without bodily injury. This also explains why a slave can generate liability for the master's lost labor. A slave is a person with respect to the categories of human injury. One who confines him and prevents the master's work can therefore be liable under lost labor even if the wrongdoer did not himself use the slave. Accordingly, Tur's phrase concerning one who forcibly takes a slave and performs work should not be read as limiting liability only to cases where he actually used the slave. Even if he merely prevented the master's labor, the lost-labor category can apply. The mention of work is useful because where the wrongdoer also derived benefit, his liability can encompass the full benefit even where the master's precise lost labor was smaller.
