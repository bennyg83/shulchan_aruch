Nimukei Yosef in HaGozel writes that this ruling follows only the opinion that does not impose liability for indirect damage, but according to the opinion that does impose such liability, which we follow, he is liable because he placed the materials where the owner could no longer retrieve them. The other authorities do not agree. It appears that since at the time of dyeing the finished item was worth the value of the wool and dyes, he caused no damage at that time. Its later decline in value occurred by itself, so there is no indirect damage, and he may say, 'Here is your property before you.' Baal HaMaor expressly supports this, for he writes that the Gemara's alternative explanation involving dyeing a monkey and the like does not apply according to our ruling, implying that the explanation involving the dye's later depreciation does apply even according to our ruling. Ramban likewise writes this in Milchamot. Perhaps Nimukei Yosef understood the dye's depreciation to have occurred at the time of dyeing. Consider this carefully. Ibid.
