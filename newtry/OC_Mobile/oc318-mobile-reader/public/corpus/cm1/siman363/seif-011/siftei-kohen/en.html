One who had, etc. Torat Chaim asks why the Gemara did not establish the case as one who robbed wool and dyed it with his own dye, where there is a question whether the dye creates an identifiable enhancement upon the wool. If there is such enhancement, the owner must reimburse the robber for the dye when recovering the wool, because a robber acquires the added value; if not, the owner could say that nothing of the robber remains with him. He answers that the Gemara wished to establish the case according to all opinions, including the view that a reversible change, such as dyeing that can be removed with soap, nevertheless effects acquisition. According to our ruling above in siman 360 that such dyeing is not a change, the question would remain one of unresolved law. I do not agree. Even if the dyeing is not an acquiring change, the owner must reimburse the robber under the law of one who improves another's property without authorization, either for the enhancement or for the expense. The issue whether the dye has a legally identifiable substance upon the wool does not alter that obligation. See Tosafot and the Rosh there, and the end of siman 372.
And the wool declined in value. This means that it declined afterward. If it had already declined at the time of dyeing, the dyer is liable, as explained below.
He is exempt. The Nimukei Yosef writes in the chapter HaGozel that this applies only according to the view that does not impose liability for indirect damage, but according to the view that does impose such liability, which is the accepted halacha, he is liable, because he placed the materials where they could no longer be recovered. This is not the opinion of the other authorities. Rather, since at the time he dyed the wool its value equaled the value of the wool and the dyes, and only afterward did the dye fall in value, he caused no damage at the time of dyeing. There is therefore no indirect damage here, for the later decline occurred by itself, and he may say, 'Your property is before you.' This is also explicit in Baal HaMaor, who writes that the explanation that he used the dye to color refuse does not apply according to us, since we judge indirect damage, whereas the explanation that the dye later declined in value does apply even according to us. Ramban likewise writes in Milchamot that the Rif's question concerns dye that became cheaper after it had been used, and not dye used on refuse. Perhaps the Nimukei Yosef understood that the dye was already cheap when he applied it. See Sema, seif katan 27, and what I wrote above in siman 362, seif 6.
If the robbed party seized, etc. The Tur concludes: If one stole dissolved dyes and dyed his own wool with them, he must pay the value of the dyes even if they produced no enhancement. The Sema cites this in seif katan 27. It is derived from the Gemara in Bava Kamma 101a: 'Let him say, Give me my dyes that you ruined.' The Beit Yosef and Bach did not identify the source from which the Tur derived this ruling.
