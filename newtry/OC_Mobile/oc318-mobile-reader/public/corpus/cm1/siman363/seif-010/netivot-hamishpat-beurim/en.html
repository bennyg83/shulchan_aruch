Even if he rented it from Reuven for less than its value. At first glance this is difficult from siman 341, where heirs who slaughter an animal believing it belonged to their father pay only the discounted value of cheap meat. Here too the renter can say: I believed I rented lawfully; had I known I needed to pay the full market rental, I would not have taken such an expensive dwelling. Ketzot HaChoshen raises this question. In my view land-rental differs because Mechaber also says that the renter may recover from Reuven what he had to pay the true owner. Indeed, even if the true owner removes the renter at the beginning of the rental, before the renter has paid the owner anything, the fraudulent landlord can owe the renter the full market value of the lost lease where rental prices increased. Rental for a day is treated like a sale for that day, and ordinary warranty against eviction includes appreciation. Even authorities who dispute this in special mortgage structures agree in ordinary rental. Thus the renter entered the transaction relying on the fraudulent landlord's warranty. A buyer or renter who entered under a seller's warranty differs from heirs or gift recipients, who have no one from whom to recover. This is analogous to the distinctions concerning a creditor who seizes improved property from a purchaser: the purchaser may recover certain amounts from his seller because he entered the transaction on that basis, while heirs stand differently. Therefore the renter must pay the true owner the full value of the benefit received, because he does not ultimately suffer that loss; he can recover it from the person who rented him the property. If the robber merely lent the house free of charge, the borrower need pay the true owner only the value of the benefit he actually received. If he is a person who ordinarily does not rent housing and therefore received no measurable monetary benefit under the ordinary rule of "one benefits while the other suffers no loss," he can be exempt. All this is where the occupant did not know the property was stolen. If he knew, he must pay the true owner the full market rental value.
If he had no right to rent it out. This implies that with movable property, which generally may not be rented onward without permission, the rental income belongs to the owner. I was uncertain about a tenant who gives another person permission to live in the house free of charge together with him. Must that additional occupant pay the landlord? One might say no. The landlord could not have rented the same space to someone else once the house was already rented, so this resembles a courtyard not ordinarily rented out, where the occupant can be exempt. In my view the additional occupant must pay. Living in a house causes some wear. The exemption for a courtyard not ordinarily rented rests in part on the principle that an inhabited house is better maintained than an empty one. But here the house is already inhabited by the tenant, so the landlord receives the preservation benefit regardless. Adding another occupant creates only additional wear. That is why a tenant may be forbidden to sublet to a larger household. The added number of residents can cause real deterioration. Therefore this is a case where one person benefits and the other suffers at least a small loss. The added occupant must pay the value of his benefit.
