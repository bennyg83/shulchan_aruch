Some say he must pay Shimon rent. Shiltei Gibborim cites Avi HaEzri that if the true owner is absent and has no agent or anyone attempting to rent the property, an occupant can be exempt because the property is treated as not standing for rent. He distinguishes the case where one rents a house from Reuven and it is later found to belong to Shimon by saying Reuven's act of renting can count as activity on Shimon's behalf. But Hagahot Maimoniyot, the source of the rule, says expressly that payment to Shimon is required only where Shimon or his agent was present and trying to rent the property. If neither Shimon nor any representative was available, Reuven's unauthorized rental alone does not turn the house into income-producing property for Shimon. Therefore Shiltei Gibborim's distinction is difficult.

If he had permission to sublet it. Nimukei Yosef says that if the tenant was allowed to sublet, the subrent belongs to him because he had already acquired the rental right. This differs from Rabbi Yosei's cow, where the renter supposedly has no right to profit from another's property. The cases are indeed different. In Rabbi Yosei's case the second borrower pays for the body of the cow, which still belongs to the original owner. The first renter cannot make commercial profit from the owner's cow. A rented house, however, gives the tenant a present rental right. If the agreement permits subletting, the tenant is selling or renting his own acquired use right. The later rent is therefore his own commercial gain, not profit made from the landlord's retained property.
One who says to another, "Live in my courtyard." Rashbatz treats the occupant's use as potentially compensable even without an express rental agreement. Rashba, cited by Ran, says more broadly that one who provides another with support without stating that it is a gift is presumed to act by way of loan, not gift. Ran elsewhere appears to say the opposite, that a transfer given without specification is presumed a gift. The cases can be distinguished. Where an existing debt is present, an unexplained payment is naturally credited to the debt rather than treated as a gift. Where no debt exists, a bare transfer can more plausibly be a gift. The Jerusalem Talmud cited by Rashba involved a person who had lent money and then allowed the borrower to stay in his house. When the lender later demanded the loan, the borrower claimed a setoff for rent. Since a debt relationship already existed, the housing arrangement can readily be understood as non-gift. Thus there is room to say that in a truly debt-free relationship, unspecified benefit can be presumed a gift, whereas in a relationship containing a debt or other commercial context, it may be presumed compensable. The later authorities leave the issue unresolved in some circumstances.
