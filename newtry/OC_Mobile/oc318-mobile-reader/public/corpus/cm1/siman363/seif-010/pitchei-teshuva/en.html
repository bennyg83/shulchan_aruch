Dwell in my courtyard. See Baer HaGolah, letter 40, who cites Bach's discussion of Terumat HaDeshen and concludes that in a dispute among the authorities the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. See Shach and Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 70 seif katan 28. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 119, discusses a dispute between a father-in-law and his son-in-law after the daughter died and the son-in-law remarried. The father-in-law demanded rent for approximately ten years during which the couple lived in his house beyond the years he had expressly granted them free residence. After reviewing their extensive claims, Chatam Sofer concludes that since Rama ruled that one who says, "Dwell in my courtyard," does not thereby create a duty to pay rent, no later authority can readily extract money contrary to Rama's decision. Even if the issue is reduced to the dispute between Tashbetz and Terumat HaDeshen, Bach and Shach already ruled that the burden of proof is upon the claimant, and Shach appears to favor Tashbetz. Chatam Sofer adds that the facts themselves strongly indicate a gift. The father-in-law recorded rent in his ledger only from the time the son-in-law moved into his second dwelling and made no entry for the first dwelling. Had he believed rent was owed for the earlier residence, one would expect him to record that less obvious claim rather than leave it unmentioned while recording the later, clearer obligation. Moreover, the father-in-law later declined financially while the son-in-law became prosperous; the daughter died; and the son-in-law remarried. Ordinary practice would be to demand outstanding debts at such moments, especially at remarriage and the arrangement of a new dowry. Yet the father-in-law made no demand and no memorandum, which indicates that he never viewed the prior residence as a debt and raised the claim only later because of family quarrels. The father-in-law asserted that he had repeatedly demanded rent from his daughter during her lifetime and that she deferred him from day to day. If witnesses established such demands, the debt might become clear from that point onward. The son-in-law responded that perhaps his wife had paid, since she conducted household finances. At first glance that might be a claim of uncertainty whether a known debt was paid, which ordinarily obligates payment. But because there are no witnesses to the original demand, the son-in-law can say that he does not know whether he ever became liable at all: perhaps he lived there from the outset by gift and waiver, and no demand was ever made. Even if a demand was made, perhaps his wife paid it. This creates a double doubt stronger than the ordinary case of uncertainty whether an obligation arose, and he is exempt. Chatam Sofer therefore finds no basis to extract money from the son-in-law. Nevertheless, because his position rests on the combined doubts or on the unresolved dispute between Tashbetz and Terumat HaDeshen, Chatam Sofer recommends compromise as good and proper. See there.
