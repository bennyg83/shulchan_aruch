Such as where it was a new house. Rabbi Yehudah says that one who lives in another's courtyard without permission may have to pay because he blackens the walls. Tosafot explains that once even a small loss exists, the beneficiary can be charged for the entire benefit. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia, however, limits payment to the actual loss. A difficulty is that blackening the walls is not ordinary direct damage. If repainting can restore them, the extra cost resembles indirect damage, like throwing a coin into water where recovery requires expense. Therefore, absent benefit, the resident might be exempt from the blackening itself. The resident's benefit changes the analysis. Once his use produced some loss to the owner, he can be charged for the benefit he received. This explains why lack of benefit in the upper-story case matters even though some blackening occurred. Without benefit, the blackening alone may not create ordinary direct tort liability.
And he caused him loss by blackening the walls. Maggid Mishneh says the resident pays the entire benefit once he caused even slight loss. Although the builder had not yet been reimbursed for his construction costs, the landowner's courtyard acquires the building for him retroactively because it stands on his land. The builder then has a monetary claim for value, but the house itself belongs to the landowner. Therefore, when another resident uses the house and slightly damages it, the rule of full benefit against slight loss applies. A difficulty arises from the case of one who built a mansion on orphan property. There the Gemara's discussion might have expected slight wall blackening to count as loss. The better answer is that the orphans were minors. Courtyard acquisition works through agency, and minors lack ordinary agency. Their courtyard therefore may not acquire the building retroactively in the same way. This is why the analysis differs.
