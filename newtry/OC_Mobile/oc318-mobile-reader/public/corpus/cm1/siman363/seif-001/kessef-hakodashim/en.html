And he performed work with it. One person left a tavern and another entered. A large wooden fixture used for selling liquor remained from the first tenant, and the second used it for several days. Since the second is not a person who would ordinarily rent such a fixture, especially where he had his own similar equipment, and he used the former owner's only because it remained in place, and the first owner likewise is not a person who ordinarily rents out such equipment, there is strong reason to exempt the second from rent. This is especially so because the fixture was worn and moving it might have broken it. The case is even stronger than the rule in siman 363 exempting one who benefits from another's property in circumstances of no loss. If the fixture broke because of his use, however, he is liable, since it was not lent to him voluntarily and he is an unauthorized borrower, which has the status of a robber and becomes liable even for accidents from the time of taking. If it broke by itself because of age, perhaps he is exempt. Even if moving it might have prevented decay, perhaps he was not obligated to undertake that effort, since he never accepted guardianship. Yet because an unauthorized borrower is treated as a robber, once he cannot say, 'Here is your property before you,' he may be liable. Much depends on whether the use itself was entirely a case of one benefiting while the owner suffered no loss. A related question arises where a tenant entered premises relying on what one valid witness told him concerning the amount of rent. If the landlord is in possession of the money, perhaps the landlord must take a Torah oath, because the dispute concerns last year's rent and thus money rather than the land itself. If the tenant holds the money, perhaps his claim based on a valid witness is treated as definite and the supporting witness may exempt him from an oath. But if there is a partial admission, an assisting witness does not exempt one who is obligated to swear, and one who cannot swear may have to pay. There may also be a migo if the tenant could have claimed that he never entered on the assumption of paying rent at all. On the other hand, once he entered with intent to rent, he may be liable for the full ordinary rent, and his claim that someone misinformed him as to the amount may not help, especially because he should have realized that one witness is not necessarily reliable enough to fix the rent. This requires careful study.
