[Seif 1] Or it deteriorated permanently. This means that it weakened on its own, like the other cases listed here. The Sema explains likewise. Rav Pappa says in Bava Kamma 96b that the Mishnah's “became old” includes even permanent weakening. Rashi explains that although the change does not affect the whole body, permanent weakening resembles old age because it does not recover. This language necessarily concerns deterioration occurring by itself, just as old age does. The novelty is that even a partial but irreversible physical change effects acquisition. Tosafot and the Nimukei Yosef likewise explain that permanent weakening is a change comparable to a lamb becoming a ram. Any legally significant change may occur naturally or through human action. The Mishnah's examples, old age, a cracked coin, spoiled fruit, all establish liability according to the original value even though the change arose naturally, because the visible and irreversible change transfers the object to the robber. If the deterioration is reversible and occurred naturally, the robber returns the object as it stands. If he caused even reversible deterioration directly, the authorities dispute whether he must pay. The Rosh and Rabbeinu Chananel impose liability because direct bodily damage is compensable even when the animal can recover; other French authorities treat it as temporary loss of use, for which there is no separate payment regarding an animal. The stronger view is Rabbeinu Chananel's, and the Rosh appears to accept it. The Rama's preference for exemption is unsupported by the law of a borrower whose animal weakens naturally, because direct damage differs from natural deterioration. A borrower is liable even for permanent deterioration occurring naturally, because he is liable for accidents. Permanent weakening is like partial death, “what difference whether all of it died or half of it died?” Reversible natural weakening is not considered lasting damage, and the borrower is exempt if he returns the animal alive. If it dies before recovering, however, payment includes the weakening, even though recovery had been possible. The same distinction applies to a thief and robber. When the object is no longer available for return, even reversible depreciation becomes part of the payment; when it is returned and the depreciation is not permanent, he may say, “Here is your property before you.” The rule “principal according to what he stole” means that whenever payment is legally required, its measure is the value at the time of theft. In price fluctuation or reversible weakening, payment generally arises only when the object itself is unavailable. In visible permanent weakening, the object is treated as changed and payment arises even while it remains present. This is analogous to the distinction between a cracked coin, where the damage is visible and the robber pays the original value, and a coin merely invalidated by decree, where the damage is not physically evident and he may return it as is. Partially spoiled produce is different because ordinary partial spoilage does not negate the identity of the produce. The Gemara's discussion of damage that is not visible likewise supports this distinction. Visible damage may create liability even when it occurred naturally, while nonvisible damage generally depends on direct causation. Tosafot's question whether Chizkiyah, who treats nonvisible damage as damage, might distinguish between direct and natural causation can be understood by comparing reversible bodily weakening, where one favorable factor remains and direct action is therefore decisive. Rav Pappa, however, understood Chizkiyah's statement broadly, as equating nonvisible damage with visible damage, including the consequences of change. This resolves the sugya. Accordingly, the Shulchan Aruch's “deteriorated permanently” means deterioration occurring naturally. If permanent, the robber acquired it through change and must pay the depreciation according to the original value. If reversible and natural, he returns it as is. If caused directly, he is liable even when recovery is expected, in accordance with Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rosh. The Tur's concluding phrase “he pays as a worker” must also be understood as payment according to the reduced wage of an idle worker, since the loss is only indirect causation, not full wages.
