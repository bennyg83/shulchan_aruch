Slaves that aged. See Shach, seif katan 3, who says ye'ush does not help for a slave just as it does not help for land. In my view the reason ye'ush does not normally help for land is the principle stated by Ramban in the chapter Eilu Metziot: ye'ush operates only on something that has left the owner's legal possession. If an object remains legally in the owner's domain, despair does not transfer it. Movable stolen property leaves the victim's legal control immediately. Neither the victim nor thief can ordinarily consecrate it before ye'ush: the thief because it is not his, and the victim because it is not in his possession. Therefore ye'ush can later operate. Land is different. Land is not stolen in the same legal sense, and the owner can consecrate it even while another person occupies it. It remains legally in his possession. Because ye'ush does not operate on property still in one's possession, despair ordinarily does not transfer land. But if the owner cannot recover the land in beit din, for example because he has no witnesses, it can be treated as outside his legal control. Bava Metzia 7 says that property one cannot recover judicially cannot be effectively consecrated. In such a case ye'ush can operate even regarding land. This resolves Shach's difficulty in siman 236, where the presence of governmental courts can be evidence that the owner despaired because he believed he could not recover the land even there. Once the land is judicially unrecoverable, it is no longer legally in his possession for this purpose. It also reconciles the Yerushalmi passages implying ye'ush can apply to land. A slave differs from land even more strongly. A slave can become genuinely lost, such as by fleeing or being captured. The Torah's verse concerning returning every lost item can include a slave. If a slave escapes and the owner despairs, there is no reason ye'ush should not operate. The impossibility of a land-aveidah rests on the fact that land remains in place; a slave can leave the owner's reach. Therefore even in robbery, where a slave is practically lost from the owner, ye'ush can operate. The rule that land is not stolen does not automatically extend to slaves for this point. The Gemara's statement that a runaway slave can be sold does not prove otherwise. That case can concern a slave who has not completely escaped but merely refuses to accept a writ of freedom. Even after ye'ush, the owner's remaining prohibition-related authority can still allow him to issue a writ of emancipation, just as one who declares a slave ownerless can later issue the document needed to complete freedom.
And they aged. Gittin 12 says that one who wounds another person's slave pays the slave's loss of work and medical expenses to the master. This suggests that the usual injury payments applicable to a free person can, in their appropriate form, apply to a slave for the master's benefit. Bava Kamma teaches that pure unemployment loss can exist where a person is locked in a room and prevented from working. If so, why does one who steals another's slave and merely detains him appear able to say, "Here is yours before you," without paying the master's lost labor? The answer is that the master's right to the slave's shevet is not a direct claim for interference with the master's earning capacity. Merely causing another person's ox or donkey not to work does not create such a payment. Rather, the primary injury obligation is owed to the slave himself, because the slave is a person subject to mitzvot and his bodily injury is compensated like that of a free person. The master then acquires what the slave acquires, under the rule that whatever the slave acquires belongs to his master. Therefore the master receives shevet only where, had the slave owned his own labor, the wrongdoer would have owed shevet directly to the slave. In the ordinary case here, the thief neither locks the slave in a room nor forces him to work. The slave remains free to labor. No shevet is owed to the slave, and therefore none passes to the master. The master's own loss resembles lost work of an ox or donkey and is not separately recoverable. But if the wrongdoer locks the slave up, or compels him to perform the wrongdoer's work, he is liable for shevet, namely the value of the labor the slave was prevented from performing for himself or his master. If the slave is a skilled pearl-driller and is forced to chop wood, compensation is measured by the lost skilled labor.
Here is yours before you. See Shach, seif katan 7, who argues that it is unreasonable for a thief or robber to be treated more leniently than a custodian. His words are difficult. Tosafot in Bava Kamma explicitly write that a custodian is liable for temporary deterioration that later reverses and for partial rotting where a robber can be exempt, because a robber is not a custodian. The custodian's liability can exist even without a formal legal change in the object. Moreover, according to the view that change does not acquire, a robber can sometimes say "here is yours before you" even after a change, while a custodian is certainly liable for damage. Thus custodial liability does not depend solely on change. Still, Shach's practical conclusion can be defended for a different reason. Damage that occurred in the custodian's premises through negligence is often no stronger than damage caused unintentionally by an ordinary person. For damage that is not externally recognizable, Gittin says an inadvertent damager can be exempt while an intentional damager is liable. A negligent custodian can therefore be exempt in that class of invisible damage. By contrast, where the object suffers a real recognizable deterioration, such as permanent emaciation or partial rotting, an ordinary damager is liable even without intent. A custodian who negligently allowed it is likewise liable. I was uncertain about an artisan given grain to make beer who negligently allowed Pesach to pass so that the product became prohibited. Does he lose his wage even if the invisible-damage rule exempts him from paying principal damages? It appears that he does lose his wage. According to the view that an artisan acquires the improvement in the object, he plainly loses the improvement that was his own. Even according to the view that an artisan does not acquire the improvement, the case resembles what I wrote above in siman 305 concerning agency wages lost when the entrusted property is stolen, even where the worker was exempt under shemirah b'baalim. The artisan's wage is not fully earned until he returns the completed work. If through his own negligence the result cannot be returned in the contracted condition, he can lose the wage even where technical damage liability is absent.
