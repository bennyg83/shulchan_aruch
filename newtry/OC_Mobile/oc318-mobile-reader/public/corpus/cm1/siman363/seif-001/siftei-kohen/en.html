Or if he robbed slaves, etc. A slave is legally comparable to land. The Rif reportedly revised his ruling late in life and held that if slaves aged, the robber cannot say, 'Here is yours before you,' because Rav Nachman ruled that creditors do not collect from slaves. The authorities rejected that inference. Tosafot and the Rosh explain that slaves are treated as land for biblical laws but as movable property for rabbinic matters, yet this distinction is strained, especially because many authorities hold that liens are biblical while still ruling like Rav Nachman. The correct explanation is not that the legal classification changes, but that a creditor does not rely on slaves because they can flee. Thus, for laws explicitly written in the Torah, slaves remain like land, while for creditor reliance, prozbul, and acquisition of movables by means of them, practical reliance is absent. Rashbam's own language supports this: creditors rely on immovable land that cannot disappear, but not on slaves. Baal HaMaor, Nimukei Yosef, Ramban, and other authorities likewise indicate that Rav Nachman's rule rests on lack of reliance, not on a general reclassification of slaves as movable property.
And they aged. In my opinion, 'aged' is not exclusive. Even if the slaves burned and no longer exist, the robber is exempt according to our ruling that slaves are like land. The Raavad distinguishes, saying that deterioration may be returned as is, but if the slave dies or burns the robber pays because slaves can be acquired through despair. That distinction is not convincing. If a slave is analogized to land, the analogy is complete: he remains in the original owner's domain and is not legally stolen. The passages in Bava Kamma and Shevuot support this. The case of a captive slave who becomes free after despair is exceptional, either because Scripture specifically grants effect to despair in wartime captivity or because conquest operates even against land, as shown by the rule that Ammon and Moab were purified through Sihon. The Rashba explains at length that ordinary despair does not acquire a slave, just as it does not acquire land. Accordingly, even if the slave burns, the robber is exempt. Raavan states similarly, and this is the principal ruling.

In this country, etc. Tosafot, the Rosh, Tur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Shiltei Giborim, and Maharshal rule that even if the government invalidated the coin, the robber may say, 'Here is yours before you.' Maharshal adds that this is so even if every kingdom has invalidated it and it no longer circulates anywhere. This is the alternative opinion recorded by the Rama.
And some of them rotted, etc. If all of them rotted, he pays their value at the time of the robbery. Hagahot Ashri explains that the distinction concerns produce rotted together with infestation; if it rotted through worms, it is treated as sound produce. Otherwise there would be no reason to distinguish between all and part. According to this, the Sema's explanation is not precise.
Here is yours before you. Maharshal rules that with objects forbidden for benefit one may say this only in the case of a robber, not a custodian. In my opinion this is incorrect. His proof that the first clause of the Mishna is unanimous while the latter follows Rabbi Yaakov poses no difficulty, for the entire Mishna may follow Rabbi Yaakov while the first clause is also accepted by the Sages, a common Talmudic structure. Tosafot also understand the passage that way. The Gemara does not ask who authored the Mishna, because its authorship was already clear; it seeks only a Tanna who recognizes return in the case of prohibited benefit and concludes that all agree. Maharshal's distinction between a robber and a custodian or tortfeasor is both conceptually difficult and contradicted by the Gemara's derivation from 'he shall return the robbery that he robbed.' The conclusion explicitly includes leaven that remained over Pesach and similar forbidden objects. The same follows from the passage in HaNizakin. His remaining arguments are likewise unpersuasive.
