Cracked. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 93.
The king. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes likewise, as do the Shiltei Giborim and Maharshal, chapter HaGozel, siman 12, who writes that even if all kingdoms in the world invalidated the coin so that it circulates nowhere, the rule is still as stated. Shach.
All of them. This is not literal, for immediately afterward he writes that only if some rotted may the robber say, "Here is yours." Rather, a majority is like all; where exactly half rotted, it is treated like a majority. Sema.
And broke. According to what was explained in siman 362, seif 10, and the dissenting view there. Ibid.
And aged. Although an aged slave may be worth nothing and resembles an animal that has aged, the robber may nevertheless say, "Here is yours," because a slave is treated like land. So writes the Sema. The Shach proves at length from the Gemara and authorities that a slave is treated like land for all matters, with no distinction between rabbinic and Torah law. He therefore writes that even if the slave burned, the robber is exempt as with land. This appears primary.
And was invalidated. This applies while the coin itself remains extant. If it no longer exists, he pays its value at the time of the theft, meaning in the new coinage. So write Tosafot and Rabbeinu Yerucham, and Maharshal likewise. Shach.
Some of them. On the Gemara's distinction that all had rotted, Hagahot Ashri explains this as rot from infestation. If they rotted through worms, they are treated as sound. So too in the Yerushalmi. Meaning, otherwise there would be no reason to distinguish between all and some. According to this, the Sema's explanation cited earlier is not exact. Ibid.
Here before you. The Shach writes that Maharshal's ruling that "Here is yours" in property forbidden for benefit applies only to a robber and not to a custodian does not appear correct. See his objections and rejection of Maharshal's proofs.
