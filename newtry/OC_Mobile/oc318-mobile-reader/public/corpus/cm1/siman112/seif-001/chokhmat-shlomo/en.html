Seif 1, in the gloss: Some say that even if he wrote “property that I will acquire,” he may retract so long as he has not yet acquired it, while others disagree. Note: In my humble opinion there is a proof concerning this dispute from Gittin 50. The Gemara there discusses a debt document presented against heirs, through the end of the sugya. Rashi explains that by Torah law superior land is subject to collection because the debtor subjected the land to the creditor and the land became like a guarantor, and a guarantor becomes obligated by Torah law from the verse “I will guarantee him.” This implies that if a guarantor were not obligated by Torah law, the land likewise would not be subject by Torah law. Accordingly, consider a clause granting a lien on property the debtor will later acquire. If the lien does not take effect until the debtor actually acquires that property, then at the moment the property is acquired it would resemble a guarantor undertaking liability after the loan was already given. Such a guarantor is not obligated without an additional act of acquisition. The after-acquired property would therefore not be subject by Torah law. At most it would be subject rabbinically because of the enactment intended to preserve access to credit. If so, according to Rava, collection from heirs in such a case should be only from inferior land. Why, then, does the Gemara challenge Rava from a baraita saying that even superior land can be collected, and suggest that this means the debtor explicitly wrote superior land into the document? The Gemara could have answered that the case concerns superior land that was after-acquired property. Such land would be subject only rabbinically and therefore collection from heirs should indeed be limited to inferior land. This answer would be simpler than establishing the baraita where all the superior land was swept away. It follows that the lien on after-acquired property must take effect in some legal sense immediately when the undertaking is made, so that the debtor cannot retract before acquisition. It is therefore not comparable to a guarantor who undertakes only after the money has been advanced. If the debtor could retract until he acquires the property, the lien would begin only at acquisition and the difficulty above would remain. This appears a strong proof for the view that he cannot retract. Examine carefully.
