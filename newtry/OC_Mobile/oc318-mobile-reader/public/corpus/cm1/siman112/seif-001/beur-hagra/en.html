And some say, since... Their proof is from the Gemara's challenges from debt documents and improved property, which imply that after-acquired property can become encumbered even where the standard document does not explicitly say so. The passage in Bava Basra 44b that says "perhaps he wrote after-acquired property" reflects ordinary drafting practice: standard documents expressly included responsibility and after-acquired property. Thus where part of the relevant clause is written, the rest can follow by implication.
Some say that even... From Bava Basra 157b, where the halachah is to divide. The rationale is that when the debtor later writes a second lien, he is presumed to retract from the first creditor to the extent necessary to place the creditors on equal footing, analogous in some respects to one who sells future fruit according to Rabbi Meir. See Maggid Mishneh, Mordechai, and Tosafos.
And some disagree. They interpret "they divide" as Rashbam does. Although Rashbam elsewhere says that after-acquired property may become subject to the later creditor, this is according to the view that the later lien takes effect. The validity of later-dated documents also requires an explanation of how after-acquired property is treated. A lien is not identical to a conveyance of future property; indeed Tosafos explains that liens can be stronger than acquisitions in this respect.
