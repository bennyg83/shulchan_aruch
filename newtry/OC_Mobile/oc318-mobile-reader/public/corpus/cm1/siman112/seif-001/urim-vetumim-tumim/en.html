But if he did not write an after-acquired-property clause, it is not encumbered. This follows the Rambam's view that we do not say an omitted after-acquired-property clause is a scribal error. The difficulty raised against him is that, if so, how could he rule without qualification that postdated loan documents are valid? He should have specified that such a document must contain an after-acquired-property clause. Otherwise, perhaps the borrower acquired property between the actual loan date and the written date, and that property was not encumbered to the creditor. Because the document was postdated, however, the creditor might later seize it from a purchaser as though it had been encumbered, contrary to law. This is the Gemara's difficulty in Bava Batra 157. The Shach in seif katan 1 seeks to answer that according to the Rambam, whenever a loan document comes before us we assume the parties stipulated concerning after-acquired property. See there. This is strained in the Rambam, and most authorities do not agree. It is more plausible that if a document appears before us without an after-acquired-property clause, we assume no such stipulation was made, because it is absent from the writing and the document holder has the weaker hand. However, a postdated document is different. Since we rule that witnesses are presumed to have acted properly, if there had been no such stipulation, they would have acted improperly by postdating the document. We may therefore assume that the debtor stipulated concerning after-acquired property. By postdating, the witnesses did not weaken the debtor beyond what he had authorized. They could have written the document from the original date together with the after-acquired-property clause. Their omission of that clause from the later document only weakens the creditor. There is therefore no reason to invalidate the document. This is logically sound. The basic difficulty can also be answered differently. Ordinary responsibility is treated as a scribal error even without express stipulation because the borrower surely intended ordinary responsibility, since a person does not throw away his money for nothing. Likewise, witnesses commonly cannot write a document immediately on the very day testimony is delivered to them, lest sunset arrive before the writing is completed. It is therefore routine to write the document later and date it by the day of writing. If omission of an after-acquired-property clause would invalidate every such document, witnesses and scribes would be forced to complete every instrument on the precise day of the transaction, which is burdensome. Thus when the borrower authorizes the writing of a loan document, his ordinary intention includes encumbering property acquired between the delivery of the instruction and the actual writing, so that the witnesses can write later without invalidating the instrument. As to this short intervening period, the debtor's intention is treated as though he had expressly stipulated, because postdating is common and valid and he accepted that practice. But this does not mean that he intended every property he might ever acquire or inherit to remain encumbered forever in the absence of an express stipulation. The Gemara's difficulty remains sound according to its initial assumption that after-acquired property cannot be encumbered at all. If such property cannot legally be encumbered, the debtor's intention cannot solve the problem. According to our ruling that it can be encumbered, however, there is no difficulty. There is another answer. Ordinarily we do not suspect that the debtor will acquire new property after the loan and then sell it. Such an event is not common enough to justify invalidating all postdated documents. Tosafot in Bava Batra write that acquiring or inheriting property is not an event ordinarily expected. See also below in seifim 2 and 3, where Ramban holds that absent proof we assume property remained in the debtor's possession from earlier days. One point, however, requires attention. If after-acquired property is not encumbered, then even improvement added by a purchaser cannot be collected by the creditor, as stated by the Gemara there. If the debtor borrowed in Nisan, the field then appreciated and improved, and he sold it to Levi in Sivan, the creditor has no claim to the appreciation occurring after Nisan, because it is like property acquired and sold afterward. But if the witnesses postdate the loan document to the beginning of Sivan, the field already appears in its improved and appreciated condition as of the written date, and the creditor will collect that added value unlawfully. This is the force of the Gemara's question: if after-acquired property is not encumbered, how can postdated documents be valid? A field naturally changes in value over time, and its improvement is an ordinary concern. According to our ruling, however, after-acquired property can be encumbered.
Moreover, the Maggid Mishneh writes below in siman 115 that with respect to improvement, no explicit after-acquired-property clause is needed, because it is treated as though the borrower expressly stipulated to encumber the improvement. If so, there is no concern that a postdated document will wrongfully seize improvement. As for the rare possibility that the borrower may acquire or inherit an entirely new field, we do not invalidate documents because of such an uncommon event. This resolves the matter. Furthermore, as I wrote above in siman 104, since a lien is d'oraisa, even without an express after-acquired-property clause the lien against the debtor himself may attach immediately when he acquires a new field, just as it attached to his property at the time of the original loan. Alternatively, at the original loan itself the Torah may have encumbered not only the debtor's present property but also what he would later acquire, because a person's property stands as his guarantor. See above in siman 104 and siman 111, seif katan 10, where I used this approach to explain Ramban. By Torah law even an oral creditor could collect from purchasers. The Sages restricted collection because ordinary loans are made privately and have no public notice, in order to protect purchasers. Once a written loan document exists, however, the loan has publicity. What difference should it make whether the document expressly says "after-acquired property"? The answer is that when a purchaser hears that the document lacks such a clause, he relies upon that omission and assumes the creditor did not encumber later property in that form. Thus the purchaser's protection depends upon the wording. Now consider a loan made in Nisan but written in Iyar. We rule that witnesses generate publicity only from Iyar, the date of writing, and not earlier. If the debtor acquired property before Iyar, between the loan and the writing, and sold it in Sivan, the creditor may properly seize it. By Torah law the lien attached to it when it was acquired. As for publicity, the purchaser heard of the loan only from Iyar onward, exactly as though the loan itself occurred then. At that time the field was already in the debtor's hands. Why, then, did the purchaser buy it? There is no purchaser-protection concern. Thus there is no injustice in a postdated document. This reasoning works if liens are d'oraisa. But according to the opinion that liens are not d'oraisa, the lien upon the later field exists only by force of the written instrument. If the borrower instructed the witnesses in Nisan but the field was not then within the instrument's lien, the witnesses cannot postdate the document to Iyar and thereby create a new lien upon that property without the borrower's authorization. The Gemara's difficulty is therefore clear according to Shmuel, who is precisely the one uncertain whether an after-acquired-property clause is effective, and Shmuel holds that liens are not d'oraisa, as shown there by his ruling that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. This also resolves an additional difficulty. The Gemara seeks to prove from the validity of postdated documents that an after-acquired-property clause is effective, and rejects the proof by saying that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. Yet a baraita in Bava Batra 171 says that Rabbi Yehudah also holds a postdated document valid. Does Rabbi Yehudah hold that a person can transfer something not yet in existence? He is among the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir. According to what we have written, however, Shmuel himself agrees that Rabbi Yehudah holds that a lien is d'oraisa, as appears in Bekhorot 48 and 49. A postdated document is therefore plainly valid according to Rabbi Yehudah. The Gemara sought a proof specifically from the anonymous Mishnah, because Shmuel would not lightly dispute an anonymous Mishnah. It then answered that the anonymous Mishnah may follow Rabbi Meir, as anonymous Mishnayot commonly do, especially in the laws of antedated documents, where even Reish Lakish establishes the Mishnah according to Rabbi Meir. This analysis also answers another difficulty. The Gemara is uncertain in a case of one creditor, then another creditor, followed by acquisition of property, whether the later-acquired property is encumbered to the first or second creditor. Why not resolve the question from the validity of postdated documents? Suppose the debtor borrowed from one creditor in Nisan, from another in Iyar, and acquired a field in Tammuz. If the later-acquired field is encumbered to the later creditor, then if the first creditor's witnesses postdate his Nisan document to Sivan, he will appear to be the later creditor and may seize the field unlawfully, even though the true priority belongs to the Iyar lender. According to the first answer above, there is no difficulty because we do not generally suspect acquisition of an entirely new field; the relevant ordinary concern was only appreciation of a field that is later sold. As to the improvement of that same field, there is no priority problem. If the document predates the purchaser's acquisition, the creditor properly reaches the improvement; if it postdates the purchaser's acquisition, the creditor cannot seize the field at all. According to the second answer, the ordinary case of a valid postdated document concerns one without an express after-acquired-property clause. At the Gemara's initial stage it was forced to assume such a clause, but once we rule that liens are d'oraisa, the Mishnah may be understood literally, with no after-acquired-property clause written. In that case the question of whether later-acquired property belongs to the first or second creditor does not arise through the written clause at all. This gives the Rambam substantial support. This can also explain the Gemara in Bava Batra concerning one who sold a cow or cloak. The Gemara answers that he bought and sold it immediately. Why does that not solve the case of land as well? According to what was written for the Rambam, an ordinary postdated document remains valid as to land because the lien is d'oraisa and, even without an express clause, the Torah lien can attach. Movables encumbered through land, however, are different. Their lien depends entirely upon writing and is not a Torah lien. A postdated document without an after-acquired-property clause would therefore be defective as to them. When the Gemara says that he bought and immediately sold, it does not necessarily mean on the same day. Rather, the witnesses testify that from the time of purchase until the sale they did not leave him and he did not borrow in the interim, as Ramban explains. One might still ask that he could have borrowed earlier and the witnesses postdated that earlier debt into the interval between purchase and sale. Such a possibility matters only if postdated documents can create the lien. At this point the Gemara had not yet raised a concern for an express after-acquired-property clause, and ordinary documents do not contain one. A postdated instrument over such movables would therefore be invalid under the Rambam's framework. Hence there is no concern in the cow or cloak case. But as to land, the postdated instrument could remain valid because of the Torah lien. When the Gemara then asks that perhaps the debtor expressly stipulated an after-acquired-property clause, we earlier asked why the seller could not have canceled the prior lien. According to this analysis the answer is clear: if an express after-acquired-property clause existed, perhaps the document was validly postdated, so a prior cancellation would not solve the concern. Examine this carefully. See also seif katan 3.
Everything that is fit to become encumbered, etc. See Urim, where I cited the Shach's answer to the difficulty from Bava Batra 44. The Gemara asks, "Let us be concerned that perhaps he stipulated concerning after-acquired property," which implies that such a clause is not automatically supplied as a scribal error. The Shach answers that the passage concerns movables encumbered through land, since it follows the case of one who sold a cow or cloak. With respect to that form of lien, omission of an after-acquired-property clause is not treated as a scribal error. This is a good answer. The distinction stated by the Rama, that where responsibility is expressly limited in one respect we do not treat an omitted further responsibility as a scribal error, also resolves this difficulty. In the Gemara's case the debtor expressly specified responsibility over movables through land. He therefore would have needed to specify after-acquired property separately. Nevertheless, the Shach's answer is especially useful in understanding the flow of the Gemara. The Gemara first answers that the seller purchased and immediately sold the property, and then asks that perhaps he had stipulated concerning after-acquired property. Tosafot and Ramban in their novellae ask briefly that, if so, the same answer should permit testimony concerning a field, because the seller would not be preserving it for a creditor. Their explanations are strained. According to what we have written, the first answerer certainly knew that there could be an after-acquired-property concern. He merely held that absent an automatic scribal-error rule, we do not suspect an express clause strongly enough to disqualify testimony. But if omission of after-acquired property is itself a scribal error, then whenever a loan document comes before us we automatically treat later property as encumbered. Thus once we already fear that a creditor may later appear with a written instrument, the after-acquired-property concern follows automatically. Accordingly, the Gemara's first answer is precise. With a field, even if the witness bought and sold it immediately, he cannot testify, because if a creditor appears with a loan document we automatically treat later-acquired land as encumbered, and the immediate resale does not help. With a cow or cloak, however, even if a creditor appears with a document containing a movables-through-land clause, there is no automatic assumption that after-acquired movables were included. The first answerer therefore considered it too remote to suspect that the document would expressly contain both the movables-through-land clause and an after-acquired-property clause. The Gemara then asks why we should not nevertheless fear even that expressly written combination, since any plausible path of personal interest disqualifies testimony. This exchange itself is strong proof for those who hold that omission of an after-acquired-property clause is generally a scribal error. Even according to those who reject that rule, there is another difficulty. Bava Metzia 14 asks how Shmuel's statement that responsibility is a scribal error can be reconciled with Shmuel's statement that a lien must be specifically discussed with the parties before being written. Why not answer that the latter statement concerns an after-acquired-property lien, in the same way that improvement may require express discussion if the creditor might otherwise take all of it? It is strained to answer merely that the unqualified term "lien" means every kind of lien. The Sema is surprised that the Tur attributes to the Rosh the view that an omitted after-acquired-property clause is also a scribal error, because this does not seem consistent with the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. There the Rosh writes that sometimes a creditor takes all of the purchaser's improvement, namely where the creditor received an after-acquired-property clause but the purchaser did not. See siman 115 in the Sema and Derishah, who explain that the Rosh means the seller expressly stipulated to the purchaser that he would not recover through after-acquired property. That explanation is unnecessary. The Rosh writes this only to explain Shmuel's statement that a creditor collects "the improvement" without saying merely half the improvement. The phrase is not absolute, because sometimes the creditor takes all the improvement, where the creditor has an after-acquired-property clause and the purchaser does not. But Shmuel himself holds that even ordinary responsibility in a sale is not automatically supplied as a scribal error. Certainly, then, he would not treat after-acquired property as an omitted scribal clause. An ordinary purchaser according to Shmuel therefore lacks such a clause unless it was actually written. The Rosh's explanation is perfectly consistent. This also explains the Gemara's question in Bava Batra against the opinion that two creditors divide later-acquired property. The baraita says a creditor collects improvement, and the Gemara asks that if the property is divided, the creditor should collect only half the improvement. Why does the Gemara not answer that the case is one in which the purchaser did not receive an after-acquired-property clause? Because the whole purpose of the baraita is to teach from which type of assets improvement is collected, namely that the purchaser who seeks reimbursement for improvement collects only from free property. Why should the baraita be forced into an uncommon case where the seller expressly stipulated that the purchaser would not recover through after-acquired property, when an ordinary case could teach the same principle? If omission of the clause is a scribal error, the ordinary purchaser should recover half the improvement, and the baraita's language is therefore difficult. Shmuel, by contrast, holds that sale responsibility itself is not a scribal error. According to him, the ordinary case is precisely one in which the purchaser has no after-acquired-property clause, so the baraita naturally says the creditor collects the improvement in full. But the Gemara's later discussion proceeds according to the accepted halachah that responsibility in a sale is a scribal error. In the ordinary case the purchaser therefore does have the relevant protection and recovers half the improvement. This is consistent, and it is additional proof that an after-acquired-property clause is likewise supplied as a scribal error.
He may retract. This is the Rashba's view. The Shach strongly inclines to rule against him, both from logic and from the Gemara, arguing: why should one imagine that after the debtor has already encumbered future property, he may retract so long as he has not returned the loan money? The creditor lent him money precisely on that basis. This argument is difficult to understand. First, how can one say that the creditor lent specifically in reliance upon property the borrower had not yet acquired? What prevents the borrower, before acquiring it, from never acquiring the field at all? More fundamentally, according to Rabbi Meir, if one sells the future fruits of a palm tree and receives payment, and that money is then spent, the seller can nevertheless retract before the fruits come into existence. Even if the buyer cries, "Return my purchase money," the money is merely a debt and the sale is void. The same principle should apply here. Moreover, the authorities dispute why, in a case of one loan followed by another loan and then acquisition of property, the creditors divide it. One explanation is that both liens take effect simultaneously; another is that the debtor retracted from the first lien when he created the second. But everyone agrees that the Gemara's possibility that the later creditor alone receives the property can be explained only through retraction. The Ri Migash says this explicitly in his novellae to Bava Batra, as does Rashbam and the other authorities. There is no other plausible reason why the later creditor alone should prevail. The Shach suggests that the first creditor is presumed to waive because he benefits from the second creditor's money, which enables the borrower to buy the property. Apart from the fact that this does not explain inheritance from the borrower's father, where no second creditor's money produced the property, even in the division case one could say only that the first creditor is content for the borrower to acquire land with the second loan because he will at least share a lien in something tangible. But if the later creditor receives everything, what benefit does the first creditor gain by waiving his right? This explanation cannot stand. The only basis is retraction. If the Gemara itself entertained the view that such retraction is effective, how can the Shach call it logically implausible? His complaint is not against the Rashba but against the Gemara. Thus there is no force in his logical argument. The Shach also argues from the validity of postdated documents. Perhaps the borrower borrowed in Nisan and expressly encumbered after-acquired property, then in Iyar generally retracted from all prior after-acquired-property liens, while the witnesses postdated the loan document to Sivan. If the borrower later acquires property in Tammuz, the postdated document would make the lien appear later than the retraction and the creditor might seize from a purchaser unlawfully. But the same difficulty exists against the Gemara's own possibility that later-acquired property is encumbered only to the later creditor because the debtor retracted from the first creditor. If retraction is legally effective, why are postdated documents valid? The difficulty I raised above in seif katan 1, that postdating might transform the first lender into the apparent later lender and give him improper priority, can be answered according to the Rashba's own position that omission of an after-acquired-property clause is a scribal error. That automatic rule applies only to an ordinary document, where we presume the parties intended the best form of responsibility. But the theory that the later creditor alone receives the property because the debtor retracted from the first applies only where the debtor expressly wrote an after-acquired-property clause to the later creditor, thereby showing retraction. Where no clause is written, we do not say that the scribal-error rule itself proves retraction. On the contrary, we presume that the righteous remnant of Israel does not act wrongfully and that the debtor did not retract, and his failure to specify a further responsibility clause may reflect precisely that. Thus the ordinary postdated document creates no concern of retraction. The Shach's particular objection still applies to the Gemara: what if the debtor expressly created the lien and later retracted before the postdated writing? The answer must be that this is too remote a concern. We do not suspect that a person will deliberately make himself one who repeatedly retracts from his obligations, for the remnant of Israel does not act wrongfully. The permissibility of postdating therefore does not disprove the legal power to retract. Since the Gemara's possibility that the later creditor alone receives the property necessarily rests on retraction, and the Rashba and Mordechai explain even the division rule as partial retraction, and below in siman 115 I establish that the Rosh likewise holds this, the Rashba's ruling that the debtor may retract is highly persuasive. I do, however, have a doubt where the debtor writes an after-acquired-property lien "from now," stating that when he later acquires or inherits property it shall be encumbered from now. Can he retract according to the Rashba? The Rashba compares this lien to Rabbi Meir's sale of the future fruits of a palm tree, regarding which Rav Huna says in Bava Metzia that the seller may retract before the fruits come into existence. Yet the Rashba himself, in the third chapter of Kiddushin, and likewise Ramban, conclude that this is only where the seller did not say "from now." If he says "from now," even in the future-fruit case he cannot retract. Similarly, if one says, "You are betrothed to me when you convert," and adds "from now," he cannot later retract. Since a lien is stronger than an ordinary acquisition, perhaps the same is certainly true here. One might answer that although below in siman 258 we rule that a dated document implies "from now," and likewise an ordinary kinyan is treated as effective from now, perhaps for this specific purpose such implications are insufficient and the words "from now" must be express. This would explain why Bava Batra, in the discussion of one who sold a cow or cloak, labors to find a case in which the witness is not preserving property for his creditor, ultimately answering that he bought and sold immediately, and then asks that perhaps he had encumbered after-acquired property. The Gemara is forced into the remote case where witnesses testify he had no land, as Ramban notes. Why not simply answer that before buying and immediately selling, he canceled before witnesses every after-acquired-property lien he had ever created? If he could retract before acquisition, that would remove the concern. According to the foregoing, the answer is that perhaps he expressly created the lien "from now," in which case retraction is impossible. Yet this would produce a significant novelty. In the Gemara's case of one loan, another loan, and then acquisition, if the first creditor received an express "from now" clause, retraction would be impossible and the property should be encumbered solely to the first creditor. The authorities do not state such a distinction. It therefore appears that Rabbi Meir's law is different. According to Rabbi Meir, a true acquisition can take effect upon something not yet in existence, and an express "from now" can therefore prevent retraction. But according to the Sages, a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence. The enforceability of an after-acquired-property lien is only because the Sages strengthened lien law. An express "from now" does not create a present proprietary hold in something that does not yet exist. Until the property comes into existence, the debtor retains the power to retract. It is enough that the Sages made the future lien effective once the property appears; we should not add a present irrevocable acquisition where the underlying object does not yet exist. This also resolves the earlier difficulty concerning postdated documents. If the Gemara says that the later creditor alone receives the property because of retraction, why are postdated documents valid? The Gemara can still answer, as it does elsewhere, that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. Under Rabbi Meir, a clause covering after-acquired property together with "from now," or a dated document whose date proves present effect, can give the first creditor an irrevocable lien, and there is no concern that postdating will reverse priority. The Gemara's question whether the later creditor alone or both creditors share is according to our ruling like the Sages. It could have established the later baraita concerning improvement according to Rabbi Meir as well, but it chose the more direct true explanation according to the Sages. This may also explain why Shmuel says simply that a creditor collects improvement rather than half the improvement, which was the Rosh's difficulty. Shmuel may be stating his rule according to Rabbi Meir, as Rashbam explains in Bava Batra. According to Rabbi Meir, where the first creditor has an express "from now" clause, the later creditor does not share in the subsequently acquired property. As to the earlier question why the Gemara does not simply establish that witnesses testify the debtor canceled all after-acquired-property liens, according to what I wrote above there is an additional answer. With land, an omitted after-acquired-property clause is treated as a scribal error, whereas with movables it is not. If one says the debtor canceled such a lien, the same answer should then allow him to testify concerning land, which the Gemara does not permit. Thus the cancellation answer is unavailable in the structure of the passage. This ruling of the Rashba fits together with his own view that an omitted after-acquired-property clause is a scribal error. See what I wrote above concerning the Rambam. Likewise, if the debtor wrote an after-acquired-property clause and property came to him while he was a goses, and his heirs then sold it after his death, there is room for doubt. Bava Batra 127b says that even Rabbi Meir agrees that one cannot transfer future property that comes to a person while he is a goses. Perhaps the same applies to a lien, because at that moment he is not capable of making an acquisition or creating a lien, so the prior clause cannot attach. On the other hand, a lien may be stronger. If the reason is that a Torah lien attaches automatically when the debtor acquires property, what difference does it make that the property comes while he is a goses? The lien attaches of itself; the document serves only to provide publicity. But if no Torah lien exists in such later property, the question remains. Examine this carefully. And one should examine Shmuel's view that without an after-acquired-property clause a creditor does not collect from heirs. In Bekhorot the Mishnah says a woman does not take her ketubah from property that is merely prospective. Why speak specifically of property that falls to the heirs after death?
Even if it fell to the husband during his lifetime while he was a goses, the after-acquired-property clause could not take effect, and certainly not if it came after death. It is strained to say that the Mishnah follows the opinion that liens are d'oraisa, for then the fallen-house Mishnah could likewise have been established that way instead of according to Rabbi Meir. This is especially difficult according to Ramban, who holds that according to everyone a creditor does not collect from heirs without an after-acquired-property clause. We must therefore say that the Sages strengthened a lien more than an ordinary acquisition, so that even property acquired while the debtor is a goses can become encumbered. Examine this carefully.
