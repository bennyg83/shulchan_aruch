Some say that, since we rule that liability omitted from a document is a scribal error, etc. Although a student does not ordinarily decide between the authorities, I will decide this issue. Liability omitted from a document is treated as a scribal error even where it is known that no express condition of liability was made, as explained above in siman 39, seif 4. This applies to property already owned by the debtor, because a person does not throw away his money. A lender or purchaser is presumed to have relied upon the property visibly held by the borrower or seller at the time. Property acquired later is different. The lender should have stipulated expressly, because there is no reason to presume that he relied upon property not yet present. Where a written document exists, however, we presume that the lender or purchaser also stipulated regarding later-acquired property, since parties to a written transaction ordinarily employ every available protection. In that sense the omitted after-acquired-property clause is a scribal error. This reconciles authorities who say that such a clause is implied with those who say it must be expressly stipulated. They do not disagree. It also answers the Maggid Mishneh's question upon the Rashba, who says that an omitted after-acquired-property lien is treated as written, from the Gemara in Bava Batra 44b, which asks whether perhaps such a clause was expressly given. The Gemara's concern arises precisely because there is a document, in which case an unstated clause is treated as if written. Independently, that passage concerns a cow or garment, which are movable property. Even movable property already owned is not ordinarily encumbered without an express agav clause in the document, and an after-acquired-property lien over future movables certainly cannot be presumed. Gidulei Terumah questions the Maggid Mishneh on another basis, but he did not understand the Maggid Mishneh's point. The question is why the Gemara had to say, 'Perhaps he expressly gave an after-acquired-property clause,' rather than simply treating that clause as implied. This distinction also resolves the Maggid Mishneh's question upon the Rambam. The Rambam rules that property acquired after the loan is not encumbered when it is known that the loan was made without such a condition, but if the condition was made the creditor collects. He does not say that an ordinary written document lacks the implied clause. Thus, where a document comes before us, we presume that the parties stipulated regarding later-acquired property, unless it is clearly known that they did not. This explains why the Rambam validates antedated debt documents generally without requiring him to state separately that they contain an after-acquired-property clause. The Semag necessarily has the same view. The Maggid Mishneh's inference from Baal HaItur is also not compelled. Baal HaItur merely says that the clause must have been said; he may agree that a written document is presumed to contain it. Indeed, Baal HaItur too validates antedated debt documents generally without requiring an express clause. See the responsa of Mahari ibn Lev, rule 12, siman 68, and Rabbi Shmuel Kohen, part 2, siman 44 and part 3, siman 83.
Some say, etc., that he may retract, etc. It appears to me that the dissenting authorities are correct, both logically and from the Gemara. Logically, since a person can encumber property he will acquire later, how could he retract a lien already granted? The lender advanced his money only because of that lien. One who wishes to retract must either repay the loan or honor the lien. The same applies where a seller encumbered future property for warranty. Since the purchaser relied upon that warranty and the sale itself cannot be rescinded unilaterally, neither can the future-property lien. The Gemara in Bava Batra 157 proves that future property may be encumbered from the rule that antedated debt documents are valid. If future property were not encumbered, such documents would allow wrongful collection from purchasers. If the debtor could freely retract, the same concern would remain, since perhaps he retracted before witnesses. We are ordinarily vigilant against every possible wrongful seizure from purchasers. The Rashba cites Rav Huna's rule regarding the sale of future palm fruit, where even according to Rabbi Meir the seller may retract before the fruit exists, and argues that a rabbinic lien cannot be stronger than Rabbi Meir's acquisition. This proof can be rejected. There the seller retracts from the entire sale. Here the lien remains so long as the loan is unpaid, and in a sale the seller cannot retract from the principal transaction. Moreover, Tosafot prove that a lien is stronger than an acquisition even according to Rabbi Meir, since a person may create a lien over property not expected to come into existence. The Rashba also cites the rule that where one borrowed twice and later acquired property, the creditors divide, arguing that if the first lien were irrevocable the property would belong solely to the first creditor. In my view this is not based upon retraction. The Sages presumed that later property is acquired partly through the funds of the second creditor, and that the first creditor himself is willing to share so that the second creditor will extend funds. When the Rashbam and Mordechai discuss whether the debtor may retract, they mean whether the later loan operates as a presumptive waiver by the first creditor, who knew that the debtor was receiving new funds, not a literal unilateral retraction from the earlier lien. See the responsum of Maharit, siman 128.
