They were not encumbered to the creditor. Either because the lender did not rely upon property the debtor did not yet own, or because no lien at all can attach to property that was not in the debtor's possession at the time of the loan.
While they remain in the debtor's possession. They are like movable property, which beit din compels the debtor to use to repay his creditor. If the debtor dies and the property passes to heirs, the conclusion of the Mechaber and Rama is likewise that the creditor may collect from it, as explained in the preceding siman.
But if he did not write, etc. General warranty is treated as a scribal omission because one who lends money ordinarily does so only on the assumption that the debtor's existing property is responsible. That reasoning does not extend automatically to property the debtor may later acquire or inherit, which the parties may never have contemplated. Therefore the principle that a person does not throw away his money for nothing does not itself establish an after acquired property clause.
And some say, etc. Shach in seif katan 1 understands that if a note comes before us without an after acquired property clause, we presume that the parties did in fact stipulate such a clause and that the scribe omitted it. But if we know that they did not stipulate it, then unlike ordinary warranty we do not treat silence as equivalent to an after acquired property clause. This is a novel distinction, and I have found no other authority who states it.
Everything that is fit to be encumbered. With respect to ordinary warranty we say that omission is a scribal error. Shach writes that if a debtor expressly encumbered movable property together with land but did not include after acquired property, subsequently acquired movable property that he later sold is not included merely by scribal presumption. The after acquired clause must be stated expressly. This is correct. See Tumim.
If he wrote part of it. Likewise, if he wrote that movable property was encumbered together with land but omitted after acquired property, we do not say that the omitted clause was a scribal error. See Tumim.
He may retract. Even according to Rabbi Meir, who holds that a person may transfer property not yet in existence, he may retract before the property comes into existence. The same applies to a lien on property not yet acquired. Before the debtor acquires or inherits it, he may retract and declare the lien canceled. It appears that the creditor may then demand immediate repayment, saying, 'I lent you on the basis that your future acquisitions would be encumbered to me. Since you withdraw that undertaking, return the money I lent you.' This applies even before the due date, as explained above in siman 39 seif 4. See Tumim. I wrote that according to this opinion, even if he used language of 'from now' concerning the future property, he may still retract until the property is actually inherited or acquired.
And some disagree. They hold that although Rabbi Meir allows retraction from a sale of future property before it comes into existence, a lien is stronger than a sale and cannot be retracted. Shach rules like these authorities. In Tumim I wrote that his proof is not decisive and that the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract. See there. See Tumim also where an after acquired property clause was written, property fell to the debtor while he was dying, and the heirs then sold it. Whether the lien took effect requires study.
