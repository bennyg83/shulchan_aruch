The effectiveness of an after acquired property lien is itself a rabbinic enactment. By strict Torah law it is difficult to understand why lien should be stronger than acquisition, since one cannot ordinarily convey property that does not yet exist. The problem remains even according to the view that liens are biblical, because the debtor's property that comes only after the loan was not present as guarantor at the time of the loan. Maharit distinguishes between property not yet in existence and a beneficiary not yet in existence. He suggests that a debtor can subject future property because the lien rests now on his body and obligates him to transfer the property once it arrives. That explanation confuses personal obligation and property lien. A person can certainly undertake a personal obligation now to pay one hundred even though he does not presently have the money, because his body exists and is obligated. But a lien that will allow collection from later purchasers must attach to the property itself. If it rested only on the debtor's body, purchasers would never become subject to it. The distinction is evident from the rule that according to some authorities an after acquired property lien can be revoked before the property comes into existence, whereas a personal undertaking to pay cannot simply be revoked because the person does not yet own the money. Thus personal obligation and property lien are fundamentally different. The correct explanation is that after acquired property lien is not Torah law at all. Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef explicitly say that the Sages strengthened this lien so that people would be willing to lend. The enactment applies where the borrower is already personally obligated to pay and the only problem is lack of property available for collection. The Sages then allow the creditor to reach property acquired later and later sold. Where the alleged borrower himself never became obligated, however, the enactment has nothing on which to operate. That is why one cannot use this doctrine to create a personal obligation to a person not yet in existence. The Sages strengthened collection for an existing debt; they did not invent an obligation where strict law created none. This is the proper conceptual distinction.
The Shach says that if the debtor did not write an after acquired property clause to the purchaser, the creditor can collect the entire appreciation rather than only half. Lechem Mishneh asks why the Gemara did not establish the baraita in exactly that way: the creditor had an after acquired clause and the purchaser did not. Tosafot's answer to another question resolves this. The baraita says the creditor collects principal from encumbered property and appreciation from free property. Tosafot asks: if the debtor later acquired free property, why can the creditor reach the purchaser at all? The purchaser should say, "I left you property from which to collect." Maharsha explains that the debtor acquired the later property only after the creditor had already foreclosed, so at the time of foreclosure no free property existed. Maharshal explains that the later property was acquired after the sale to the purchaser, so the purchaser could not say it had been left free at the time of his purchase. Either way, the purchaser must himself have an after acquired clause in his sale. Otherwise he could not later collect the principal from property acquired by the seller only after his purchase. Thus the baraita necessarily assumes that the purchaser had an after acquired property clause. Therefore it could not have been established in the case proposed by Lechem Mishneh, and the Gemara still had to explain "the appreciation" as half the appreciation.
Rashba holds that even after a debtor writes an after acquired property clause, he can revoke it before the future property comes into existence, because the lien is no stronger than Rabbi Meir's acquisition of future property. Rashba derives this from the case of one who borrowed, borrowed again, and then acquired property: if the first creditor could not revoke or lose his priority, the later creditor should receive nothing. The Shach rejects Rashba and says there is no concern that a debtor secretly revoked the after acquired clause before the property arrived. His proof from the law of postdated documents is not decisive. We generally do not suspect that a person has changed his position in the interim without evidence. Even under the view that witnesses acquire rights for the creditor from the time of signing, if the debtor expressly revokes before delivery the acquisition can fail. Yet the Gemara does not normally suspect such revocation merely because it was possible. Nor is the Shach's argument sound that because the borrower cannot retract from the loan itself until he repays, he cannot retract from the after acquired lien. The loan obligation and the property lien are distinct. A worker can retract in the middle of the day even after receiving advance wages, though the money remains a debt. So too the borrower can remain personally liable while revoking an unvested future lien. The difficulty in Rashba's own reasoning is different. Nimukei Yosef says Rashba agrees that if no revocation occurred, the future lien relates back to the original time. If so, in the case of two loans followed by acquisition, why should the creditors divide? If borrowing from the second creditor is not itself a revocation of the first lien, the first lien should relate back and take priority. The ordinary rules concerning two loan documents also indicate that merely entering a second loan does not revoke an earlier lien. This point leaves Rashba's application of revocation to the case of two creditors difficult. The Shach also cites Tosafot as saying lien is stronger than acquisition because, according to Rabbi Meir, it can reach future property even when its later acquisition and sale are not likely. But Tosafot's question itself is difficult. Rabbi Meir plainly allows a person to say, "This field, when I acquire it, shall be yours," even though acquisition is still in the future. Once the debtor later acquires the field, the lien can attach immediately, before any later sale. The sale is not the event creating the lien. The issue therefore does not prove that lien is inherently stronger than acquisition. This requires further study.
