There is a textual error, and it should read that according to the Rambam responsibility for after-acquired property is not automatically presumed in a document. See also Shach seif katan 2. Tumim questions the Shach's entire discussion. The Shach writes that since the creditor does not return the money when retracting from the lien, he cannot retract. But regarding one who transfers a davar shelo ba la'olam according to R. Meir, he may retract even though he does not return the money. The Shach also compares this to a later-dated document and raises concern that perhaps the debtor retracted. Tumim responds that we do not ordinarily suspect such retraction, because the remnant of Israel does not act deceitfully, and he supports this with several proofs. I would add that the Shach could have asked more simply: whenever a creditor collects from purchasers by virtue of an after-acquired property clause, why do we not fear that the debtor retracted from that clause before acquiring the property? Tumim concludes that the Rashba's view is primary. Ketzot HaChoshen raises a question from the Rosh in Bava Metzia. The Rosh writes that where a deed of sale was written for the first purchaser in Nisan but not delivered, and the seller then sold the same field to a second purchaser in Iyar, wrote and delivered that sale, and only afterward delivered the deed dated Nisan to the first purchaser, we do not apply the principle that witnesses acquire on behalf of the recipient from the time of signing, because the first deed was already nullified by the second sale. Ketzot infers that in a loan document, if the debtor sold property or borrowed from another creditor between writing and delivery, this is not considered a retraction, and therefore perhaps with an after-acquired property clause as well a second loan does not constitute retraction. There is no difficulty. The Rosh's reasoning concerning acquisition from the time of signing is as follows. If the seller sold the same field to the second purchaser, the first deed becomes entirely ineffective, because at the time the first deed is later delivered the seller no longer has any right in that field. For the deed to be delivered effectively, the seller or grantor must possess at the time of delivery the right described in the deed. If he has that right, he may deliver the deed, and by delivery the recipient acquires retroactively from the time of signing. But where the seller has already sold the field to another, he has no remaining right either in the field or in the first sale deed, which says that he is selling his own field, and therefore that deed has become void and cannot be delivered. A loan document is different. Even if the debtor sold some of his fields, the loan document and its lien have not become wholly void, because the debtor still has rights and obligations under the document with respect to his other property. Since the document remains capable of delivery, once it is delivered the creditor's rights relate back to the time of signing against all relevant property. Even if the intervening act is treated as a retraction, the later delivery renews the acquisition retroactively. Here, however, where the debtor first pledged future property to one creditor and then later pledged that same future property to a second creditor, the second pledge is certainly a retraction from the first. After that retraction there has been no renewed transfer to the first creditor. What the Shach writes regarding Tosafot likewise presents no difficulty upon careful study, but this is not the place to elaborate because the practical ruling is clear.
