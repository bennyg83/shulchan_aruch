And when did he acquire it? See Sema seif katan 9, who asks why witnesses that he acquired the property are not sufficient here, as they are in the first clause. There is a textual error there, and it should read that for this reason the following seif requires the heir to bring witnesses. In my view the matter is straightforward. The principal basis of these poskim is the rule of "here it was found, here it was," meaning a present chazakah, as explained by the poskim and by Sema siman 60 seif katan 20 in several places. Maharit explains this as a present-status presumption and compares it to R. Nachman's rule concerning whether a donor was healthy or a shechiv mera. We presume that as the matter is now, so it was at the time the doubt first arose. Therefore, if witnesses testify that a person was established in possession of a field, meaning that they saw him residing in it, then if, for example, the loan document is dated Nisan and in Tammuz he was seen living in the field, while witnesses know that he acquired it but do not know whether he acquired it in Adar before the loan or in Iyar after the loan, we apply the present chazakah. Since by Sivan it was certainly his, because witnesses establish his possession, we say that just as it was his in Sivan, so it was his on the doubtful date, Nisan. But if the witnesses never saw him in possession and merely witnessed an act of acquisition without knowing its date, the doubt concerns the very day of acquisition. Perhaps it preceded Nisan, or perhaps he acquired the field only later. The witnesses merely know that he acquired some field and cannot establish when this field entered his ownership. Moreover, the principle of "here it was found, here it was" applies to the place or state in which an item is found, allowing us to assume continuity in that place or state. If we merely witnessed an acquisition and do not know whether it was before or after the loan, we cannot say that because the property belongs to him now it must have belonged to him earlier. That is not within the category of this presumption; the original chazakah is stronger. A proof appears from the laws of tumah. In the beginning of Niddah the present-state presumption is applied, yet in Niddah 9, where a person wrapped himself in his garment and there is a doubt whether it contacted tumah before it contacted pure items or only afterward, the pure items remain pure. We do not say that because the garment is now impure it must already have been impure before. Where the true present-state presumption applies, even in a public domain and against a presumption of purity, the result may be impurity. It follows that this case is outside that category. So too here, only where he was seen residing in the house do we say that as he is found there now, he was there previously. If we saw only an acquisition and do not know whether it preceded or followed the loan, there is no present chazakah that can determine the doubtful date. Nor does the later sale prove that he owned it earlier, because he was never seen residing there. In the law of chazakah of land, until he is seen residing there for at least one day, even a later sale does not itself establish that it was his. Therefore no present chazakah applies here. If, however, he was seen residing in the property even one day before the sale, then the purchaser must bring proof of when he acquired it.
