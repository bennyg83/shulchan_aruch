And when he acquired it. The Ir Shushan omits the requirement that the purchaser establish when the debtor acquired the property. He apparently understood that once the purchaser proves by witnesses that the debtor bought it and did not inherit it from his ancestors, the burden shifts to the creditor to prove that it was acquired before the loan. But the Shulchan Aruch and Tur both state that the purchaser must establish when it was acquired, and the Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, part 4, ruling 2, cites Ramban: "The purchaser must bring proof that the debtor acquired it and on what date he acquired it." The Sema and Bach therefore distinguish between two types of proof. If the creditor has only witnesses that the debtor purchased the land, that does not suffice to extract it from the purchaser, and the purchaser need not establish anything further. But if the creditor has witnesses that the land was held as the debtor's property, then it does not help the purchaser merely to prove that the debtor once bought it, because we presume he acquired it long ago, before borrowing from this creditor. The purchaser must therefore prove the date of acquisition. I do not understand their reasoning. If we apply a presumption backward in time, why should evidence of possession be stronger than evidence of acquisition? Possession itself arises from acquisition. There is no stronger proof that land belonged to the debtor than witnesses who saw him acquire it. The main point is Ramban's own concluding explanation. He writes that the purchaser must prove both acquisition and its date because otherwise we leave the property in the debtor's established ownership and say that it and his ancestors' estate belonged to him from long ago. According to the Sema's explanation, how could we say it belonged to his ancestors when the purchaser has brought witnesses that the debtor purchased it? At most we could say he bought it long before the loan. Ramban's language expressly includes the possibility that it had always belonged to his family, which is inconsistent with their reading. It therefore appears that even where the purchaser produces witnesses that the debtor acquired the field, we may still say that the land was historically the debtor's or his ancestors' property, and that the acquisition witnessed by these witnesses was a later reacquisition from this very purchaser after the debtor had previously sold it to him. Such sequences are common enough that postdated sale deeds are disqualified. Even if the witnesses say that the debtor bought it from another person, perhaps this purchaser first sold it to that other person and the debtor then bought it from him. The purchaser must therefore establish when the debtor's witnessed acquisition occurred, showing that it preceded the debtor's sale to him. Once that is established, he need not prove anything further, because there is no longer a basis to revert to a remote ancestral presumption. This explanation is admittedly somewhat strained, but it is preferable to attributing an entirely new and unexplained distinction to Ramban.
The first opinion is primary. The Sema asks that below in siman 250 the Rama rules without qualification like the Mechaber that where one gives movables "from today and after death," the heirs cannot say that perhaps the donor acquired those movables only afterward, when he could no longer transfer property not yet in his possession. The Sema distinguishes between movables and land. Movables move from place to place, so we can properly invoke the presumption that an item found in a particular domain is presumed to have been there previously as well. Land, by contrast, remains fixed in its place and does not move at all, so the principle "here it was found and here it was" does not apply in the same manner, because the physical land itself never changes location. The Shach asks where the Sema found such a distinction. He says rather that the authorities simply disagree: Maharam and Rashba hold differently, and the Rama relied here upon what he wrote in this siman. The Shach's approach is preferable. Nevertheless, some version of the Sema's distinction appears necessary. In the next seif the Mechaber cites the view of the Geonim, which is the Ramban's position cited by the Baal HaTerumot as proof for his law, that an heir who claims the donor acquired the property only later must bring proof of that later acquisition. Why should this be so? Once the heir proves that the donor acquired it, the prior presumption should be gone and we should say it was acquired only now. Even according to the Sema and Bach, who distinguish between witnesses to acquisition and witnesses to established possession, as discussed above, the Geonim and Shulchan Aruch should not have stated the rule without qualification. They should have distinguished: if the assets were established as the donor's property, the heir must prove that the donor acquired them later; but if they were not previously established as his and are merely now found among his effects, it should suffice for the heir to prove that he purchased them. We must therefore distinguish between land and movables. The Geonim spoke about movables, and regarding movables everyone agrees that we apply "here it was found and here it was." Therefore, with movables, the heir must establish when the donor acquired them. Ramban compares land to this only where the debtor had an established chazakah in the land, because in such a case we likewise say that it had been in his possession from long ago, perhaps from his ancestors. The matter still requires study. Rashi, however, seems to imply the opposite. In Bava Kamma 14b he explains that movables are not encumbered because one may say the heirs acquired them after their father's death. Clearly Rashi does not mean that this possibility itself technically defeats collection in every case. If the father had deposited those same movables with others, or even if the creditor himself held them as a deposit but had not expressly seized them during the father's lifetime, they still would not be legally encumbered as inherited movables, even though no one could say the heirs acquired them later. Moreover, Rashi himself in Bava Kamma 39 appears to treat even an innocuous ox as movables of orphans. Rather, Rashi's meaning is that a lender does not rely upon movables because the debtor can hide or remove them. Even if they later reappear and the heirs use them openly, the heirs can say they acquired them only afterward. For that reason movables never become subject to the ordinary lien. This implies that Rashi does not apply "here it was found and here it was" to movables. Land is different: the creditor relies upon it because heirs cannot plausibly say they acquired it only after death where it is placed in the ancestral chazakah. If this were a firmly established distinction, it would resolve the difficulty raised above concerning the Gemara's answer in the case of one who sold a cow, that witnesses saw him buy and immediately sell it. Tosafot and Ramban ask that the same answer should apply to land. One could answer that the existence of witnesses to the purchase does not by itself solve the problem of interested testimony. We do not yet know whether the witness owes anyone or to whom he owes. We merely fear that perhaps he is indebted and seeks to preserve the property before a creditor. It remains possible that his creditor is a relative of those very witnesses who testify that he bought and immediately sold the property. Those witnesses would then be disqualified from giving testimony that harms their relative, yet the creditor might still collect if their testimony is disregarded. We would have to say that this Gemara supports those who hold that the creditor bears the burden to prove that the property was in the debtor's hands at the time of the loan. Once independent witnesses establish that the debtor bought and immediately sold the cow, any creditor who later appears is told to bring proof that the cow was already in the debtor's possession when his lien arose. In truth he cannot produce such proof, because the witnesses are telling the truth that the purchase and resale were immediate; he cannot be expected to produce false witnesses. This works for movables, such as the sold cow. With a sold field, however, we may place the land in the chazakah of the debtor's ancestors, and the purchaser bears the burden of proof. We may therefore still fear that the seller owes a relative of the witnesses who testify to immediate purchase and resale, and their testimony would be ineffective against that relative. Ramban there, in the discussion of "with these witnesses," also raises the possibility of relatives among the witnesses; see there. One may still say that Rashi agrees that with movables we do apply "here it was found and here it was" once it is independently established that the item was at some moment in the ancestor's possession. The rule then preserves it in that same domain backward in time. In Rashi's case, however, the orphans say, "We ourselves bought it." Since the movables are presently in the orphans' possession, we do not transfer them backward into the father's ownership without proof. In any event, the Gemara's immediate-purchase answer remains evidence for the view that the creditor must bring proof. There is, however, a difficulty with the Beit Yosef's rulings. Here he records both Ramban's view and the dissenting view. Below in siman 250 he rules simply like the Geonim that the heirs must bring proof, because we apply the presumption "here it was found," even though the heirs are presently in possession. Above in siman 104, seif 5, he writes that where it is not established that the debtor owned the movables when he borrowed from the first creditor, we presume a sequence of first loan, second loan, and only afterward acquisition, so that we rule the creditors divide. If the second creditor already collected, what he collected remains collected. In the Beit Yosef he even cautions that many great authorities erred in this principle. It is true that his warning may be directed specifically to the case where the second creditor already collected. Otherwise it would be strange to call an opinion "error" where earlier authorities openly disagreed. In a case of prior collection the second creditor can plead kim li according to the authorities who support him, and we do not remove property from one in possession. Nevertheless, the difficulty remains because in siman 250 the heirs are likewise in possession, and the Beit Yosef still rules simply that the donee prevails by the presumption. In truth, the Baal HaTerumot's case in siman 104 concerns money. With money one certainly does not presume that a debtor had been accumulating the same coins for a long time and keeping them in a treasury. Maharanach distinguishes similarly in a responsum; see there. The Baal HaTerumot also follows his own opinion against Ramban, holding that the recipient of a gift bears the burden of proof. But according to the Mechaber there is still difficulty. What appears in resolution is that the Mechaber understood the Baal HaTerumot's silence as significant. The Baal HaTerumot states the rule without mentioning the famous dispute among the authorities. This indicates that the case is different because, according to the Sages, a person cannot transfer property not yet in existence. An after-acquired-property lien therefore has no ordinary Torah basis. It exists only through rabbinic enactment to avoid closing the door before borrowers, as Rashbam writes in Bava Batra. With respect to a rabbinic institution, wherever there is room for doubt we keep the property under the Torah baseline. Therefore we presume that the debtor acquired the assets only later and the creditors divide. According to this approach, however, the reading must be as I wrote above, not the Sema's version that perhaps the debtor acquired the property before both loans, but rather that he acquired it between the two loans. Even then the first creditor's claim to it depends on the rabbinic after-acquired-property lien, as I explained at length in siman 104. If one follows the Sema's reading that perhaps the debtor acquired the property before both loans, the first creditor's lien would be a Torah lien, and the difficulty would remain: why should his position be weaker than that of the recipient of a gift? I found, however, in Mordechai at the end of the chapter Mi Shemet in Bava Batra that Rabbi Avraham writes as follows: whenever a creditor seeks to seize assets and it is not known whether those assets were acquired before or after the loan document was written, the creditor must prove when they were acquired. If he cannot prove it, he takes only half. Otherwise, many loan documents and ketubot containing after-acquired-property clauses would create a serious stumbling block, because sometimes there are no witnesses to when a particular field was acquired, and the earlier creditor might seize all of it even though by strict law he is entitled to only half. This clearly implies that, absent the concern for judicial error, strict law would place the property in the earlier creditor's chazakah backward in time. Otherwise no special enactment or policy would be needed; ordinary doubt would already prevent full collection. Rabbi Avraham's concern is that courts might routinely award everything because of the retrospective chazakah even in cases where the debtor actually acquired the property later. Therefore he rules that the earlier creditor receives only half. One may ask in the opposite direction: sometimes the debtor truly acquired the property earlier, in which case all of it belongs to the first creditor, yet we give him only half. Is that not also an injustice? The answer must be that the first lender can protect himself when taking the loan document by having the witnesses record what inherited property and land the borrower presently owns. If the borrower does not wish to disclose his assets, "a borrower is servant to the lender," and no one forces the lender to extend the loan. By contrast, the second lender cannot reliably prove what property the debtor did not own earlier. "We did not see it" is not proof, and even the debtor himself cannot be trusted to destroy the first creditor's rights. Therefore, to avoid the more unavoidable danger, the law divides. This seems clearly to be Rabbi Avraham's intention. Accordingly, this concern applies specifically between an earlier and later creditor. With a donor and donee, however, can the recipient force the donor to make a public inventory of all his movables? People ordinarily do not disclose all of their possessions. Thus there is equal potential injustice in both directions. If all the property is given to the donee, perhaps the donor acquired it only later; but if it is withheld, perhaps he already owned it at the time of the gift and it truly belongs to the donee. The law therefore returns to its ordinary chazakah and applies "here it was found and here it was." This provides some reconciliation of the Mechaber's rulings. Examine this carefully.
