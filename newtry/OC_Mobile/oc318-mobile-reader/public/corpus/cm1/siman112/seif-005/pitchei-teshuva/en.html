He is considered a purchaser. See Baer Heitev, which cites the Shach as explaining that the Mechaber's phrase "and she then married" is not necessarily exact, and that the same would apply where she had married earlier. However, Responsa Tashbetz, part 2, siman 255, states explicitly to the contrary. He writes that if there was an oral debt against property that came to the woman only after she married, such as where the decedent had borrowed during his lifetime and the property later descended by inheritance to this married heiress, the oral creditor may collect from that property under the law governing oral loans collected from heirs. One cannot say that the husband is deemed the first purchaser of these assets, because before they descended to his wife they were already subject to the creditor's lien. The husband acquired no greater right in the inherited property than his wife received from her predecessor. Tashbetz records that he so ruled in practice. This is also cited in Shaar Mishpat. See further Responsa Amudei Or, siman 113.
