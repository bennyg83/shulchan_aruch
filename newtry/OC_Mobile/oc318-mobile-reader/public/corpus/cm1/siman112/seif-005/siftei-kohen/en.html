Reuven, etc. This ruling requires study. In any event, it appears to me to be an unresolved question of law, and if the creditor seized property it is not removed from him, as I shall explain. Baal HaTerumot derives this ruling from the discussion at the end of the chapter Yesh Nochalin concerning a woman who borrowed and consumed the money and then married, where the Gemara asks whether the husband is treated as a purchaser or as an heir, and concludes that he is a purchaser. This accords with the ruling of the Rif. Rashbam, however, rules in the name of Rabbenu Chananel, and agrees with him, that in such a case the husband is treated as an heir because otherwise the creditor suffers a loss. The Tur cites both views in Even HaEzer, siman 91, while the Mechaber rules without qualification like the Rif and the Rosh. In my view this requires study. If the creditor seized property he may say, "I rely upon Rabbenu Chananel and Rashbam." A careful study of the sugya indicates that their view is primary. The Mordechai, Hagahot Ashri in the name of Rabbenu Baruch, Rabbi Eliezer bar Natan, Tosafot in Nedarim, and Hagahot Ashri in the name of Raavyah all support this ruling. Piskei Rekanti likewise records the dispute and concludes in the name of Maharam that, since the authorities disagree, property already seized is not removed on account of doubt. Thus the very case of a woman who borrowed and consumed the money is an unresolved question of law. The Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer cites only Rashbam in the name of Rabbenu Chananel and does not mention these other authorities. Had he seen them, he might not have ruled as he did. Moreover, even the Rif and Rosh did not declare it certain that the husband is a purchaser. The Gemara left the question unresolved. They ruled that the creditor may not collect from the husband because the husband is in possession, and because the creditor who lent orally to a woman who was likely to marry brought the loss upon himself. Their language, "the question was not resolved; therefore an oral creditor does not collect from the husband," shows that the ruling rests upon doubt and possession. Accordingly, if the creditor seized property, it is not removed from him. Baal HaTerumot also rules in the present case, where the document lacked an after-acquired-property clause, only because the husband is in possession, not because the husband's status as purchaser is certain. The Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber likewise often state the practical ruling without qualification where the underlying Gemara remains unresolved, because the possessor prevails. The Maggid Mishneh expressly distinguishes the clear case of a written loan from the unresolved case of an oral loan. I later found that Maharam Alshich, responsum 17, states explicitly that even according to the Rif and Rosh, if the creditor seized property in the case of a woman who borrowed and consumed the money, it is not removed from him. In the present case, however, the loan to Reuven was documented, but the document omitted an after-acquired-property clause. Here there is even greater reason to permit the creditor to retain property. The rationale of the Rif, as explained by the responsa of Rashba and Ramban, is that an oral lender to a woman likely to marry should have anticipated that the husband could claim that she had repaid. That rationale does not apply to a properly documented loan to Reuven. Even according to the Mordechai's broader formulation, the oral lender should have anticipated her marriage, since she was always capable of marrying. A lender to Reuven himself cannot reasonably be said to have forfeited his money merely because he failed to include an after-acquired-property clause. Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber apparently maintain that he nevertheless brought the loss upon himself by omitting that clause, but this is not the primary reasoning, particularly if the husband is treated as an heir. Still, I am not worthy to reject their ruling and that of the later authorities. For practical law, however, it appears clear that where the document simply omitted an after-acquired-property clause, the matter is at least doubtful and property seized by the creditor is not removed from him, for some authorities hold that responsibility for after-acquired property is included through a scribal presumption even when not written. Indeed, even if the document expressly stated that future acquisitions would not be encumbered, if the creditor seized property now held by the husband, it is not removed from him for the reasons stated above. It also follows that Baal HaTerumot, the Tur, and the Mechaber discuss nichsei melog, contrary to the Sema, who understands the passage as referring to nichsei tzon barzel. The ruling is derived from the question whether a husband is a purchaser, a question relevant to nichsei melog. With nichsei tzon barzel, where the husband accepts responsibility, everyone agrees that he is treated as a purchaser. The language "property she brought into the marriage" is equally appropriate to nichsei melog when the husband did not accept responsibility. The Sema's objection from siman 103 has already been answered there. See the responsum of Mahari ibn Lev, book 1, principle 12, responsum 74, at length.
And she then married, etc. The wording is not intended to limit the ruling to a woman who borrowed before marriage and only afterward married. Baal HaTerumot uses this formulation because he derived the law from the case of a woman who borrowed, consumed the money, and then married. The same ruling applies where she was already married and Reuven then died. According to this view, the creditor may not collect from the husband because the husband is treated as a purchaser. This is evident from the end of the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui and from the Tur, Even HaEzer, siman 91. See the responsa of Maharit, simanim 9, 33, and 34, and Maharshadam, siman 189.
