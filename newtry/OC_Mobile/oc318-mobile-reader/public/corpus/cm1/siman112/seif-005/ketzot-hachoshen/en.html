The Shach treats the husband's status in his wife's property as a legal doubt. His reasoning is that the case derives from the law, "she borrowed and consumed," which itself is uncertain, and the husband is therefore protected by possession. He concludes that even without an after acquired property clause, if the creditor seized from the husband's property we do not remove it, even where the loan document expressly says that future property shall not be subjected. I am astonished. A creditor's lien is ordinarily on land, and physical seizure has no force in a doubt concerning land. Tosafot says explicitly that seizure does not decide a doubtful land right. If the Shach means movables subjected together with land, his first argument based on an ordinary unstated after acquired clause cannot fit, because everyone agrees an express after acquired clause is needed for movables together with land. There may still be room for seizure today after the Geonic enactment making heirs' movables available to creditors, if the husband is treated as an heir. Yet even that is questionable. Maggid Mishneh indicates that seizure based only on a doubtful rabbinic enactment does not defeat a Torah baseline. Where movables were not expressly subjected together with land, collection itself depends on later enactment. A creditor cannot necessarily prevail merely by seizing under a doubtful extension of that enactment.
