And she then married, etc. The Shach raises two objections. First, he understands that the reason an oral creditor cannot collect from a husband, who is treated as a purchaser, even though in a case of loss the Sages sometimes treated a husband as an heir, is that we plead for the husband that perhaps the debt was repaid. He cites Responsa Ramban no. 56 for this. If so, here there is a written document and only an after-acquired-property clause is missing. The borrower cannot plead repayment, so the husband should be treated as an heir and the creditor should collect. Second, the Shach argues that in the case of an oral loan we say the lender caused his own loss because he should not have lent without a document. But here he did take a document. Why should the mere omission of an after-acquired-property clause be called self-inflicted loss? As to the first argument, it is clear that the Gemara's entire discussion of a woman who borrowed, consumed the money, and then married, and whether the husband is treated as purchaser or heir, cannot be limited to a loan where repayment can be pleaded. If that were the only issue, then even if the husband were an heir we would still plead repayment on his behalf. If the woman admits the debt, her admission cannot prejudice her husband. This is obvious. Even Rabbenu Chananel and those who hold that the Sages treated the husband as an heir even regarding an oral loan agree that the wife's admission cannot obligate him. Further, how could the Gemara prove anything from the rule that a widow is supported from her husband's estate? There is no repayment concern there, unlike an oral loan. It is therefore clear that the Gemara discusses circumstances where repayment is not a concern, and nevertheless the Rif and those who follow him conclude that, as to an oral loan, the husband is treated as a purchaser. Accordingly, in the cited responsum Ramban merely adds the repayment argument to make the result certain, because he did not decide between the Rif and Rabbenu Chananel and those who hold that even for an oral loan the husband is treated as an heir. In the case asked of him, since repayment was also a concern, everyone agreed that the creditor could not collect. His own language indicates this when he writes, "Even if we say the Sages treated him as an heir." See there. Know also that the cited responsum did not necessarily issue from the Rashba. The printed collection assembled responsa from numerous earlier geonim, as is evident from its introduction. The Rashba himself holds that where a document expressly encumbers movables and land, even without agav, collection may be made from heirs under the law of the Gemara, as I wrote above in siman 107. Yet the cited responsum says explicitly that so long as an agav clause is absent, collection cannot be made from the husband, and its language implies this even if the husband is treated as an heir. That also cannot be explained by a repayment concern, since there is a written document. It follows that the responsum follows the view of Tosafot mentioned above, that even in this case collection is not made from heirs. It also says expressly that movables are not collected from the husband even if he is treated as an heir. Where, then, is the enactment of the Geonim that creditors collect movables from heirs? Necessarily the responsum follows Rabbenu Tam's view above in siman 107 that the Geonic enactment was not made in every circumstance. Thus one cannot rely upon this responsum as representing the Rashba's own doctrine. By contrast, the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer cites an actual responsum of the Rashba which clearly agrees with the Baal HaTerumot: even with a written loan, if there is no after-acquired-property clause, the husband is treated as a purchaser and the creditor collects nothing from him. See there. The Shach's second argument, however, has force. Why should omission of an after-acquired-property clause be called self-inflicted loss? The Rashba and Tur themselves are not proof, because they hold that an omitted after-acquired-property clause is a scribal error. Their case must therefore be one where the document expressly excluded collection from after-acquired property. There the creditor plainly caused his own loss by expressly waiving the lien that would otherwise be supplied automatically, because a person is not presumed to throw away his money. But according to the Baal HaTerumot and Mechaber, who do not treat the omission as a scribal error, we must say that this clause is not ordinarily written, and the principle that a person does not throw away his money does not apply. Why, then, call the loss self-inflicted? It appears that they understand "he caused his own loss" differently. The phrase does not mean that he actively harmed himself. Rather, we observe that he himself did not protect against the risk. When he lent orally, the borrower could sell his assets at any moment and leave the lender with nothing. The lender accepted that risk. Why should we now protect him by canceling the Sages' enactment that treats the husband as a purchaser, when the lender himself did not care about this potential loss? It is comparable to knowingly abandoning one's property. The same logic applies where the document lacks an after-acquired-property clause. The creditor accepted that the borrower could at any moment sell later-acquired property and the creditor would be unable to collect it. Since he himself did not protect against that risk, why should we undo the rabbinic rule treating the husband as purchaser in order to save him from precisely that loss? The reasoning is clear. The Shach further writes that if the creditor seized property he can plead kim li like Rabbenu Chananel and those who hold that even for an oral loan the husband is treated as an heir. That is correct in principle. But seizure does not apply to land. As to movables, if the document did not contain a movables-and-land clause that would permit collection from heirs under strict law, one cannot invoke the later Geonic enactment through the rule that the husband is an heir. We do not say that the Sages uprooted their own enactment in order to make room for a later enactment, for we do not create an enactment upon an enactment. Even regarding return of an appraisal, which is a law of the Gemara itself, the Sages did not uproot their purchaser classification. Certainly they did not do so merely because of the Geonic enactment.
