And she then married. See Shach seif katan 6, who concludes that this is a safek in the law and that if one party seized property, beit din does not remove it from him. Tumim objects that seizure is ineffective with respect to land, while with respect to movable property, even if the husband is treated as an heir, movable property of heirs is not encumbered except by the later enactment of the Geonim. Even in a case where an assessment would ordinarily revert, since that rule is itself only an enactment we do not displace the enactment treating the husband as a purchaser, and certainly not merely because of the Geonic enactment. In my view there is no difficulty. In the case of Rava bar Sharshom in Bava Batra 32, he seized produce on account of a debt owed to him, even though movable property of heirs is not encumbered and even though post-death appreciation and produce are not subject to the creditor's lien, yet his seizure was effective. The reason must be that whenever land remains to the heirs, there is also a personal debt obligation resting upon the heirs. If the debtor's personal obligation disappeared entirely at death, no property lien could continue, just as one who sells a debt instrument and then waives the underlying debt causes the assignment to fail because the personal obligation has been extinguished. It follows that a form of personal obligation remains upon the heirs. Tumim writes this in siman 66 seif katan 43. Therefore Rava bar Sharshom could seize the produce because land remained to the heirs and he seized movable property on account of the lien he possessed against that land. Tumim states this as well in siman 106. This also explains our case. The rule that seizure does not help in a doubtful land case applies where the doubt concerns ownership of the land itself, namely whether it ever left the prior owner's possession. There we leave the land with the prior owner. Here, by contrast, even though the land is after-acquired property, it was subject to the creditor's lien during the debtor's lifetime and remains so after his death, as Tumim writes in siman 120 seif katan 7. The only new uncertainty created by the woman's marriage is whether the husband is legally a purchaser, in which case the land left the creditor's lien, or an heir, in which case it remains encumbered. We cannot invoke the prior owner's chazakah against the creditor, because the previous legal state was precisely that the land was encumbered to the creditor. Once there is a doubt whether the creditor retains a property lien in the land, there is correspondingly a doubt regarding the personal obligation resting on those who hold through the debtor, and he may therefore seize movable property on account of that doubt, just as Rava bar Sharshom could seize movables based on his claim that he retained a lien against the land. This appears correct to me.
