He is considered a purchaser. See Baer Heitev, who writes that the Mechaber's phrase "and she then married" is not precise, and that the same would be true if she had married first. See Responsa Tashbetz, part 2, siman 255, which explicitly states the opposite. He writes that if there was an oral debt against property that came to a married woman only after her marriage, for example where the person from whom she inherited had borrowed during his lifetime, then died and the property descended to this married heiress, it appears clear that the oral creditor may collect as an oral creditor collects from heirs, as explained in the chapter Shum HaYetomim. One cannot say that the husband is considered the first purchaser of these assets, because before the inheritance fell to his wife the assets were already encumbered to the creditor and the husband never acquired them free of that lien. Since they came to the wife by inheritance, the husband cannot be treated as a purchaser with respect to property of her decedent. Tashbetz says that he ruled this way in practice. Shaar Mishpat also cites this. See there further, and see Responsa Ammudei Or, siman 113.
