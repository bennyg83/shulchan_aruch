Some say they divide the disputed half. At first glance this is difficult, because we should say that an uncertainty cannot remove from a certainty, as in the case of a doubtful heir and a yavam. Were it not for the second creditor, the first would certainly have a lien on the whole. I later found Maharit, part 1, raise this very question concerning one who borrowed, then borrowed again, and afterward acquired property: why should the two creditors divide equally, when the first creditor's lien is certain and the second's is doubtful? There is no difficulty according to Tosafot in Yevamot 38a. Tosafot explains that the yavam is called a certain heir as against the doubtful heir only because the yavam is certainly an heir of the grandfather, while the other is uncertain whether he is an heir at all. In the case of property falling upon a mother and son, however, Binyamin is not a certain heir to the entire remaining half, because if Rachel died before Yosef, Reuven too would inherit. So too here, the first creditor is not certain as to all the after-acquired property. Since property acquired after both loans may become subject to the second creditor as well, the first creditor has no certainty in the disputed half.
Which they divide. See the Sema, who writes that if the later creditor claimed that the property was acquired between the two loans, he would take it alone. The Tur did not write this only because acquisition in the interval is uncommon. If the later creditor does make that claim, however, it is a valid claim. The Bach asks why the later creditor should not be believed to take half through a migo that he could have claimed, 'It was acquired between the two loans,' which would entitle him to the whole. There is no difficulty. This is the exact structure of the Mishnah of two holding a garment, where one says, 'All of it is mine,' and the other says, 'Half is mine.' The latter receives only one quarter, despite a migo that he could have claimed the whole. The early authorities already ask this, and all the answers given there apply here as well. The Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Metzia asks why the one claiming half receives a quarter at all, given the principle that an uncertainty cannot remove from a certainty. It answers that he receives the quarter because he has a migo that he could have claimed the whole. According to that approach, there is likewise no contradiction with the rule discussed in seif 3, where the later creditor otherwise receives nothing because an uncertainty cannot displace a certainty. He receives the disputed quarter because he has a migo that he could have claimed the property was acquired between the loans and that the whole was his.
Which they divide. Maharit, in a responsum in Choshen Mishpat, asks against Beit Yosef that we should establish the property according to its present status. Where there is no original chazakah, perhaps we follow the chazakah at the time the object is found, as in Bava Batra, chapter Mi Shemet, concerning a gift deed stating that the donor was sick and lying in bed. The Gemara connects this to the dispute of Rabbi Yaakov and Rabbi Natan as to who must bring proof whether the donor was sick or healthy. Maharit argues that, especially according to those who rule like Rabbah and Rabbi Natan, we follow present condition, and even according to Abaye, who leaves money in possession where both possibilities are equal, perhaps that is only because monetary possession is stronger than bodily chazakah. Where no one has a monetary chazakah, as here where we are uncertain to which creditor the property became subject, we should follow its status when found. We have already demonstrated at length in Kuntres HaKlalim that present-state chazakah does not operate in this way. Rabbi Natan's reason is not mere present condition, but the general presumption that people are healthy, as Kiddushin, chapter Asarah Yuchasin, explains in the case where a father betrothed his daughter on the road and she betrothed herself in the city and is found to be a bogeret. The Gemara says that Shmuel can agree even with Rabbi Natan, because there everyone is presumed healthy and the person who seeks to remove himself from the presumption of health must bring proof. Tosafot there explains that Rav's rule, 'she is now a bogeret before us,' is also not based on a general present-condition chazakah but on the ordinary likelihood that the relevant signs appeared in the morning. Tosafot in Bava Batra similarly writes that the Kiddushin sugya contradicts a broad reading of present chazakah in Mi Shemet, because Rabbi Natan's reasoning depends on the majority presumption of health. Tosafot at the beginning of Niddah writes the same. Thus, in an evenly balanced doubt with no original chazakah, we do not simply follow the state in which the matter is now found. Beit Yosef's ruling is therefore correct.
