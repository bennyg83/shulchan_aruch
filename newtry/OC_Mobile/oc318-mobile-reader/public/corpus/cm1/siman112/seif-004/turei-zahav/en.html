(Seif 5, that a husband is a purchaser of his wife's property). Sema asks that here the husband is treated as a purchaser even during her lifetime, while in siman 103, regarding reversal of an appraisal, this applies only after the wife dies. He distinguishes between melog property and iron-flock property. I am astonished, because the present law comes from Sefer HaTerumot and is derived from the end of Yesh Nohalin, where the Gemara cites the enactment of Usha that a husband is a purchaser of his wife's property. The law in siman 103 comes from HaMafkid, page 35, where the same enactment of Usha is cited. They must therefore be understood consistently. There is in fact no difficulty. In HaIshah Shenaflu, page 78, the Gemara distinguishes between the husband's rights during her lifetime and to the produce, and the enactment of Usha concerning the body of the land. Before Usha the husband was already a purchaser with respect to the produce, but not the body of the land. Usha treated him as purchaser of the land itself after her death. Thus, regarding a borrower who later acquires property, the wife's creditor cannot seize it from the husband even during her lifetime because he is at least a purchaser of the produce. In siman 103, however, appraisal is not reversed to a purchaser because a purchaser descends into the land intending to retain the land itself. That reasoning does not apply to a husband during his wife's lifetime, when he is only a purchaser of the produce. Only after her death is he a purchaser of the land itself. A husband is no better than a creditor, who also eats produce, yet an appraisal is reversed to him. One might ask why Sefer HaTerumot derives the present rule from Usha, which concerns after death and the body of the land, rather than from the Mishnah concerning lifetime produce. The answer is that the enactment of Usha embraces the entire classification, as shown by the Gemara's question whether the Mishnah already taught the enactment. This, in my view, makes the Tur's position clear.
