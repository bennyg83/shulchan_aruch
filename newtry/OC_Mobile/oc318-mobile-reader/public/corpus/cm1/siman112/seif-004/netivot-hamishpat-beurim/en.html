They divide the half that is disputed. Although the case concerns two parties making definite claims, as appears from Shach seif katan 8 where he asks why one should not be believed through a migo, and therefore one of them is certainly making a false claim and the proposed division cannot literally be the truth, nevertheless division is appropriate here. This is unlike the case of the disputed third maneh in siman 76, where the holder does not know from whom he borrowed and, when both claimants are certain, the money remains deposited. There, if the money belongs to one claimant it does not belong to the other, so dividing it would necessarily take property from the true owner and give it to the dishonest claimant. Here, even if one creditor's factual claim is correct and the property became subject first to his lien, it is nevertheless also subject to the other creditor, because the first creditor does not own the property outright but merely has a lien. Even if the debtor pays the first creditor with money and removes his lien, the property then remains subject to the second. Indeed, if the later creditor comes first to collect the land, beit din gives it to him, and when the earlier creditor later appears he can seize it from him. Therefore there is no reason to leave the property deposited here. Since the first creditor cannot presently prove his priority sufficiently to collect, the second may collect, and if Eliyahu later comes and reveals that it belonged to the first creditor's lien, the first can then recover from the second. This is the same reason that when the first creditor is overseas we allow the second to collect. There is no purpose in leaving the property idle, because even if it is in the other's hands the true earlier creditor can later recover it. Certainly the rule cannot be kol d'alim gavar, because Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia write that this rule applies only where neither party is presently considered in possession. Here both are treated as in possession because the property is encumbered to both. In that sense the case also resembles the disputed third maneh in siman 76, where the debtor holds the money for both claimants. This also resolves the apparent contradiction from siman 104 seif 5 concerning movable property pledged together with land. There we treat the case as one who borrowed, borrowed again, and only then acquired the property, and the creditors divide equally. At first glance this contradicts every view here, because even the opinion that orders division here says that only the disputed half is divided, resulting in three quarters to one and one quarter to the other, whereas there they divide equally. The distinction is that land, even when after-acquired, is subject to a lien while in the debtor's possession. Indeed, after-acquired land that the debtor later leaves to heirs may be collected, as stated in siman 111 seif 20, and Tumim explains this in siman 104. Movable property is different. It bears no lien at all merely by remaining in the debtor's possession, because movable property of heirs is not encumbered. Beit din compels payment from movable property only because of the mitzvah to repay a debt. Thus, regarding movables, if they were not in existence at the time of the loan they were never encumbered, while if they were then in existence and expressly pledged together with land, they were encumbered. Tosafot in Ketubot 76 explain that a chazakah of possession is stronger than the present-state presumption, even stronger than a bodily chazakah absent a definite claim against a doubtful one. Since the dispute over the movables is whether they were ever encumbered, the present-state presumption cannot establish the lien against the opposing chazakah of possession. On the contrary, the debtor's chazakah leads us to assume that he acquired them after the loan and they were never encumbered. Sema seif katan 14 likewise relies on the chazakah of possession. We therefore do not proceed against those movables by virtue of a lien, but only by compelling fulfillment of the mitzvah to repay, and that mitzvah applies equally to both creditors. Here, however, regarding after-acquired land, even if the debtor acquired it after both loans it is nevertheless subject to both liens. There is no chazakah in favor of the debtor, because either way the land is encumbered. The only doubt is between the two creditors, namely to which lien it is primarily subject, and they are both considered in possession of that lien. The case therefore resembles one litigant saying, "It is all mine," and the other saying, "Half is mine," where the first receives three quarters and the second one quarter.
