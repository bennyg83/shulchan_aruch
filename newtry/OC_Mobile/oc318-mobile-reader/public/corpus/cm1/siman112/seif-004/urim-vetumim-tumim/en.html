Some say that they divide half, etc. The Bach asks that the first creditor should have a migo: he could have claimed that the debtor acquired the property between the two loans, in which case the entire property would be encumbered to him. The Shach answers that this resembles the Mishnah in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, where one person says, "It is all mine," and the other says, "Half is mine." The latter is not believed by a migo that he could also have claimed the whole garment. This does not suffice according to Tosafot's explanation there that the migo fails because it would be a migo used to extract property. In that Mishnah both parties are holding the garment, so each is considered in possession of his half. Here, by contrast, neither creditor is in possession of the land. Moreover, their rights are merely liens. There is not even a final absolute lien where no after-acquired-property clause was written, since the borrower could still sell the property. The first creditor therefore cannot readily be characterized as seeking to extract property from a possessor.
Therefore, one must use the Shach's first answer: if the property was acquired between the two loans, it is encumbered to the first creditor. See what I wrote at length above in siman 104. From the language of all the authorities, however, it appears that they understood the present case as one where each creditor makes a definite claim. One says the debtor acquired the property at one time, and the other says he acquired it at another. In my view that cannot be correct. If both claims are definite and the matter can potentially be clarified by witnesses who can establish when the land was acquired, why should the court order division? We should apply the rule below in siman 139 that where a factual doubt can be clarified, the law is kol d'alim gevar, whoever is stronger prevails until proof is brought. It is strained to answer that because beit din must effect collection, and without court collection neither creditor has a right to enter the borrower's land, kol d'alim gevar cannot apply. As I wrote above in siman 104, most authorities hold that formal court collection is not indispensable and prior seizure can be effective. Thus the mere fact that this is lien property does not explain why the ordinary rule for a clarifiable doubt should disappear. It is therefore clear that the present case concerns uncertain claims on both sides. Each creditor asserts his right only as a possibility, because neither knows when the debtor acquired the land. In a case of shema against shema, kol d'alim gevar does not apply. Its rationale is that the party who truly knows the right is his will exert himself more strongly to retain it, but that rationale does not exist where neither party knows. In such a case division is preferable according to everyone. See what I wrote below in siman 139. Once the case is shema against shema, there is no difficulty from migo, because a person who claims only uncertainty has no migo from a definite false claim. In practical law, however, the result stated by the Shach and in Urim remains correct. A further difficulty remains. Why does the Mechaber state this law specifically where no after-acquired-property clause was written? The same uncertainty can exist where both creditors have such clauses. According to the Sema's approach, one could say the Mechaber teaches a novelty. Without an after-acquired-property clause, one might have thought we should say perhaps the debtor acquired the property between the two loans and therefore only the later creditor has the relevant after-acquired lien because the first lacks such a clause. Where both have clauses, by contrast, acquisition between the loans would still favor the first creditor. But according to the Shach's correct analysis, this distinction does not explain the text. Perhaps the Mechaber holds that where both documents contain after-acquired-property clauses, our rule that one borrower, two creditors, and then acquisition leads to division is itself based on unresolved doubt: perhaps the newly acquired property is encumbered to the first creditor, or perhaps to the later one. Where no after-acquired-property clause was written, however, the reason the creditors divide later-acquired property is not a separate unresolved lien priority but simply the absence of any rule of priority between them. If so, where both documents contain the clause, the first creditor has a double doubt in his favor. Perhaps the debtor acquired the property before both loans; and even if he acquired it after both loans, perhaps the law is that later-acquired property is encumbered to the first creditor. Even authorities who reject using a double doubt in monetary law when one seeks to extract property from a possessor, as I wrote in the laws of kim li and seizure, may agree that where neither party is in possession a double doubt is effective, as Tosafot indicate. The first creditor would therefore prevail. That would explain why the Mechaber limits the present ruling to a case without an after-acquired-property clause. In that case, if the property was acquired after both loans, division follows as a matter of law, so there is only one remaining doubt, whether acquisition occurred before or after. This requires study. I am more inclined to the opposite explanation: these authorities do not distinguish one monetary doubt from two. A fundamental rule is that property subject to unresolved ownership or lien is divided, and we do not weigh the number of speculative pathways on each side. Accordingly, where no after-acquired-property clause was written, if the debtor certainly acquired the property after both loans, the law of division is itself certain. We therefore first set aside that definite half for the first creditor and that definite half for the second. Only the remaining issue, whether the property was actually acquired earlier, creates doubt. The first creditor consequently receives three quarters and the second one quarter. Where both creditors have after-acquired-property clauses, however, even if the debtor acquired the property after both loans, the very rule of division rests on an unresolved question whether the later property is encumbered to the first creditor or to the later one. The later creditor can therefore say: the entire field is within the unresolved question, because perhaps it was acquired later and the rule is that the later creditor receives it all. The fact that the first creditor has two possible routes to victory rather than one does not change the monetary status. The entire field remains within the house of doubt as to whether it belongs to the first or second creditor, and they therefore divide it equally. This explains a textual difference. Here the Mechaber cites two opinions, yet under both opinions the first creditor receives three quarters. Above in siman 104, seif 5, he writes that where it is unknown when the debtor acquired the property, we treat it as a case of first loan, second loan, and then acquisition, and the creditors divide. He does not say that the first creditor receives more. It is difficult to say his language there is merely negative and directed only to the rule that if the later creditor already collected he keeps what he collected, because the seif begins with prior collection. Even so, stating simply "they divide" would mislead readers if the true allocation were three quarters and one quarter. See what I wrote above in seif katan 5 regarding Rabbi Avraham in Mordechai, whose language likewise indicates equal division. According to the present explanation, there is no difficulty. Siman 104 concerns documents with after-acquired-property clauses, and in that case the first creditor has no greater share than the second. Here the Mechaber addresses the different case without such clauses, in which the first creditor indeed receives three quarters. This also explains why the Rama, who explicitly decides like the first opinion in seifim 2 and 3, does not decide between the opinions in the present seif. He does not append his usual conclusion that one view appears correct in law. This indicates that here he did not incline decisively to one side, as the Sema notes in seif katan 14. The Sema explains that the reason is that neither party is in possession of the field. But that is difficult. Above in siman 104 the Rama did not gloss upon the Mechaber's rule that we treat the property as later-acquired and divide it, which implies that he accepts it. Why there did he not cite the dissenting view based on "here it was found and here it was"? According to what I have written, the Rama's hesitation here is for a different reason. As I explained in siman 104, this case resembles the dispute of a safek and a yavam. Since the first creditor certainly receives one half under any scenario, his definite right to that half may give him superior standing even with respect to the remaining half that is doubtful. It is an application of the principle that a doubt does not remove a definite right. Therefore the Rama did not decide against the view giving the first creditor the larger share. This reasoning exists only where the first creditor certainly takes half. Above in siman 104, where no portion is definitely his and the entire field enters the unresolved lien question, that consideration disappears. The case returns to the general rule of doubt, and therefore the Rama agrees there that the creditors divide. Examine this carefully.
