In truth, Responsa Maharam provides no proof, because there the community did not then hold funds belonging to the official; rather, their practice was to advance money to him. Since they held nothing of his, the present principle did not apply. But Responsa Rosh, kelal 69, implies that a person does not become a guarantor merely because he holds the debtor's money, unless he expressly refers to that money and the undertaking occurs through ma'amad sheloshtan. The Ritva in Kiddushin likewise appears to prove this. He argues that the recipient in ma'amad sheloshtan may return to the original debtor, for otherwise why would a special rule of ma'amad sheloshtan be necessary without an apparent rationale? Once the recipient releases the first debtor, the second debtor should in any event become liable as a guarantor at the time of the transaction. Yet even if we say that the recipient may return to the first debtor and therefore did not release him, so that the second is not a guarantor at the time the money was advanced, the question remains. Ma'amad sheloshtan includes a case where the depositary actually holds the debtor's property. If merely holding the debtor's funds sufficed, the depositary should become liable as a guarantor even though the guaranty arose after the original advance. Tosafot likewise imply this. They ask, according to the opinion that ma'amad sheloshtan applies to a deposit, why the special rule is needed, since the owner could simply say, "Acquire it for him." They do not ask the same question regarding ma'amad sheloshtan of a loan on the ground that the new debtor should be a guarantor, even though Tosafot hold that the recipient may return to the original debtor. We must therefore say, contrary to the Rama's broad formulation, that merely holding the debtor's funds does not create guaranty. However, it is possible to distinguish. Mahari Weil's ruling may apply only where, at the very moment the person said, "I am guarantor," the debtor had no assets at all. In that situation, the funds in the prospective guarantor's hands were already subject to Rabbi Natan's lien and were therefore legally obligated to this creditor just as they were to their owner. The guaranty can then take effect as though it were made at the time the money was advanced, because the creditor could have collected those very funds from the holder, and refrained from doing so only because the holder said, "I am guarantor." This resembles a guaranty undertaken at the time of the advance and is therefore binding. By contrast, where the debtor then had other assets, the funds in the holder's possession were not subject to this creditor's lien. In such a case there is plainly no basis for guaranty merely because the holder possesses money of the debtor. Why should he become bound when he himself owes nothing to this creditor? What difference does it make that he happens to hold the debtor's money? This distinction resolves all the objections raised against the Rama, and it has sound reasoning. Nevertheless, Responsa Mahari Weil itself does not prove the Rama's general rule, and the Shach's distinction between those very coins and other money is unpersuasive. In a loan, which is given for expenditure, what difference is there between these coins and other coins? Rather, Mahari Weil's case concerned a wife who conducted the household's business, with all her husband's affairs and transactions carried out through her. She undertook the guaranty while the money was in her hands. She is therefore effectively regarded as holding it for her husband, because she acts as his agent. The present rule consequently requires study. But where Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply, there is no doubt that this ruling has no basis and does not apply at all.
