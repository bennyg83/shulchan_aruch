For we are concerned that he may have repaid him. Baer HaGolah cites the Maggid Mishneh, also brought by the Beit Yosef, who says that although this rule is not explicit in the Gemara it is straightforward, and that even from a kablan one may not collect without clear proof of nonpayment. The Bach disputes this with respect to a kablan. In his view the Rambam said this only regarding an ordinary guarantor. An ordinary guarantor's liability arises only if the borrower did not pay, so where he does not know whether the borrower paid, his position is like 'I do not know whether I owe you.' A kablan, by contrast, is obligated immediately and is released only if the borrower later paid. His uncertainty is therefore like 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' which ordinarily obligates payment. The Shach below, however, sides with the Maggid Mishneh against the Bach. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 42, discusses a case where the lender sued the borrower and guarantor at the due date. The borrower admitted nonpayment and was adjudged liable, but because he had no assets the guarantor was ordered to pay. Later the lender sued the guarantor, who said: perhaps the borrower paid you afterward, and the borrower is now absent. VeShav HaKohen questions the Bach's rationale. Once the guaranty was certainly undertaken, perhaps uncertainty whether the borrower later paid is itself 'I do not know whether I repaid you.' More fundamentally, even if it resembled uncertainty whether one ever became liable, the guarantor should be exempt for a different reason: a guarantor is liable only where the borrower remains liable. If the borrower can legally say 'I paid,' the guarantor too is exempt. This reason is explicit in Maharam as cited by the Mordechai and is also given by the Gra. A practical difference concerns seizure: according to the Bach's formulation, if the lender seized property of the guarantor without witnesses he could retain it; according to the latter reason, he could not, because he has no enforceable claim against the borrower and therefore none against the guarantor. In the actual case, however, VeShav HaKohen rules that the guarantor must pay. Once the guarantor had already stood in beit din and was ordered to pay because the borrower had admitted the debt and lacked assets, his liability became definite. He need not require the lender to sue the borrower again. Thus his later statement, 'Perhaps the borrower paid you afterward,' is like 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' and he is liable according to all opinions.
And he is exempt by virtue of a migo. See Baer Heitev, which notes that the Shach approves the objection and that Maharshal also disagrees. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 16, concludes that it is difficult to extract money from the present possessor. This applies where the guarantor accepted liability for unavoidable accidents on the same level as the borrower. In that case the borrower may be discharged once the property is transferred to the guarantor, just as one who delivers it to a person with whom the owner regularly deposits may be discharged. If the guarantor is only an unpaid or paid custodian and the property is lost through an unavoidable accident, so that the guarantor himself is exempt, there is no basis to exempt the borrower.
