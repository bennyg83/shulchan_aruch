Even if he did not formally become guarantor, etc., because he is reliable, etc. The Shach, seif katan 7, cites Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter HaGozel, siman 24, who rules that the adviser is exempt unless the lender said, 'See, I am relying on you.' Even according to the view below in siman 306, seif 4, that one who shows a coin to a money changer need not say, 'I am relying on you,' Yam Shel Shlomo holds that here the adviser is exempt unless those words were said, because the lender was not compelled to act on the advice. Responsa Mahari Weil writes that the case is no worse than showing a coin to a money changer. The Shach questions the Rama, because the Rama cites Mahari Weil here, but below in siman 306 rules that with a money changer one must say, 'I am relying on you.' If so, he should have required the same words here. See Tumim, where I wrote that this depends on why those words are required. If the reason is, as the Shiltei Giborim writes, that ordinarily a person shows the coin to several people, then here the facts themselves may show reliance. If the reason is like the Rosh in Bava Kamma, and also the Baal HaMaor and Ramban, that the adviser is liable when the party is present and sees that the other will rely on him, then in Mahari Weil's case that is exactly what happened and there is no difficulty. The Rama's ruling is also correct, because the adviser knows that the lender will lend only on the strength of his statement, and the act has not yet been done, so there is no need for the express words, 'I rely on you.' See there. I further concluded that even Rabbeinu Ephraim, who holds regarding a purchase that an adviser is exempt because the questioner was not obligated to follow his advice and the adviser can say, 'Who asked this of you?', agrees here with the Rama, because the lender acted solely on his statement and the adviser spoke before the loan was made. This is contrary to the Shach, who understood Rabbeinu Ephraim like Yam Shel Shlomo. Nevertheless, according to Rabbeinu Ephraim, perhaps the lender must expressly say, 'I rely on you.' The Rama, however, does not follow Rabbeinu Ephraim, whose position is compelled only by a difficulty that does not arise according to the Rama's approach. See there. I also gave another answer in Tumim to Rabbeinu Ephraim's difficulty and proved clearly that the precise wording, 'because he is reliable,' is necessary. See there for the reasoning.
He is obligated to pay, etc. He should not have advised him in that manner, because no person can truly know what property another has. Even a father concerning his son, or a son concerning his father, may see silver, gold, and money and yet not know whether it belongs to him or is encumbered to others. However, where the adviser was his brother and knew that a substantial inheritance had fallen to them and that he had no prior debts, but afterward an unforeseen cause or loss occurred that stripped him of his property and the adviser did not know of it, this is considered unavoidable accident and the adviser is exempt, even if the lender said, 'See, I rely on you.' He need not even take a hesset oath that he did not know, since the claim against him is only uncertain. So writes the Maharshal, cited by the Shach. Certainly, if the debtor was reliable at the time and later deteriorated financially, the adviser is exempt. The Sema writes likewise. See Shiltei Giborim at the end of Bava Batra, who discusses these laws at length.
