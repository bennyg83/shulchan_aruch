The Shach in seif katan 8 objects that this entire rule is derived from the law of one who shows a coin to a money changer, yet there the Rama himself rules below in siman 306 that liability requires the customer to say specifically, "I rely upon you." In truth, if the reason such language is required in the coin case is as the Shiltei Giborim writes in Bava Kamma, namely that the money changer may assume that the customer will show the coin to others as well and will not rely solely on his word, then the same requirement would apply here. The lender would have to say expressly, "I rely upon you." But according to the Rosh, who rules in Bava Kamma that one need not say "I rely upon you," because ordinarily one who shows a coin does so in order to know whether he may release the person who gave it to him, the Shiltei Giborim's rationale cannot be accepted. If the payer had already been released, why would that establish that the recipient will not also show the coin to others? Rather, according to the Rosh we do not assume that after showing it to this expert he will continue showing it to others, for otherwise each expert could deflect responsibility to another and the customer would ultimately bear the loss. The reason some authorities nevertheless require the phrase "I rely upon you" is different. The money changer may claim: I thought you had already released the person who gave you the coin, and therefore I had no reason to examine it carefully or disparage your transaction, just as one does not ordinarily emphasize to a buyer that his purchase was poor. The phrase "I rely upon you" informs the expert that the transaction has not yet been finalized. The Rosh, however, holds that such express language is unnecessary, because ordinarily the customer has not yet released the payer; otherwise, why is he asking for an examination at all? It appears that the Rama understood that the authorities do not disagree with the Shiltei Giborim's concern as a general matter. Rather, those who require the phrase "I rely upon you" hold that even after releasing the payer, a person may still ask an expert whether the transaction was advantageous or disadvantageous. It is natural for people to want to know, perhaps so that others will not deceive them in the future, or so that they themselves will not be suspected of fraudulently circulating counterfeit coins. This also appears to be the view of the Baal HaMaor and the Ramban in Milchamot, who agree that if the money changer was present when the sale occurred and the buyer accepted the coin, it is as though the buyer said, "I rely upon you." If the Shiltei Giborim's rationale were decisive, why would the expert's presence matter? He could still think that the buyer relied on his own expertise or had previously shown the coin to someone else. Surely the Baal HaMaor and Ramban do not require that the expert remain physically attached to the buyer all day. It is therefore clear that they follow the Rosh's rationale: absent the statement "I rely upon you," the expert may think the transaction has already been completed and thus has no reason to scrutinize carefully. If he is present and sees that the transaction has not yet been completed, he is liable. This is clear. Accordingly, in the incident of Mahari Weil from which the present ruling originates, where the adviser himself was present when the lender accepted the borrower and told him, "He is reliable," all authorities would certainly impose liability, as the Baal HaMaor writes and the Ramban agrees. Indeed, even in the Rama's formulation, where one tells another to lend to a borrower because "he is reliable," the adviser knows that the loan has not yet been made, because he is instructing him to make it. This is equivalent to saying, "I rely upon you," and therefore all agree that he is liable. For this reason, even Rabbenu Ephraim, who holds that where the question concerns a transaction the questioner was not obligated to undertake, the adviser is exempt and may say, "Who asked you to rely upon me?", would agree in such a case, because it is equivalent to an express statement of reliance once the adviser knows that the loan has not yet been made. Still, this point requires study, because according to Rabbenu Ephraim perhaps the lender must state explicitly, "I rely upon you." In truth, however, the Rama does not follow Rabbenu Ephraim. Rabbenu Ephraim made his distinction according to the authorities who do not require the phrase "I rely upon you." He was troubled by the passage in Bava Batra that even if the claimant tells the purchaser, "Go buy it," he may nevertheless later challenge the purchase and say that the second person is more convenient for him. He therefore needed his distinction. But according to the accepted ruling that liability requires "I rely upon you," there is no difficulty from Bava Batra, because no such statement was made there. Even according to what I wrote, that where the adviser knows the transaction has not yet occurred it is equivalent to such a statement, one may answer that in Bava Batra the claimant can say: I did not know that you had not yet purchased it; I thought you had already bought it, and therefore I had no reason to reveal my claim to you. It further appears that liability applies specifically when the adviser says, "Lend to him, because he is reliable." If he merely says, "Lend to him," without vouching for his reliability, he is not liable. Otherwise, why was it necessary above in siman 126, in the law of ma'amad sheloshtan where the depositary was poor and the parties acted deceitfully, to derive the transferor's liability from the Yerushalmi? According to the Baal HaTerumot's presentation of the Ramban's view, even where the transferor knew explicitly that the new debtor was poor and misled the recipient, he is exempt because the recipient should have investigated. Yet if mere instruction to accept a person as debtor imposed liability, then regardless of that discussion, the recipient took this person as his debtor on the transferor's word. Through ma'amad sheloshtan the first debtor is discharged and the second enters in his place, which is effectively like lending to him. Why then should the first not be liable for the second's solvency? We must conclude that because the transferor merely assigned the debt and did not say, "He is reliable," he is exempt, since the recipient should investigate for himself, as the Ramban writes. But when he expressly says, "He is reliable," the recipient may respond: why should I investigate? I assumed you would not lie, for the remnant of Israel does not speak falsehood. This also resolves Rabbenu Ephraim's objection from Bava Batra, where the purchaser consulted the claimant and the latter said, "Go buy it." There he did not say, "This purchase is secure," nor was the consultation necessarily about the seller's solvency; he may merely have been asked whether the field was good or the like. Regarding such a statement, no authority would impose liability. One must say specifically that the borrower is reliable. There is therefore no difficulty.
