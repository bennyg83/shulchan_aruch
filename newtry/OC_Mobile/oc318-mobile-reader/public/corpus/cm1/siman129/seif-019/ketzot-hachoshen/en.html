The rule says that the creditor may proceed unless the kablan guarantor waived the borrower's debt. The Shach writes that under the law of Rabbi Natan's lien, the kablan should be unable to waive the borrower's obligation, just as an intermediate creditor cannot waive a debt already subjected to his own creditor. Tumim answers that the borrower does not become obligated to reimburse an ordinary kablan until the kablan actually pays the creditor. Before payment, the borrower still owes the original creditor. After the kablan pays, the original creditor's lien has ended, so Rabbi Natan's lien cannot operate. Tumim therefore constructs the creditor's claim as though the creditor converts the kablan's obligation into a new loan: "The obligation you owe me as kablan I treat as received, and I thereby lend you a new amount." If the kablan had already waived the borrower's liability before that conversion, the creditor cannot later reach the borrower. With all the praise given to this explanation, it confuses two kinds of kablan. In the ordinary case, where both borrower and kablan are liable to the creditor, the borrower indeed need not reimburse the kablan before the kablan pays, because the borrower's debt still runs directly to the creditor. Here, however, the case is one in which the kablan himself took the money from the creditor and then handed it to the borrower. The creditor has no direct claim against the borrower except through Rabbi Natan. The kablan owes the creditor, while the borrower owes the kablan. The borrower's debt to the kablan therefore exists immediately, regardless of whether the kablan has already paid the creditor. The borrower took the money from the kablan as a loan and must repay him. Since the creditor is owed by the kablan and the borrower is owed to the kablan, this is the classic case of Rabbi Natan's lien. The kablan cannot waive the borrower's debt to defeat the creditor. This is straightforward.
