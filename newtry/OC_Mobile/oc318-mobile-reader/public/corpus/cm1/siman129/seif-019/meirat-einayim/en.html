And he may sue only the absolute guarantor. Although the absolute guarantor will afterward seek reimbursement from the borrower, the Sages nevertheless enacted that the creditor should not initially deal directly with the borrower. Perhaps the borrower will have no unencumbered property and the creditor will seek to collect from property sold by the borrower. By law he cannot collect from the borrower's encumbered property until the absolute guarantor who received and delivered the money has sworn that he has nothing. Therefore he must first proceed against the absolute guarantor. This is what the Mechaber writes below, at the end of seif 21: regarding an absolute guarantor, etc., unless the absolute guarantor has no property. Meaning, the creditor already sued the absolute guarantor and he swore that he has nothing; only then does the creditor turn to the borrower's purchasers. Likewise, what the Mechaber writes here, that if the absolute guarantor has nothing the creditor collects from the borrower under Rabbi Natan's rule, is not literal. Rather, after the absolute guarantor swears that he has nothing, the creditor collects from the borrower and from his unencumbered property, and if he has no unencumbered property, from property the borrower sold, in the manner explained in siman 130.
Unless the absolute guarantor waived the debt to the borrower. Wherever it is not yet firmly known that the borrower has no property, Rabbi Natan's rule has not yet taken effect against the person indebted to him, and the absolute guarantor can waive that debt, as explained in siman 86, seifim 2 and 5; see there.
Some say the same applies to a guarantor. Meaning, the same rule applies to an ordinary guarantor who received and delivered the money: the creditor has no direct claim against the borrower.
