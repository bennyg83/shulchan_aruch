The Shach's suggestion that these authorities hold that even under Rabbi Natan's rule he may forgive is itself difficult for the Shulchan Aruch, which ruled above that he cannot forgive.
Therefore it appears that the language of Baal HaTerumot and Maggid Mishneh requires independent explanation. They cite Rabbenu Tam and Ramban for the proposition that the kablan may recover from the borrower by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule. But Rabbi Natan's rule itself is explicit in the Gemara: one extracts from this debtor and gives to that creditor. Why should this borrower differ from any other debtor? It must be because precisely such a case requires the authority of these Rishonim. One could argue that the borrower does not become obligated to reimburse the kablan until the creditor has actually proceeded against the kablan under Rabbi Natan's rule. Before the kablan pays the creditor, the borrower is not yet obligated to pay the kablan, for all the authorities state that until the guarantor pays, the borrower owes him nothing. Once the kablan pays the creditor, however, the creditor's lien has already terminated, so how can the creditor then proceed against the borrower under Rabbi Natan's rule? The Rishonim therefore teach that even before the kablan has paid, where the kablan has no assets, Rabbi Natan's rule may be invoked against the borrower. Why is that so? The reason appears to be that we instruct the creditor to say to the kablan: The debt that you owe me by reason of your kablanut I regard as though I have received it. It is now fixed upon you as a distinct loan, as though I lent you one hundred, and the kablanut debt has been satisfied. The borrower is therefore obligated to pay you, and I proceed against him under Rabbi Natan's rule, because you now owe me under this newly fixed loan. This is simple and fitting. According to this, the Rabbi Natan lien upon the borrower's payment arises only at the time this new loan is fixed, not earlier. Before that point, as explained, the borrower was not obligated to pay the kablan. At the moment of the fixing, the original kablanut obligation is treated as discharged, since it is as though the creditor received payment, and a new obligation arises from the loan fixation. Therefore, if the kablan had previously forgiven the borrower, his forgiveness preceded the creditor's Rabbi Natan lien. The rule that a debt subject to Rabbi Natan's lien cannot be forgiven does not apply. Thus, in either direction, the creditor cannot recover if the kablan already forgave the borrower. If the creditor proceeds on the original kablanut obligation, the borrower may answer: so long as the kablan has not paid, I owe him nothing. If the creditor proceeds on the basis of the new loan fixation, treating the kablan as having paid, then the kablan's prior forgiveness preceded that lien. Examine this carefully.
