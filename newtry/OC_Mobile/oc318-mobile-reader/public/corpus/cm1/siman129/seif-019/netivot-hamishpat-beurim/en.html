The kablan took the money from the creditor. It appears that this applies only where the kablan took the money in his own name, or took it without qualification from the creditor and then gave it to the borrower. If he came to the creditor as the borrower's agent and said, 'The borrower sent me to you so that you should give him the money,' he is not considered one who received and delivered the money personally as kablan. A person's agent is legally like the person himself, so it is as though the borrower received the loan directly. Likewise, if the creditor gave the money to the kablan merely as an agent to carry it to the borrower, he is not treated as one who personally received and delivered it, for the same reason. It further appears that if he was kablan for a sale on credit, and the buyer had already acquired the movable goods by kinyan sudar, then the kablan went and took the goods from the seller, even without qualification, and delivered them to the buyer, he is not treated as one who personally received and delivered the creditor's property. The special rule applies to loan money because the lender may still retract from the loan and the particular coins have not yet been acquired by the borrower. The kablan who takes them is therefore taking the lender's money. But where the buyer has already acquired the goods and can take them against the seller's will because the acquisition was completed in reliance upon the kablan's undertaking, the kablan is not taking the seller's property. He is taking property that already belongs to the buyer and handing it to him. This is clear.
Unless the kablan waived it. See Shach, seif katan 38, who concludes that the kablan cannot waive the debt. Tumim writes that since the law is that the kablan cannot claim from the borrower until after he has paid on the borrower's behalf, the borrower's obligation to the kablan does not take effect before such payment. If the kablan waived before paying, his waiver is therefore effective. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that this applies only to an ordinary kablan, but where the kablan personally received and delivered the money, the borrower is a full borrower from the kablan. In my view this is incorrect. Even where the kablan personally received and delivered the money, the borrower becomes obligated to him only when the kablan pays on his behalf. The borrower's obligation began only because the kablan exposed himself to loss for this loan. The borrower never told the guarantor, 'Borrow for yourself and lend the money to me.' He merely asked him to be guarantor or kablan for him. As against the borrower, the guarantor therefore has only the law of one who has paid the lender on the borrower's behalf. The special rule that a kablan who personally received and delivered the money has the law of a borrower applies only as against the creditor. Even if the borrower never instructed him and he voluntarily received and delivered the money, he has the law of a borrower toward the creditor. No posek distinguishes, for the law of siman 130, between an ordinary kablan and one who personally received and delivered the money. If the creditor were a convert who died without heirs, thereby releasing the kablan, the borrower would likewise be released from the kablan, because the borrower became liable only so that the kablan should not suffer a loss because of this loan. Although the obligation is certainly comparable to a condition that applies from now, since the kablan can collect from encumbered property dating from the time of the original loan, a condition that applies 'from now' begins at once but is completed only later, as Sema writes in siman 155. Therefore, if the kablan waives before the obligation is completed, the lien of Rabbi Natan has not yet taken effect and the waiver is effective. Nevertheless, one should not depart from Shach's ruling.
