To notify him. The beit din notifies the borrower, while the creditor advances the messenger's fee and later collects it from the borrower or guarantor, similar to siman 106 seif 1 concerning collection from a borrower's property in his absence. The Sema divides the law into three cases. Beit din does not send notice with a thirty-day period unless the guarantor asks them to notify the borrower in the hope that he will pay himself. On their own initiative, beit din sends only where the messenger can go and return within three days. This is the correct reading; some editions of the Sema contain an error. See Derishah. This is where the borrower's location is known and the messenger can notify him within thirty days. If he cannot, or his location is unknown, beit din need not send at all. If the guarantor wishes to search for him and requests time, he is given whatever time is reasonably necessary. The Shach writes that the correct division is different: if the borrower's location is known and he is within thirty days, the creditor must trouble himself to notify him; if his location is unknown, the guarantor must make the effort and is given thirty days; if it is known that he is farther away than thirty days, the guarantor is given the time reasonably needed. This is also evident from the Tur and resolves the apparent conflicting implications. If the borrower was present in the city when payment became due and the creditor allowed him to leave the city, the creditor may not proceed against the guarantor. Mabit, part I, siman 220. See Responsa Radbaz, part II, siman 41. Knesset HaGedolah.

Rabbeinu Yerucham distinguishes between a borrower who was already violent and beyond ordinary judicial control before the loan, in which case we say that the creditor lent with knowledge of that condition, and one who became so later. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite Rashba as rejecting the distinction, because the creditor may say that from the outset he lent only in reliance on the guarantor. Even where the guarantee was undertaken after the money was given, we see that the creditor was not satisfied until this person became guarantor through a kinyan. The Sema nevertheless concludes that Rabbeinu Yerucham's view is primary, for otherwise the creditor should not have accepted an ordinary guarantor but should have required a kablan. Bach rules, however, that if beit din has already attempted coercion and the violent borrower persists, or he is close to the government and coercion risks serious harm, all agree that collection may be made from the guarantor.
And once he brings him. If the borrower fled from beit din without paying and without swearing that he has no means, the creditor cannot collect from the guarantor until he first pursues the borrower and has him swear. Therefore, if the guarantor brought him to beit din on Friday afternoon, when the court could not adjudicate the matter and administer the oath, it is treated as though he had not brought him to beit din at all, and the guarantor must bring him again. This is clear and is also stated in Darkei Moshe. Sema.
