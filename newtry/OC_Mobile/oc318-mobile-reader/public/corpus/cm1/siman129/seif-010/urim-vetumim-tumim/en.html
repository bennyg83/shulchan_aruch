Within thirty days, etc. See Sema seif katan 29, who divides the law into three cases. If beit din sends on its own initiative, it does so only where the borrower can arrive within three days. If the guarantor asks that a messenger be sent, the period is the time needed for the messenger to go and return within thirty days. If the guarantor himself wishes to pursue the borrower, beit din allows such time as it deems necessary for the journey there and back. With slight changes, this is his formulation. The latter two distinctions are indeed correct and accord with the Tur, as the Bach also writes. But the distinction that where beit din sends of its own initiative the time is three days, whereas at the guarantor's request it is thirty days, is strained. Obviously the entire sending is at the guarantor's request, since he refuses to pay until they hear what the borrower has to say. The Sema's principal proof is the question raised in the Derishah: how can the Tur fix the period at thirty days when the Rosh at the end of Bava Batra writes that if the borrower is not present, they notify him and wait for him according to the rule stated in HaGozel, where the period is only three days, namely that the messenger leaves on Monday and returns on Thursday? To resolve this, the Sema distinguishes between a request by the guarantor and a sending initiated by beit din. Apart from the fact that this distinction itself is difficult, it still leaves the Tur problematic. Why did he omit the law, cited by his father the Rosh, of beit din sending on its own initiative, yet mention a thirty day period when the guarantor requests it? Moreover, where does the Tur indicate that his case is specifically at the guarantor's request? He writes simply that if the borrower is not before us, and if he is near enough that the messenger can go and return within thirty days, without mentioning such a condition. It therefore appears that this distinction is incorrect. In siman 106, concerning collection in the debtor's absence, the Tur writes in the name of his father the Rosh that if the debtor can be notified, they notify him, and the period is thirty days. The Rosh writes this explicitly in chapter HaKotev and in responsum 73. Yet in siman 114, concerning collection from purchasers, the Tur writes that if the debtor can be notified, they notify him, which means the ordinary period of three days. We must therefore distinguish between collection in the debtor's absence, which requires more time, and collection from purchasers. As the Perishah himself writes explicitly, the purchaser stands in the debtor's place and is the creditor's litigant. Now, since it is clear that collection in the debtor's absence requires thirty days, note that Bava Batra 174 states regarding a judge who allowed a creditor to descend to the debtor's property before first demanding payment from the debtor, that Rava praised the ruling because a person's property serves as his guarantors, and the Mishnah teaches that one does not first demand from a guarantor. Thus, the restriction on collection in the debtor's absence derives from the rule that one does not first demand from a guarantor. If the period there is thirty days, as the Rosh and Tur state explicitly, then all the more so with an actual guarantor, for that law itself is learned from guaranty. It is therefore clear that the Rosh's reference to HaGozel is a scribal error and should read HaKotev, where the Rosh fixes thirty days for collection in the debtor's absence. This is plain and certain. The Derishah's further proof from Rabbenu Yerucham, who gives only three days, is no proof, for Rabbenu Yerucham likewise gives only three days for collection in the debtor's absence, as the Beit Yosef cites him above in siman 106. Since we rule that thirty days are required there, the same applies here. This is simple, and we should not invent distinctions unknown to the early authorities.
If he is a violent man. The Sema writes that according to Rabbenu Yerucham, if the borrower was already known to be violent before the loan, the creditor cannot proceed first against the guarantor. The Rashba disagrees, and the Sema inclines to Rabbenu Yerucham. There is some proof for the Rashba from the Gemara's initial attempt to derive guarantor liability from the verse, I will guarantee him, from my hand you shall demand him. The Beit Yosef cites this verse. Tosafot Yom Tov wondered at it because Rav Chisda challenges that verse as describing a kablan, since Yehudah said, Place him in my hand. But there is no difficulty. Rav Chisda follows his own view later in the Gemara that all such formulations are terms of kablanut, including I will guarantee him when preceded by Place him in my hand. According to Rava, however, whom we follow, all formulations are ordinary guaranty except Give him and I will give, so I will guarantee him remains language of guaranty even though he said Place him in my hand. Thus the question disappears. Yet how could Yaakov first demand from the ruler of Egypt, and what benefit would Yehudah's guaranty provide? Necessarily, because the ruler was violent, Yaakov could immediately demand from the guarantor. This proves that even when the guarantor knew from the outset that the borrower was violent, collection may be made from him. The principal ruling is therefore like the Rashba, Darkei Moshe, and Beit Yosef.
