He must notify him, etc., within thirty days. In the Perishah and Derishah I wrote that beit din sends notice to the borrower, the creditor initially pays the messenger's expenses, and afterward recovers those expenses from the borrower or guarantor, similar to the rule in siman 106:1 concerning collection from a debtor's property in his absence. I further explained that the law divides into three situations. Beit din is not required to allow a full thirty days merely to send notice unless the guarantor asks beit din to send to the borrower in the hope that he will pay himself. On their own initiative, beit din sends only where the messenger can go, notify him, and return within a short period specified by the authorities, meaning where the borrower's location is known. But if he cannot be reached within that period, or his location is unknown, beit din need not send at all. If the guarantor himself wishes to make the effort to locate him and asks time for that purpose, he is given whatever reasonable time is required, as Rama states immediately afterward. Or if he is a violent person. Rabbeinu Yerucham distinguishes that if the borrower was already known to be violent before the loan was made, we say that the lender knowingly lent on that basis. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite the Rashba as rejecting this distinction, because the lender may say: I lent him only in reliance upon the guarantor. Even where the guarantor entered only after the loan, we see that the lender did not feel secure until this person became guarantor through a kinyan. In my view, however, Rabbeinu Yerucham's position is primary. If the lender wanted to rely on the guarantor despite knowing the borrower's violence, he should have required an absolute guarantor rather than an ordinary guarantor.

And once he brings him before beit din, the guarantor is discharged. Meaning that if the borrower then flees from beit din without paying and without swearing that he has no means to pay, the creditor cannot collect from the guarantor until he again pursues the borrower and causes him to take that oath. Therefore Rama continues that if the guarantor brought the borrower on Friday afternoon, when beit din could not adjudicate and administer the oath, it is as though he had not properly brought him before beit din. The responsibility remains upon the guarantor to bring the borrower back at a time when the case can be heard. This is clear and Darkei Moshe writes likewise, contrary to the implication of the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef.
