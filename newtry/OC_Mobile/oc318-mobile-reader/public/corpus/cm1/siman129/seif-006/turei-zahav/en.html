(Seif 8) And the guarantor must pay. According to Sema, seif katan 25, the reason is that guaranty itself is not treated as an asmachta. This is difficult in comparison with two people bearing the same name, Yosef ben Shimon, in siman 49, which Sema himself cites. There too neither of the two is individually obligated. It appears that the rule removing asmachta from guaranty applies only where the underlying obligation is valid. The benefit the guarantor receives from the lender's reliance binds him despite the contingent nature of guaranty. If the underlying obligation itself is an asmachta, however, there is no enforceable debt and the lender never truly relied on the guarantor. Rashba's case therefore must be understood differently. The guarantor did not guarantee the asmachta itself. Rather, borrower and lender arranged that if the borrower failed to return the object he would pay a fixed sum, and the guarantor guaranteed only that monetary obligation. The question's wording, and Rama's formulation that “he furnished a guarantor for the money,” show that the borrower undertook to pay the object's value at a fixed date and the guarantor guaranteed that payment. Before then he remained only a borrower. Rashba also writes that an acquisition was made from the guarantor. Thus the guaranty itself was an absolute obligation, not conditional, and was not an asmachta. One cannot say that the borrower's own undertaking also ceased to be asmachta merely because of an acquisition, since only an acquisition before an eminent beit din removes asmachta.
