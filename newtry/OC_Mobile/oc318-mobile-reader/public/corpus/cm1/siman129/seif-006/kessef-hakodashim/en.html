Property in a person's possession is presumed his. Although we ordinarily presume that what a person holds belongs to him, and in the case of a householder with another person living in his home the law depends on possession as explained in siman 138, there is room for doubt regarding a son supported at his father's table. Even though clothing dyed specifically for the son, even a minor, may belong to the son, the general presumption may still favor the father. Perhaps one can presume that the father authorized the son to sell, similar to the presumption that a husband authorized his wife's garments to be pledged. But when the father is absent this may not apply. Similarly, just as livestock that walks on its own does not create an ordinary presumption of ownership, grain found at a mill does not necessarily belong to the mill owner, because many people deposit grain there awaiting milling. A grain warehouse that is ordinarily rented out may nevertheless support a prior-owner presumption, since goods are placed there only through a rental arrangement. A mill is different, because grain may be left there simply in anticipation of being milled. Even if several people are partners in the mill, a partner can acquire property from an outsider through the mill premises, because a jointly owned courtyard can acquire for a partner from a third party and the mill is a guarded courtyard on their behalf. If grain was sold there on terms that the buyer could not remove any quantity before paying for what he took, perhaps the entire quantity serves as collateral for the mill partner who sold his own grain. This matters regarding a creditor's right to collect from the grain where there is no custom giving the seller priority for the goods themselves. As for liability for the grain, however, perhaps the seller bears none, since it was merely left there in the customary manner and he did not accept guardianship. Still, because it thereby secures him, there is a substantial basis to regard it as a pledge.
