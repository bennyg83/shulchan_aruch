But if the borrower said, "I did not borrow." See Shach, seif katan 19, who writes cryptically that the Beit Yosef refers to the borrower and says, "and see my work." His work is not available to us. See Nachalat Tzvi for what appears to me to be the Shach's intent. See the Bach, who challenges this ruling from the sugya in the chapter Yesh Bekhor: can there be a case where the borrower is exempt and the guarantor liable? He answers that this principle applies only where the borrower is exempt as a matter of law. If the borrower is legally liable but the lender cannot collect from him, for example because the borrower falsely denies the loan and swears falsely while the guarantor admits the loan, the guarantor is plainly liable. He is no better situated than where the borrower is overseas or is a violent person from whom collection is impossible. See there. The Bach's words require study. In the sugya in Yesh Bekhor and in the Shulchan Aruch above at the end of siman 49, concerning two people with the same name where one bought a field from the other, the lender cannot collect from the purchaser because he cannot collect from the borrower. Yet the borrower's legal exemption there likewise arises because he denies being the borrower, and the purchaser of the field knows that he is lying. Even so, the purchaser is exempt. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 44, also expresses surprise at the Bach and discusses the matter at length. According to the explanation of Tumim here and at the end of siman 49, one might perhaps reconcile the Bach, but VeShav HaKohen there demonstrates difficulties in Tumim's interpretation of the sugya. He concludes that this gloss of the Rama requires very great study for practical halachah. With a kablan, however, in such a case it appears that he is liable. See there at length.
