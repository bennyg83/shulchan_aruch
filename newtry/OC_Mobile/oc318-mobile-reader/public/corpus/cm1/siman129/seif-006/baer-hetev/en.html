Where a kinyan was made. The Sema writes: From here I brought proof that the Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and the Tur also agree that where a kinyan was made from the borrower, the creditor may collect from encumbered property even if no document was written at all, provided the witnesses to the kinyan are before us and remember the time of the kinyan. Their dispute here concerns only a guarantor. See above, the beginning of siman 39, and siman 43 seif 16, and see there. See also siman 39 seif 4 concerning the rule that omission of a clause of liability is a scribal error in the case of a guarantor. Even according to one who holds that a kablan requires a kinyan, if the document states, “We accepted upon ourselves with the full force of all properly executed documents of lien and kablanut, in accordance with the enactment of the Sages,” and contains other expressions strengthening the obligation, no additional kinyan is required. Maharam Mintz, responsum 33. Knesset HaGedolah.
Like a document. The Sema explains that it is treated like a document accompanied by a kinyan, since a guarantor who undertakes before a beit din does not require a kinyan. Maharam Mintz, responsum 33, writes that because documents customarily state, “I acknowledge as though this were done before an eminent beit din, not as an asmachta,” it is regarded as though the document was executed before beit din, and a guarantor before beit din does not require a kinyan. Even if this clause was omitted, since it is customary to write it, it is regarded as written. Knesset HaGedolah objects: Rambam's rule that a guarantor before beit din becomes obligated refers to one who undertook the guarantee to the beit din itself. If he merely became a guarantor in the presence of beit din, but not to them, he requires a kinyan. Thus it does not help to deem the document executed before beit din. Perhaps Maharam Mintz understood the clause to mean that he undertook the guarantee to them, but this too is difficult, because the reason no kinyan is required when he actually undertakes to beit din is the benefit that beit din trusted him, and that benefit is absent when the document merely says it is as though done before beit din. Such language is mere embellishment and does not create the obligation. It is also strained to say that a litigant's admission is effective as though he admitted before beit din. The responsa of Radbaz, part I, responsum 218, were later printed, and there I found this same position.
