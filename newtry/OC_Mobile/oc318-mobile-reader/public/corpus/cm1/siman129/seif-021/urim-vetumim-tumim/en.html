They cannot redirect him, etc. See Knesset HaGedolah and Gidulei Terumah, who write that in responsum 848 the Rashba inclines to the view that where there is a guarantor and the borrower has property already sold and encumbered to the creditor, those purchasers may direct the creditor to the guarantor. In responsum 692, however, concerning a creditor who bound himself in connection with a borrower's sale of his field, cited by the Rama above in seif 8, the Rashba ruled that even where the borrower has only encumbered property, the creditor does not collect from the guarantor. Knesset HaGedolah and Gidulei Terumah suggest that the Rashba retracted because of the difficulty from Gittin, where the Gemara says that the borrower's assets were destroyed rather than simply saying that he had assets and sold them. I do not understand that difficulty. Tosafot already challenge Rashi there because the case concerns a kablan, and the Rashba himself in his novellae raises the same point and concludes like Tosafot that if the borrower has assets, even a kablan is collected from only from inferior land, just as from the heir himself. See there. That issue therefore has nothing to do with our question whether, where the borrower has sold encumbered assets, the creditor must pursue those purchasers or may collect from the guarantor. A kablan can certainly be collected from at the creditor's choice. The issue there is only whether the collection is from inferior land. If the Rashba followed Rashi's understanding that even a kablan cannot be collected from when the borrower has assets, that would correspond to the rejected view that a kablan is collectible only if the creditor expressly said From whomever I wish I may collect. Ramban rejected that view, and the decisors do not mention it. No one paid heed to it. I would have suggested that there is a scribal error in the Rashba's responsum and that his proof is from Get Pashut in Bava Batra, not from Gittin, and that a printer mistakenly supplied a tractate reference. There too the Gemara asks, This works according to the one who says, and the plain sense is that the borrower has no assets, as stated, and his circumstances are difficult. One might then ask why the Gemara did not answer that he had assets and sold them. From this the Rashba could have inferred that if so the father would be exempt, because where the borrower has assets the creditor cannot turn to the guarantor. Yet even this is difficult because the case is one of a kablan, and from a kablan the creditor may certainly collect even where the borrower has assets. Thus the question remains why the Gemara did not answer that he had assets and sold them. We must say, as Gidulei Terumah suggests, that the Gemara knew the facts of the incident, namely that the assets were destroyed. There is therefore no proof. One might alternatively say that the Rashba understood that the Gemara did not establish the case as one where the borrower had assets and sold them because the question, But a guarantor for a ketubah does not become obligated, also relates to the Mishnah's statement that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says a guarantor for a woman's ketubah must cause her to vow not to benefit from him. That too requires the Gemara's answer that guarantor there is imprecise and means kablan. One could then ask, this works according to the one who says, and in the Mishnah it cannot be answered that the borrower had assets and sold them, since then the purchasers could redirect the creditor to the guarantor. Nor can one answer that he is a kablan, because Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel himself holds that even a kablan cannot be collected from if the borrower has assets. Therefore the Gemara answers that the assets were destroyed, an answer that also explains the Mishnah. On that basis the Rashba could have derived his ruling. This is initially attractive. Yet it too may be rejected. Why require the husband to make a vow? The wife could assign her ketubah by a writing and delivery, or write to the kablan that she has received payment, and then he could collect from the purchasers in her place. What device would there be here to deprive the guarantor's assets? If the device is in truth aimed at purchasers rather than the guarantor, then why does the Gemara in Arakhin ask, We learned guarantor, we learned consecration, but we did not learn purchaser, when the entire issue of the guarantor is actually because of purchasers? Necessarily, the case is one where there are no assets at all, and there is no proof in either direction. The responsum therefore requires study, for it appears to follow the rejected view that a kablan cannot be collected from unless the creditor said From whomever I wish. Further study is required.
