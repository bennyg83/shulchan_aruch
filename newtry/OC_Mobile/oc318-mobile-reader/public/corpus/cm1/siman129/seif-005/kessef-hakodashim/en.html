The opinions in siman 129 dispute whether a note stating, "I may collect from whichever of them I wish," requires the creditor first to proceed against the principal debtor. If the instrument does not identify which obligor is the principal borrower, there is strong reason to say that each is independently liable from the outset. Even if witnesses later testify that one was in fact the principal borrower, the omission of that distinction from the instrument may itself indicate that the parties agreed to equal immediate liability. Oral testimony would not necessarily contradict the writing, but the document may be treated as embodying a deliberate stipulation. I encountered a case where a person produced a handwritten instrument saying that he could collect from whichever of two parties he wished, and sued the one who admittedly had not received the loan proceeds. That person signed only after the money had already been advanced, and no pledge was released on the strength of his signature. According to the Shach in siman 42, even though a person can obligate himself voluntarily, an undertaking analogous to guaranty and involving asmachta requires a kinyan. A mere note may not suffice. Here, the handwritten paper originally contained only the sum, and the words stating that a kinyan had been made were added later before it was presented to beit din. The signer was plainly unfamiliar with legal instruments and did not know that such kinyan language was routinely inserted. Under the Shach's rulings in simanim 40 and 61, one cannot say that he knowingly accepted liability merely by signing. Without an actual kinyan, or an explicit acknowledgment that a kinyan had been made, I could not impose liability upon him on the basis of that writing, especially since he also claimed that the principal borrower had already paid.
