Only regarding the lien of a ketubah. With an ordinary guarantor of a ketubah who is not an absolute guarantor, there is generally no obligation, according to the Tur without a kinyan even if undertaken at the time of the marriage arrangement, and according to the Rambam and Mechaber even with a kinyan. The reason is that he assumes he is merely facilitating a mitzvah and that no real loss is being caused, because a woman prefers to be married even under difficult circumstances. An absolute guarantor, however, is liable even without a kinyan. A guarantor for a dowry also has the consideration that he is acting for a mitzvah, but lacks the rationale concerning a woman's preference to be married, and therefore the authorities dispute his liability. This is why the Mechaber omitted the rule and Rama supplied it. According to all opinions, however, a guarantor for a dowry may retract before the marriage, even if a kinyan was made, because no act has yet been performed in reliance upon him. This follows all the more from the rule at the beginning of siman 131, and Rama states it plainly in Even HaEzer siman 102:6. Our customary guarantees are generally for the penalty resulting from breach, because embarrassment prevents retraction, rather than guarantees for the dowry itself. Rabbeinu Yerucham also writes that one who gives money for investment at shared profit and obtains a guarantor for the profit binds the guarantor even without a kinyan. Although the principal did not lose money in reliance upon that guarantee, the guarantor was not acting merely to facilitate a mitzvah. This too can be learned from the laws of guarantors for a ketubah and dowry.
Wherever a kinyan is required, if no kinyan was made, he cannot collect even from unencumbered assets, even if he wrote him a document. The wording is precise. Rama says, 'wherever a kinyan is required,' rather than simply saying that a written document is ineffective, because there is a dispute on that point, as stated in seif 4. He means that according to the opinion requiring a kinyan, a document alone is insufficient, even a separate document written specifically for the guarantor. The Tur writes similarly. When the Mechaber later states in seif 7 that if the guarantee was written after the witnesses' signatures the creditor may collect only from unencumbered property, which implies that at least such property can be collected, that case is one where a kinyan was made, as is clear from the preceding discussion. Nevertheless, we compel him to fulfill his oath. A practical difference arises if the person who gave his solemn hand pledge dies. His heirs are not obligated merely because of his oath. Even during his lifetime beit din does not descend to his property to collect, but places him under a ban until he fulfills his oath. Likewise, if he later seeks release from the oath and it is annulled, that release is effective after the fact according to Rabbeinu Tam. None of this would be true if he were legally obligated under the law of guaranty. The Rivash states these distinctions explicitly, and they are cited by the Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe. This differs from the Ir Shushan, who writes that because the person's obligation arises only from his oath and not from guaranty, if he pays the creditor the borrower need not reimburse him. That does not appear correct. Since at the borrower's request he gave the creditor his solemn hand pledge to pay and thereby obligated himself, it is as though the borrower asked him to pay on his behalf. Even if he does not bear the formal title of guarantor, the borrower must at least reimburse one who paid his debt at his instruction, as explained above in this siman.
