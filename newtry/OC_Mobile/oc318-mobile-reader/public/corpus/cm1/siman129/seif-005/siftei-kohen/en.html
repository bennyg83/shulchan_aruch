["Wherever a kinyan is required, if no kinyan was made he cannot collect even from unencumbered property, even if a document was written. The Sema explains that the Tur did not simply say that a document is ineffective because this point is disputed, as stated in seif 4. This is not compelling. It is possible that even the Raavad agrees that a document is weaker than a signature made after the undertaking. The practical law requires study. Rabbenu Yerucham writes that one who gives money for a half profit arrangement and obtains a guarantor for the worker's share of the profit is secured even without a kinyan. Although the lender has not yet suffered an actual loss, the guarantor is not performing a mitzvah and therefore becomes obligated like a guarantor at the time the money is given. He means that the investor arranged for a guarantor to secure the share of profit due to the recipient for his labor. This is treated as a guaranty given at the time of the transaction, because without that profit undertaking the recipient would not have agreed to perform the work. It is as though he had been hired for a stated amount to conduct the business. The recipient, however, may not guarantee the investor's share of profit, and if he produces another guarantor for that profit, that guarantor is treated as one who guaranteed after the money was given and is not bound without a kinyan, as I explained in Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 170 seif katan 5 regarding interest."]
["If he is in a place where they are not accustomed to acquire in this manner. See siman 201 seif 2, where the Sema notes that in some places merchants customarily acquire through such a practice. There he discusses a handshake without an oath. Perhaps his point is that the practice is no weaker than situmta, so even the promisor's children must honor it if he dies in a place where that custom is established."]
["Nevertheless, we compel him to fulfill his oath. The practical difference is that if the one who gave his hand in oath dies, his heirs are not bound by his oath. Even during his lifetime beit din does not descend to his property; rather, he is placed under ban until he fulfills his oath. Also, if he later obtains annulment of the oath, the annulment is effective after the fact according to Rabbenu Tam, unlike a case where he was legally bound as a guarantor. This is clear. So writes the Sema."]
["One who gave a gift to another, etc., and some disagree. Rabbenu Yerucham writes that the first view is primary. The Sema explains that at the time of the gift the situation is treated like the time money is given, not like a later guaranty, unlike the Ir Shushan, who prefers the latter view because there is no benefit of being trusted by the creditor. In my view the Ir Shushan is correct. This is also clearly the view of the Raavad and Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of gate 35, whose language I cited above in siman 126 seif katan 76. Rabbenu Yerucham's statement that the first view is primary and that Tosafos in Gittin supports it is not a proof. Tosafos require both elements in the case of a guarantor for a ketubah because such a guarantor performs a mitzvah and the creditor suffers no loss. But where the creditor does suffer loss, even though a mitzvah is involved, or where no mitzvah is involved, the guarantor can become obligated, each case according to its rules. In a gift, where the recipient has suffered no loss, the guaranty resembles one given after the money, and therefore still requires a kinyan."]
