Leave him, and I will be a guarantor. Meaning, leave him alone for now, and if he does not pay you later, I will pay. But if the creditor completely releases the borrower on the basis of the guarantor, the law is discussed in seif 3 in the Rama; it is like a guarantor who undertook the obligation at the time the money was given. So write the Sema and Shach. The Shach notes that this is explicit in a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 126. See also Responsa Maharshdam, siman 88, and Responsa Rama, siman 33.
At the borrower's instruction he is liable, etc. The Sema explains that the borrower expressly said, 'Pay.' See Tumim, where I wrote that this explanation is difficult to understand. If so, what novelty does the Rama teach? Numerous Mishnayot and sugyot state this, especially the Mishnah in Shevuot where one tells a shopkeeper, 'Give to my workers,' and the shopkeeper swears and collects. The Shach writes that the borrower said, 'Become my guarantor,' contrary to the Sema. See Tumim, where I wrote that this too is difficult, because this point is itself disputed by the Rambam and Raavad below in siman 130. I therefore proposed a correct explanation, which may perhaps also fit the Shach's wording. The borrower said, 'Become my guarantor and pay.' One might have thought that his intent was only, 'If you become obligated by the law of guarantorship, then pay,' and since in truth he was exempt as guarantor, payment would be like paying another's debt without authorization, for which he is not reimbursed. The Rama therefore teaches that he is nevertheless liable because he expressly said, 'Pay,' making it a payment of his debt with his knowledge and consent.
Whether privately, etc. Meaning, even without witnesses, because witnesses are needed only to prevent falsehood, as explained in simanim 189 and 195. It is difficult, however, why a kinyan helps here without beit din chashuv, since a guaranty is an asmachta. The authorities write that a guarantor at the time the money is given is not subject to asmachta because in consideration of the trust shown him he fully obligates himself. We must therefore say that even here there is some benefit of trust in the guaranty. For the same reason, a guarantor appointed by beit din becomes bound even after the money was already given and without a kinyan, because beit din relied on him. See Sema.
I am your guarantor for one day, etc. The source is a responsum of the Rashba, which has been explained in various ways. The Shach, seif katan 6, cites the Bach that if this was said when the money was given, the guaranty is valid, and the meaning is: if you allow one day to pass without pressing him for payment, I will then be exempt from my guaranty. The Taz writes likewise. See Tumim, where I rejected their interpretation and concluded that the correct meaning in the Rashba is: for the one day during which you refrain from pressing him, I am guarantor. If there was a kinyan he becomes bound; without a kinyan he does not. See there. The Shach further writes that one who writes, 'Whoever lends to so and so, I am a kablan guarantor,' is discussed in Maharshdam, who compares it to saying, 'Whoever feeds him will not lose,' and holds that it does not create a guaranty. See Tumim, where I noted that this contradicts the Shach's own view in Yoreh Deah, siman 235, where he follows the Perishah that such language creates liability. In Tumim I concluded against the Shach in both places, but in opposite directions: where one says, 'Whoever feeds him will not lose,' I agreed with the Taz in Yoreh Deah that he is exempt; whereas here, if he writes in his own hand, 'Whoever lends to him, I am guarantor,' he is liable. I brought proof from a Mishnah, and Maharshdam himself agrees. I am astonished at the Shach, for the law here is clear that he is liable.
