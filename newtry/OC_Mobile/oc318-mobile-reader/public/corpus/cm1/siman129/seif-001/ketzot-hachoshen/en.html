If one writes, "Whoever will lend so and so money, I undertake for him as guarantor," Maharashdam inclined to rule that no guaranty arises because the writer never gave a direct instruction, "Give him." He compares it to the phrase, "Whoever feeds him will not lose," used in vows. The Shach elsewhere accepts that one who says, "Whoever feeds him will not lose," must reimburse the feeder, yet the feeder is not legally the speaker's agent. Tumim argues that the Shach contradicts himself. If the speaker must pay the feeder, why is one who says, "Whoever lends, I will pay," not a guarantor? He cites Rosh's proof from King Saul's declaration that whoever struck Goliath would be enriched, and the Mishnah, "Whoever brings me news that my wife bore a male shall receive a maneh." After reflection, the Shach is correct. A guarantor requires something more than a bare personal undertaking. Guaranty is conditional and resembles asmachta. Even according to Rambam, who permits a person to undertake an unconditional monetary obligation by speech under appropriate circumstances, a guarantor becomes obligated at the time the lender gives the money because the lender acts upon the guarantor's instruction. Rashi says that the guarantor fully commits himself because the lender acts as his agent, as though the guarantor himself extended the loan. The phrase, "Whoever feeds him will not lose," can create a personal reimbursement obligation without creating agency. That is precisely why it is permitted when the beneficiary is prohibited by vow from receiving benefit from the speaker. If the feeder were the speaker's agent, the benefit would be prohibited. Thus the phrase can obligate reimbursement while still lacking the agency required for guaranty. Accordingly, saying, "Whoever lends him money will not lose," or, "I will serve as guarantor for whoever lends him," does not necessarily create the halachic relationship of guaranty. The lender did not lend pursuant to a direct command. A statement, "I am a kablan guarantor," is also not the same as the classic formula, "Give him and I will give you." A kablan obligation too is conditional, and where the borrower has known assets one often proceeds first against the borrower. Mere speech therefore does not replace the need for the guarantor's instruction and the lender's reliance upon it. Maharashdam himself, who proved payment liability from King Saul's declaration, apparently followed Taz's approach that if the speaker is liable to pay, that itself shows agency. On that premise, the statement could also create guaranty. The Shach takes the different view that reimbursement and agency are separable. Therefore the examples of King Saul and the promised reward for good news prove only a personal undertaking, not guaranty. In my own analysis of siman 40, I concluded that even according to Rambam a person does not ordinarily become monetarily obligated by bare speech except through a formal admission structure, with the necessary evidentiary formulation. If so, where "Whoever feeds him will not lose" is not agency, even the personal reimbursement obligation may require further legal basis. But within the Shach's own approach, his distinction between reimbursement and guaranty is correct.
