Seif 1, gloss: If the borrower says, “I repaid you,” and the loan was oral, the borrower takes a hesset oath and both he and the guarantor are exempt, etc. Note: The Sema writes that an arev kablan is not exempt. I was uncertain about a case where the borrower says, “I do not know whether I repaid you.” It appears from seif 12, where even if the borrower died the creditor cannot collect from an ordinary guarantor, that where the borrower is uncertain whether he paid, the ordinary guarantor is exempt, while an arev kablan remains liable. The reason is that an ordinary guarantor is not liable until the borrower is first pursued. Although once the borrower's liability is established the guarantor's obligation relates back, if there is uncertainty whether the borrower already paid, then with respect to the guarantor it is an uncertainty whether he ever became obligated. The borrower himself says, in effect, “I do not know whether I repaid you,” but the guarantor may say, “I do not know whether I ever became obligated to you.” An arev kablan, however, undertakes an independent obligation and may be sued immediately. His obligation is certain, and the later doubt whether the borrower paid is therefore equivalent for him as well to “I do not know whether I repaid you,” and he must pay. This also explains the Midrash Tanchuma at the end of Vayigash, which asks what type of guarantor is liable to pay and identifies Yehudah as such a guarantor. The difficulty is why Yehudah initially said, “Behold, we are servants to my lord, both we and the one in whose hand the cup was found,” without invoking his guarantee, yet after Yosef said that the others might go up in peace Yehudah confronted him because of that guarantee. The brothers themselves were uncertain whether Yosef had framed Binyamin or whether Binyamin had in fact stolen the cup. Yehudah's guarantee covered an unavoidable mishap, not Binyamin's deliberate theft. Had Yehudah been an ordinary guarantor, this doubt would exempt him. But since he was an arev kablan, he would remain liable even in doubt. Still, where there are strong indications supporting exemption, even an arev kablan is exempt, as the Sema explains. At first there appeared to be such an indication: if Yosef was fabricating the charge, why place the cup specifically with Binyamin rather than another brother? Yet one could answer that Yosef desired Binyamin especially, as the Midrash states. On the other hand, another Midrash says that if theft is found among a group of ten, all are seized. Had Yosef framed any other brother, Binyamin would have been seized with the rest, so there was no need to single him out. That again suggested that Binyamin had acted deliberately, freeing Yehudah from his guarantee. Therefore, when Yehudah first offered that all should become servants, he did not protest on the basis of his guarantee. But when Yosef said, “Far be it from me to do this,” meaning Egyptian law did not seize them all, the proof disappeared. Yosef might indeed have framed Binyamin because he specifically wanted him. Yehudah's liability as an arev kablan therefore became relevant, and he approached Yosef. This is why the Midrash emphasizes the type of guarantor who is liable immediately, before pursuit of the borrower, and identifies Yehudah as that guarantor.
