One who lends his fellow, etc. The Mechaber copies Rambam. Rambam first states the case where the lender demanded payment and someone volunteered as guarantor, and afterward the stronger case where the lender was actually choking or pressuring the borrower. Both teach that a guaranty undertaken after the money was already given does not bind without a kinyan. One might have thought that where he saw the borrower's distress and intervened to save him, he fully obligated himself. The Tur reverses the order but likewise treats it as a progression. Rashi gives both underlying explanations.
He said to him, "Leave him." Meaning, "Leave him for now, and if he does not pay you later, I will pay." But if the lender fully released the borrower in reliance on him, the case is treated like a guaranty given at the time the money was advanced, as explained in the gloss to seif 3.
However, if he paid the lender at the borrower's instruction, etc. Darkei Moshe quotes this formulation, although Hagahot Maimoniyot states more broadly that even where the borrower merely told him, "Be my guarantor," if the guarantor then pays, the borrower becomes obligated to reimburse him, because the request is like saying, "Pay my debt." Rama's narrower wording accommodates Rambam's view in siman 130 that a guarantor who voluntarily pays need not be reimbursed merely because the borrower had asked him to guarantee. Still, once the borrower expressly instructs him to pay, any person, not merely a guarantor, would ordinarily be entitled to reimbursement. The point is that a guarantor who did not acquire an enforceable guaranty against the lender can nevertheless have a reimbursement claim against the borrower when he paid at the borrower's instruction.
But if they made a kinyan with him, etc., whether between him and the lender, etc. Even without witnesses the kinyan works, because witnesses were created only to resolve falsehood. One might ask why an ordinary kinyan suffices without an important beit din if guaranty after the loan is an asmachta. The answer is that even here there is some reliance and benefit: the lender trusted him, and together with the kinyan this creates genuine commitment. For the same reason a guarantor appointed by beit din can become bound even after the money was given and without a kinyan, because the authority and trust of beit din supplies the reliance.
"I am your guarantor for one day," etc. Rashba explains that such language is too ambiguous: it does not specify whether the guaranty is that the borrower will go on an errand for one day or merely that he will not leave the place for a day. Therefore even a kinyan does not create a defined obligation. For the same reason, witnesses who testify that someone guaranteed his fellow "for one day" do not necessarily contradict his oath that he was not a guarantor in the legally operative sense.
