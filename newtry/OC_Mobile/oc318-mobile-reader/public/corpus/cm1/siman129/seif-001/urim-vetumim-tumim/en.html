At the borrower's instruction he is obligated, etc. The Sema explains that the borrower expressly said to him, "Pay him," see there. This is difficult. What does the Rama teach us by saying that if he instructed him to pay, the borrower must reimburse him? This is an explicit Mishnah: if he said to a shopkeeper, "Give to the workers," the shopkeeper swears and collects, and there are many similar Mishnayot and passages in the Gemara. If, as the Shach appears to understand, the case is that the borrower said, "Become my guarantor," this too is difficult, because how could the Rama state without qualification a rule that is disputed by the Rambam and Raavad below in siman 130? In truth, the Hagahot Maimoniyot itself does not address that dispute at all. It speaks of a case in which the borrower told him, "Become my guarantor," and he became a guarantor to the creditor through a kinyan, until he was ultimately required to pay. One might have thought that since a guarantor requires a kinyan in such circumstances, he likewise needs a kinyan in relation to the borrower. The Hagahot Maimoniyot therefore teaches that no kinyan is needed vis-a-vis the borrower, because at worst he is one who paid another's debt with that person's knowledge. This is clear to one who examines the source. But the Rama's wording cannot comfortably be read that way. It therefore appears that the Rama speaks where the borrower said, "Become my guarantor and pay." One might have thought that if he had merely said, "Pay," it would be obvious that he appointed him as his agent, and since the agent acted on his instruction the borrower must reimburse him. But when he said, "Become my guarantor and pay," his meaning could be: if you become legally obligated to pay by virtue of guaranty, then pay the creditor for me; if you are not legally obligated, do not pay, because I said "become my guarantor," not merely "pay." Since in fact the guaranty was not legally effective, as explained in the Shulchan Aruch, he was not required to pay, and one might therefore have thought that the borrower never authorized such payment, so that this is like one who pays another's debt without his knowledge. The Rama therefore teaches that this is not so. Ultimately, the borrower did tell him to pay, and the payment is not categorized as payment without his knowledge. This appears to me to be the Rama's intent. Perhaps this is also what the Shach intended when he wrote that the case is one in which the borrower said, "Become my guarantor," without further explanation.
For one day it is nothing. See Shach, seif katan 6, who cites the Bach that if this statement was made at the time the money was advanced, he becomes a guarantor. The meaning, according to the Bach, is: if you allow one day to pass without pressing him for payment, I am released from my guaranty. The Taz writes similarly. This must mean that where an ordinary loan is for thirty days, he is saying that if, after the due date, the creditor allows the stated day to pass, he will no longer be a guarantor; but if the creditor demands payment on that day and nevertheless cannot collect, the guarantor remains liable. This reading of the wording is strained. One who examines the Rashba will see that the case is instead as follows. The creditor was pressing the borrower to pay, and the borrower said to him: leave him alone for one day and do not trouble him, and I will be your guarantor for payment. The phrase "I will be your guarantor for one day" means: in consideration of the one day during which you refrain from pressing him, I undertake guaranty. The Rashba then rules that if this was accompanied by a kinyan, he becomes obligated; without a kinyan, this is a guaranty undertaken after the money was advanced, and he does not become obligated at all. Anyone who examines the Rashba will see that there is no doubt about this reading. The Shach further writes there that one who writes, "Whoever lends to so and so, I am his guarantor and kablan," should be compared, based on Responsa Maharashdam, to one who says, "Whoever provides food will not lose," and therefore does not create a guaranty. But the Shach thereby contradicts his own ruling in Yoreh Deah, siman 235, where he follows the Perishah that even one who says, "Whoever provides food will not lose," is obligated to pay, although the provider is not acting as his agent. In truth, on that issue the Taz there and the Maharashdam are correct that he is exempt, for the Ran and the Raah write this explicitly in Ketubot, see there. Nevertheless, that rule has no bearing on one who says, "Whoever lends, I will pay," or, "I will be guarantor," as the Maharashdam himself writes. Responsa Rosh, kelal 68, ruling 9, proves that such a person does obligate himself, from the verse concerning whoever would strike Goliath, that the king would enrich him greatly, which the Gemara treats as an enforceable monetary undertaking. I would add the Mishnah: "Whoever informs me that my wife gave birth to a male shall receive one hundred." See the Ran there in the name of the Rashba, who writes that this is not an asmachta at all and is binding. The Beit Yosef likewise writes in siman 207 that one who says, "Whoever tells me that my wife bore a male shall receive one hundred," becomes obligated. What basis is there to say that such an undertaking is binding, while one who says, "Whoever lends one hundred to so and so, I will repay it," is not? To the contrary, the latter is even more firmly within the category of obligation, because the lender advances the money in reliance on his undertaking, and the speaker fully commits himself to whoever will lend. Otherwise, why did the Gemara regarding appointment of a provider use the indirect formula, "Whoever provides food will not lose," rather than the clearer formulation, "Whoever provides food, I am his guarantor or kablan"? It must be because such explicit language unquestionably creates liability. I am therefore astonished that the Shach copied this statement when the Maharashdam himself presented it only in the course of discussion rather than as his final conclusion.
