For one day. See Baer Heitev regarding one who wrote in his own hand that he would guarantee anyone who advanced money to a certain person, and Responsa Maharashdam on the point. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat write that such a guarantor is certainly liable, like one who says, "Whoever feeds him, I will repay," and they reject Ketzot HaChoshen's contrary view. Baer Heitev further cites the ruling that once the borrower has repaid the original loan, the guarantor is released and is not automatically bound for a second loan. Ketzot, above in siman 48, cites this from Responsa Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 45, and derives that even with a transferable note the lien, once discharged, does not automatically revive. He acknowledges a possible distinction, namely that in a transferable note the maker may have initially obligated himself that whenever the note is presented it may be collected, even after prior payment, but he questions where such continuing intent is demonstrated. Taz likewise appears to hold that even a transferable note does not revive once its lien has been discharged. The Shach, however, clearly distinguishes the cases. Here he cites Maharashdam and calls the rule obvious, while in siman 48, seif katan 2, he writes that even the Bach agrees regarding a transferable note. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 21, concerning a related case. Reuven arranged with Shimon that Shimon would transport Reuven's merchandise to a certain place at Shimon's risk. Shimon deposited objects as security with Levi, a stakeholder, and Levi gave Reuven a stakeholder's certificate stating that after fourteen days whoever produced the signed certificate would receive the objects or a specified sum. The certificate did not state the true reason for the security because the risk involved evasion of customs duties. Shimon successfully completed the first transport, delivered the merchandise safely, and Reuven returned the certificate to Shimon. Several days later Reuven and Shimon made a second arrangement, without Levi's knowledge, for another shipment at Shimon's risk, relying on the same objects still held by Levi. Reuven again received the old certificate from Shimon. The second shipment failed and was confiscated by customs. Reuven then demanded the security from Levi according to the certificate. Levi answered that Shimon owed him money, that his role as stakeholder ended when the first shipment was successfully completed, and that he had then seized Shimon's objects for his own debt. He had never agreed to the second arrangement. Beit Ephraim rules at length for Levi. Once Shimon successfully completed the first shipment, the objects ceased to have the status of a pledge and became an ordinary deposit in Levi's hands, as explained in siman 72. Levi was therefore entitled to seize them for his own debt. Shimon's later attempt to pledge them again to Reuven could not prejudice Levi, because Reuven never consulted him. Had Levi expressly agreed and allowed Reuven to rely on him, there might have been liability for causing loss if Levi then failed to disclose his own seizure. But where he was never told of the second transaction, he bears no responsibility. The fact that the old certificate had not been destroyed does not preserve the first lien. When the first shipment was safely delivered, the certificate's obligation ended and the certificate should have been returned. Retaining an expired document does not revive it. Even if Shimon later attempted to use it for a second pledge, that is ineffective because Levi never renewed his delivery of the certificate. Had Levi himself redelivered it, one might compare the case to the Sema in siman 48 concerning a handwritten instrument that may be reused where no encumbered property is collected, or to the Shach's distinction for an instrument payable to whoever presents it. But where the owner of the objects merely handed over Levi's old stakeholder certificate again, without Levi's participation, it is ineffective. Beit Ephraim further writes that even if such reuse of the certificate could in theory affect the security, Levi could say that he intended his certificate to cover only the first transaction. One cannot infer from its general language that he agreed to repeated future uses. Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 38, rules that one who wrote that he was guarantor to anyone who advanced a specified sum may say that he guaranteed only the first advance and not a later advance after the first was repaid. Mishpetei Shmuel, siman 45, rules similarly. Ketzot uses this to argue that even a transferable note does not revive once paid. Moreover, Levi's possession prevails independently. Even other creditors of Shimon could not displace him merely because Reuven claims that Shimon repledged the objects. A debtor cannot sell or pledge property that another creditor has already validly seized. Beit Ephraim goes further: if in the first transaction Shimon had handed the objects to Reuven only for Reuven to carry them to Levi as stakeholder, and Levi on receipt declared that he was seizing them for his own debt against Shimon, Levi's seizure would still be effective. Reuven never acquired the objects as a pledge merely by transporting them to Levi. Even if Reuven protested that he had advanced money relying on them, he admits that they were not delivered into his possession as a pledge but only entrusted to him for delivery to Levi. Levi's receipt therefore need not be treated as acquisition on Reuven's behalf. Indeed, even if Reuven expressly told Levi, "Acquire these objects for me as a pledge," Beit Ephraim argues that this would not necessarily help where Reuven had not yet advanced a presently due loan but only had a future claim for possible loss of merchandise. The liability had not yet arisen, and there was no presently existing debt for which acquisition could be made. In addition, if Levi merely accepted the objects as security for Reuven without an effective acquisition, he could later retract and retain them for himself, analogous to the Shach's rule where one tells an agent to pay a creditor and the agent later retains the funds for his own claim. Beit Ephraim then develops a broader point: even if Levi himself were not a creditor, but other creditors of Shimon appeared, since movable property has no priority of liens they would share equally. Reuven would not have the superior status of a creditor holding a pledge unless the pledge had actually been transferred into his possession with a valid acquisition. Merely saying that property deposited with another shall be security for a creditor does not necessarily create a pledge. He further questions whether even physical delivery of objects as security for a future contingent liability creates a valid pledge, because there is no present monetary acquisition and pulling possession is generally an act for acquiring the body of property, not merely a lien. He adds that even where a person expressly undertakes liability for accidents, such an undertaking generally requires either a kinyan or the benefit of being trusted. Here there was no kinyan and the very demand for collateral shows that the creditor did not rely on the guarantor's trustworthiness alone. If no personal obligation took effect, a pledge cannot create one by itself, just as in Kiddushin the rule is, "A maneh is not here, and a pledge is not enough." A person can encumber property for an existing obligation, but a lien does not create an obligation that never arose. Likewise, even if a contingent loss later occurs, dedicating property in advance as security for a liability that may arise in the future may fail because the underlying obligation was not yet in existence. Beit Ephraim compares this to the rule of a thing not yet come into existence and analyzes at length the Taz in Even HaEzer, siman 50. He concludes from Sefer HaMakneh and Ketzot in siman 207 that where one gives a pledge now to secure a liability that may arise later, even if the pledge is physically in the creditor's possession, there is serious reason to say that it does not acquire the status of a pledge because the debt itself is not yet in existence. Certainly where the object was not then in the creditor's possession it cannot acquire that status. Accordingly, if other creditors exist they may also collect, as the Taz states expressly. Even where the object is held by a stakeholder who now wishes to seize it for the beneficiary, that does not help if the stakeholder did not originally acquire it on the beneficiary's behalf. A later attempt to seize for one creditor where it harms others is ineffective. All the more so in the case before Beit Ephraim, where the first stakeholder arrangement had ended and Levi himself was a creditor seizing for his own debt. The owner's and Reuven's later handshake or agreement that the object already held by Levi would again secure a future loss has no power to take it from Levi. Beit Ephraim concludes that every one of these grounds independently supports Levi, and certainly their combination does. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that he has not reproduced every detail of that lengthy responsum, and that in his own view the ultimate ruling in the particular case still requires study. He refers to his separate work for a fuller discussion.
