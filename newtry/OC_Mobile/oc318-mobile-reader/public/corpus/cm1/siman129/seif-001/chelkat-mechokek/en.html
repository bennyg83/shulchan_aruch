A responsum from the eminent author of Tosafot Yom Tov, Rabbi Lipman, to the eminent Rabbi Yonah. The question concerned a dangerously ill man whose name was changed during his illness in the customary manner. On that same day he gave his wife a get to exempt her from chalitzah, and he died. The arranger wrote both names, placing the newly given name first and the original name afterward, but did not write "who is called" before the original name. One might argue that the new name should not have been written at all because it was never established: he was not called to the Torah by it and did not sign a document with it, for he died that same day. Maharam Yaffe writes along these lines. Also, "who is called" was omitted, and some opinions would invalidate on that account. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer 129 discusses the various views, and there is also material in Responsa Maharit. Please instruct me whether in practice she may be exempted from chalitzah. Since this is already after the fact, perhaps one can say that the yavam cannot claim, "I became prohibited to the chalutzah's sister," merely because of a questionable get. The Tosafot Yom Tov responds: What you cite from Maharam Yaffe is essentially the Shulchan Aruch's rule that where the new name never took hold and no one calls him by it, it has been uprooted and should not be written. That does not prove our case. Here, although the name was not yet established over time, it was publicly assigned that day in the synagogue, the arranger and at least a minyan knew of it, and his death does not automatically erase that fact. There is, however, another possible basis for invalidation suggested by Maharit. In a case discussed there, some authorities argued that once the man died, there were no longer two names. According to one version, the original name was entirely uprooted; Maharit challenged this and argued the reverse, that when the change of name did not help and the person died, the change was only for life and failed, so after death people return to the original name. Even in places where at the time of the name change they say, "He shall no longer be called by the former name," people later continue using the original name, as Scripture itself continues to call Yaakov by that name even after "Your name shall no longer be Yaakov." If so, perhaps after death the change-name has fallen away, and writing it in the get would amount to changing his name. Yet Maharit himself earlier writes that where the arrangers did not know of the new name and wrote only the original one, had they written only the new name before it became established, the get would nearly have been void and she might even be permitted to the yavam. This may imply that writing both names would be valid. In any event, Maharit can be read in more than one way, and therefore the woman should not be released without chalitzah. The author then responds to the Tosafot Yom Tov's analysis. A careful reading of Maharit shows that he never intended to invalidate the get retroactively merely because the man later died and the changed name ceased to be used. We judge according to the name he bore at the time the get was given. If we feared his subsequent death and omitted the new name, we would have to fear the opposite even more, because most sick people recover. Had he recovered, the second name would certainly have become established, especially for aliyah to the Torah, and the first name might have fallen from use. Even if the changed name later ceased after death, at the moment of delivery it was at least a public byname, and there is no more public byname than a new name assigned in the synagogue while people pray for his recovery. Maharit mentioned the later death only to refute the authorities who claimed that the original name had already been entirely uprooted when the new name was assigned. He said, in effect, that the eventual death reveals that the original name remained primary. But he did not mean that the later death retroactively invalidates a get containing the new name. Had that been his central position, he would have begun with it and said that even writing the new name together with the old one was invalid after death. Instead, his principal point elsewhere was different: a newly assigned name not yet commonly used may be too weak to stand alone. In his view, one should not write only the new name while relegating the original name to a general formula. But writing both names expressly, especially without the phrase "who is called," was never held by any authority to invalidate the get merely because the secondary name preceded the primary one. Rivash writes that a defect arises where only the secondary name is written and the principal name is swallowed into a general clause, and some authorities are stringent even where the wording "who is called" wrongly makes the principal name appear secondary. But where both names are written expressly, primary and secondary together, order alone does not invalidate. All the more so in our case, where everyone knew of the new name on the day of delivery and the man was in need of mercy so that the decree might be torn. There is no basis to invalidate because that name was also written. Moreover, Maharit's very premise is not universally accepted. The new illness-name may indeed be regarded as primary even if most people still use the former name. Beit Yosef's discussion of the ruling of Rabbi Peretz shows that the principal doubt arises only where the second name has completely fallen from use. Under another explanation in Beit Yosef, Rabbi Peretz's case is essentially ours: the question was merely whether the newly added name, although not yet established, should be written first; at minimum it certainly would not invalidate the get if written. Thus, where both names were written, there is no reasonable basis to invalidate the get, particularly since the formula did not say "who is called."
