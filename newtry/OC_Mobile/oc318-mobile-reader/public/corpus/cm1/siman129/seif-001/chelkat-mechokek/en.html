Responsum from the great rabbi, author of Tosafos Yom Tov, our teacher R' Lipman, of blessed memory, to the great rabbi R' Yonah, of blessed memory. Regarding the question he asked him: Peace to the master and his Torah at every time and season, my beloved, the gaon and glory of Israel, my relative by marriage R' Yonah, may his Rock preserve him. I received your letter, in which your honor wrote as follows: I come with a small question. In my district there was a dangerously ill man whose name was changed during his illness, as is customary. On that very day he gave a get to his wife to exempt her from chalitzah, and he died. The arranger of the get wrote both names, placing the changed name first and then the original name, but he did not write regarding the first name 'who is called.' There is room to say that the changed name should not have been written at all, because he had not yet been established by it, neither having been called to the Torah by it nor having signed a document with it, for he died that very day. Maharam Yaffe likewise writes this. Also, he did not write 'who is called,' and there are opinions that invalidate on this account. Beit Yosef discusses this at length in Even HaEzer, siman 129, and brings all the views, and some of this also appears in a responsum of Maharam of Trani. Let your honor therefore instruct me as to the correct practical ruling, whether she may be released without chalitzah. There is room to say that this is a post-facto case, for this man could say, 'I do not wish my wife's sister to be forbidden to me,' and this suffices. End of his words. Regarding what your honor wrote in the name of Maharam Yaffe, these are indeed the words of Shulchan Aruch. Your honor presumably cited Maharam Yaffe because he omitted what Shulchan Aruch writes, 'There are those who require,' etc. I will write shortly whether he was correct to omit it, or whether even according to Shulchan Aruch, who records it, the halacha follows the anonymous ruling rather than the 'there are those who require.' For now I say that your honor has no proof for our case. There, the case is not one where he died that very day. On the contrary, he remained alive and was never called by the second name at all, so that the name was uprooted and forgotten, and therefore should not be written. Here, however, although the new name had not yet become established, that is not called its uprooting or being forgotten. Since on that day his name was changed publicly in the synagogue, and the arranger of the get and at least a minyan knew of it, even if his death released him to life in the World to Come, this is not an uprooting of the name at all. Yet for another reason it appears to me that the get should be invalidated, as Maharam of Trani writes at the end of responsum 125 in part 1, reporting the reasoning of those who invalidate the get in his case: since the man died, there are no longer two names, but only the changed name, while the first name was entirely uprooted. Maharam of Trani challenges that view. On the contrary, he says, since he died despite the change of name, the name associated with life changed to death and he returned to the first name. It is well known that when a person's name is changed during illness and the change does not help, and his decree is not torn up, one of the four things that tear up a person's decree, and he dies, afterward he is mentioned only by the first name, because his name was changed only on condition that he live. End of Maharam of Trani's words. Although the invalidating authorities reasoned as they did because in those lands, at the time of changing the name, they say 'he shall no longer be called,' as appears from their words, Maharam of Trani's challenge is nevertheless sound. He holds that, on the contrary, the first name remains primary, because even though they say this, people continue to call him by the first name, often even most of the time. The name Yaakov itself proves this, for Scripture says, 'Your name shall no longer be called,' and nevertheless Scripture afterward is full of the name Yaakov. If so, his challenge remains: when the man dies, there is no effective name change at all. It would therefore appear that the get is invalid if the changed name was written, because this is like writing an incorrect name. Although earlier in that paragraph Maharam of Trani writes concerning a get whose arrangers did not know that the man's name had been changed and wrote only the first name, that had they written only the changed name, since it had not yet become customary and was not known even to a minority, it would have been close to not being a get at all, that does not necessarily mean that writing both names would invalidate it. Indeed, one can explain his intent as applying only where the changed name alone was written. If so, his words may also be understood like those of Maharam of Trani at the end of the responsum quoted above. Nevertheless, since it is possible to understand him as saying that even writing both names would not produce a valid get, we have not escaped doubt. He therefore concludes that the woman should not be permitted without chalitzah. The author of Tosafos Yom Tov continued at length in that responsum with matters not relevant to the present ruling. I copied only the portion necessary to understand what my father and teacher, the author, of blessed memory, wrote in objection to this ruling.
Here is what my father and teacher, the author, of blessed memory, wrote. Regarding the author of Tosafos Yom Tov's conclusion that the get should be invalidated based on Maharam of Trani at the end of responsum 125, and his understanding that Maharam of Trani rejected the view of those who held that because the man died there remained only the changed name and the first name was entirely uprooted, arguing instead that once he died there was no changed name at all and therefore the get would necessarily be invalid if the changed name was written, careful examination of Maharam of Trani shows that he never intended to invalidate a get retroactively merely because the man later died and the changed name ceased to be used. We look only at the name he had at the time the get was delivered. If we were to fear that he might die and therefore omit the second name, there would be even greater reason to fear the opposite, for most sick people recover. If he recovered, the second name would certainly become established; people might call him only by that name, and he would surely be called to the Torah by that name, in which event the first name could be entirely displaced. What can one answer? That even if it were later displaced, at the time of the get he was still called by the first name and it was at least a temporary byname. All the more so must we say this of the changed name: even though he later died and the changed name ceased, at that moment they called him by the new name, hoped for Hashem's salvation, and, since most sick people live, intended this name to avert the decree. There is no more public byname than that. Had Maharam of Trani intended to invalidate the get because the changed name was later nullified, he would not have written this only incidentally at the end as a rhetorical objection. He should have made it the foundation of the ruling: before asking whether the get is valid because the arrangers wrote only the first name and omitted the changed name, he should have said that even if they had written the changed name the get would be invalid because the man died and that name was nullified. Plainly he meant only to refute the opposing view that, since it was not known by what name he would be called if he recovered, at that time he had only the changed name and the first had been wholly uprooted. To this Maharam of Trani responded that, on the contrary, since he died it became revealed that the first name remained and they were correct to write it. That is the essence of his words. It never entered his mind to invalidate the get because the subsequent death retroactively nullified the changed name. It is true that earlier he writes, for another reason, that the get would have been close to invalid had they written only the changed name. That follows his own view that the changed name should not be written as the principal name until it has already become commonly used. He is forced to explain the Semak's statement that the changed name is primary even though people regularly call him by the first name as meaning that the changed name is the principal and more frequent name, while the first is still mentioned daily but less often. Thus, for example, the second name might be used seven times while the first is used three times. According to his position, writing only the changed name and subsuming the first under 'and every other name' might invalidate the get because the changed name was still only a minority usage. But no authority ever suggested invalidating when both names were written together, especially without writing 'who is called,' as the arranger did in the case brought before the author of Tosafos Yom Tov. Even if the changed name were secondary and used by a minority, the get is not invalidated merely because the secondary name precedes the principal name. Invalidity is discussed only when one writes the secondary name alone and includes the principal name under 'and every other name,' and according to some authorities even when one writes 'who is called' with the principal name. But where both the principal and secondary names are written together, there is no objection. So rules Shulchan Aruch, and it is clear and simple. It is inconceivable to invalidate merely because a secondary name was joined to the principal name, especially in our case, where everyone knew this secondary name and on that very day they supported him with prayers and he needed Hashem's salvation to tear up his decree. Why should writing this name as well invalidate the get? All this is according to Maharam of Trani. In truth, however, his approach is not universally accepted or compelling. The changed name is genuinely primary even when most people continue to call him by the first name, as is clear from Beit Yosef in this siman. Maharam P only had doubt when the second name was completely uprooted. According to Beit Yosef's second explanation of Maharam P's incident, it is essentially our very case. His wording implies that his only uncertainty was whether, before the second name became established, it should already be written first l'chatchila; if it had not yet become established, it should be written afterward. This itself shows that it cannot be contemplated that a get would be invalid if only the changed name was written. If a name that is certainly secondary does not invalidate, all the more so a name that may be secondary or may be primary. Certainly in our case, where both names were written, invalidation is inconceivable. In truth, on the day of delivery he had both names, especially since the formula 'who is called' was not written. So I found written.
