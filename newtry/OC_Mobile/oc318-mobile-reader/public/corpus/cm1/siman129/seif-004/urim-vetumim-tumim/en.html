A guarantor who appears after the signatures on the document, etc. Through the end of this seif the rules depend on different explanations of the Gemara. The Tur cites many approaches, which I will summarize. The Ramban holds that the Gemara's statement "the halachah is that in all of them a kinyan is required" excludes a guarantor whose undertaking is written after the witnesses' signatures. Such a guarantor does not require a kinyan. The Sema appears to understand that according to this view a written document everywhere functions like a kinyan. That cannot be correct. If so, the Ramban's objection to the other views would return against his own. If Ben Nanas agrees that a kinyan works and disputes only whether a document alone suffices, how can the Gemara say simply "the halachah is that a kinyan is required," a point Ben Nanas himself accepts? It should have stated the practical ruling of Rabbi Yishmael, whom we follow, that a document suffices. We cannot say the halachah follows Ben Nanas, because in chapter HaNose the Gemara says Rabbi Yochanan disagrees with Ben Nanas and we follow Rabbi Yochanan. But if Ben Nanas also disputes the effect of a kinyan because he treats document and kinyan alike under the concern of asmachta, then a different problem arises. Why does the Gemara say Rabbi Yochanan disagrees with Ben Nanas? According to Reish Lakish, who agrees that a kinyan creates liability, the case is already unlike Ben Nanas. One could then explain Rabbi Yishmael similarly. We must therefore say that in the ordinary cases everyone requires a kinyan rather than a document. Rabbi Yishmael and Ben Nanas do not disagree about that. Their dispute concerns specifically a guaranty written after the signatures. Rabbi Yishmael holds that such a written undertaking alone is effective, while Ben Nanas disagrees. We rule like Rabbi Yishmael. We still need to understand why a guaranty written after the signatures is stronger than an ordinary written undertaking. If the reason is that because it is physically attached to the loan document we view the lender as having extended credit in reliance upon it, the Gemara's challenge to Reish Lakish remains difficult. A post-signature guaranty resembles somewhat a guaranty made at the time the money was given. It is strained to say the Gemara nevertheless treats it no differently from "I owe you a maneh in a document," where there is no asmachta concern at all. Perhaps the explanation is that according to Rabbi Yochanan, a guaranty written after the signatures can in fact have been made at the time of the loan. The loan document might have been completed and delivered to the lender before the money was actually advanced, after which the guarantor wrote his undertaking after the signatures and the lender then gave the money. Witnesses are permitted to write and deliver the document before the actual loan in the circumstances explained above in siman 39. The guaranty would then be post-signature in form but contemporaneous with the extension of credit. This possibility exists specifically according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that a person can obligate himself through a written document even without a prior debt. According to Reish Lakish, who disagrees, the witnesses should not deliver an operative loan document until they actually see the money given. A guaranty added after the signatures would therefore necessarily be after the money was already delivered. The Gemara can accordingly challenge Reish Lakish: his framework does not fit Rabbi Yishmael's ruling that a post-signature guaranty can bind without kinyan. This resolves the matter precisely. The other authorities hold that a kinyan is indeed required for a guaranty written after the signatures, and according to the Rambam even a guaranty written before the signatures requires one in the relevant case. Their answer to the Ramban's objection can be stated simply. A kinyan removes an asmachta concern when the transaction otherwise does not require that kinyan. The fact that the party voluntarily performs an unnecessary kinyan shows that he is strengthening the obligation and undertakes it wholeheartedly, removing asmachta. But where the kinyan is itself legally necessary merely to create any obligation, it provides no additional evidence of deliberate commitment and therefore may not remove asmachta. Accordingly, the Gemara's reasoning is sound. Rabbi Yochanan's position is unlike Ben Nanas. According to Reish Lakish, a person cannot create the underlying obligation without a kinyan, so Ben Nanas can hold that such a necessary kinyan does not remove asmachta. According to Rabbi Yochanan, however, a person can obligate himself through a document. If he nevertheless also makes a kinyan, that additional act is unnecessary for basic liability and must serve to strengthen the obligation. It therefore removes asmachta. The Gemara can thus say that Rabbi Yochanan's rule does not fit Ben Nanas. This also answers the Gemara's question in Gittin that one needs a "document of transfer." At first glance, if the case already involved a kinyan, why would a document be needed? According to what I have written, if the document itself were absent, the kinyan would be necessary merely to create the obligation and would no longer operate as the extra act that removes asmachta. Another explanation is possible. Ordinarily, Rabbi Yishmael's statement about a post-signature guaranty is difficult to interpret as involving a separate kinyan because he states the rule without qualification. But we rule like Rabbi Elazar that witnesses to delivery effect the document. If there are no delivery witnesses, who creates the guarantor's lien at all? As explained above in siman 40, without witnesses to delivery there is no operative written obligation. If there are delivery witnesses, the creditor may even collect from encumbered assets. We may therefore need to say that a separate kinyan occurred. This reasoning depends on following Rabbi Elazar for monetary documents. Reish Lakish, however, says that the halachah follows Rabbi Elazar specifically in gittin, implying not necessarily in monetary documents. For him there is therefore no reason to force Rabbi Yishmael's case into one involving a kinyan. The Gemara can consequently challenge Reish Lakish directly. This is precise. Even if a separate kinyan would ordinarily allow collection from encumbered assets, there is no difficulty with a post-signature guaranty being described as collectible only from free assets. The case can involve a guaranty written in the guarantor's own handwriting after the creditor's document. Such writing can remove asmachta or establish personal liability while still lacking the publicity needed to collect from encumbered property. There is therefore no need to emend the Tur's text from Ramah to Ramach. It appears further that the Tur holds, according to the view that a kinyan by itself creates a public lien collectible from encumbered assets, that a proper document can likewise do so wherever a document is an effective mode for guaranty, whether before or after the signatures. Conversely, according to the view that a kinyan by itself does not create that publicity, a document alone likewise may not. A practical difference follows regarding a guaranty written before the signatures. According to the first view, the borrower's own loan document need not contain a separate kinyan. Once the document says, "and so and so is guarantor," the guaranty is part of a completed written obligation before the borrower's lien is finalized and can bind. But according to Rav Hai Gaon's view that even a kinyan does not by itself allow collection from encumbered assets, the same is true of the written guaranty. The case then requires that the borrower himself made a kinyan, so that the phrase "and so and so is guarantor" refers back to and joins that kinyan. This explains why the Tur states the law of a guaranty written before the signatures twice, a repetition that troubled the Bach. Under the first view, the borrower need not have made a kinyan. Under Rav Hai Gaon, the borrower's kinyan is required. This is what the Tur means when he concludes that otherwise the guaranty is like a document without kinyan. The Perishah struggled with that phrase, but the words are straightforward: the writing can establish the guarantor's personal liability, yet without the needed publicity or attached kinyan it does not allow collection from encumbered property. This is precise and provides the key to the corresponding provisions in the Shulchan Aruch. As for the Perishah and Sema's question, if the document merely says "so and so is guarantor," who testifies to the guaranty, there is no difficulty. First, the phrase may be in the guarantor's own handwriting. Second, even where witnesses attest to the guaranty, Tosafot in chapter HaNose explain that their testimony can establish the guaranty itself without generating the publicity needed for collection from encumbered assets. The document records the guaranty because the witnesses know it occurred, yet the lien can still remain limited in its reach. This is precise.
