Only after the money was advanced. Responsa Rishmei Sheelah, siman 51, discusses a case in which judges ruled that once wool had been placed into the buyer's containers, any later guarantee was a guarantee undertaken after the extension of credit. The author writes that were he not hesitant to disagree, he would say otherwise. In the case before him the wool was sold on credit, and commercial practice was plainly to issue a written obligation for the price, particularly where the amount was substantial and the sale involved merchandise subject to claims of overreaching. A seller is careful to have the obligation fixed in a written instrument for a stated cash amount. Therefore, before the instrument was written, the sale was not yet fully completed and the seller could still retract. He compares this to siman 190, seif 7, regarding land in a place where transactions are customarily completed by deed, where payment alone does not complete acquisition until the deed is written. Likewise, siman 190, seif 17, provides that where the seller is pressing for payment, even movable property that was physically pulled into the buyer's possession may not yet be fully acquired. So too in this case, as long as the seller was pressing for the written debt instrument, the buyer had not finally acquired the wool even though he had pulled it into his possession. Accordingly, before the instrument reached the seller, the rationale that binds a guarantor at the time of the original advance still applied: in return for the creditor's reliance on him, the guarantor obligates himself, and the creditor advances on his faith. The case is no worse than one where, at the guarantor's request, a creditor returns a debt document or pledge, which siman 129, seif 3, treats as a guarantee at the time of the advance. Thus, before execution of the credit instrument, this sale resembled the ordinary moment of initial extension of credit. The Talmudic discussions requiring a guarantee to be undertaken at the time money is advanced concern ordinary loans of cash, where the parties do not customarily condition completion of the loan on preparation of a formal debt instrument, because witnesses or a pledge may suffice. Similarly, the Rambam's ruling in siman 129, seif 4, that even a guarantor who signs before completion of the instrument may require a kinyan if he joined only after the money was advanced, concerns an ordinary cash loan in which writing a note was not an original condition but was agreed upon only afterward. The author concludes that although this reasoning appears sound to him, because he found no explicit statement of the rule in an early authority he hesitates to rely upon it in practical halachah.
