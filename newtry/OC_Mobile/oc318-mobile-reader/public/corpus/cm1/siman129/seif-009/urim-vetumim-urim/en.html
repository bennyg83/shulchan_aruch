And he must include, etc. Sema and Perishah explain that the case concerns an oral loan. Therefore the oath is imposed upon the borrower, and if the borrower would not swear that he still owes the lender, the guarantor would be exempt. With a written loan, however, the borrower is not believed even with an oath to say that he paid. In such a case, when the lender comes to collect from the guarantor, the lender must swear that the debt is still outstanding, not the borrower, just as Tur and Mechaber write in siman 114 concerning a lender who comes to foreclose from purchasers. See Tumim. Sema's comparison of a guarantor to a purchaser, so that the Mishnah rule that one who collects from encumbered property must swear would apply equally to guarantor property, is doubtful to me and creates a major practical difference. A purchaser knew nothing of the loan and never voluntarily entered its lien, so the Sages imposed an oath even against an uncertain claim. A guarantor, by contrast, enabled the loan to be made. Why should the lender be made to swear merely because the guarantor raises an uncertain claim? See Tumim, where I proved from Baal HaTerumot against Sema. Nimukei Yosef also requires an oath at collection, but his words imply a different case, where the borrower is not before us. Even Nimukei Yosef is difficult for me unless this is understood as part of the Geonic enactments. The matter requires study.
Until after thirty days. This is Rambam's ruling. Maggid Mishneh explains, and Bach likewise writes, that it applies where the guarantor has no money or movables, like the rule concerning an ordinary borrower in siman 100. The Shach explains instead that it applies even where the guarantor has money. From the moment the guarantor becomes liable, it is as though he has now received a fresh loan, and an unspecified loan is not payable until thirty days. See Tumim. Although the Shach's explanation fits Rambam's wording better, as practical law it requires study because no trace of such a rule appears in the Gemara.
