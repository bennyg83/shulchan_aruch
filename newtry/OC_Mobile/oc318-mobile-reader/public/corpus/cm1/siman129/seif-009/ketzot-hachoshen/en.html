That it is still owed. If the borrower refuses to swear, it appears that the guarantor is exempt, just as where the borrower refuses to swear that he has no assets. The guarantor is not liable until the borrower swears both that he has no means and that the debt remains outstanding. Regarding a written loan, Sema writes that the creditor swears to purchasers that the debt remains unpaid. This applies where the borrower himself did not swear that the debt remains unpaid. If the borrower swore, a written loan should not be worse than an oral loan. Tumim objects to the Sema and argues that the creditor need not swear. He cites Baal HaTerumot's uncertainty whether the rule of Rav and Shmuel, that a person cannot bequeath an oath to his children, applies when collecting from purchasers, perhaps showing that a guarantor is not subject to such stringency. He also notes that Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Geonim requires an oath when collecting from a guarantor only where the borrower is absent, and even then perhaps only by Geonic enactment. These proofs are not compelling. Even if collecting from a guarantor is not treated with the full stringency of heirs so as to invoke Rav and Shmuel's rule, an oath may still be required. Where the borrower is absent, the creditor certainly must swear by law, because collecting from a guarantor should not be easier than collecting from a debtor in his absence. Even where the borrower is present, if the borrower does not swear that the debt remains owed, the creditor must take that oath. Thus the Sema is correct. However, in an oral loan where the borrower is present and admits that the debt remains owed but then flees before he can swear, such as where he appeared on Friday and escaped after Shabbos, the lack of the oath should not block collection. This is no worse than the oath that the borrower has no assets: if the borrower appeared on Friday and fled after Shabbos, the guarantor is not thereby released merely because the oath was not formally taken. The principal matter is the borrower's admission. Regarding a written loan, the creditor himself takes the required oath. Perhaps the same should therefore apply to an oral loan where the borrower admitted and fled: the creditor should take the oath before collecting from the guarantor, and should not collect without it, because an oral loan should not be stronger than a written one.
