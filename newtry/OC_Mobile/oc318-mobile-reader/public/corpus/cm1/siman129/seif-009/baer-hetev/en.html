To include. It appears that this concerns an oral loan. Therefore the oath is imposed on the borrower, and if the borrower would not swear that he still owes the debt, the guarantor would be discharged. With a written loan, however, the borrower is not believed even with an oath to say that he repaid. Accordingly, when the creditor seeks payment from the guarantor, the creditor swears that the debt is still owed, not the borrower, just as the Tur and Mechaber rule in siman 114 regarding a creditor who seeks to collect from purchasers.
Days. Bach explains that the thirty-day period applies only where the guarantor has no money or movable property, as stated in siman 100 regarding a borrower, and this also appears from Maggid Mishneh. The Shach, however, writes that it appears even where the guarantor has money. From the moment the guarantor becomes liable, it is as though the creditor made him a loan at that time, and an unspecified loan is not payable until thirty days. If it is clear that the borrower has absolutely no property and the guarantor admits this, but merely fears that perhaps the borrower paid, the guarantor is not made to swear on that point, because this oath is imposed only by extension from another oath. Maharshal Yonah. Whether the rule of arranging a debtor's basic needs applies to a guarantor is disputed, as discussed in siman 97. Therefore, in practice, the party in possession may say that he relies on the authorities who hold that such an arrangement is made for a guarantor. Knesset HaGedolah.
