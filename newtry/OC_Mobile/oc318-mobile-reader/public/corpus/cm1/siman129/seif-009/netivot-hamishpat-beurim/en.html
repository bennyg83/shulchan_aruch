And he must include in his oath. See Sema, seif katan 25, who writes that if the borrower did not swear that he still owed the money, the guarantor would be released. His meaning is that if the borrower did not swear because he claimed that the debt had been paid, the guarantor would be released. But if the borrower admits the debt and merely refuses to swear because he is violent or recalcitrant, the guarantor remains liable. Otherwise Sema should have stated the greater novelty, that even if the borrower simply refuses to swear the guarantor is exempt. Moreover, Beit Yosef explains that this oath is only a gilgul arising from the creditor's oath that he owns no other property, and the same rule applies to that principal oath itself, as stated in Rama at the end of siman 10. Further, if the guarantor's liability depended upon the borrower's oath only because of concern for collusion, the borrower should not be believed even by taking an oath, since a person is not believed to impose a loss upon others merely by swearing. Rather, the guarantor is released only when the borrower denies the debt, because the borrower's obligation itself has been uprooted. When the borrower admits the debt and the obligation remains, the guarantor is likewise liable. This is clear. Ketzot HaChoshen's distinction between a borrower who fled and one who did not has no substance. In the case of a loan documented by a written instrument, Sema writes that the creditor must swear. Tumim is uncertain, but Sema is correct, because collecting from a guarantor is exactly like collecting from the borrower's property in his absence, which requires an oath. A person's property itself has the law of a guarantor. Tumim's proof therefore falls away. Collection from a guarantor is treated as collection in the debtor's absence, not merely as collection from encumbered property.
