And he must include in his oath, etc. See Sema seif katan 28, who writes that where the loan is evidenced by a document, the creditor must swear that he has not been paid, as the Tur and Mechaber wrote above in siman 114 regarding a creditor who comes to collect from a purchaser of the debtor's property. The Sema thus equates the law of a guarantor with that of a purchaser, holding that the enactment requiring one who collects from encumbered property to swear applies likewise to one who seeks payment from a guarantor. In my view, however, the cases are distinguishable. Purchasers knew nothing of the loan and did not enter its obligation at all. Therefore, when the creditor comes to seize their property, the Sages imposed an oath even though their claim is only uncertain. A guarantor, by contrast, knew of the loan, entered the guaranty, and the lender advanced the money in reliance upon him. What source is there that the Sages imposed such an oath upon an uncertain claim here? This is not truly collection from encumbered property. The guarantor is effectively part of the loan itself, for the lender lent in reliance upon him. What led me to this distinction is that the Baal HaTerumot, gate 14, initially was uncertain whether one who comes to collect from encumbered property, and therefore must swear, is subject, upon the creditor's death, to the rule that a person does not bequeath an oath to his children. He ultimately resolved the matter from the words of the Baal HaIttur. See there. See also what I wrote above in siman 108 concerning the responsum of the Rosh, which implies that even such a case is governed by the rule of Rav and Shmuel. Yet if the oath required of one collecting from encumbered property is included in the rule that an oath cannot be bequeathed, how can the Gemara state explicitly that Rav Pappa held, regarding a guarantor, that the case was included in the principle, Give them no more than the enactment itself, and then ask, Is it not true that a guarantor ultimately proceeds against the heirs? See there. This implies that but for that reason the case would indeed fall under the limitation, even where the borrower died, and certainly where the borrower is alive. It is therefore demonstrated from the Gemara that this oath is not governed by Rav and Shmuel. The matter is not doubtful, but clear: a guarantor is not included in the enactment concerning one who collects from encumbered property. Hence the Baal HaTerumot's uncertainty gave no proof from a guarantor. The Sema's ruling therefore requires study, for no decisor states that a guarantor is treated as encumbered property. I later saw that the Nimukei Yosef writes in the name of the Geonim that the creditor must swear as one who collects from orphans, but there he speaks where the borrower is not before us. If the borrower is present and admits that he has not paid, he cannot compel the creditor to swear. For this reason the Nimukei Yosef compares it to collection from orphans, not from encumbered property, which would imply an oath even when the borrower is present. This differs from the Sema. Even this, however, does not appear correct in light of the foregoing unless it is only an enactment of the Geonim. Further study is required.
Until after thirty days, etc. See Shach seif katan 23, who explains that this is because only then does the liability and lien upon the guarantor begin, and it is therefore treated like an unspecified loan, whose term is thirty days. Although his explanation fits the language of Rambam and Tur better, the ruling itself requires study. What source did Rambam have for treating the guarantor as though a new loan now arose, so that the rule of an unspecified thirty day loan applies? The guarantor became obligated from the outset when the creditor lent to the borrower, and the debt has existed for a long time. Merely because the borrower lacks funds and the creditor therefore turns to the guarantor, why should this be regarded as a new loan in the guarantor's hands? We have never heard such a principle. Further study is required.
