But if the borrower says, 'I never borrowed.' See Shach, seif katan 19, who writes that the Beit Yosef's statement refers to the borrower, and adds, 'See my book.' His words are obscure, and that book is not available to us. See Nechalat Tzvi for what appears to be the Shach's intention. The Bach challenges this ruling from the Gemara in Bekhorot: can there be a case in which the borrower is exempt while the guarantor is liable? He answers that this principle applies only when the borrower is legally exempt. Where the borrower is truly liable but the lender cannot recover from him because he falsely denies the loan and swears, while the guarantor admits that the loan was made, the guarantor is certainly liable, just as he would be if the borrower were overseas or were a violent person from whom collection was impossible. This requires study. The case in Bekhorot, and the ruling above at the end of siman 49 concerning two people with the same name, likewise involves a borrower who is exempt because he denies being the borrower, even though the purchaser from him knows that his denial is false, yet the purchaser is exempt because the lender cannot collect from the borrower. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 44, therefore strongly questions the Bach. Although the Tumim's approach here and above at the end of siman 49 might provide an answer, VeShav HaKohen demonstrates that the Tumim's interpretation of that Gemara cannot stand. He concludes that the Rama's gloss here requires very great study for practical halachah. With a kablan, however, in such a case it appears that he is liable. See there.
