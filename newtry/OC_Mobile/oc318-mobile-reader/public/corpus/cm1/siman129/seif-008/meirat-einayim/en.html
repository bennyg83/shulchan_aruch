If the borrower sold land and the creditor himself signed as a witness, the guarantor is discharged because the creditor caused his own loss, etc. These words are based on a responsum of the Rashba, but the source does not say that the creditor signed 'as a witness.' The facts and the responsum show that the creditor signed the deed of sale as one who consented to the borrower's sale to the purchaser. The Rashba therefore ruled that the creditor caused his own loss by releasing the purchaser, and can no longer collect from the guarantor. One may collect from an ordinary guarantor only when the borrower has nothing from which to collect, whether unencumbered property in his hands or property sold after the loan. Here the borrower had land from which the creditor could have collected, but the creditor voluntarily relinquished his rights against the purchaser. For this reason the Rashba concludes that the rule applies only to an ordinary guarantor. From an absolute guarantor the creditor may still collect, because even if the land had remained in the borrower's hands the creditor could have chosen to collect from the absolute guarantor instead. This also resolves the apparent contradiction with siman 111:17, where the Tur and Mechaber rule that if a borrower sells his property and the creditor signs the sale document, the creditor does not thereby lose his lien, because he can say, The second purchaser was more convenient for me. The Rashba's responsum cannot therefore mean merely that the creditor signed as a witness. The wording of the responsum shows that he affirmatively consented in a way analogous to writing to a later purchaser, I have no claim against you, which fully removes his rights. There is another possible distinction. In the Rashba's case the borrower had specifically mortgaged an upper story to the creditor and also supplied a guarantor in case the collateral was somehow lost. Since the creditor's primary reliance was upon that specific mortgage, perhaps his consent to its sale destroyed his claim against the guarantor. But this is strained, because the responsum does not base the law upon the fact that the property had been specifically mortgaged. The first explanation is therefore the correct one. Rama, and the Ir Shushan following him, were not precise in stating the case merely as one where the creditor signed as a witness.
