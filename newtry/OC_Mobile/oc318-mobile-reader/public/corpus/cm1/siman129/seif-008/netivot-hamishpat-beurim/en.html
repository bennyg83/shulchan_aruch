The borrower takes a hesset oath and both he and the guarantor are exempt. If property of the guarantor was seized without witnesses and the guarantor has a migo, the seizure is effective, because a migo has evidentiary force comparable to witnesses.
I never borrowed. See Shach, seif katan 19. Tumim writes that where the borrower is a Jew, if the guarantor simply said, "I guarantee," and the loan was made without witnesses, the guarantor is liable. Since he did not condition his guarantee on the presence of witnesses, he cannot later complain that he relied on proof of the loan, as appears from simanim 58, 98, and 121. Yet Tumim writes that where the borrower is a non Jew the guarantor is exempt. The plain implication of the poskim in the cited simanim, however, is that even in the case of a non Jew the guarantor can say that he relied upon him.
The guarantor is obligated to pay. See Shach, seif katan 20. The primary ruling is like the Bach. The Bach and Tumim explain that where the law otherwise requires a kinyan, a kinyan is still necessary. The novelty is only that even though the borrower himself is exempt because no binding acquisition took effect upon him, the guarantor can obligate himself if the guarantor performed a valid kinyan. The Rashba compares this to one who says, "Give a maneh to so and so," which is not stronger than saying, "Give me a maneh." If one who says, "Give me a maneh, and if I do not repay by a specified date I will pay such and such an additional amount," is subject to the law of asmachta and is exempt, then certainly the same issue of asmachta can arise where he says, "Give a maneh to another person." In Shach, seif katan 24, there is also a textual error that must be corrected in accordance with this explanation.
