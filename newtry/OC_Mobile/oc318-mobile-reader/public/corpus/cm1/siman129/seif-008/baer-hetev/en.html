First. Meisharim writes that if a dayan erred and authorized the creditor to descend to the guarantor's property before suing the borrower, the creditor is removed from that property. This is evident from the incident in Bava Batra 172a. Darkei Moshe; Sema.
And a guarantor. A kablan, however, is not discharged, as Rama writes in seif 15. The Mechaber's statement in seif 16 that if the kablan claims that perhaps the borrower paid, his claim is accepted, is different because there is a defect in the creditor's case there: he had a document and claims that it was lost. So writes the Sema. See Responsa Rashach, book II, siman 27; Responsa Maharit, siman 112; and Responsa Maharshadam, siman 39.
I borrowed. The Sema explains that the guarantor admits that the borrower borrowed, and therefore the guarantor is liable, since there is no possibility that the borrower paid. One who says, “I never borrowed,” is regarded as saying, “I did not repay.” Darkei Moshe. See the Shach.
Liable. The Rashba concludes there: Although the Sages said that one does not collect from a guarantor first, seemingly indicating that the guarantor is not bound while the borrower remains liable, this is not so. That rule applies where the borrower himself is liable, but here the guarantor obligated himself in something for which the borrower was not liable. This also resolves the possible difficulty from siman 49, where the Tur and Mechaber rule concerning a guarantor for two persons having the same name that just as the creditor cannot sue either one, he cannot sue the guarantor. There the debt rests on the borrower, and the guarantor is not liable unless the borrower lacks means of payment. See siman 130 seif 2 in the gloss. Sema. The Shach writes that Bach ruled, as practical halachah, that money may not be extracted from the guarantor unless a kinyan was made from him, even if he became guarantor at the time the article was borrowed, and this appears correct. See Responsa Rashach, book II, siman 11. The Shach further maintains that the difficulty from siman 49 remains, as is evident from Bechorot 48a, and Bach agrees. The Taz answers that here the lender and borrower arranged their transaction in a manner involving asmachta, while the guarantor undertook only for the stated sum and not for the underlying asmachta. This is evident from the wording of the responsum. He further writes that a kinyan was made from the guarantor for this obligation, and therefore it was not an asmachta as to him. One cannot object that the borrower's own obligation should likewise cease to be an asmachta because there was a kinyan, since only a kinyan before an eminent beit din removes the defect of asmachta.
As a witness. The Sema writes that Rashba's responsum does not say that the creditor signed as a witness. Rather, he signed the deed of sale to indicate his consent to the borrower's sale to the purchaser. The Rashba therefore ruled that the creditor caused his own loss by releasing and waiving his claim against the purchaser and could no longer collect from the guarantor. This also avoids a contradiction with siman 111 seif 17, where a borrower sold his property and the creditor signed as a witness on the deed, yet did not lose his rights because he may say, “The second purchaser is more convenient for me.” Thus the present case cannot concern merely signing as a witness, and Rama's wording is imprecise. The Taz writes that Rama's words are correct. Here the creditor does not claim that the second purchaser is more convenient for him, but expressly says that this purchaser is equally acceptable and that he willingly canceled the lien because he relied on the guarantor. Therefore the guarantor is discharged, since by the creditor's own account he caused his loss. A similar principle appears in siman 146 seif 17.
