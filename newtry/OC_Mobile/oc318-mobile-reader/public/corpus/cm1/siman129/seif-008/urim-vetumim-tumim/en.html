But if the borrower says, "I never borrowed," the guarantor must pay. Regarding a non-Jewish borrower, the Baal HaTerumot, Gate 35, part 1, law 26, leaves this question unresolved in the name of Raavyah. See Bach and Shach seif katan 19, who explain that perhaps the guarantor undertook liability only if the non-Jew would admit the loan, but not if he denied it. This is strained. Once the Jew became guarantor, why should the non-Jew's later denial matter? In my view the doubt is different. If the Jewish guarantor was physically present when a Jew lent money to the non-Jew without witnesses, accepted the guaranty, and remained silent, there is no doubt that the guarantor must pay when the non-Jew later denies. Rather, the case is where the guarantor said to the Jew, "Lend to the non-Jew and I will be guarantor." The lender then met the non-Jew privately and lent without witnesses. The guarantor later knows that the loan was in fact made because the non-Jew himself told him afterward, but now the non-Jew denies the loan. The guarantor can argue: "When I told you to lend to the non-Jew, I meant that you should lend before witnesses so that he would have no ability to deny. By lending without witnesses you caused your own loss." With a Jewish borrower, who retains a presumption of honesty and regarding whom "the remnant of Israel will not act unjustly," when a person says simply "give him" or "lend him," we do not infer a requirement of witnesses. The Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia says similarly in the discussion of a shopkeeper's ledger. But an ordinary non-Jew cannot be assumed to maintain the same halachic presumption of truthful repayment. A general instruction to lend him may therefore naturally mean to lend in a provable manner before witnesses, not privately. The guarantor has a plausible defense that the lender departed from the contemplated form of lending and thereby created the possibility of denial. This is why the matter requires study. Admittedly, the Baal HaTerumot's brief wording makes it difficult to be certain that this was his precise case. Nevertheless, the legal distinction itself appears correct even if it was not what the source intended, and the issue warrants study. This is precise. There is another consequence. Our ruling here, that if the borrower denies ever borrowing while the guarantor admits the loan the guarantor must pay, appears to contradict the Shach above in siman 49, seif katan 20. There the Shach discusses two people with the same name, Yosef ben Shimon, where one guaranteed the debt of the other and it is unknown which is the borrower and which the guarantor. He rules that the creditor cannot collect. That seems incorrect in light of our rule. The creditor can approach one Yosef ben Shimon and say, "You are the one who borrowed from me. Pay me." That person must either admit or deny. If he denies having borrowed, he necessarily identifies himself as the guarantor of the other Yosef ben Shimon. The creditor can then approach the second. If the second admits the loan, he must pay, and certainly the guarantor is also identified. If the second denies the loan, the creditor returns to the first and says, "You yourself admitted that the second was the borrower and that you guaranteed his debt. Had he admitted the loan he would have been liable. His denial is treated as a statement that he did not repay, under the rule that one who says 'I never borrowed' is as though he says 'I did not repay.' You, who admit his borrowing and your guaranty, must therefore pay." This seems exactly like the ordinary case where the guarantor admits that the borrower received the money but the borrower denies the loan. The borrower escapes because of his denial, while the guarantor remains liable. I do not see a distinction. This is not the same as the Gemara's case of one guarantor who guaranteed debts involving two people with the same name and does not know which of them is truly the borrower. There the guarantor himself does not know which person is making a truthful denial and which is lying. He can say, "How can I pay for the borrower when I do not know who the borrower is?" Since the true borrower has not been identified, the guarantor's obligation cannot be fixed despite the fact that he would be liable one way or the other. In our case, however, the first person's own admission is like one hundred witnesses. By denying that he borrowed, he necessarily admits that the second was the borrower and that he was the guarantor. The second's denial does not undo that admission. The first knows, according to his own words, that the second borrowed and did not repay. He is therefore an ordinary guarantor who admits the loan while the borrower denies, and he must pay. One should not object from the Gemara in Bekhorot concerning one Yosef ben Shimon who purchased a field from the other. Why not say there too: "You admit that you were not the borrower, so the other was the borrower, and this field is therefore encumbered to me. What difference does it make that he denies the loan when you yourself know the field is subject to the debt?" The answer is that when the alleged borrower denies the loan and there is no effective proof through the document identifying him, the claim against him has the status of an oral loan. Even if the loan truly occurred, an oral loan does not create the same collectible lien against land in a purchaser's hands. Since the document is not effective to establish which person borrowed, the land lien falls away even if the purchaser privately knows which one borrowed. Our guaranty case is different because the guarantor's personal obligation depends on his own admission of the loan, not on enforcing a lien against purchased land. The Shach's ruling therefore requires study.
With respect to a borrower, this is an asmachta, etc. The guarantor is liable to pay. The Shach in seif katan 20 agrees with the Bach that a kinyan is required, as stated in the responsum of the Rashba where there was a kinyan. In truth, the Bach writes that a kinyan before an important beit din is required in order to remove the asmachta, and he understands the Rashba's reference to a kinyan as meaning before an important beit din. This, however, is not implied by the responsum, which mentions only a kinyan. It must therefore be that the Rashba follows the view of the Raavad below, siman 131 seif 12, and as appears from the Rama, that guaranty is not subject to asmachta. It therefore appears that the Rama understood the Rashba according to the Rashba's own position, as the Maggid Mishneh writes on Rambam, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah, in the name of the Rashba, Ramban, and Ran in chapter HaNose, that a person cannot obligate himself in something for which he is not already liable merely by words, but only through a kinyan. They bring proof from one who agrees that an unpaid custodian shall have the liability of a borrower. See the Ran there at length. Accordingly, one who undertakes to pay for an object more than its value cannot bind himself to the excess merely by words. The benefit he receives from being trusted relates only to the object itself and its value. To obligate himself beyond its value requires a kinyan, for by what otherwise would he become liable for more than the object's worth? Mere words do not suffice. Hence they required a kinyan. See above, siman 40, Shach seif katan 4. According to the Rambam, however, a person may undertake a new obligation by a verbal declaration before witnesses. As the Ran explains his view, it is not specifically where a kinyan was made from him, for saying, You are my witnesses, likewise suffices when an unpaid custodian agrees to assume the liability of a borrower. If so, here too, at the time the object is delivered, he may undertake the obligation verbally. Since we rule above in siman 40 like the Rambam, as appears from the unqualified ruling of the Shulchan Aruch and Rama, the Rama properly omitted the requirement of a kinyan here. According to the Rambam, where one undertakes an obligation he did not previously owe, no kinyan is needed at the time the object is delivered, and the obligation takes effect through words. This is correct. Examine it carefully.
