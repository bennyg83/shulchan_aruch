He should not first claim, etc. Meisharim writes that if a judge erred and allowed the creditor to descend to the guarantor's property before first suing the borrower, that collection is reversed. This is evident from the Gemara in Bava Batra. See Darchei Moshe and Sema.
And he and the guarantor are discharged. But a kablan guarantor is not discharged, as the Rama writes in seif 15. The Mechaber's statement in seif 16 that if the kablan claims that perhaps the borrower paid, the law is with him, is different because there is an adverse circumstance there: the creditor had a note and claims it was lost. So write the Sema and Shach. See Responsa Rashach, part 2, siman 27, and Responsa Maharit, siman 112.
I never borrowed. The Sema explains that the guarantor admits that the loan was made, and therefore he is liable, because there is no concern that perhaps it was repaid, since anyone who says, 'I never borrowed,' is as one who says, 'I never repaid.' See Darchei Moshe. Regarding a gentile borrower, the Baal HaTerumot leaves the law requiring study. The Bach and Shach explain that perhaps the guarantor undertook responsibility only if the gentile admitted the loan, not if he denied it. This explanation is strained. I therefore concluded in Tumim that the case is where the guarantor said, 'Lend to the gentile and I will be guarantor,' and the creditor lent privately without witnesses. The gentile now denies the loan. Even if the guarantor later learns that the loan really was made, perhaps he is exempt because the creditor should have lent before witnesses, preventing the gentile from denying. This concern applies to a gentile. With a Jew, when the guarantor simply says, 'Give him,' the creditor may give without witnesses. See there at length. Although this is somewhat difficult to fit into the Baal HaTerumot's wording, the law itself appears correct to me. It requires study.
The guarantor is obligated to pay. See Tumim, where I raised a powerful difficulty from this ruling against the Shach above in siman 79, who holds that if one person named Yosef ben Shimon guaranteed another Yosef ben Shimon and we do not know which is the borrower and which the guarantor, the guarantor is exempt. This requires study. I already raised the issue above in siman 49 and repeat it here more fully because it remains very difficult.
The guarantor is obligated to pay. The Sema cites the Rashba's responsum, which concludes: Although the Sages said that one does not collect from a guarantor first, this does not mean that a guarantor's obligation exists only when there is an obligor. Rather, whenever the borrower is himself liable, one first collects from him; but in this case the guarantor personally obligated himself for something the borrower was not liable for. The Shach, seif katan 20, agrees with the Bach that a kinyan is required. The Bach further holds that it must be a kinyan before a beit din chashuv, and understands the Rashba this way, in order to remove the asmachta. In Tumim I concluded that this is not the implication of the Rashba. Rather, the Rashba follows his own view that a person cannot create a new personal obligation merely by words without a kinyan. Therefore, how would the guarantor become obligated for more than the actual value without a kinyan? According to our ruling like the Rambam, however, that a person can obligate himself by words even in something he does not otherwise owe, the Rama properly omitted any requirement of kinyan.
And the lender himself signed as a witness. The Sema writes that in the Rashba's responsum the lender did not sign as a witness. Rather, he signed the deed of sale to indicate that he agreed to the borrower's sale to the purchaser. Therefore the Rashba ruled that the lender caused his own loss by releasing and waiving his claim against the purchaser, and could no longer collect from the guarantor. This also resolves the apparent contradiction with siman 111, seif 17, where the Mechaber rules that if a borrower sold his property and the lender signed as a witness on the deed, the lender did not lose his rights because he may say, 'The second purchaser was more convenient for me.' The present case must therefore not concern signature merely as a witness, and the Rama's wording to that effect is imprecise. See Sema. See Taz for his resolution, which requires study.
