Even if it remained in his possession for a long time. See Shach, seif katan 2, who questions Sema. Shach did not see Perishah, where all of this is explained in siman 157. Sema should be read as follows: "Although I wrote in Perishah, siman 157, that after a very long period people ordinarily become particular, nevertheless..." His intent follows Derishah there, where Tur is understood to disagree with Tosafot and to hold that even after a long time the partner is not believed. The reason is the continuing chazakah that the property remains partnership property. Once it is known to have belonged to the partnership, the fact that it sits in one partner's premises does not prove exclusive ownership. The other partner may have allowed the location as a loan, rental, or for another reason. This resolves Shach's objection. Shach suggests that Tur speaks of a long period during which partners still generally are not particular, while Tosafot speak of an even longer period when they are. That is strained. Tur says without qualification, "even if it remained a long time," and in siman 157 he uses the same language in the very context where Tosafot say that over time partners do become particular. Shach further argues that once partners ordinarily become particular, the matter should be like property belonging to a stranger. This is not compelling. Partnership possession differs from ordinary possession, like the rule in siman 150, seif 3, that property originally received as a pledge can lack ordinary chazakah indefinitely. Even according to those who disagree there regarding land, the distinction is because land chazakah rests on the owner's failure to protest while another consumes produce for three years. Movable-property chazakah rests mainly on the question, "How did this item come into your hands?" Once there is an established explanation for how it came into a partner's hands, later delay does not by itself create a new chazakah. The rule that a craftsman who leaves his trade, or a craftsman's son, may eventually acquire chazakah is likewise stated only according to one opinion, and even that view can be limited to cases where the person's legal identity and circumstances changed. So long as he remains a craftsman or partner, the original reason for possession remains and there is no chazakah no matter how long the item remains. Therefore Sema's ruling is primary, and Taz also appears to agree. If, however, there are witnesses that one partner specifically delivered the object to the other as a deposit, and witnesses now see and identify the object, even Shach agrees that the holder is not believed to claim purchase. Even an ordinary stranger, who certainly would object to prolonged possession, must return an item where there are both delivery witnesses and witnesses who currently saw it. Shach's discussion concerns only a case where witnesses know that the item was partnership property, not where they know exactly how it entered the holder's possession.
Provided he cannot claim, "It never happened." At first glance one might try to prove that, if the stones did not remain in his possession for an unusually long time, even a migo does not help. Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Batra explain that the Mishnah teaches that one partner is not believed to say that he alone built the wall even through a migo of purchase, and they add that although at the beginning of HaBayit VeHaAliyah partners are said not to be particular with one another, the case there concerns property that remained in one partner's possession beyond the ordinary period. This is difficult whichever way one reads it. If there are witnesses that one partner removed the stones when the other was absent and witnesses currently see the stones and identify them as the stones of the joint wall, then why does duration matter? Even against an ordinary stranger who is certainly particular, witnesses to delivery and current possession defeat a purchase claim, as explained in siman 133. If there are no witnesses currently seeing the stones, Tosafot could simply have said that the holder has a migo that he returned or never received them, and the Mishnah nevertheless teaches that his claim, "I alone built it," is not believed because it is a migo against witnesses. Why invent the new distinction that partners become particular only after a long period? Maggid Mishneh, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 157, also specifically requires a long period, and this suggests that before such a period even a migo may not help. Rashi at the beginning of HaBayit VeHaAliyah gives a reason: partners are not particular about lending one another space, so even when the property sits in one partner's domain it remains legally regarded as sitting in the domain of both. If so, a migo would be a migo used to extract property from the other partner's effective possession, similar to Tumim's rule concerning freely roaming livestock. Tosafot therefore needed to establish their case after a long period, when partners do become particular and the holder's domain begins to support his possession. Nevertheless, Shulchan Aruch and the general language of the poskim imply that a migo can help even without such a long delay. The matter therefore requires care.
