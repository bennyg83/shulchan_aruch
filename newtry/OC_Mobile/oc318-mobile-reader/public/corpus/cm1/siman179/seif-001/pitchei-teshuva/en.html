One partner has no possessory claim. See Responsa Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat 12, concerning Reuven, Shimon, and Levi, who were partners in a commercial undertaking at the court of a nobleman. They executed a fully effective partnership agreement under which each would bear one third of the expenses and receive one third of all payments. Levi was the principal active partner. When the account reached a very large sum, the partners obtained from the authorities an assignment directing that ten thousand reichsthalers be paid elsewhere toward the debt. Levi took the assignment in his own name alone, without their knowledge. When they learned of this they were very angry and demanded that he hand it over. He refused, and only after great pressure gave them a written acknowledgment that they had a share in it according to the accounting that would be made between them. They then argued before beit din that they did not feel secure leaving such a valuable document in his possession, especially since it stood in his name alone. They asked that he surrender it because they could not collect without Levi's participation. Levi answered that according to his accounting he had already advanced much more than his one third share, so that most of the money represented by the assignment belonged to him, and he therefore refused to deliver it. The Shev Yaakov ruled at length that the two partners were correct. Levi was at least obligated to place the assignment in escrow until collection, so that all three would collect together. He had acted improperly by having it issued in his name alone. Although the others had originally trusted him to conduct the business, that trust concerned matters they could investigate before paying him money on the basis of his accounting. They had not entrusted their money itself to his exclusive control, certainly not such a large sum. The principal basis of trust in a partnership is the stringent partners' oath. That safeguard does not adequately protect them regarding this assignment, because Levi might claim that the instrument was lost. According to the Rama and many early authorities, one who negligently loses documents may be exempt from payment. The Shach disputes this and rules like the Rambam that negligence does create liability, and he likewise requires an oath that the document is not in the guardian's possession. Nevertheless, Levi, as the party in possession, could argue that the law follows the Rama and the majority of those early authorities. It was therefore reasonable for the partners not to leave the instrument in his hands even for one day. For the same reason, Levi was not required to hand it to either one of the other partners individually. Rather, it should be deposited with a trustworthy person, with the community, or with beit din, or placed in an iron box to which each partner holds a key. One cannot answer that Levi could not falsely claim loss because he would then be unable to present the assignment for collection. He could collude with the person against whom the assignment was drawn to pay him its value privately, as the Sema explains in siman 64. The Shach's objection there is only that this is not a valid migo, but the possibility of collusion still creates a real concern. Nor may Levi claim that because he physically holds the assignment he is believed to say that most of it belongs to him because he advanced more than his share. There are two answers. First, even according to his own claim he admits that the others own part of it, and they do not wish even that smaller share left under his exclusive control. Second, he is not considered a possessor with a superior claim at all. Since it is known that the instrument arose from the partnership business, it remains jointly held by all partners in equal shares. As stated in Tur and Shulchan Aruch, siman 179, one partner has no chazakah in property known to belong to the partnership. In this case Levi also has no migo that he could have denied the matter entirely or claimed that he had returned it, because the partners hold his written acknowledgment that the assignment is in his possession. Nor can one say that he has a migo that he could have claimed the entire debt represented by the assignment belonged to him because he had already repaid the other partners their shares. That would amount to a claim of repayment, but it is implausible here. The assignment had not yet been collected, so he had no obligation to advance from his own pocket a very large sum before collection. Only if the partners had transferred their shares to Levi in exchange for such payment could that argument arise, and that would be equivalent to saying, "I purchased your shares." A partner is not believed to make such a claim even regarding an item in his possession that is known to have come from the partnership, as explained in siman 179. He further rejects the opposing argument that siman 179 applies only where witnesses saw each partner contribute money and saw them purchase the partnership property. That distinction is without substance. The Shulchan Aruch speaks there of a case in which partnership ownership was established by witnesses to the partners' contributions because there was no written partnership agreement concerning a known business. Here, however, they possessed a written partnership agreement governing this very commercial enterprise, and nothing could more clearly identify the asset as partnership property. He shows the same principle from Sefer HaTerumot and from the Ran in the chapter HaBayit VeHaAliyah. The opponent also argued that the fact that the assignment was issued in Levi's name strengthened his possession. That too is rejected. Even where an instrument stands in the name of the eldest brother, this is no proof against property known to belong to the brothers or to a partnership. The rule that registration in one person's name can have evidentiary significance applies only where the property is not already known to belong to the joint estate. At the end of the responsum he records that another great authority agreed with him that the ruling was correct and that Levi must place the assignment in escrow, adding that one who does not rule such a case in this manner is not fit to judge.
