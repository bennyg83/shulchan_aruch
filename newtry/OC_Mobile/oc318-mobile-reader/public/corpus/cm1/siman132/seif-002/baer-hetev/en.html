By document. The general rule is that a husband is treated as a purchaser of his wife's property for this purpose. Therefore, an oral debt owed by the wife cannot be collected from her husband, while a written debt can. It follows that even a written debt may be collected only from land that she brought into the marriage, or, under Talmudic law, from movables that she had bound together with land. See simanim 60 and 113. The Sema adds that even after the enactment protecting ordinary purchasers from collection out of movables that had been bound with land, that enactment should apply only to a purchaser, not an heir. Here, where otherwise the creditor would suffer loss, the husband should not have been treated as a purchaser, as discussed in siman 103. Yet Rama at the end of siman 112 implies that even for this purpose the husband is treated as a purchaser. See siman 114 seif katan 2. The Shach questions the Sema. By strict law the husband is an heir, and the only reason an oral creditor loses is that he should not have lent without a document and thus caused his own loss. Once he did lend by document, he should collect even if he did not expressly bind movables with land. All this concerns movables no longer in existence. If the actual movables are still present, the creditor collects from them even on an oral loan. The wife's loan is no worse than money she borrowed, where if the actual money remains it can be recovered. Likewise, when she married, the creditor's lien rested on these movables and they remain present.
