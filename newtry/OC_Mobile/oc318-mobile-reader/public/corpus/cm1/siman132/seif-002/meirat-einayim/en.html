Her law is like one who borrowed and then married. This is when the guaranty obligation has already taken effect, namely when the borrower has no means to pay. Then her case resembles one who borrowed before marriage. But if the borrower still has the money, it does not resemble a loan, for where money from a loan is still intact the husband must return it to the lender, as Rambam writes in chapter 26 of Hilchot Malveh and Rama in Even HaEzer 91:4, even where it is an oral loan. By a guaranty, while the money remains with the borrower there is no basis to compel either the woman or her husband, only the borrower.
She is not obligated to pay, etc. The general principle is that with respect to this matter a husband is treated as a purchaser of his wife's property. Therefore an oral debt against her cannot be collected from him, since her property in his hands is encumbered property; a written debt can be collected from it. It follows that even a guaranty or loan established by document cannot be collected from the husband except from land she brought into the marriage, or, under Talmudic law, where she encumbered movable property to the creditor together with land, as with any purchaser. See above, siman 60, and siman 113. Yet it appears that even according to the later enactment protecting purchasers from collection of movable property encumbered together with land because of market stability, this applies specifically to a purchaser, not to an heir. A husband was not treated as a purchaser where that designation would cause loss to others holding valid debt instruments, as I wrote above in siman 103. However, Rama's wording at the end of siman 112 in the gloss implies that even in this respect a husband is treated as a purchaser. See the Derishah.
