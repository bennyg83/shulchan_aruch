See Baer Heitev quoting the Shach, who questions the Sema and argues that where the loan was made by deed, collection should be possible even if movable property was not expressly made subject through agav. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, in his glosses to Bava Batra 139b, writes that in his opinion the Sema is correct. The rule that a husband is not treated as a purchaser where doing so would cause loss to others applies where, if he were treated as an heir, he would be liable by the original law. Then classifying him as a purchaser would create the loss. But where even an heir would not be liable under Talmudic law and liability exists only because of a later enactment, one may say that the husband, who is generally treated as a purchaser, was never included in that enactment. Thus, without an agav clause, the creditor does not collect from the husband's movable property, because movable property was not subject to creditors by original law and only the Geonic enactment created such collection. Conversely, where original law would allow collection even from purchasers and only a later ordinance limits collection, one may say that the husband remains an heir for that ordinance. This also answers the Sema's difficulty from the Gemara. Rabbi Akiva Eiger further questions the Shach's statement that if the movable property itself remains in existence the creditor may collect it even on an oral loan. The Rambam and Mordechai indicate that the special rule concerning loan money still intact applies specifically to the very money lent, in which the lender retains a particular connection. Even there, Nimukei Yosef and Maggid Mishneh dispute the rule because the money was given to be spent. But ordinary movable property that the woman brought into the marriage is certainly governed by the husband's status as purchaser. Tumim, Netivot HaMishpat, and Beit Meir likewise challenge the Shach. See also Beit Shmuel and the responsa cited there.
