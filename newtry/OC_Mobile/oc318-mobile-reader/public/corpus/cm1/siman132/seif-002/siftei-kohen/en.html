She is not obligated to pay. The Sema, seif katan 4, writes that even after the enactment protecting commerce, under which a creditor may not collect from a purchaser movable property encumbered to him together with land, this applies only to a purchaser, but he may collect from an heir. A husband was not treated as a purchaser where another would thereby suffer a loss. This is correct, as stated at the end of the chapter Yesh Nochalin, that in a case involving another's loss the husband is treated as an heir. The reason for the opinion that an oral loan is not collected here is that the lender caused his own loss by lending without a document to a woman who was likely to marry. The Sema's words, 'But from what the Rama wrote above at the end of siman 112,' mean that in the Rama's view the husband was treated as a purchaser even where another suffers loss. He should also have referred to the Tur and Mechaber there, for their words likewise imply this, and they are based upon the Baal HaTerumot. I have already raised a serious objection to their words there. Moreover, even where the movables were not expressly encumbered together with land, the Sema and Bach assume that the creditor cannot collect them from the husband, but this requires study. It appears that he may collect, since by strict law the husband is an heir. The only reason an oral lender loses is that he should not have lent without a document. Where he did lend by document, even without a clause encumbering movables together with land, he should collect. This is certainly so according to what I wrote at the end of siman 112 in the name of Rabbi Mordechai Yaffe and the glosses to the Perisha, that the reason an oral debt is not collected is that the husband may plead payment. I wrote there that Responsa Rashba and Ramban imply the same. It therefore follows plainly that a written debt may be collected from movables even without such an encumbrance. All this concerns movables no longer extant in their original form. If the movables remain identifiable, the creditor collects even an oral loan from them. This is no worse than money borrowed by her, which is collected if the money itself remains. Here too, when she married, his lien already rested upon the movables, and since they remain extant he collects from them.
