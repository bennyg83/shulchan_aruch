The husband is treated as though the wife borrowed and afterward married. At the end of Perek Yesh Nochalin the Gemara asks whether a husband is a purchaser or heir. Rav Pappa points to Ravin's report that a widow is supported from the deceased's property, even after the daughter who inherited later married, which implies that the husband is treated as heir where that preserves the widow's maintenance. Abaye initially questions whether this proves anything, since the Mishnah concerning Jubilee says the husband does not return his wife's inheritance at Jubilee, which would suggest heir status. Rav Ashi ultimately answers that the Sages sometimes treat the husband as heir and sometimes as purchaser, choosing the classification that benefits him. For Jubilee they treat him as heir to avoid his loss. For the enactment of Usha they treat him as purchaser to protect his rights against purchasers from his wife. In Ravin's case they treat him as heir so the widow does not lose maintenance. The purchasers from the wife are the ones who caused their own loss by buying property from a married woman. Rashbam explains the Jubilee proof by saying that if the husband were a purchaser from the time of marriage, that purchaser status would continue even after the wife's death and the property should return at Jubilee. Tosafot objects that during her lifetime he has only fruits, and a fruit interest is not ownership of the body. The Shach, in siman 103, seeks to defend Rashbam by saying that according to Rosh and Tur the husband is already purchaser of the body during the wife's lifetime, because after the enactment of Usha he can remove purchasers of melog property even while she lives. Thus after her death the purchaser status would merely continue. This does not fully resolve Tosafot's objection. Even if the husband is called purchaser of the body during the marriage, in the Jubilee era any purchase itself is only temporary and therefore has the legal character of a fruit interest. Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute whether such a temporary Jubilee purchase is enough to count as ownership of the body for first fruits. According to the view that a fruit interest is not ownership of the body, after the wife's death the husband could still receive the actual body by inheritance. Thus saying he was a purchaser during life does not necessarily mean his post death ownership remains purchase. Rashbam's words can instead be defended through Rabbi Meir's own position. The Mishnah that says a husband does not return the inheritance at Jubilee is Rabbi Meir. Rabbi Meir holds that a fruit interest can count like ownership of the body. The Gemara in Gittin demonstrates this from one who purchased a field from his father and then the father died. According to Rabbi Meir, the earlier fruit purchase is strong enough that the son's later inheritance does not create a new inherited body in the ordinary way. Likewise, the Mishnah there says a firstborn takes double in land that returns to the father at Jubilee, and Rashi explains that if the temporary purchase were full body ownership the land would be only expected property and the firstborn could not take double. Therefore, according to Rabbi Meir, if the husband were a purchaser during the wife's lifetime, even a purchase that in the Jubilee regime grants only use would be treated as sufficiently body like to prevent the wife's later death from supplying him a new inherited corpus. His status would remain purchase and the land should return at Jubilee. Since the Mishnah says it does not return, the husband must be treated as heir. Rashbam is therefore correct. This also explains why the ordinary argument that a debtor may intentionally grant the creditor strong rights so that a future heir treats the debt as presently possessed cannot simply be imported here. Where Rabbi Meir treats a fruit interest as body ownership, the prior holder has no separate body left to transmit. The husband's inheritance cannot begin only at death because, under the purchaser hypothesis, the legally body like right already existed during marriage. Thus Rashbam's reasoning stands.
