Her law is like one who borrowed and then married. This is so only once the guaranty's liability has matured, meaning the principal borrower lacks the means to pay. If the borrower still has the money, it is not comparable to a woman who borrowed and later married. With an actual loan, if the loan money itself remains in her hands, the husband must return it to the lender, even if the loan was oral. But with guaranty, so long as the money remains with the principal borrower there is no present claim against the woman or her husband. Sema. I discussed this at length in Even HaEzer.
She is not obligated to pay. The general principle is that the husband is treated as a purchaser of his wife's property. Therefore an oral debt against her cannot be collected from him, because her assets in his hands are like encumbered property. A written debt can be collected from the land she brought into the marriage, or, under Talmudic law, from movables that she encumbered together with land. Sema and Bach hold that even after later enactments, the husband remains like a purchaser for this purpose. The Shach disagrees in some cases, reasoning that by strict law the husband is an heir and was treated as a purchaser only where the creditor caused his own loss by lending orally. See Tumim. I concluded like Sema and Bach. I also rejected the Shach's further suggestion that if the actual movables remain identifiable, collection is allowed even on an oral debt. In my view, even with an actual loan the husband is not liable from those movables, all the more so in guaranty.
