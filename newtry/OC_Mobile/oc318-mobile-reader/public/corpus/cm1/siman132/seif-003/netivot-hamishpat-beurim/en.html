He may collect from whichever of them he wishes. Tumim questions this ruling. Maggid Mishneh derives it from the case in the chapter Beit Kor concerning two brothers who divided inherited property and a creditor who comes to collect, where the creditor may collect from whichever brother he chooses because a person's property stands as guarantor for his debts. He therefore applies the same rule to two guarantors and does not accept Tosafot's explanation there that the case concerns an apotiki. Yet Mechaber rules in siman 107 that even between two heirs a creditor may take only half from each, where each possesses enough land. Why then does Mechaber rule here that with two guarantors the creditor may collect entirely from whichever one he chooses? Ketzot HaChoshen asks the same question in siman 107. In my view Mechaber's rulings are consistent. The rule in siman 107 that a creditor takes half from each of two brothers, although the Gemara in Beit Kor indicates that he can collect from either one, rests on the principle that brothers who divide an inheritance are treated as purchasers because there is no retroactive clarification. Rashi at the end of Gittin, chapter HaSholeach, explains that each brother is regarded as having inherited half of his allotted portion and as having purchased the other half from his brother in exchange for the rights he relinquished in the other brother's portion. Tur in siman 107 states that where an heir sells inherited property, the creditor cannot collect from the heir, even if the sale proceeds remain intact in his hands, but must proceed against the purchaser. It is also explained there that if the heir sold one parcel and inherited property remains with the heirs, the creditor cannot proceed against the purchaser because the purchaser can say, 'I left you property from which to collect.' Therefore, after division, each brother is treated as holding half of his field by inheritance and half by purchase from his brother. The creditor can seize only the inherited half. As to the purchased half, the brother may say that property from which to collect remains with the other brother, namely the half inherited by him. Thus the creditor must proceed against the other brother for his inherited half. Even according to Tosafot in Gittin, who explain that there is also uncertainty whether everything received by each brother really originated with the other and was acquired from him, while his own inherited portion is in the other brother's hands, the creditor may at least collect half in either event. If the entire portion was purchased from the other brother and nothing inherited remains with that brother, the creditor can seize it because there is no remaining inherited property elsewhere. But he cannot take the second half, because perhaps an inherited half remains with the other brother. Accordingly, even where the brothers divided many fields and each holds property far exceeding the debt, it remains possible that only property equal to half the debt in each brother's hands is inherited, with the remainder purchased from his brother. Collection cannot be made from purchased property while inherited property remains in the other brother's possession. For this very reason, the chapter Yesh Bechor teaches that an oral loan cannot be collected from brothers after division because they are treated as purchasers. The Gemara in Beit Kor, which allows the creditor to collect from whichever brother he chooses, follows the opinion that there is retroactive clarification, under which the brothers are not treated as purchasers.
