He may collect from whichever one of them he wishes. There is some difficulty in the Shulchan Aruch's ruling. Rambam's proof for this law, as the Maggid Mishneh and Gidulei Terumah explain, is the case of brothers who divided an estate and a creditor came and took one brother's share. Since the creditor may proceed against whichever brother he wishes even though a person's property is viewed as his guarantor, Rambam infers that where there are two guarantors the creditor may likewise proceed against either one. Rambam was not satisfied with Tosafot's answers that the case concerns a field made an apotiki or a situation where one heir lacks land of the proper quality. But above, siman 107 seif 7, the Mechaber rules that a creditor does not collect the whole debt from one heir where each heir received enough land for a complete field. Rather, he takes a proportional field from each. If so, the same should apply to two guarantors, since Rambam's rule was derived from heirs. Yet above the Mechaber ruled like Tosafot, limiting full collection from one heir to an apotiki, while here he cites Rambam first and presents the contrary view only as Some disagree, implying that Rambam's rule is primary. The Maggid Mishneh's own words also seem inconsistent. In Hilchot Malveh VeLoveh chapter 25, he writes that Ramban disputes Rambam and interprets the case of brothers as an apotiki. Yet in Hilchot Nachalot chapter 10 he writes in the name of Ramban and Rashba that where each brother has intermediate quality land, the creditor may collect from whichever one he wishes, apparently agreeing with Rambam. Knesset HaGedolah already raises this difficulty. In truth, however, there is no contradiction. Ramban himself, in his novellae to Bava Batra, first asks why the creditor takes one brother's share rather than proportionally from both, and answers either that only one brother had intermediate land or that one brother's land was made an apotiki. He then adds that where both have equivalent intermediate land and the creditor comes against them, he may choose which one to collect from, citing the Yerushalmi that where one document covers two fields, beit din may assign whichever field it chooses, and if the creditor himself sues one owner he may collect from that owner. At first glance this seems self contradictory. If the creditor may choose either brother, why did Ramban need to establish the earlier case as an apotiki? The Nimukei Yosef seems similarly inconsistent. In Bava Kamma he explains the case of the creditor taking one brother's share as an apotiki, since otherwise the creditor collects proportionally, as indicated by the rule that a firstborn contributes a double share toward a debt. Yet in Bava Batra he writes that the division is subject to the known rule that a creditor may seize from whichever heir he chooses, citing the same Yerushalmi. The resolution is that the right of choice exists where there are only two fields, one in each heir's possession. Since a creditor cannot be given half of a field when that half would be below the minimum legal size, he must take one complete field, and then he may choose from whom to take it. This is the precise case described by the Yerushalmi, one document on two fields. But where each heir has multiple complete fields and the debt can be satisfied proportionally by taking a whole field from each, the creditor must do so and cannot take everything from one heir. This is also the meaning of Ramban and Nimukei Yosef. Their language is broad, but their proof from the Yerushalmi, which speaks specifically of two fields, makes the limitation clear. This also resolves the Rosh. Gidulei Terumah asks that the Rosh, both in his rulings and responsum 79, writes that where each brother's proportional share of the debt can be collected as a complete field, the creditor must take from each. Nevertheless the Rosh accepts Tosafot's answer that the case where the creditor took one brother's share involves an apotiki. Why not answer that each brother's proportional part was only half a field, so the creditor could take a whole field from one? Pilpula Charifta also notices this and suggests that once the Rosh adopted the distinction between a full field and half a field, he withdrew from Tosafot's apotiki answer. That is strained, and cannot fit the responsum where the Rosh states both points. Rather, the Gemara's phrase that the creditor took one brother's share implies a substantial complete share. If each half were below the minimum field size, then according to Rabbi Asi's rule that the dispossessed brother takes one quarter in land and one quarter in money, his quarter of the remaining land would likewise be below the minimum division size. Such a division could not be made; the law would instead be gud o agud, one party taking all the land and paying the other in money. Therefore the quarter of land mentioned there must itself constitute a legally divisible field. It follows that the full share seized by the creditor was several times that amount and thus plainly capable of proportional division. The Rosh's question is therefore strong, and the case must be an apotiki. This explains Ramban and Nimukei Yosef as well. When they say that where each brother has intermediate land the creditor may choose either one, they mean where each has only one complete field, so some single field must be taken. They do not mean where each has two or more complete fields, in which case one complete field is taken from each. The Yerushalmi itself supports this because it speaks only of one document over two fields, not four fields. The Nimukei Yosef's reason that the brothers divided knowing that a creditor might later take from whichever one he chooses likewise refers to the uncertainty present at the time of division. They did not know what creditor would come. A small creditor might come whose entire claim equals only one field, and such a creditor could choose either heir because he cannot take half a field. They therefore divided subject to the risk that one heir's allotted field might be taken. That same background assumption remains relevant even when a larger creditor later comes and takes an apotiki field. This is also the meaning of Tosafot in Bava Kamma, who explain that two purchasers of land are treated differently from a case where one took land and one money because with two land purchasers each is exposed to possible creditor collection and they divided with that risk in mind. Maharam of Lublin and Mahari Ben Lev ask that the Gemara there concerns an apotiki, in which case only one field is exposed. Gidulei Terumah correctly rejects Maharam's answer. The proper answer is that at the time of the division the parties did not know which creditor might later appear or which field might have been designated as an apotiki. Each knew that perhaps his own field would turn out to be the one specially designated and taken. They therefore divided on the understanding that the one whose field was seized would bear that loss. Tosafot's reasoning is sound. The Maggid Mishneh is likewise consistent. In Hilchot Nachalot, when he cites Ramban that if each has intermediate land the creditor may choose, he refers to the case of one document on two fields, one complete field in each heir's hands, exactly the situation Ramban derives from the Yerushalmi. In that setting there is no alternative to choosing one, since the creditor cannot be given half a field. But where the debt can be collected proportionally by taking a complete field from each, Ramban does not allow the creditor to collect all from one heir. Although the Maggid Mishneh's wording is unqualified, examination of Ramban's source makes the intended case clear. This returns us to the difficulty with the Shulchan Aruch. Since above he ruled like the authorities who distinguish between a proportional share that is less than a whole field and one that is itself a complete field, while here he rules even without any such limitation that one of two guarantors may be made to pay the entire debt, Rambam's broad guarantor rule appears inconsistent with the position adopted above. As practical law, the view of Tosafot, Ramban, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef appears more persuasive: where proportional collection can be made from each guarantor without fragmentation, the debt should be divided. The Tosefta cited by Ramban supports them, and so does the law that a firstborn contributes a double share toward a debt. Gidulei Terumah notes an apparent contradiction in the Rosh, because the Rosh initially uses that law as proof that the creditor collects proportionally, but later says that the baraita's purpose may merely be to teach that a firstborn bears a double obligation and not to dictate the creditor's collection method. In my view the Rosh's latter comment does not erase the earlier proof. The Tosafists certainly knew that possible explanation, but the continuation of the baraita, which allows the firstborn to renounce his double portion and thereby avoid the double contribution, shows that the distribution of payment is genuinely tied to the inherited shares. Thus the proportional rule remains significant. In any event, the weight of these authorities supports proportionate liability where each guarantor can satisfy his share. The Mechaber's unqualified preference here for Rambam therefore requires study.

The Shach lengthens in rejecting this, but no lengthy discussion is needed. The Maggid Mishneh explains Rambam, as stated above, as deriving his law from brothers who divided an estate, for a person's assets are his guarantors, and therefore a creditor may collect the whole debt from one brother. Yet there we rule explicitly that if one brother paid more than his share, he may recover the excess from the other brothers, as is clear from the Gemara and its entire discussion. Even according to the Nimukei Yosef, who explains that the brothers divided with this possibility in mind because the creditor may choose from whom to collect, their understanding was still that the one who paid would recover half from his fellow. The same must therefore apply to two guarantors, since Rambam's very rule concerning them is learned from the case of brothers. There is no basis to distinguish the cases. This is clear and simple.
