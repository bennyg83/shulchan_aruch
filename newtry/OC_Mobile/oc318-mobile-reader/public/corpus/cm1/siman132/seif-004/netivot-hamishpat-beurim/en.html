See Shach, seif katan 5, who writes that when the creditor collects the entire debt from one guarantor, that guarantor may claim half from the other even according to the opinion that the creditor could originally collect from either guarantor. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 77 questions this and compares it to two purchasers who bought two fields at the same time. If the creditor collects from one purchaser, that purchaser cannot recover from the other. This comparison is difficult. Two people who jointly become guarantors are exactly like two people who jointly borrow. What difference is there between two people saying, 'Give us money and we will owe you,' and their saying, 'Give money to so and so and we will owe you'? Telling a person to give money to a third party is legally equivalent to receiving it oneself. Thus, if a woman says, 'Give money to so and so and I will become betrothed to you,' the kiddushin is effective because it is as though she received the money herself. The same applies here. Even if the guaranty was undertaken after the loan, if two people jointly say, 'We obligate ourselves to you by kinyan sudar if this borrower does not pay,' they become guarantors for one another. This is no different from two joint borrowers, who are guarantors for one another as explained in siman 77, or from two people who jointly assume an obligation. But if each one separately became guarantor, one guarantor has no claim against the other. The same distinction applies if the creditor waives one guarantor's liability. If they jointly undertook guaranty by saying 'We are guarantors,' they are like joint borrowers. The comparison to two purchasers is entirely inapposite, because the purchasers certainly did not become partners in guaranty. Likewise, if each guarantor accepted liability separately and not in the presence of the other, one cannot later recover from the other. A purchaser whose field is first seized can reach a later purchaser only by virtue of warranty. In the present setting, warranty does not create such a claim. Even where a purchaser bought one field and left another with the borrower, and the creditor seized the purchaser's field because the other field was unavailable, the purchaser cannot recover from the borrower if he purchased without warranty, since a person may buy land even for a single day. Recovery exists only because the purchase included warranty, and even where nothing was stated, we presume that ordinary purchase was with warranty. Yet even an express warranty, in a place where documents do not record hours, takes effect only from the following day. Therefore, the first purchaser whose field was seized cannot necessarily recover from another purchaser who bought afterward that same day. None of this rationale applies to two people who jointly became guarantors. See Shach, seif katan 5, where there is a scribal error. It should read: 'Therefore, since this one paid the entire debt...' In the passage citing Rambam's view that where two became guarantors the creditor may collect from whichever he chooses, the word 'not' is also a scribal error.
