See Baer Heitev, who cites the Shach as saying that this requires study and that because the loan was made by document, collection can be made even though movable property was not encumbered together with land. See Rabbi Akiva Eiger's novellae, collected notes at the end of the volume, on Bava Batra 139b, who writes that in his view the Sema is correct. Only where movable property was expressly encumbered together with land can collection be made; without such a clause it cannot. The reason is that the rule that a husband is not treated as a purchaser where doing so would cause loss to others applies only where, if he were treated as an heir, he would be liable under the Talmudic law. In that case treating him as a purchaser would itself create the loss. But where even if treated as an heir he would not be liable under Talmudic law and liability exists only by later enactment, one can say that a husband, who generally has the status of purchaser, was never included in that enactment. Tosafot make a similar distinction concerning return of appraised property. Accordingly, the Sema is correct. Without a clause of movable property together with land, the creditor cannot collect, because the husband is treated as a purchaser. One cannot object that this harms others, because even if he were an heir the creditor could not collect movable property under Talmudic law; the later Geonic enactment is the only source of liability, and the husband as purchaser may not have been included in it. In a case involving property subject to an ordinary land lien, however, the logic reverses. Under strict law collection from purchasers is possible, and only because of a later enactment do we sometimes refrain. One can then say that the husband is treated as heir and was not included in that enactment. This also resolves the Sema's question from the Gemara. Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds that there remains room to analyze movable property designated as a specific apotiki. See there further. Baer Heitev further cites the Shach as saying that so long as the movable property still exists, the creditor may collect from it even on an oral loan, because it is no worse than the loan money itself. Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes that these words are surprising. The source for the rule concerning existing loan money is the Rambam, chapter 26 of Hilchot Malveh, and the Mordechai in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, and there it is clear that the rule applies specifically to the actual money of the loan, in which the lender retains a special connection. Even regarding actual loan money, Nimukei Yosef at the end of Yesh Nochalin and the Maggid Mishneh dispute the rule, because money borrowed to be spent is like other movable property. But ordinary movable property that the woman brought into the marriage is plainly treated as purchased property in the husband's hands. Rashbam likewise writes so. Therefore the Shach's words require study. Tumim also challenges the Shach on this point, and Netivot HaMishpat agrees. Beit Meir, Even HaEzer, siman 31, seif 4, likewise expresses surprise at the Shach. See also Beit Shmuel there, the responsa at the end of Minchat Yaakov, siman 2, and further discussion in Beit Meir there.
