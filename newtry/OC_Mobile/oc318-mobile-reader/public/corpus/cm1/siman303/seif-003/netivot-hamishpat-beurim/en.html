He is exempt from accidents. See Shach, seif katan 5, who says he swears that the event was unavoidable and still collects his wages. This is true only of the wages for guarding already performed before the accident. From the time of the accident until the end of the agreed period he is not paid, because neither party anticipated that the guarding would become impossible. The owner's obligation therefore ends from that point. The rule also concerns an accident affecting the object, which is comparable to an accident occurring to the employer's property. But where the accident occurs to the worker himself, such as severe illness that prevents him from guarding, then according to the view that even a paid custodian can be exempt from the underlying loss, he can nevertheless lose even the wages for the period already worked if the employer received no benefit from the work, as explained in simanim 310 and 334. The oath to collect wages is a Mishnah-level oath even in categories where there is no Torah custodial oath, such as hekdesh and debt instruments. It resembles the shopkeeper's oath. Although an ordinary claimant might be unable to collect because the employer says, "I do not know whether I became obligated," Chazal imposed an oath-and-collect rule where the worker acted at the employer's instruction. Whenever a person performs a valuable service at another's request, the recipient can become obligated to pay upon the performer's oath even though the recipient did not personally observe every detail. This differs from a person whose monetary obligation depended on a pure condition that has not been independently proved.
Armed robbers. See Shach, seif katan 6. Even if the custodian was not actually present, armed robbery can still be an exempting accident because the loss itself bears the legal character of unavoidable violent taking. If he was present and could not save, even ordinary thieves can sometimes be treated as an accident, as Sema explains.
If he could not save. See Shach, seif katan 7. The text should read: if the guarded object was burned, since he could not save the object we do not say the case was not an accident merely because his own person was threatened. Shach further says that if this were an unpaid custodian, he would be exempt. One who examines Rama's responsum sees that the governing distinction is this: for a paid custodian, as long as the unavoidable force affects only the custodian and not the object, the law can treat the event as negligence with respect to the object. Thus even if the custodian is personally threatened with severe bodily harm if he tries to save the object, a paid custodian can still be liable under the stricter view because from the standpoint of the object the event was preventable. An unpaid custodian, however, is exempt from accidents affecting his own ability to guard. Since we rule in siman 388 that coercive monetary loss can count as an accident, all the more so genuine threats to life can exempt an unpaid custodian. Ketzot HaChoshen similarly limits the special stringency to a paid custodian.
Therefore he must pay much. The printed ruling is difficult if understood as a case where the property could not possibly be saved even by others. In such a case the property would be effectively ownerless, and anyone who endangered himself to rescue it could acquire it. Moreover, if it would have burned in any event, why should the custodian owe its value? The responsum of Maharam cited by Mordechai shows the real case. The custodian could have saved the object from the fire itself, but violent men prevented him from attempting rescue because he feared they would throw him into the fire. Had the property actually burned, he would be exempt because the object suffered a fire accident. But if the men instead stole the property, and the custodian was not present in a position to resist, then under the view that even theft under severe coercion remains "theft," the paid custodian can be liable. What difference is there whether an abnormal wind prevented him from saving from theft or non Jews threatened him not to save? If the custodian was literally present and the robbers physically prevented him from saving, however, even a theft can be treated as unavoidable and he can be exempt. According to Shach's opinion above, which exempts a paid custodian for truly unavoidable theft, the custodian is exempt here as well.
