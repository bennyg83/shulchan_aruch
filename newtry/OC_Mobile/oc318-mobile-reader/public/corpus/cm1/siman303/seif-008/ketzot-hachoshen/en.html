With other shepherds and with staffs. Rama was asked about a paid custodian whose deposited property was kept with his own property. A fire broke out. He saved other goods from the room, and elsewhere he was busy rescuing his own property, but did not save the chest containing the deposit. Rama ruled this negligence for a paid custodian because he could have saved the deposit had he chosen to do so. Personal pressure arising elsewhere does not automatically become an unavoidable accident. Shach inferred from Rama's use of the word negligence that even an unpaid custodian would be liable. I do not agree. An unpaid custodian is exempt when he guards in the ordinary human manner. If he is occupied saving his own property from a fire, failure to abandon his own property to save another's can be reasonable human conduct. Rama's principal proof was from Rashba's rule that even an accident affecting the paid custodian's own body does not necessarily exempt him unless the accident reaches the deposited object itself. That is a paid-custodian standard. Calling the conduct "negligence" therefore means negligence relative to the heightened standard of a paid custodian, not necessarily ordinary negligence sufficient to obligate an unpaid custodian. The same applies to the rule that one wolf is not an unavoidable accident. A paid custodian may be expected to confront ordinary danger or hire assistance. An unpaid custodian who would reasonably suffer bodily harm by doing so need not expose himself. Therefore a single wolf can still be an exempting circumstance for an unpaid custodian where confrontation would involve real personal danger.
