Even if he surrounded it with a wall of iron. Tosafot asks why a paid custodian who claims theft through an overwhelming accident is still liable. The Torah made a paid custodian liable for theft, and ordinary theft is itself close to unavoidable accident. Therefore, once Scripture imposed liability for theft, it imposed liability for theft even when the custodian exercised extraordinary care, unless the event falls into the distinct statutory category of armed robbery or captivity for which Scripture gave an exemption. This is why the Gemara need not establish the case specifically as ordinary theft. Even if the custodian buried money deeply, became mentally incapacitated, fell asleep through unavoidable circumstances, or suffered some other overwhelming inability to guard, if the property was nevertheless stolen the Torah's special theft liability can remain. The Torah singled out armed robbery as exempt, not because its physical force is necessarily greater than every other theft, but because Scripture classified that event separately as captivity. This is Tosafot's basic approach.
The rule should nevertheless be limited to theft. Loss is normally closer to negligence. Scripture did not make a paid custodian liable for a loss caused by a true overwhelming accident merely because ordinary loss is compensable. Accordingly, even Rabbah, who exempts a custodian who entered town when people ordinarily enter or slept when people ordinarily sleep, can agree that if the object was stolen during that time the paid custodian remains liable. His exemption concerns a loss or animal attack occurring while he guarded in the normal human way. The other Amoraim say a paid custodian is paid precisely to exercise heightened guarding. That reasoning is needed where a wolf attacked or a similar event occurred. For theft, Tosafot's stronger statutory rule applies even where he exercised extraordinary care, such as enclosing the object behind an iron wall. Maharshal rejects Tosafot and says Rabbah's ordinary-human guarding can exempt even theft. In my view the Gemara does not say that. The passage speaks of a shepherd whose animal slipped into water, not a theft. Thus Tosafot's distinction between theft and other accidents remains persuasive.
