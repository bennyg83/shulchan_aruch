At the time people normally sleep. I am surprised this is stated as "some say," because everyone agrees that a paid custodian is liable where the theft occurred while he was sleeping at the ordinary time. Bava Metzia 93 rules like R. Chisda and Rabbah bar Rav Huna that a paid custodian is liable in such a case. The dispute is only over a truly overwhelming bodily accident, such as being overcome by abnormal sleep or another severe incapacity. Those who exempt there hold that the Torah exempted only accidents affecting the object, not ordinary conditions of the custodian himself. But ordinary nighttime sleep is certainly not an exempting accident for a paid custodian.
Unless he was present and unable to save. See Sema, seif katan 3. The same applies to armed robbers: the object has the legal category of captivity or violent taking, not ordinary theft. A paid custodian is exempt only when the unavoidable event is one affecting the object itself. An unavoidable event affecting only the custodian's body does not ordinarily exempt him from the special Torah liability for theft. Even where the object itself is attacked, if the event retains the legal name of ordinary theft, the Torah can make the paid custodian liable despite the difficulty of preventing it. Rosh's statement in Bava Kamma that if he sat with the animal and armed robbers came he is exempt should not be read as requiring literal physical presence. Rosh's point is that armed robbery is a violent event in the object and therefore exempting. If the custodian is present and is himself prevented by overwhelming force from saving the object, he can be exempt even where the event would otherwise be called theft. Mordechai's case of a fire, where he feared the attackers would throw him into the flames if he tried to save the property, shows this. Ketzot HaChoshen tries to say that even Rabbah, who exempts where the shepherd sleeps at the ordinary time, would still impose liability if the event is called theft. In my view that cannot be correct. Bava Metzia's challenge from the baraita defining accidents as "and Sheba fell upon them" would then be answered simply by saying that the baraita comes to teach that any event retaining the legal name of theft is liable, even when the custodian slept normally. The Gemara does not answer that way. Therefore, according to Rabbah, ordinary proper guarding can exempt even when the event would otherwise be described as theft.
