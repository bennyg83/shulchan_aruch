Maggid Mishneh writes that even if the animal died a year later the custodian can be liable where he cannot swear that the later death was unrelated to the earlier harsh treatment. In my view, if it is clearly established that in the interim the animal returned to ordinary full health, we do not continue to suspect the earlier event. Even with a get, once a sick person walks normally without support we can presume ordinary health and do not attribute a much later death to the earlier illness. The stricter custodial rule applies where the item remains in the custodian's hands and the initial negligence can still plausibly be linked, even remotely, to the later accident. But once ordinary human assessment establishes that the first danger ended, the law of "beginning in negligence and end in accident" no longer applies. I was uncertain about a robber who exposed an animal to harsh heat and returned it, after which it later died. A robber is not a custodian; his obligation rests on return. If both sides are uncertain whether the death resulted from the earlier act, this resembles "I do not know whether I repaid," where the claimant too is uncertain and the defendant is exempt. Likewise, harsh exposure alone is not direct damage if an unrelated person who is not a custodian would be exempt when the later death is only uncertain. The liability exists principally as "beginning in negligence" within custody. Therefore, if a custodian mistreats the animal, returns it while still sick from that act, and it later dies, he remains liable because the return was not complete. Rosh says explicitly that one who returns a sick animal need not be accepted by the owner until it recovers. But if after the mistreatment the animal clearly recovered fully, the initial negligence is ended. A later accidental death does not revive liability. There is no sensible basis to say that an animal healthy for years remains permanently under the first custodian's risk. Thus the principal rule is: once it has clearly returned to ordinary health according to common assessment, the first negligence has ended. If it was returned while still ill from the negligence, the return is incomplete and liability can remain.
