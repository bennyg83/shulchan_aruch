A shepherd encountered a thief and began provoking him. Maharik rules that if the shepherd's words caused the thief to come, the paid custodian is liable even if he intended only to frighten the thief away. A paid custodian must exercise heightened care and may not create danger through such speech. His wording implies that an unpaid custodian who acted with a sincere protective intent may be exempt. Nimukei Yosef gives a similar distinction where an unpaid custodian gave a deposit to his mother without telling her it was a deposit because he thought she would guard it better. Such a reasonable protective judgment can exempt an unpaid custodian but not a paid custodian, who must investigate more carefully. Maharik also requires proof that without the shepherd's provocation the thief would not have come. Mere uncertainty about causation does not create liability. This differs from some cases of fire where the custodian cannot prove that the object was certainly destroyed without his fault. The distinction concerns the allocation of evidentiary burden and requires further analysis. See siman 340.
