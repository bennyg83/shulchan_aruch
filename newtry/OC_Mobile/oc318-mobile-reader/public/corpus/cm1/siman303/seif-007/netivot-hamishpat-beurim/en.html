And he began provoking him. See Sema, seif katan 7, who says that even if the shepherd intended benefit, he can be liable. The fact that Mechaber states this only regarding a paid custodian and not an unpaid one indicates that only a paid custodian is liable when the intention was for the owner's benefit. Maharik says so explicitly, and Ketzot HaChoshen brings proof from siman 291: an unpaid custodian can say, "I intended for the owner's benefit." Maharik further says that where it is uncertain whether the robbers would have come without the shepherd's words, we do not extract merely on that doubt. In my view that limitation applies to Maharik's case, where the ultimate theft was certainly unavoidable and the doubt concerns only an earlier possible negligence. An earlier negligence that may have contributed to a later accident is only a secondary matter included by gilgul in the oath. We do not apply "unable to swear, pays" to such a secondary doubt. Here, however, the doubt can concern the very moment of theft: perhaps the robbers came only because of the custodian's own provocation. If so, the theft itself occurred through negligence. That is part of the principal oath, and one unable to swear can be liable. Therefore Mechaber can state the rule broadly.
