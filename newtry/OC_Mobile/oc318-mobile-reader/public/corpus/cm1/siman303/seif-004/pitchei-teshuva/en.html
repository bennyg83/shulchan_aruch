And not through others. See Baer Heitev citing Shach. The printed wording of Responsa Rama contains a scribal error. It should read that where, while saving his own property, the item he was guarding burned, we do not say that because he could have saved the guarded object the event ceases to be an unavoidable accident for every custodian. A further printed phrase should likewise read "even if he was an unpaid custodian." Netivot HaMishpat, however, rules that an unpaid custodian is exempt and only a paid custodian is liable, and cites Ketzot HaChoshen. Noda BiYehuda, siman 36, also rules so, adding that even the liability of a paid custodian is arguable, though he would not rule against Rama, whereas the exemption of an unpaid custodian is clear. Responsa Shav Yaakov, siman 23, likewise rejects Shach's understanding of Responsa Rama and notes that Rama's own proofs point the other way. He discusses partners Reuven and Shimon whose partnership goods were stored in Reuven's house. A fire broke out nearby, and Shimon and his household did not come to assist. Reuven and his family therefore devoted themselves to saving the partnership property, which greatly exceeded Reuven's personal property, and Reuven had to entrust some of his own belongings to others, from whom some were stolen. Reuven wished to retain from the partnership property half the value of what he certainly lost because he had been occupied saving the partnership goods. Shav Yaakov distinguishes. If the partnership agreement obligated Reuven to manage and protect the business, he has the status of a paid custodian. Under Responsa Rama, even if his own goods are lost while he saves the entrusted property, that personal loss does not make the event an unavoidable accident with respect to his custodial duty. Since he was obligated to save the partnership property even at the cost of his own loss, Shimon need not reimburse him. If, however, no such condition existed and Reuven undertook the protection voluntarily, he is at most an unpaid custodian and is not required to sacrifice his own property to save Shimon's. The case then resembles one who saves another's donkey while losing his own where the other party is absent and cannot be consulted, and Shimon must reimburse the loss attributable to the rescue. One might argue that Reuven had to save the goods anyway because he owned half of them, so Shimon should owe only for the benefit received. Shav Yaakov rejects this by analogy to partners in siman 181: in a place of loss a partner could have separated his own share and said, "I am saving for myself." Reuven could have saved only his half and then turned to his own goods. By choosing instead to save the entire partnership property, including Shimon's much more valuable share, he acted for Shimon's benefit and at the expense of his own property. Shimon therefore owes reimbursement, but only in proportion to the additional time and effort needed to save Shimon's share beyond what Reuven would have needed to save his own. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 16, also writes that this distinction is sound. Failure to abandon one's own property in order to provide an enhanced level of protection is negligence only for a paid custodian, not for an unpaid custodian. Chatam Sofer goes further: even a paid custodian is subject to that enhanced obligation specifically when he receives explicit payment for guarding, because the owner can say, "I paid you for this." Those who are classified as paid custodians only by legal inference, such as from the benefit of being selected over others or from the reputation gained by being trusted, may be exempt in such a case. He applies this to a charity society administrator whose society pledges and his own property were stored in a stone vault during a fire on Yom Kippur. While he saved his own goods from a wooden-roofed area, the stone vault unexpectedly cracked and the society's pledges burned. Although the administrator was generally a paid custodian as a partner in the society and perhaps also because of the reputation of trustworthiness, Chatam Sofer exempts him. He did not receive explicit guarding wages, so he need not provide this extraordinary degree of protection. Moreover, he reasonably believed the stone vault safe from both thieves and fire and properly turned first to his own goods that were exposed under a wooden roof. He was not bound to anticipate that the stone vault would fail. Chatam Sofer also rejects the claim that he should have invited outsiders to remove the pledges, since opening the vault to strangers could itself create danger. Nor was he obligated to violate Yom Kippur restrictions on carrying merely to save another's money. The owners themselves would not have been permitted to order such carrying expressly, so their custodian cannot be held liable for failing to do more than they themselves could lawfully have done.
