Even though it overpowered him and fell. At first glance, since an unpaid custodian is exempt when an animal goes up by itself, that initial event cannot be full negligence. It is more like theft-risk. Why then is a paid custodian liable when the animal later overpowers him and falls? This seems like a beginning comparable to theft and an end in accident. The distinction is this. Where the later accident is only remotely connected to the earlier event, a beginning comparable to theft can be insufficient. But where, had the custodian not allowed the first event to occur, the later accident certainly could not have happened, the causal connection is direct enough to impose liability. Here, if he had sat and guarded so that the animal never ascended, it certainly would not have fallen from that place. Thus the first failure is the necessary cause of the later loss. The same resolves the earlier difficulty concerning a son who failed to tell his mother that money was a deposit, and the case of an agent whom the principal failed to warn not to mix a particular substance. If the son had told his mother, we presume she would have buried and guarded the deposit properly. If the agent had been warned, he would not have made the mistake. Because the later accident would certainly have been avoided absent the first omission, the first custodian is liable.
