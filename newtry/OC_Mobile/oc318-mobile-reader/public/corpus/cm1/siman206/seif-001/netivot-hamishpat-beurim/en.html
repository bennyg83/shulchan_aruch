It is sold to you. Tur uses the language, "I will sell it to you." Mahara Aharon Sasson, siman 133, writes that Tur follows the opinion in siman 245, seif 4, that if one says "from now," even future language such as "I will give" can work. Mechaber here writes "it is sold to you" because he follows the opinion that "from now" does not cure the future-tense language of "I will give." See Sema, seif katan 1, who struggles to explain why the word "from now" is needed. In my view it is indeed necessary. If one says, "When I sell it, it is sold to you from now for one hundred," the issue is not merely that the first sale could not take effect until the second sale was already complete, and that one cannot transfer an item presently in one's possession to take effect only after it has left his possession. That would not be the correct explanation, because the words "when I sell" naturally mean "when I decide to sell." Rather, the requirement of "from now" follows because the case concerns kinyan sudar. A kinyan sudar ordinarily must take effect immediately; if the acquisition is deferred, the cloth has already returned to its owner. Therefore the stipulation must be framed as taking effect from now, contingent upon the future event. Mahara Aharon Sasson further writes that if the seller uses language of personal obligation, "from now" is unnecessary. The same should be true if the document concludes, "and we made a kinyan from him," in a form that restates the kinyan within the document as an obligation. This resembles siman 212, seif 1, in the gloss. This is also the type of case discussed in Rashba's responsa cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 195. Even where the document uses future language such as "I will sell," if the kinyan is restated in the document in obligation form, the undertaking can be effective.
You shall not sell it except to me. This rule is very difficult. Such language by itself does not create an acquisition. A valid present kinyan normally requires language such as, "When I decide to sell, it is sold to you from now," together with kinyan sudar. If the case includes both "when I sell, it is sold to you from now for one hundred" and also "I will not sell it except to you," the wording of Rama does not support that reading. Moreover, "you shall not sell except to me" is language spoken by the buyer, while the seller is the one who must bind himself. It should have read, "I will not sell except to you." Furthermore, Maharik explicitly indicates that even if one says, "When I sell it, it shall be sold to you for one hundred, and I will not sell it except to you," if the property is later sold to another for a higher amount, the second purchaser acquires. The phrase "when I sell" means when I voluntarily decide to sell. When the later sale arose through coercive circumstances or a different transaction, the first promise does not necessarily govern. Nimukei Yosef in Bava Metzia, chapter Eizehu Neshekh, says similarly concerning "When you sell, sell only to me at this price," that if it is sold for a higher price, the second transaction can stand. The source of Rama's law is Maharik, root 100. There the case concerns partners for a fixed term who agreed that during the partnership, if one wished to sell his share, he would not sell it to anyone except the other partner. Such an agreement can be effective because a partnership arrangement can be completed by their mutual agreement even in language that would not ordinarily create a new kinyan. See there. Rama should therefore be understood as dealing with a comparable setting in which no independent property kinyan is required, but only an effective partnership condition. For example, partners can agree that one will not sell his share except to the other. Similarly, Reuven can sell to Shimon on an express condition that Shimon not resell except back to Reuven. In such cases Rama's language makes sense. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 5 of Hilchot Mechirah, raises a similar difficulty against Rivash siman 145, which uses this wording, and answers that the seller personally obligated himself in the language of obligation. But Rama cannot easily be explained that way. Even if we did say the seller created a personal obligation to sell only to the first party, there would still be a problem. The first party would not thereby own the body of the object; he would have only a personal claim against the seller. If the seller then validly sells to a second purchaser, the second acquires completely. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that because the seller is obligated to sell to the first party, the first has something like a debt instrument and can collect from the later purchaser through the law of encumbered property. I am uncertain about this. The obligation is not an ordinary monetary debt but only an undertaking to sell a particular object. Once the second purchaser has validly acquired it, the original obligor is no longer able to perform. This resembles an obligation to perform something no longer within his power, which may not create a collectable lien against the object, as I wrote in siman 60. Ketzot's additional statement that a person cannot obligate himself regarding a specified item has already been discussed at length in siman 203.
