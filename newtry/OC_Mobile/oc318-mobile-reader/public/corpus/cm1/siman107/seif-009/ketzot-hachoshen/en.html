The Tur cites Rosh that where heirs divided their father's land and one sold his share, the father's creditor collects half the debt from the purchaser and half from the remaining heir. Sema explains that because both heirs bore the father's debt equally, one heir cannot sell his share and thereby impose the whole debt on the other. That is not the true basis of Rosh's words. Rosh means that the enactment, "One does not collect from encumbered property where free property is available," applies where the original debtor himself left free property. It does not apply in the same way where a purchaser or heir later transfers property, because the creditor's claim against both of them is already only by way of foreclosure. This is the same rule stated in Ketubot: if Reuven sold all his fields to Shimon and Shimon later sold one field to Levi, Reuven's creditor may choose either Shimon or Levi, subject to the quality of the fields. Rashi explains that Shimon is not the creditor's debtor. The creditor comes against him only through foreclosure. This raises a difficulty from siman 111, where heirs who sell their father's property are treated as holding encumbered property, and the creditor does not take from those buyers when free inherited property remains. My son Rabbi David explained that the result there rests not on the enactment alone but on the rule, "Whatever right the first purchaser had, he sold to the second." A purchaser who retained another equally suitable field could have directed the creditor there, and that defense passes to the next purchaser. The same principle can operate for an heir, since the creditor's basic collection from heirs is only from inferior quality land. Thus siman 111 need not contradict Rosh's rule here. The case of people who seize the property of a convert remains difficult, since there they are not debtors either, yet the last acquirer is first exposed. This requires further study.
