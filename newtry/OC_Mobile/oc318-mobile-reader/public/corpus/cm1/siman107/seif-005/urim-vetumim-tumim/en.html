Nowadays, etc. The Tur distinguishes between seizure during the debtor's lifetime and seizure after death. The commentators ask that after the enactment of the Geonim, which subjects movables of heirs to creditors, the distinction should have no practical effect. The Bach gives a strained answer. According to what I wrote above, however, the Tur holds that the Geonic enactment did not extend to a deathbed gift. A practical difference therefore remains where the debtor gave his movables as a deathbed gift: seizure during his lifetime can establish the creditor's right, whereas a first seizure only after death may be ineffective against the recipient. There is another difference according to what I concluded above. If one creditor's document expressly subjects movables and land while another creditor's does not, the first creditor collects the movables by the law of the Gemara itself, whereas the second reaches movables of heirs only by the Geonic enactment. If the second creditor seizes only after death, his statutory right cannot defeat the first creditor's stronger legal lien. If, however, he seized during the debtor's lifetime, his collection was itself valid under the law of the Gemara, and he acquires through that seizure. The Tur's distinction therefore remains meaningful even nowadays.
