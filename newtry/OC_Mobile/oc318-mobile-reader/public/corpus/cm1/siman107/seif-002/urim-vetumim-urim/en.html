A general ban. If the deceased gave his property as a deathbed gift and a creditor seeks to collect from the recipient, whether the creditor may collect movables is disputed among the authorities. The possessor may claim certainty like the opinion supporting him, even where the gift was made to children who would otherwise inherit. But if beit din sees that the purpose was merely to evade the creditor, the court may use its authority to defeat the scheme.
Reuven died. The principal explanation is as the Shach and Maadanei Melech write. The creditors fear that if burial expenses are first paid from the estate there will not remain enough to satisfy their debts, and afterward they may be unable to recover from the heirs. Strict law would let the creditors collect first, leaving burial to charity if necessary. Therefore they seek to delay burial until their claims are secured. If the person delaying burial is one of the seven close relatives upon whom burial is personally incumbent, he may not disgrace the deceased in this way. This does not contradict the rule that a wife may sometimes delay burial, because burial is not imposed upon her in the same manner. Chacham Tzvi suggested that the case concerns violent heirs from whom later recovery would be difficult. That explanation is untenable. One may not disgrace the dead merely because the sons are violent.
