Even if the father's land was inferior and the heir possesses superior land, he cannot discharge the creditor with his own superior land. Baal HaTerumot explains the reason as concern that the inherited land may itself be stolen. The Sema writes that this is not limited to money: if the heir wants to discharge the creditor with his own movable property, he may do so, because movables can be carried and sold wherever the creditor wishes. I am astonished. What is the source for extending the rule to the heir's movable property and thereby disagreeing with Beit Yosef? Beit Yosef writes explicitly in siman 102 that where the deceased left only land and the heirs seek to discharge the creditor with their own movable property, the creditor may refuse, since those movables were never subject to his lien. This is also implied by the Tur in siman 104. Beit Yosef then says that if the heirs want to pay cash, even cash their father never left, it appears they may. The debtor's normal obligation is payment in money, coin has no identifying mark, and cash the heirs possess can be treated like cash that might have been inherited. Moreover, they could sell the inherited land and pay money. Thus Beit Yosef expressly distinguishes cash from other movable property. The Tur in siman 107 might superficially suggest otherwise, because after saying that an heir may discharge the creditor with money even though he did not inherit money, he says that if the heir has neither his own movable property nor money, but only land, and wants to give that land to protect purchasers, the creditor may say, 'I want the property your father left.' This might imply that if he has his own movables he may use them. But the opening statement is precise: the heir may discharge him 'with money,' not with other movables. In any event, Beit Yosef's additional reason about coin lacking identifying marks is unnecessary. Even a purchaser can discharge a creditor with cash, as stated in chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, though no theory of inherited cash applies to a purchaser.
I was also uncertain where a later creditor collected land first and an earlier creditor then comes to remove it from him. Can the later creditor pay the earlier one money and keep the land? At first glance he should be no worse than a purchaser or heir. Yet perhaps a purchaser's ability to pay money is connected to the doctrine that an appraisal may later be returned. Strictly speaking the purchaser cannot extinguish the earlier creditor's lien by offering some other asset, for the creditor can say, 'I want my lien.' The law allows money because the purchaser's position is tied to the rules of appraisal and redemption. A later creditor confronting an earlier creditor is different. Both are merely creditors. There is no special appraisal-return status between them. The earlier creditor may therefore be entitled to say, 'I want the specific encumbered property,' and the later creditor cannot force him to accept cash.
