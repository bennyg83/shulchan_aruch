There are those who say that he does not collect. See Sema, seif katan 15. The Drishah, Knesset HaGedolah, and Tumim object from the Tur in siman 104 concerning Reuven, who borrowed orally and died, after which his son Chanoch borrowed by deed. The creditor by deed has priority and collects the land, while Chanoch must pay damages because by imposing a lien he damaged the deceased's property. Their answers are strained. In my opinion, the matter can be explained as follows. The Shach in seif katan 8 writes that whenever the authorities obligate the heirs to pay under the law of direct causative damage, that is so even if the seller did not receive money. But if he did receive money, he is independently liable because he profited. Certainly the Shach means this only according to those authorities who obligate the heirs. According to the authorities who exempt them, the Tur and all the authorities clearly imply that they are exempt even where the proceeds they received are still in their hands, as Tumim and Ketzot HaChoshen also write. It further appears that the principle of profiting at another's expense applies here only according to the view that property liens are d'oraisa. He then profits from something belonging to another, since the creditor's lien is his property, and the heir damaged it while profiting from it. But according to the view that a lien is not d'oraisa, he is exempt even where he profited. Proof comes from Bekhorot 48, where Rava establishes the case as one in which there are only five sela and all agree with Rabbi Asi that brothers who divided an estate are half heirs and half purchasers, all agree that a loan obligation written in the Torah is not treated like a written deed, and all agree with Rav Pappa that an oral loan is collected from heirs but not purchasers. Their dispute concerns five but not half of five. According to the Shach, why should the inability to collect from purchasers matter? Each brother should still be liable because by selling his share he damaged the lien and profited, since he received his brother's share in exchange for his own. The answer is that the Shach in siman 39, seif katan 2, writes that Rava and Rav Pappa in Bava Batra hold that a lien is not d'oraisa, and according to that view there is no liability even where one profits. The reason still requires explanation. Shmuel holds in Bava Metzia 14 that seller's responsibility is not presumed from silence, because a person may buy land for a single day. This shows that in land subject to a creditor there are two distinct interests: the landowner owns the body of the field, while the creditor has a lien. We therefore assume that the purchase money is paid for the owner's proprietary body of the field. Accordingly, the heirs' exemption is clear. According to Shmuel, just as they are exempt toward the purchaser because the money they took was the price of a sale that might last only a day, so they are exempt toward the creditor when they sold to a powerful non-Jew. They did not deal with the lien at all. They received the sale price only for what belonged to them, the body of the land. There is therefore no enrichment from the creditor's property. They are also exempt as damage causers, because damaging a lien is merely indirect causation. Since they touched only their own property and not the lien itself, this is indirect causation of indirect causation. Even those who hold that responsibility is presumed do not reject the concept that one may buy land for a day. Rather, they hold that the scribe presumably erred because the parties surely stipulated responsibility, or if they did not, the seller surely forgot, since he intended responsibility. Even where responsibility was expressly accepted and the price reflected both the sale and responsibility, responsibility is merely a personal obligation of the seller. The principal sale of the field is only of what the seller owns, namely the body of the land, not the creditor's lien. He has not dealt with the lien at all. Although the sale to a powerful non-Jew damages the lien, the Tur holds that one is not liable merely for damaging a lien. By contrast, where the son borrowed by deed, as the Tur rules in siman 104, the body of the property remained his and he dealt only with its lien. The money he received was therefore received in exchange for a lien belonging to another, and he profited from another's property. He is therefore liable. According to this, even if the purchase money is still in the purchaser's hands, the creditor cannot restrain it, because the price is for the proprietary interest belonging to the heirs, not for another's lien. Tumim writes the opposite, but I do not understand his proof. Ketzot HaChoshen seeks to answer from the Ran in Chullin 131 regarding one who damages priestly gifts. The Ran writes that when one intends to take priestly gifts for himself and changes them by dyeing or eats them, he does not pay even though he profited, because he has extinguished their mitzvah and there is no monetary obligation. But if the king seizes his grain for a debt, since he himself did not extinguish the mitzvah and he has profited, the mitzvah remains upon him, because what difference is there between the gift itself and its monetary value. Ketzot applies this here and writes that if the heirs sold the property the creditor cannot collect from the proceeds, because they extinguished his lien and there is no monetary obligation. But where, at the end of siman 114, Levi took the property for a debt, the heir did not extinguish his mitzvah to pay his father's debt, and it therefore remains upon him, analogous to the king seizing the grain. These words are difficult. In the case of priestly gifts the owner has only a mitzvah obligation, not a monetary one, so when he actively nullifies the mitzvah he has only violated the mitzvah and there is no monetary liability. Here, by contrast, if he is within the category of one who damages a lien, he is liable where he profited even if he intended to extinguish and steal the lien. If he is not considered one who damages a lien and is exempt despite enrichment for the reason given by the Tur, then all the more so he should be exempt where the matter occurred involuntarily. Moreover, if there is upon him a mitzvah to pay and beit din compels fulfillment of that mitzvah, he should certainly be liable even after selling the property. The mitzvah is not specifically to satisfy the debt with that very asset. Priestley gifts are different because the mitzvah is to give that particular object, such as the first shearing. Once he steals and dyes it, he has immediately nullified the mitzvah. But with the sale of land subject to a creditor, there is no indication that the mitzvah has been nullified, because he can still fulfill the mitzvah of paying a creditor using his own money.
