Some say that he does not collect. The Tur writes that if the heirs sold movable property, all agree that the creditor cannot collect from the proceeds, because the heirs' movable property was not encumbered to the lender under strict law. Even after the enactment of the Geonim, the enactment applied only while the movable property remained in the heirs' hands. If they sold it first, we revert to Torah law. As to land that they sold, the Tur initially thought they should be liable from the proceeds because they damaged another person's lien, for what greater damage could there be than moving the creditor's security to a place from which he cannot collect? His father the Rosh, however, ruled in a responsum that this is not considered damaging another's lien because the heirs did not physically damage the land itself. In the Perishah I asked that this appears inconsistent with the Tur's ruling at the beginning of siman 104 that a later creditor who sells land encumbered to an earlier creditor to a gentile must compensate him. The same difficulty applies to the Mechaber, who rules that way in siman 104 but records a dispute here. I resolved it from the Rosh's own responsum. He explicitly distinguishes heirs: at the time they sold, they sold their own property, because we rule like Rava that a creditor acquires only from the time of actual collection onward. A later creditor in siman 104, however, had no ownership in the land he sold, only a lien, while the land was already encumbered to the earlier creditor. This also resolves the Raavad's responsum cited by the Tur at the end of siman 104. See the Derishah.
