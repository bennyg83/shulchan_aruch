There is an opinion that says. This opinion holds that the creditor collects from the proceeds. The Sema writes that if the heirs sold movables, all agree that the creditor cannot collect from the proceeds. The Shach writes that even there Rav Hai Gaon holds that the creditor does collect the proceeds. I found no such statement in Rav Hai. His ruling concerns heirs who sold a small estate, meaning land. He treats the money received from the sale of that land like movables left by their father, which after the Geonic enactment are subject to creditors. But if the heirs sold movables, allowing the creditor to follow the proceeds would require an enactment built upon another enactment, and there is no basis to assume that the Geonim went that far. Why create such an unnecessarily broad dispute? The simple reading of Rav Hai is limited to land that the heirs sold. It is true that the Tur's language can be read as the Shach understood it, but Rav Hai's own words do not support that extension.
And some say that he does not collect. See Urim. It appears that if the purchase money is still in the purchaser's hands, the creditor may prevent its payment to the seller. The Ran, explaining the Yerushalmi in Ketubot concerning a widow who sold estate property without specifying whether the sale was for maintenance or for her ketubah, writes that if an oral creditor comes she may say, "I sold for maintenance," while against a written creditor she may say, "I sold for my ketubah." The Ran explains that the purchase money is still in the purchaser's hands and the creditor seeks to take it. The fact that the Ran does not establish the case after the money has already reached the widow, with the written creditor arguing that she impaired his lien, indicates that once the money is in her hands he cannot remove it from her. Yet while the money remains with the purchaser the creditor does have a claim against it. This shows that the proceeds are not yet considered fully in the seller's possession while they remain with the purchaser. The distinction is also reasonable.
That he does not collect. See Urim, where I wrote that the Sema correctly distinguishes between an heir who sells liened property, whom the Tur does not regard as one who damages another's lien, and a later creditor who sells property subject to an earlier creditor, who is indeed treated as damaging the earlier creditor's lien and must pay. The Shach rejects this distinction entirely and seeks to say that the Tur above in siman 104 disagrees with his father the Rosh, who held that such a sale is not actionable damage to the lien. I am astonished, because the Ramban himself appears contradictory unless the Sema's distinction is made. In his novellae to Bava Batra, in the discussion of heirs who sold land in a small estate, the Ramban writes that the heirs owe nothing and are not regarded as having damaged the creditor's lien. Rabbenu Yerucham says the same in his name. The Beit Yosef in siman 108 cites the Rashba similarly, that heirs who sell land subject to their father's oral debt need not surrender the sale proceeds. Yet the Baal HaTerumot, shaar 43, part 4, din 16, writes explicitly in the Ramban's name that if a later creditor sells movables subject to an earlier creditor to a non Jew, the later creditor has damaged the earlier creditor's lien and must pay. The Beit Yosef at the end of siman 104 cites the same rule in a responsum of the Rashba. The only coherent distinction is that an heir sells his own inherited property, so his sale is not characterized as damage to another's lien, whereas a later creditor who disposes of property that is not substantively his as against the earlier lien is treated as a tortfeasor. This is clear. One scholar cited in the responsa Penei Moshe, siman 76, suggested a further distinction: if the earlier creditor's due date had already arrived when the later creditor sold the property, the seller is liable, but if the debt was not yet due he is exempt. The Penei Moshe rejects this completely. Yet according to the opinion discussed above in siman 103, seif 6, that after maturity a creditor can sell collected property without the debtor's permission or further judicial process, there is substantial basis for the distinction. The Baal HaTerumot explains Rava's principle that a creditor collects only from now onward, so the debtor's prior sale remains effective, as applying before the due date. After maturity, the creditor's practical ownership is stronger and he can sell without permission. The Rosh's reason for exempting an heir or seller from liability is that the property was still legally theirs when sold, because a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. On the cited opinion, once the due date has passed that reason weakens or disappears. The earlier creditor's right has matured to the point that a sale can indeed be viewed as damaging his property interest. The scholar's distinction therefore has a legitimate halakhic basis according to that view. However, there remains an apparent difficulty in the Tur. Above in siman 104 he cites without dissent a responsum of the Raavad concerning Reuven who borrowed orally and died, after which his son Chanoch borrowed by written instrument. The Raavad rules that Chanoch's written creditor has priority over Reuven's oral creditor, yet Chanoch must reimburse Reuven's creditor because by subjecting or disposing of the estate for his own later debt he damaged the earlier creditor's lien. This seems inconsistent with a broad rule that heirs never become liable for damaging a lien. It therefore appears that the Tur's earlier grouping of a sold oral debt case with property sold to non Jews was intended only for the question whether the proceeds are treated like money left by the deceased or like newly acquired movables. It was not intended to equate the two for tort liability. The Rosh's exemption from damaging a lien applies where the lien itself remains legally intact and only practical collection has been obstructed, such as land sold to a powerful non Jew. In that situation the lien still exists, but there is a practical lion crouching over it. See below in siman 111, seif 2 and seif 13, concerning violent possessors, where property is not treated as legally destroyed because the power of oppressors may eventually fall. But where the heir sells land subject only to an oral creditor in a manner that legally cuts off the creditor's right against the purchaser under Jewish law, or where the heir incurs a written debt that by law displaces the father's oral creditor, the earlier lien itself is extinguished. That is closely analogous to making one's slave an apotiki and then freeing the slave, where the Gemara imposes liability for damaging the lien. There is no reason to think the Rosh or Tur would disagree with that explicit Talmudic principle. Indeed, the Raavad's position is easier to understand this way. Although in Hilkhot Chovel uMazik, chapter 6, he appears to exempt one who frees an apotiki slave on the ground that he acted with his own property, the lien on a slave is only d'rabbanan according to the authorities who treat a moving slave like movables and say the creditor did not fully rely on it. The Raavad may therefore distinguish between destruction of a rabbinic lien on a slave, where he exempts, and destruction of a d'oraisa land lien created by a loan, where he imposes liability. In any event, the later creditor or heir who legally extinguishes the earlier creditor's land lien can be liable, whereas one who merely sells his own inherited property without extinguishing the lien is not. The Rama's ruling in Even HaEzer siman 112 that heirs who sell a small estate are exempt concerns a rabbinic family support rule, and later authorities likewise explain it as a rabbinic matter. I also found support for liability from Tosafot in Ketubot 96b. Tosafot ask how a widow can sell estate property without specifying the purpose and later tell purchasers that she sold for maintenance while seeking to collect her ketubah from other purchasers. Would that not be deceptive if it were untrue? One could otherwise answer that she need not intend to lie at all. She may simply fear that if she expressly sells now for her ketubah, the heirs will later sell the remaining property and thereby leave her without maintenance, which cannot be collected from purchasers. By selling without specification she deters purchasers from buying from the heirs, since any purchaser would fear that she might later characterize her earlier sale as one for maintenance and then collect the ketubah from the later purchaser. This would be a powerful protective device without any necessary fraud. Tosafot did not give that answer. The implication is that the heirs would be liable for the loss if they sold away property needed for her enforceable claim, so she does not require such a device merely to protect herself from their sale. This supports liability where an heir's act truly extinguishes another's enforceable lien.
