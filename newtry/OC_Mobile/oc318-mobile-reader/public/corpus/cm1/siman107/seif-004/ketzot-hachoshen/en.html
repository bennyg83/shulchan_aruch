An heir who sold all the property inherited from his father is subject to a dispute whether the father's creditor can collect from the sale proceeds. One view says he can because the heir damaged the creditor's lien. Another says he cannot, because liability for damaging another's lien applies only where the person damaged the property itself, while here the land remained physically intact and was merely transferred to another domain. This latter position is the Rosh's responsum, rule 79, siman 10. Gidulei Terumah objects from the dispute concerning a slave made an express apotiki and then freed by his master. The authorities discuss whether the one who freed him is liable for damaging another person's lien, even though the slave's body was not physically injured. Does this not prove that destroying the legal lien alone is enough? The answer is that freeing a slave does alter the very legal body of what was subject to the lien. Before emancipation there was a slave encumbered to the creditor. After emancipation there is no slave at all, only a free person. The master has therefore destroyed the very subject matter of the lien, analogous to digging pits in encumbered land. By contrast, when land is merely sold, the land itself remains exactly as it was. The seller has only placed it in another person's domain, making litigation and collection more difficult. That is not the same category of damaging the lien's physical or legal subject. The Tur, after citing the Rosh's exemption for sale of inherited land, writes that although there appears room to distinguish, he does not argue with Rosh. Bach asks that at the end of siman 104 the Tur cites Raavad concerning Reuven, who borrowed orally from Shimon, died leaving land to his son Chanokh, and Chanokh later borrowed by document from Levi. Levi, as the later written creditor, can collect the inherited land ahead of Shimon's earlier oral loan. Raavad says beit din then writes Shimon a debt instrument against Chanokh because one who damages another's lien is liable, all the more where Chanokh used the earlier creditor's lien to pay his own debt. Bach views this as the Tur himself ruling contrary to Rosh. The two cases differ. In Raavad's case Chanokh directly benefited from the earlier creditor's encumbered property by using it to discharge his own debt. This is a case of enrichment. The principle is not merely liability for damaging a lien. The Gemara in Chullin provides the model. One who destroys or consumes gifts due to kohanim can be exempt because the money lacks a particular claimant or because the property is not ordinary privately collectible money. Yet if the king's officers seize the person's produce in satisfaction of his own debt, he must separate the gifts, because he has been enriched by them. Even where ordinary damage liability is absent, enrichment can create liability. Tosafot asks that one who ate the gifts was also enriched by saving himself the cost of food, and answers that he could have fasted. Ran gives a deeper explanation. If a person himself intentionally consumes, dyes, or sells the priestly gifts, he has uprooted the mitzvah and the property carries no ordinary personal money claim, so even resulting benefit does not create payment. But where the king's officers took the produce for his debt, he did not personally uproot the mitzvah. The mitzvah remains upon him, and because he received the benefit of having his debt paid, he must replace the value. Whether the actual items or their value were used for his benefit makes no difference. This is why Yoreh Deah rules that one who damages, eats, or sells gifts to kohanim is exempt, even though a sale produces actual proceeds and obvious enrichment. Once he himself disposed of the property and thereby uprooted the mitzvah, there is no ordinary monetary debt to enforce. The same distinction applies here. If the heir himself sells the inherited land, the Rosh exempts him from paying the creditor out of the proceeds. By his own sale he has removed the property from the creditor's reach, and the mere fact that he received sale proceeds does not create a new monetary obligation once liability for damaging the lien is denied. But in the case at the end of siman 104, Levi, the later creditor, comes and takes the inherited land in payment of Chanokh's own debt. Chanokh did not voluntarily sell away the lien. The property was used to satisfy a personal obligation of his. He has therefore been directly enriched by the earlier creditor's encumbered asset. The obligation to repay the father's debt still rests upon him, and the benefit he received is equivalent to having the property's value applied for his own use. This is analogous to the king taking produce in satisfaction of the owner's debt. Therefore Chanokh becomes liable to Shimon even though an heir who voluntarily sold the property would not owe the sale proceeds merely under the doctrine of damaging a lien. This resolves the apparent contradiction between the Tur's acceptance of the Rosh here and his citation of Raavad in siman 104.
Knesset HaGedolah discusses movables that had been subjected together with land and were later sold. He concludes that the heirs remain exempt from liability for damaging the creditor's lien, reasoning that by Torah law a creditor's lien does not attach to movables even when they were subjected together with land, and that the debtor therefore meant to subject them only while they remained in his possession. This conclusion is difficult. Acquisition of movables together with land is, according to most authorities, a Torah mode of acquisition. Moreover, as I wrote at the beginning of siman 39, according to the view that liens are biblical even movables are in principle subject to a creditor, and the Sages merely removed that lien. If the debtor expressly subjected the movables together with land, there is therefore strong reason that the lien should be effective. Rosh, in responsum 79:12, explains that lien and acquisition are distinct. Slaves can be subjected together with land even though they are not acquired incidentally with land. The reason movables subjected together with land can be collected from purchasers is that the arrangement gives publicity, like a land lien, and purchasers should have been careful. This language indicates that the force of the lien is not derived directly from the Torah acquisition of movables together with land but from the publicity the arrangement creates. If the entire reason is publicity, the law may be rabbinic, because Torah lien should not depend on whether a transaction became public. Ran, however, appears to hold that a lien on movables together with land is biblical and derives it from the acquisition mechanism. The matter therefore requires study.
