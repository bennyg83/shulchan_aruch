The Shulchan Aruch rules without dissent that where heirs divided land inherited from their father, the creditor normally collects a proportional share from each. Ramban, however, in Perek Beit Kor, holds that where each brother received land of the quality suitable for collection, the creditor may choose one and collect the entire debt from him. He compares the two inherited parcels to two guarantors, each of which stands fully subject to the father's debt. Ramban also cites the Jerusalem Talmud, which applies shuda deDayanei where one document reaches two fields. According to our Bavli, however, shuda is not used where two loan documents dated the same day have equal liens. The reason is that there is no possibility that one was truly first; both liens attach together and the creditors divide. The same should be true where one loan document reaches two equally encumbered fields. There is no factual basis to say that this field rather than that field became the sole subject of the lien. Therefore, under the approach of the Bavli, one should divide rather than use shuda. Ramban's own ruling is understandable because he holds that the creditor may choose either heir and collect the whole debt from him. If the entire lien rests upon each parcel, beit din can choose one. But according to the Shulchan Aruch, which holds that each heir's parcel is subject only to his proportional share, shuda has no place. The underlying ruling of the Shulchan Aruch itself requires study, because Maggid Mishneh cites Ramban and Rashba as ruling that where both brothers received land, a creditor may collect from whichever one he wishes, and calls that the principal rule. Maggid Mishneh similarly derives the law of two guarantors from the case of brothers who divided. Since two guarantors can each be pursued for the whole debt, there is strong support for Ramban. We left this question unresolved earlier in siman 109.
