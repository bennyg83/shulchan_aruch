Sema says that the same rule applies where the father left movables and the heirs explicitly paid the earlier creditor in exchange for those movables: the later creditor cannot then collect them. I find this difficult. Before the Geonic enactment, movables of heirs were not subject to creditors at all. If that is the setting, even without an express statement that the payment was made for the movables, the later creditor has no claim to them. After the Geonic enactment, if these were the only movables left, the heirs' payment to the earlier creditor can only have been on account of those movables, since where nothing was left there is not even a mitzvah to pay from unrelated property. If additional movables were left, the later creditor can collect from those instead. Thus the distinction based on an express statement is difficult to locate in the law of movables. The Ran and Maggid Mishneh discuss the reverse type of case, where land and movables were left and the heirs expressly discharged the earlier creditor on account of the land. There the later creditor cannot say that the payment should be treated as having been made from the movables merely as fulfillment of a mitzvah. That distinction made sense under the Talmudic law. In our time, after the Geonic enactment, the practical distinctions largely disappear. There is, however, one important point concerning movables. Since movables have no law of priority, the earlier creditor never owned a superior lien in them. So long as the movables had not actually reached his hands, the later creditor could still seize them. Even if the heirs paid the earlier creditor money and explicitly said that they were buying the movables free of his claim, that does not make the earlier creditor into one who had already acquired those movables. The principle that where the substitute is already in the kohen's hands we treat the kohen as though he had acquired the original property is itself only a special rabbinic rule and is disputed. According to the practical law, until the movables actually reached the earlier creditor, the later creditor can seize them, and if he did so we do not remove them from him. This appears clear.
