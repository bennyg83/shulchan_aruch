From their own property. See Urim. The Tur distinguishes, regarding movables left by the father, between heirs who expressly say that they are giving them in exchange for the creditor's claim against land and heirs who give them without such specification. The commentators ask what difference this makes nowadays, when movables themselves are collectible. It appears that the case involves several creditors. Movables have no law of temporal priority, so where creditors come to collect them they divide equally. If the heirs simply give particular movables to one creditor, that creditor may say, "I received them as ordinary payment. Although initially all creditors should have divided the movables, once you voluntarily delivered them to me without qualification I acquired them, and I may still collect the land because my debt is earlier and land is governed by priority." If, however, the heirs expressly say that the movables are given in substitution for the land, then the movables take the legal character of that substitute transaction. The creditor cannot treat them as an unrelated voluntary payment while preserving his full priority claim to the land. The competing creditors are therefore placed on the same footing with respect to the value substituted for the land, and they divide it just as they would have divided the movables. This explains the Tur's distinction even under current law.
