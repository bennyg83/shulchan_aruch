If the father left movables and the heirs first paid a later creditor, Sema says the earlier creditor cannot recover because there is no priority in movables, and the heirs are not treated as having damaged the earlier creditor's lien. In my view the reason is broader. When two creditors have equal rights in movables, payment to one is not normally called damaging the other's lien. If a stranger seizes for one creditor in a manner that harms another, the dispute is whether the seizure works, but we do not ordinarily say that the seizer must pay damages merely because the other creditor lost his opportunity. There may be room to distinguish. If the seizer was not acting as the creditor's valid agent and then hands the property over to the creditor, perhaps at the moment of delivery he has actually damaged the other creditor's lien, because until then the competing creditor could still have taken his share. Another possibility is that ordinary movables do not carry an actual property lien, and the obligation to pay is chiefly the debtor's mitzvah. In that setting there is no lien in the object to damage. But where movables were subjected together with land and both creditors had equal specific liens, a third party who first seized for one and then delivered to him may perhaps incur liability for damaging the other's lien. This remains to be examined. Still, the stronger practical principle is that payment to a genuine creditor from property that is equally subject to him is not treated as damaging another creditor's lien. Once one creditor actually receives it, the matter is clarified in his favor.
