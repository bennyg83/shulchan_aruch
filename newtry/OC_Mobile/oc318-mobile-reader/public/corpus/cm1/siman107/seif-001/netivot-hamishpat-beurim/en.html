We do not compel them. See Sema, seif katan 2, who cites the Ramban that beit din does not wish to compel the heirs. Tumim objects. In Ketubot 84, judges ruled like Rabbi Tarfon regarding movable property of orphans and the ruling was reversed because it was an error in a matter of law. According to the Ramban, what error was there if beit din has discretion to compel them? Moreover, why did the Geonim need to enact a new ordinance? They could simply have enacted that beit din should compel payment. He also asks from seizure after death, where we rule that property is removed from the seizer. Why should beit din act to compel and thereby cause the heirs to violate a positive mitzvah? In my opinion, although there is a mitzvah upon the heirs to pay, this is a personal obligation only. The movable property itself does not leave the heirs' ownership even if the creditor seizes it. It remains theirs, the creditor may not use it, and his retention of it is theft. Beit din likewise cannot award it to the creditor as his property. Beit din may only compel the heirs until they say, I wish to fulfill the mitzvah. Until the heirs themselves definitively transfer the property, it has not been transferred and remains stolen property in the creditor's hands. Therefore, if he seized it, we remove it from him so that he will not violate the prohibition of theft by retaining it. The fact that the heirs themselves are failing to fulfill a positive mitzvah gives him no proprietary right in their objects.
From a loan owed to the estate. Ketzot HaChoshen seeks to answer Tosafot's objection in Ketubot 81, beginning with the words We do not find, from the Mishnah in HaKotev concerning one who died leaving a wife and a debt owed to him. He writes that according to Abaye, who holds that collection takes effect retroactively, Rabbi Tarfon's case does not require Rabbi Natan's rule. Since the loan is in the hands of others and must be paid to the heirs, once collection ultimately occurs it takes effect retroactively and is considered as though it came into their father's hands in his lifetime, so Rabbi Natan's rule is unnecessary. I do not understand his words. Once the heirs have actually collected the debt it becomes movable property of the heirs. Even if we consider it as though it came into their father's possession during his lifetime, ordinary movable property that the father collected during his lifetime and then died holding does not remain subject to a creditor under Talmudic law. Rabbi Tarfon's rule is precisely that the money should not be delivered to the heirs at all, but the deceased's creditor should collect it directly from the deceased's debtor. This certainly requires Rabbi Natan's rule, for otherwise that debtor can say to the creditor, You are not my litigant. Likewise, Ketzot's attempt to answer the Ramban's objection to the view that a ketubah is not collected from a loan, from Ketubot 81 concerning one who was a creditor of his brother, is difficult. He writes that there the reason is that a yavam also does not receive property that was merely prospective, but with a debt owed to the deceased brother there is doubt whether perhaps the brother transferred it generously so that it should be considered presently held property, and therefore the husband collects. This too is difficult. At least as to the share that would come to the yavam by ordinary inheritance even if he were not a yavam, regarding which one can say the property was merely prospective, it should not be given toward the ketubah. Moreover, the result is self contradictory: against the wife claiming her ketubah he says it is prospective and therefore not subject to her claim, while against his brother he says it is presently held and therefore he inherits it.

The law is with the heirs. See Sema, seif katan 6, who explains that according to the strict law movable property was not subject to the creditor at all. See Tumim regarding deathbed gifts of movable property. One should not depart from the ruling of the Mechaber and Shulchan Aruch, Even HaEzer, that according to the Geonic ordinance a ketubah is collected from movable property given as a deathbed gift. According to the Sema's reason, however, for the Rosh, who holds that movable property is subject to the creditor by strict law, the law should favor the creditor. It therefore appears that even with respect to the debtor himself, if he possesses money, then d'oraisa he must repay with the very money that he borrowed, just as one who borrowed wheat must repay wheat when he has it. Proof is that increases and decreases in the value of the item lent fall upon the creditor, and only d'rabbanan is payment forbidden when the item increased in value because of ribbit. Consequently, movable property must also be given because it is closer to money. But as to heirs, whose obligation is only by virtue of the lien upon the assets, they may give the creditor whatever category of property they choose, just as they may give inferior land. Only where the deed expressly states land and movable property, during my lifetime and after my death, does the wording show that the debtor intended the rule after death to equal the rule during life. Therefore, the heirs must give movable property, which is closer to cash, just as the debtor himself would have been required to do.
