We do not compel them. See the Sema, who writes that according to the Tur's explanation the heirs' obligation is an aspect of the positive mitzvah of honoring a parent, a mitzvah whose reward is written alongside it, and earthly beit din is not commanded to compel performance of such a mitzvah. The Sema therefore reasons that according to the Rama's ruling above in siman 97, citing an opinion that beit din may nevertheless compel if it chooses, beit din would likewise have discretion to compel the heirs here. Most authorities, however, do not accept that opinion. The Shach disagrees with the Sema and says that the Rama relied on what he wrote above. My understanding does not incline to the suggestion that those authorities would permit a Jewish beit din, merely at its option, to compel heirs to pay a debt from movables that by law are not subject to the debt. Consider Ketubot 84b, where a judge ruled like Rabbi Tarfon that movables of orphans lying in the public domain should be given to the creditor. The ruling was reversed, and the Amoraim debate whether the judge had erred in a matter governed by an explicit Mishnah or only in judicial discretion. What error would there be if a judge were free to compel the orphans whenever he wished? He simply would have exercised that discretion by giving the movables to the creditor. Likewise, when Rabbi Yochanan wished to rule for the benefit of his creditor relative according to Rabbi Tarfon, he said, "What can I do, my opponents disagree with me." If beit din were authorized at will to compel the heirs, he could have protected his relative without disregarding Torah law by exercising that power, especially regarding movables lying in an alley or another place not fully controlled by the heirs. There is also a broader difficulty. Why did the Geonim labor to institute a new enactment subjecting movables of heirs? They could simply have enacted that batei din should exercise their existing discretion to compel payment. They would then be acting within Torah law and no new property enactment would be needed. The Baal HaTerumot raises a similar difficulty against Rabbenu Chananel. These questions can, however, be answered in part. Even those authorities who permit optional coercion may mean only children of the deceased debtor, who have the explicit Torah mitzvah of honoring their father, and Tosafot view payment of the father's debt as included in that mitzvah. The term "orphans" in the Talmudic cases can include brothers of the deceased or nephews who inherit him. Such heirs have no Torah mitzvah to honor their brother or uncle. The cases in Ketubot may therefore concern heirs of that kind, and the Geonim needed an enactment that would apply to all heirs. Even so, there remains a serious difficulty from our ruling like Rabbi Akiva that if a creditor seizes movables of heirs only after the debtor's death, the property is removed from him. This rule applies to all heirs, including children. If beit din is merely not obligated to compel children to fulfill a Torah mitzvah, that might justify judicial inaction, but why should beit din affirmatively remove property from the creditor's possession and thereby assist the heirs in failing to perform that mitzvah? According to the opinion that beit din has no authority to compel such a mitzvah, the answer is that civil law treats the property as fully belonging to the heirs, so beit din must adjudicate their property rights even though a separate mitzvah rests upon them. But according to the opinion that beit din may compel them if it chooses, once the creditor already possesses the property there is no reason for beit din to intervene on behalf of the heirs. Beit din could instead simply compel the heirs to leave the property with him. It is therefore clear that the opinion allowing optional coercion follows Rashi, the Ran, and other authorities who hold that the heirs' obligation to pay from such movables is not a Torah mitzvah at all, but only d'rabbanan. Tosafot's question, that we generally compel even performance of a rabbinic mitzvah, can be answered in two ways. Rabbinic mitzvot do not all carry identical coercive rules; the Sages sometimes strengthened an enactment and sometimes did not. Moreover, if even with a Torah mitzvah of this sort beit din is not commanded to compel, then a rabbinic analogue is one degree weaker and beit din should not compel at all. Tosafot themselves can be understood in this fashion: they may agree with Rashi that the present obligation is only rabbinic, and their question is merely why no coercion applies. They answer that even regarding the corresponding Torah category beit din is not commanded to compel, so in the rabbinic case coercion is excluded entirely. This is clear and straightforward.
To pay, etc. The Tur writes that even if the debtor's assets are deposited in the creditor's own hands or in the hands of others, a creditor cannot collect them under the law of the Gemara. This is the Mishnah in the chapter HaKotev: if the deceased had a loan or deposit in the hands of others, we rule like Rabbi Akiva that it is given to the heirs, because all other claimants require an oath while the heirs do not. The Rosh cites the Yerushalmi, which asks: suppose the debtor exempted the creditor from an oath, does one rule follow Torah law and the other not? The Yerushalmi therefore treats the heirs' possession as decisive even without the oath rationale. Accordingly, the Tur in his remazim and here rules without qualification that the heirs prevail even if the creditor was exempted from an oath. Yet the Rosh in the chapter Yesh Nochalin and in the chapter Yesh Bekhor, in discussing whether a woman's ketubah can be collected from a debt owed to the estate despite the rule that a woman does not collect prospective property, brings proof from Rabbi Akiva and writes that even Rabbi Akiva would allow a creditor who was exempted from the creditor's oath to collect property held by others. This is also the view of Tosafot in those chapters and in Ketubot 92. The Rosh therefore appears inconsistent. The following explanation is admittedly somewhat forced, but it may resolve the matter. The Yerushalmi itself needs explanation. If the true rule does not depend on an oath, why does Rabbi Akiva formulate his reason in terms of the fact that the others require an oath and the heirs do not? The Rosh says that Rabbi Akiva adds that reason to strengthen his ruling. The point may be this. The Rosh explains the Yerushalmi to mean that the heirs are legally in possession while the creditor still lacks the act of collection. According to Beit Shammai, however, a document standing for collection is regarded as though already collected. In that view the creditor is considered in possession alongside the heir, as is evident in Yevamot in the case of a collapsed house, where Beit Shammai say that they divide because both parties are regarded as possessors. Rabbi Tarfon was a disciple of Beit Shammai and followed their approach, as Rashi notes in the first chapter of Yevamot and as is reflected in the statement in Berakhot, "I reclined in order to recite according to Beit Shammai." Rabbi Akiva can therefore be understood as responding to Rabbi Tarfon on his own premises. For everyone who does not accept Beit Shammai, the property is given to the heirs in any event because the creditor is still lacking collection, as the Yerushalmi says. But even according to Rabbi Tarfon, who follows Beit Shammai and treats a document standing for collection as already collected, so that the creditor is as much in possession as the heir, the property should nevertheless be given to the heirs because the creditor requires an oath and they do not. This is what the Rosh means by saying that Rabbi Akiva stated the oath rationale merely to strengthen the case against Rabbi Tarfon. According to Rashi's explanation in Bava Metzia 37b of Rabbi Akiva's statement in a different collapsed estate case, Rabbi Akiva himself may accept Beit Shammai's concept of a document standing for collection as collected in some settings, despite being a disciple of Beit Hillel. If so, he personally needed the oath rationale there. But according to our practical ruling like Beit Hillel, the oath rationale is unnecessary. This reconciles the Rosh. In Yesh Nochalin and Bekhorot, the Rosh argues that according to Rabbi Akiva on Rabbi Tarfon's premises, the decisive reason is the oath; where the creditor was exempted from the oath, a debt held by a third party would be collectible even though it is prospective property. This proves that a debt owed to the estate is not classified as prospective property for this purpose. Our own ruling can still deny collection after an oath waiver for a different reason, namely that the creditor is not considered already in possession. The doctrine that a document standing for collection is considered collected and the doctrine of prospective property are distinct. Thus, Rabbi Akiva's response to Rabbi Tarfon teaches that prospective property is not the obstacle, while our final ruling like Beit Hillel can still give the property to the heirs because they are the possessors. Although it is difficult that the Rosh did not spell out so much, the logic itself is compelling and the two discussions can thereby be reconciled.
But the Geonim enacted, etc. The Sema writes in the name of the Rambam that in the western lands people write in debt instruments that both movables and land are subjected to the debt. Accordingly, movables may be collected from orphans by strict law by virtue of that condition. This shows that merely writing "movables and land," without the word "together with" that effects an agav kinyan, suffices to collect from heirs even though it does not suffice to collect from purchasers. The Maggid Mishneh says the same in the name of the Rashba, as do the Rashba's responsum 1914 and his novellae at the end of Kiddushin. His proof is from the rule that if one designates an ox as an apotiki and then sells it, the creditor cannot collect it because the designation has no public voice. Relative to heirs, however, no public notice is needed. Thus, express designation of movables alone should suffice against heirs. The Rosh similarly writes in Ketubot, in the chapter Metziat HaIshah, in the name of Rabbenu Tam that people write "movables and land" in order to collect movables from heirs, while the omission of an agav clause is effective to prevent collection from purchasers. Mordechai in the chapter HaNaarah says the same, and there are virtually no dissenters. It is true that Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Kamma 12 write that although public notice is irrelevant to heirs, the reason expressly designated property can be collected from heirs is that since it can be collected from purchasers it can also be collected from heirs, just as according to the view that liens are not d'oraisa a written loan is collectible from heirs because it is collectible from purchasers, even though as against heirs there is no inherent distinction between a written and oral loan. This seems to imply that without an agav clause, where movables cannot be collected from purchasers, they likewise could not be collected from heirs. The Rosh himself writes the same in Bava Kamma, that where a slave was made an apotiki the creditor can collect from heirs because he can collect from purchasers and the Sages did not wish to strengthen heirs more than purchasers. Yet in Ketubot the Rosh follows Rabbenu Tam that merely writing movables and land suffices against heirs. This requires study. In truth, one might initially infer from this the view of the Knesset HaGedolah that the document must expressly say that the movables are subject "in life and in death," and that without such language they still cannot be collected from heirs. Then the Bava Kamma cases could concern a document lacking that clause, and the ability to collect from heirs would require the separate reason that the property is collectible from purchasers. But aside from the fact that the Rosh's stated rationale does not fit that distinction, Rabbenu Tam's formulation itself would then omit the principal point. He says that collection from heirs follows because people write "movables and land," whereas on the Knesset HaGedolah's view the decisive words are "in life and in death." The Rashba likewise never mentions such language. Even if we required it in a ketubah, one could explain that a ketubah does not by strict law collect movables even during the husband's life, so a generic reference to movables might be intended only for lifetime collection. To extend it to heirs one would need "in life and in death." A loan document is different. Since the creditor already collects even the debtor's clothing during the debtor's lifetime, an express clause concerning movables would be superfluous unless intended to strengthen collection against heirs. We interpret instruments in favor of efficacy, so the ordinary clause would itself point to heirs. But in truth even a ketubah should not require the special words. The Rambam in Hilkhot Ishut writes that the custom is to write "movables and land" in order to collect movables, without mentioning "in life and in death." In the model ketubah in Hilkhot Yibum, chapter 4, he does include "in life and in death," but there he also includes an agav clause, for which "in life and in death" is certainly unnecessary. Maharil, siman 74, likewise writes in the name of Rav Yitzchak Zekenim that such language is used to allow collection of prospective property, showing that it is not needed merely to reach movables of heirs. This is strong evidence. The earlier contradiction nevertheless remains. It appears that the difference depends on whether liens are d'oraisa. If liens are d'oraisa, movables too are in principle subject to a creditor, as the Rashba explains at the end of Kiddushin. Ordinarily they are unavailable from heirs only because the creditor did not rely on them. Once the document expressly subjects "movables and land," that lack of reliance disappears, and collection from heirs follows. If liens are not d'oraisa, however, express reliance alone cannot create a lien on property that passes to heirs. Liability of heirs exists only by rabbinic enactment, analogous to collection from purchasers. The Sages' reason to impose that enactment is the need to preserve lending. Where property could in any event be freely sold without being collectible from purchasers, that concern is weak, and the law remains that heirs are not liable. Thus, under the view that liens are not d'oraisa, collection from heirs tracks collection from purchasers. This is logical and clear. The statements of Tosafot and the Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Kamma were made in the discussion of Rav Nachman, and Rav Nachman holds in Gittin 50 that a debt instrument collected from orphans takes only inferior land even if it contains an enhancement clause. Tosafot there conclude that Rav Nachman holds liens are not d'oraisa. They therefore properly made heirs depend on purchasers. According to our halakhic ruling that liens are d'oraisa, however, collection from heirs need not depend on collection from purchasers. An express clause subjecting movables and land therefore suffices against heirs. In any event, regardless of the precise theoretical explanation, this law should be accepted because the Rambam, Rabbenu Tam, the Rosh in Ketubot, the Rashba, and the Tur all agree.
They enacted. The Sema writes that according to the Tur below in siman 419, nowadays movables of heirs are collectible by strict law because the bulk of commerce is conducted through movables. They are therefore comparable to the camels of Arabia mentioned in Ketubot, from which a woman collects her ketubah. If so, the enactment of the Geonim is no longer necessary. On this understanding there is an apparent contradiction in the Rosh and in the Tur. The Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Kamma gives the rule cited by the Tur in siman 419, that nowadays movables are collectible by strict law because they are comparable to the camels of Arabia. Yet in the chapter Metziat HaIshah he cites Rabbenu Tam that some authorities indeed say that because our commerce is primarily in movables the case resembles the camels of Arabia, but Rabbenu Tam rejects this, reasoning that if so the creditor should collect even from purchasers of movables, thereby destroying the market enactment. Rabbenu Tam instead explains that people customarily write express clauses subjecting movables and land. The Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 100 already notes this contradiction and leaves it unresolved. The Tur likewise says here that movables of heirs are collectible only because of the enactment of the Geonim, whereas in siman 419 he treats collection as strict law. The key is to understand Rabbenu Tam's proof. Why should the fact that collection from purchasers would undermine market commerce disprove the debtor's substantive lien? If modern commercial reality causes the creditor to rely on movables, why should the rights of purchasers simply erase the creditor's lien? If the market requires protection, one could enact protection for purchasers while leaving the creditor's strict right against heirs intact. The answer is that a lien itself cannot arise on contradictory terms without the parties' intent. First, the debtor cannot reasonably be presumed to have subjected all his movables permanently, because then he could never freely sell ordinary goods, and the market would cease to function. We are witnesses that he did not borrow on terms that would immobilize all his property. Second, from the creditor's side, if he truly intended to rely on movables he could have written an express clause subjecting movables and land, or an agav clause. His failure to do so is evidence that he did not rely on them. Thus, the market concern is not merely an external policy overriding an existing lien. It helps define the parties' original intent and shows that no universal lien on ordinary movables arose. This is why the creditor cannot claim strict law merely because modern commerce relies on movables. He could have protected himself expressly and failed to do so. The camels of Arabia are different. There the reliance was on a specific class of principal assets, not on every movable item the debtor might need to sell in ordinary commerce. The debtor's assent to such a lien does not freeze his entire estate, and a woman's ketubah form was not ordinarily drafted with special clauses for each local class of commercial asset. It is also possible that the case concerned a place where ketubot were not written at all. There is therefore no contradiction between treating the camels of Arabia as subject by strict law and refusing to treat all modern movables the same way in ordinary debts and ketubot. In tort cases, however, both reasons disappear. The injured party had no opportunity to draft a document and protect himself, and the tort lien does not depend on the tortfeasor's consensual intent. Liability arises automatically, like an obligation written in the Torah. Thus, modern reliance on movables can make them comparable to the camels of Arabia for tort recovery even though ordinary creditors still require the Geonic enactment. The Rosh is therefore consistent: in tort he can say that collection is by strict law, while in debt and ketubah he follows Rabbenu Tam that collection of general movables rests on the enactment of the Geonim. Along this line, the previous difficulty in the Rosh may also be resolved. The Rosh may hold that during the Talmudic period merely writing "movables and land" without agav did not itself create a sufficient lien. Nowadays, however, when commercial reliance on movables is already analogous to the camels of Arabia, the only reason to deny a lien is the inference from the creditor's failure to write such a clause. Once he does write it, that negative inference disappears, and the underlying modern reliance gives the clause full force against heirs. Thus the Bava Kamma passage speaks to the original Talmudic law, while the chapter Metziat HaIshah speaks to our era, in which express writing makes modern movables equivalent to the camels of Arabia. This also clarifies Tosafot in Bekhorot 52, who write that Rabbenu Tam called the enactment of the Geonim d'oraisa. At first this is puzzling. If the reason is simply that modern commerce is in movables, Rabbenu Tam himself elsewhere rejects the analogy to the camels of Arabia. If, on the other hand, the reason is merely the customary clause "movables and land," what does that have to do with an enactment of the Geonim? According to what I have written, the Geonim's enactment operates together with the changed commercial reality and the customary writing. Since people now write the clause and rely on movables, the resulting collection has the force of the underlying law, which is what Rabbenu Tam meant by calling it d'oraisa in practical effect. The Shach in seif katan 2 writes that the Rosh in the chapter HaIshah SheNaflu says that nowadays movables have returned to the status of land by strict Talmudic law. No such statement appears in the Rosh there. The Rosh says only that after the enactment of the Geonim movables are treated like land, not that the Talmudic law itself changed. There is also a practical difference for us between movables collectible by strict law and those collectible only by Geonic enactment. The Rashba writes in responsum 1914 that even according to Rav Hai Gaon, who gives an earlier oral creditor priority over a later written creditor, when the competing property consists of movables of heirs and the later written creditor has an express lien on movables, the written creditor collects first. His right is grounded in the law of the document, while the oral creditor reaches the movables only by later enactment, and the underlying law prevails over the enactment. I found the same in the Rif's Shaarei Shevuot, shaar 2, din 13, regarding a living debtor whose movables are claimed by an ordinary creditor and by a woman's ketubah. The creditor has priority because he collects movables under the law of the Gemara, whereas the woman reaches them only by later enactment, since a ketubah did not originally create a lien on movables even during the husband's life. Mordechai in the chapter HaKotev writes the same in the name of the Raavyah in his laws of ketubot, and the Mabit cites the same from Tzeror HaKesef. The principle therefore has no meaningful dispute.
From the heirs. If the debtor gave movables as a gift on his deathbed, there is a dispute among the authorities. Tosafot in Ketubot 49b hold that a creditor can collect from such a gift. Their reasoning is that if a creditor can collect movables from heirs whose inheritance is d'oraisa, all the more so may he collect them from a deathbed recipient whose acquisition is only d'rabbanan. The Rosh in Kiddushin, in the chapter HaOmer, likewise writes that a creditor collects movables given as a deathbed gift, meaning after the enactment of the Geonim. The Baal HaTerumot, shaar 61, part 1, records the same in the name of Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, and Mordechai in the chapter HaNaarah cites authorities who agree. The Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 100 also appears to follow this view. After stating the Geonic enactment, he writes that if the husband sold the movables or gave them as a gift during his lifetime, the woman cannot collect them. His specification of a lifetime gift implies that a deathbed gift remains collectible. On the other hand, Mordechai there cites Rabbenu Shmuel that the Geonim did not extend their enactment to a deathbed gift and left such property governed by the original law. Terumat HaDeshen, pesakim siman 86, says that Rabbenu Shmuel is a lone opinion, but he evidently did not see Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, in the discussion of a widow's right to maintenance from the estate. Nimukei Yosef writes that Rabbi Yonah, the Rashba, and the Ritva all agree that the Geonic enactment did not make movables transferred by deathbed gift subject to creditors. A marginal gloss to the Amsterdam Rif likewise says, "and so wrote the Rosh," evidently meaning Rabbenu Shmuel in Mordechai, for Nimukei Yosef sometimes abbreviates Rashbam as Rosh. The Tur in siman 253, paragraph 17, also seems to follow this position. He rules that if a person on his deathbed instructed that two hundred be given to one person and other amounts to others, and a creditor's document is later produced, the creditor collects from each recipient in proportion to his share. The Tur's case must concern land or recipients who received land, because if they received movables the creditor would not collect from them. The Beit Yosef explains that this is the Talmudic law and that after the Geonic enactment movables and land are alike. The Perishah correctly asks why the Tur would state an obsolete rule without warning, thereby misleading readers. His attempted answer is insufficient. The clearer explanation is that the Tur follows the authorities who hold that the Geonic enactment never extended to a deathbed gift. It is possible that the Rosh did not accept his earlier position as final halakhah, or that the Tur knew from a responsum that his father later retracted, so the Tur is not necessarily disagreeing with the Rosh. The Rama in Even HaEzer siman 93, seif 2, also cites Rabbenu Shmuel's view. The Beit Shmuel objects that it contradicts Tosafot and the Rosh, but he did not investigate the matter fully, because Rabbenu Shmuel is supported by Rabbi Yonah, Rashba, Ritva, Nimukei Yosef, and the Tur. The matter is therefore a genuine doubt in the law. It appears that this remains so even if the deathbed gift was made to the donor's own sons. Although Even HaEzer siman 100 states that writing property to one's sons does not defeat a woman's ketubah, that is a special protection for a wife based on the ordinance of Usha discussed in the chapter HaNaarah. We have no comparable enactment empowering an ordinary creditor, and one rabbinic enactment should not be extended by analogy to another. Thus, an ordinary creditor has no special rule merely because the recipients are sons, unless beit din determines that the gift was a sham device intended solely to defeat the debt. In such a case beit din may invalidate the evasion because the donor acted improperly, and there is a public interest in preventing such schemes. As to his sons, the result can then parallel the ketubah rule.
