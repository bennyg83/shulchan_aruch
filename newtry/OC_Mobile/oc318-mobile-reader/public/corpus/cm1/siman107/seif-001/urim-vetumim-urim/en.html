Where he left land. This applies even to land that he acquired or inherited after the loan, because when he later acquired it the creditor's lien attached, as explained above in siman 104. Movable property differs because it can always be concealed, and the lender therefore does not rely upon it. See below, siman 111 seif 2, where there is an opinion that even after acquired land is not collected from heirs unless it was expressly encumbered.
We do not compel them, etc. Tur explains that honoring one's father is a positive commandment whose reward is written alongside it, and earthly beit din is therefore not charged with coercing it. Sema and Shach discuss the opinions above in siman 97 that beit din is not obligated to coerce but may do so if it wishes. Sema writes that we do not rule like those authorities, while Shach disagrees. In Tumim I wrote that according to all opinions beit din has no authority to coerce. Those authorities understand the obligation here as rabbinic, and a rabbinic obligation of this type is not coercively enforced.
We do not compel them, etc. Even if money deposited or lent by the deceased is held by others and the heirs never physically acquired it, the creditor likewise did not acquire it. Even if the deceased exempted the creditor from an oath so that the lien could take effect without delay, collection is still incomplete and the heirs immediately become the possessors. See Tumim.
But there is a mitzvah, etc. It appears that even if the deceased was not their father but their uncle, so that the mitzvah of honoring a father does not apply, there remains a mitzvah for heirs who inherited from him to discharge his debt, so that they should not enjoy his estate while leaving their benefactor within the category of a wicked borrower who does not repay. See Tumim, seif katan 1. This applies only if they inherited from him. If they inherited nothing, even children have no obligation, because we rule that honoring a father is fulfilled from the father's resources, not the child's own property.
This is the strict law. If the note states both movable property and land, even without an agav clause, that wording suffices under strict law to collect movable property from heirs, although it does not allow collection from purchasers. This applies even if the note does not expressly say 'during life and after death,' but merely mentions movable property and land. See Tumim.
But the Geonim enacted, etc. They nevertheless wrote that notes should initially include both movable property and land. Sema writes that nowadays, when most commerce is conducted through movable property, perhaps Talmudic law itself would allow collection from movable property. See Tumim for my reconciliation of Rosh. I wrote there that there is an important practical difference between collection by Talmudic law and by the Geonic enactment. Suppose there are two notes against heirs who possess only movable property, one note expressly encumbering movable property and land, and the other containing no such clause. The creditor with the express movable property clause has priority, because he collects by Talmudic law, while the other collects only by Geonic enactment. We do not allow a later enactment to displace a collection right existing under Talmudic law. See Tumim.
From a loan held by others. Even where the loan has not yet been collected, and that debtor himself owns only movable property, we must employ two principles: first, the debt is not considered merely expected property because it is already subject to the deceased's creditor through Rabbi Nasan; second, the movable property eventually collected would ordinarily be movable property of heirs, which is subject only by Geonic enactment. Even so, collection is allowed. Shach writes that all agree to this.
Whether they collected. Under Talmudic law there was a distinction: if heirs collected land from their debtor, their father's creditor could then collect that land, but if they collected movable property he could not. After the Geonic enactment, movable property was equated with land for this purpose.
The heirs have the stronger claim. By strict law a creditor collects from heirs only from land. The Geonim added movable property, but they did not enact collection from movable property when the heirs themselves wish to satisfy the debt with land. Shach writes that even if the creditor seized movable property, it is removed from him. This means seizure after death. If he seized it during the debtor's lifetime, even Talmudic law recognizes the seizure. This is simple.
