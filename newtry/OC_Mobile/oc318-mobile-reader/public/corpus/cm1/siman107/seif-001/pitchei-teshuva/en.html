Even if one rejects the Shach's view in siman 86, seif katan 5, and says Rabbi Natan's lien applies only when the debtor has no other assets, a creditor may still collect from his debtor's receivables even when other assets exist. The reason is that in contemporary commerce a person's livelihood and principal financial reliance are often his outstanding receivables, so creditors extend credit in reliance upon them just as they rely upon ordinary merchandise. Noda BiYehudah derives this from two responsa of the Rosh. In the second edition, siman 53, he develops the point further and states that it follows that a creditor may likewise collect debts owed by non-Jews, even though Rashba expressed doubt whether Rabbi Natan's lien applies to such debts. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 66, discusses Reuven, who purchased timber from a nobleman for seven hundred gold coins, paid two hundred, and agreed to pay the remaining five hundred by a fixed date and remove the timber from the forest by another date. Their written contract stated that if either deadline passed, Reuven would lose his rights. When Reuven could not pay the remaining five hundred, a wealthy Jew paid the nobleman for him. Reuven then delivered the contract to that Jew as collateral, fixed a repayment date, and agreed that if he failed to pay by then the contract would become absolutely the lender's. Reuven did not pay on time and also missed the date for removing the timber. Meanwhile the forest passed to another nobleman who refused outsiders access, and both Reuven and the lender despaired of recovering either the timber or their money. Reuven died. Later the new nobleman asked the lender for a loan. The lender proposed that the nobleman acquire the timber himself, since the owner of the forest would not dare prevent him from removing it. The nobleman agreed because he needed the money and bought the timber at a modest loss. The lender, being honest, wanted only repayment of his own money and intended to return any surplus to the heirs. The heirs, however, had other creditors and there was also a widow's ketubah. The question was whether those creditors had a right to the money. Chatam Sofer first rules that the condition in Reuven's original contract, under which he would lose the timber if he missed the deadline, was an asmachta and therefore ineffective, because the dominant halachic view is that asmachta applies even in dealings with a non-Jew. He further rules that the second Jew did not acquire the timber from Reuven merely by delivery of the contract. Even an outright transfer of such a contractual right requires proper writing and delivery of the underlying right, and the fact that a bearer may present the contract does not cure the defect where the instrument was written for the first purchaser's acquisition. In addition, the timber had been acquired through the rented place in the forest, and that courtyard was protected with reference to the first purchaser, not the second. One cannot combine two leniencies and say that the second person's courtyard and acquisition come simultaneously through a place not protected with his interests in mind. All the more so, here the transfer was not an outright sale but collateral with a condition that it become absolute upon nonpayment, which is itself an asmachta. Nor can the second Jew acquire through commercial custom, because no such custom was established. The timber therefore remained legally Reuven's. Nevertheless, Chatam Sofer concludes that the widow and other creditors cannot collect from this later payment. Reuven's original purchase from the nobleman was valid: the initial two hundred were not merely earnest money but part payment, while the balance became a debt, and the nobleman had rented him a place in the forest in which to keep the timber until the removal date. The timber was therefore acquired through his rented courtyard, and the nobleman acquired his purchase money. When the rental period expired, the land returned to the nobleman. Even absent the invalid forfeiture condition, the nobleman was thereafter neither a paid nor unpaid custodian of the timber. When the second nobleman later took control, Reuven had no enforceable claim against the first nobleman. The value later returned to the heirs therefore came to them as a new acquisition rather than as an existing estate asset subject to the earlier liens. Moreover, they had already despaired entirely of the timber because of the violent second nobleman, and under secular law they likewise had no claim. The widow and creditors therefore have no lien on this later recovered movable property of the heirs.
The law is with the heirs. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 139, addresses a suggestion that even when the land is encumbered property in the hands of a purchaser or recipient, heirs need not surrender movable property in their possession to the creditor. The suggestion relied on Maharsha to Bava Kamma 108b, who understood Tosafot to mean that a creditor may collect from encumbered land even where the free assets consist only of movable property. If so, the creditor could collect the land and the heirs could repel him from the movables, since movable property of heirs is not collected where land is available. Rabbi Akiva Eiger rejects this. Maharsha's view is singular and is not reflected in the general body of authorities. Ketzot HaChoshen below, siman 367, seif katan 4, likewise rejects Maharsha, noting that the rule barring collection from encumbered property where free assets exist applies even where the free assets are movable. The same is indicated by the laws of a deathbed gift in siman 253, where so long as there is any place to collect from heirs, even from movables, one collects from them rather than from the recipient of the deathbed gift. Even if one accepted Maharsha, it would not help in Rabbi Akiva Eiger's case, because the land had been given to a recipient who then died leaving minor heirs. A creditor cannot presently collect from minor heirs of the recipient, just as he cannot collect from minor heirs of the original debtor. In such a situation the debtor's heirs cannot force the creditor to wait until those minors mature while withholding movable estate assets. Rabbi Akiva Eiger adds that he is uncertain regarding a widow's ketubah, which may ordinarily be collected from minors because of the policy of chen. Where she could instead collect from movable assets, perhaps that policy concern does not apply and the court should not disturb the minor heirs. See below, siman 111, seif 13, seif katan 5.
