Seif 1: It is a mitzvah upon the heirs to pay their father’s debt, etc. Note: See Sema and Shach, who cite Ramban and Rosh as disputing a positive command whose reward is stated alongside it, regarding which earthly beit din is not commanded to compel compliance. The question is whether beit din nevertheless has permission to compel if it chooses. It appears possible to prove that where beit din is not commanded to compel, it is forbidden to do so. The proof is from Rosh Hashana, where the Gemara expounds the verse “and you shall do it” as a warning to beit din to compel the person to fulfill his obligation. The Gemara asks that compulsion is already derived from “he shall offer it,” which teaches that we compel him until he says, “I want.” If discretionary coercion were permitted even without a command, there would be no difficulty. One could say that “he shall offer it” merely teaches that beit din is permitted to compel in the sacrificial context. Because the Torah says the offering must be “according to his will,” one might otherwise have thought that coercion is forbidden there even though it might be permitted in other mitzvot. The verse would then teach permission to coerce, while “and you shall do it” would separately impose an affirmative duty upon beit din to do so. The fact that the Gemara does not answer this way indicates that absent a command to compel, beit din is not permitted to coerce. This also follows from Bava Batra 48a, which derives from “he shall offer it” that a compelled sale can be valid. In a mitzvah where the Torah did not authorize compulsion, the Torah evidently desires the act to be performed through genuine willingness. If beit din coerces him, the act would not have the status of a voluntary mitzvah. Once it would not count as fulfillment of the mitzvah, beit din has no independent power to coerce him merely at its discretion and thereby take another’s property. It follows that they are forbidden to compel in such a case. Examine carefully.
