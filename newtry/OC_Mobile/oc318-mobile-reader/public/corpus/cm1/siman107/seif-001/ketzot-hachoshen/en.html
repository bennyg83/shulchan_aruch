The rule that heirs must pay their father's debts even from debts owed to their father raises a broad dispute whether a widow's ketubah and an ordinary creditor are alike regarding a decedent's outstanding loans. Ramban, in his novellae to Bava Batra, analyzes the matter at length. He concludes that for an ordinary creditor and, according to one view, for a widow as well, money collected by the heirs from a debt owed to their father can be subject to collection. Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia also holds that a widow can collect her ketubah from money paid on a debt owed to the deceased, and he cites the Mishnah in Ketubot: if a man dies leaving a wife, a creditor, and money of his in the hands of others, the property is given to the weakest claimant, which is explained as the wife for reasons of social favor. Rabbi Akiva disagrees only because movables are not encumbered to creditors and heirs need no oath, not because the debt is inherently expected property for the wife. Once later enactments allowed collection from heirs' movables, Rabbi Meir HaLevi argues that a widow should likewise collect from an outstanding loan. Ramban says that proof is not decisive. If there is a real distinction between a ketubah and an ordinary creditor, the Mishnah can be read distributively: the deposit belongs to the widow, while both deposit and loan can belong to the creditor. Ramban finds a stronger proof from the baraita concerning one who was owed one hundred by his deceased brother and left a widow awaiting yibbum. The surviving brother cannot say, "Since I am the yavam, I have taken possession." Rather, the money is removed from him, land is purchased with it, and he receives the fruits. The Gemara establishes the ruling through the combination of the view that movables are subject to a ketubah and Rabbi Natan's lien. Ramban argues that after the later enactment making heirs' movables collectible, this supports collection from an outstanding loan as well. He nevertheless considers whether the baraita could follow Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, who treats a loan as presently possessed rather than expected. At first that would introduce three special stringencies into ketubah law, while the Gemara says it could not find even two. But Ramban notes that several Amoraim regard a loan as presently possessed even according to the Sages, including Abaye in other contexts. The key distinction is Abaye's rule that a creditor acquires retroactively only after actual collection. In Pesachim, where heirs had already collected land on their father's debt, Abaye's view that a creditor collects retroactively makes it as though the land had come into their father's hands during his lifetime. A creditor can therefore collect it without Rabbi Natan's rule. In the yavam case, however, the yavam has not yet collected the brother's debt. He says, "I am the heir and will keep it." So long as he has not actually collected, Abaye's retroactive acquisition cannot operate. Rabbi Natan's lien is still needed to allow the widow to reach the debt. This resolves why the yavam case can require two stringencies even according to Abaye. This distinction also answers Tosafot's question from the Mishnah in Perek HaKotev, where a man dies leaving a wife, a creditor, heirs, and money or a deposit in others' hands. Rabbi Tarfon says it is given to the weakest claimant, understood as the widow. Tosafot asks how the Gemara can say no tanna accepts two ketubah stringencies when Rabbi Tarfon apparently does. According to Abaye, there is no need for Rabbi Natan in that Mishnah once the debt is actually being collected for the heirs, because retroactive acquisition makes it as though it reached the father in life. In the yavam case, collection has not yet occurred, so Rabbi Natan is still required. This also explains Rif's citation of the yavam baraita as practical law despite Raavad's objection that the Gemara appeared to reject it by saying no tanna accepts two stringencies. That rejection is specifically within Abaye's framework. We rule like Rava that collection operates only from the time of collection onward. According to Rava, even when money is actually collected from the debtor, Rabbi Natan remains necessary to connect it to the earlier creditor or widow. Thus the Mishnah in Perek HaKotev itself shows that the law can accept the two stringencies, exactly as Tosafot asked. Rava's statement that this was what he had heard Abaye call "not a Mishnah" can therefore be understood as limited to Abaye's own theory. Ramban labored to defend the opinion that a ketubah cannot be collected from an outstanding loan, because the yavam sugya appears to say that it can. In my view the sugya can be reconciled differently. Rashi explains the yavam's statement, "Since I am the yavam, I have taken possession," as relying on his status as heir once he enters the yibbum. Yet a yavam does not inherit property that is merely expected, and Beit Shmuel rules that even in his brother's estate he does not take expected property. A loan is usually expected property. The answer is that the yavam can claim only half of a loan he himself owed to the deceased brother. In Perek Yesh Nochalin we rule that where a firstborn is himself the debtor, there is uncertainty whether his own debt is considered presently possessed by the father, because by his undertaking he may be regarded as having fully subjected the property to the father so that the father was considered in possession during life. Rashbam explains that because the firstborn may have given the father a particularly strong interest, the doubtful half is divided. The same structure applies to the yavam. If he wishes to retain half of the debt on the theory that he inherits it, he must necessarily treat that half as having been sufficiently possessed by his deceased brother during life to pass through inheritance. But if the deceased brother was treated as presently possessed of that half during life, it was also subject during life to the wife's ketubah lien. Thus the yavam cannot use the very theory of prior possession to inherit the debt while denying the widow the lien that follows from that same prior possession. Accordingly, when he seeks to keep the half treated as inherited, it is removed from him and used to purchase land from which he receives the fruits.
The Shulchan Aruch rules that if the debt instrument does not expressly say that the creditor may collect movables both during the borrower's life and after his death, the heirs may satisfy him with land rather than movables. The Sema explains that this is because heirs' movables are collectible only by Geonic enactment. That explanation would imply that if the document includes a valid lien on movables together with land, so that movables were encumbered by strict law, the heirs could no longer insist on paying with land. This is difficult. We rule that one does not collect from heirs except from inferior land, even if they possess medium quality land. That is because the creditor's Torah baseline is inferior land and the rabbinic upgrade to medium land was not extended to heirs. The creditor's preference for cash when the debtor has cash is likewise rabbinic rather than Torah law. Therefore, when collecting from heirs, one would expect the law to return to its Torah baseline and permit payment with inferior land even though movables had been subjected. The correct reading of the Shulchan Aruch is that it excludes only a document that expressly says the creditor may collect from movables both during life and after death. Such an explicit condition is effective, just as an express condition permitting collection from superior land even from heirs is effective. This is also the exact wording of the Shulchan Aruch: unless it is stated expressly that he may collect from movables both in life and after death. Thus a generic clause subjecting movables together with land is not enough. Heirs still pay according to the ordinary rule governing heirs, namely from land, unless the document specifically overrides the after death rule. The same principle appears in a widow's ketubah. Even though our ketubot commonly contain clauses subjecting movables together with land, Even HaEzer rules that heirs may satisfy the widow with land. This is even clearer, because the basic Talmudic law never gave a ketubah a preferred right to movables at all. The Geonim later allowed collection from heirs' movables where necessary, but if the heirs offer her the inferior land to which the ketubah is ordinarily limited, why should a generic movable lien make her collection right superior? The lien clause protects her if land is unavailable or if property was otherwise transferred in a manner covered by the lien. It does not determine the medium of collection. Just as all categories of land may be subject to the ketubah yet she ordinarily collects only inferior land, so too movables may be subject without giving her the right to insist on movables. Shiltei Gibborim uses the same careful wording as the Shulchan Aruch: if it is not expressly written that the creditor may collect movables both during life and after death, and the heirs wish to give land, they may do so. If the document expressly extends the collection right to movables after death, the condition is binding. This is also how Beit Shmuel's statement should be understood. His phrase, "where it is not expressly written from the movables," does not mean merely that a generic movable lien is absent. It means the document must expressly secure the right to collect movables even after death. Although the Shach writes at the beginning of siman 101 that even for a ketubah, heirs who actually possess money must pay money, Tumim questions that rule, and in my view the ordinary principle remains that heirs' estates are collected from inferior land unless a specific condition validly overrides it. Therefore even a standard movable lien together with land does not by itself prevent the heirs from satisfying the creditor or widow with land. Only an explicit life and death collection clause does so.
