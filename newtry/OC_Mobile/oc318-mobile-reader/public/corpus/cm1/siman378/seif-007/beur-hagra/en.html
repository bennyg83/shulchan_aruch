Whether with their bodies, etc. As above. It appears that the Tur repeats this to exclude a possible reading of the Rambam. In chapter 1 the Rambam uses the Gemara's language, distinguishing "they damaged" from "they were damaged," but omits Rav Pappa's qualification. One might therefore read him like Rashi, where active damage differs from passive injury. Yet in chapter 6 regarding one's property he distinguishes intent from lack of intent, suggesting a possible distinction between bodily and property damage. The Maggid Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh, however, write that the Rambam makes no such distinction.
If he did not know, etc., but, etc. This is the Tur's language, but it is difficult because the distinction between knowledge and lack of knowledge is mentioned only in the damager's domain and would more naturally belong in seif 6. Here the necessary distinction is between active damage and passive injury, as in the Gemara. It appears that a scribal error displaced this sentence. The entire passage follows Rashi.
Therefore, etc. See Beer HaGolah. Rashi and Tosafos 32a explain similarly. The Tur in the name of the Ramah, however, writes that when both parties are actively moving, even active damage is treated like mutual injury. This explains the Mishna's wording "they damaged one another." The distinction is whether both act with permission, one acts without permission, or a person stops and thereby creates an obstacle. Knowledge can then make the situation equivalent to permission. Thus the Tur and Shulchan Aruch say "whether with their bodies or with their property," teaching that bodily and property damage follow the same framework.
