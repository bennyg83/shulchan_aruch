and O'D. A:
Whether with his body, etc. Meaning the injured party's body, as explained below, and likewise the injured party's property. This is as stated there concerning one who brought an object in and one who entered, showing that the rules are equal. The same applies where the householder caused the damage. Only where one filled another's courtyard did they say that the owner may break his way out. See Tosafot there.
And specifically where he damaged him, etc.; if both had permission, etc. This entire passage is Tur's wording, but it is difficult, because it combines Rashi's approach with Rambam's. Lechem Mishneh already raised this issue. According to Rambam, where the damager has permission, where both have permission, or where neither has permission, the laws are equal: intentional damage is liable, and unintentional damage is exempt. When the damage occurs in the injured party's domain, however, the damager is liable even unintentionally. This is Rambam's explanation of the Gemara. Rashi's approach differs: where both have permission or neither has permission, one who actively damages is liable even without intent, but one who is injured automatically is exempt. In the injured party's domain the entrant is always liable, while in the damager's domain he is always exempt unless he knew of the entrant, in which case it is as though both had permission. Thus Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in seif 4 refer specifically to the injured party's domain. In the public domain, Rambam equates it with the damager's domain, requiring intent, whereas Rashi imposes liability for active damage even without intent. This also explains the different readings of the Gemara. See 50 and 81.
And some say, etc. Ramah follows Rashi's approach, but adds that if the householder knew of the entrant, both are treated as having equal permission even regarding the entrant, so if the householder was injured through him he is exempt. Rav Pappa's distinction applies to both clauses, though its principal novelty concerns the latter. The statement that where he knew they are both 'with permission' should read 'both without permission.' This is clear from the Mishna: where each proceeds normally, both have permission; if one stops, one has permission and the other does not; if one warns the other, both are without permission. See 80 and 81.
