An ordinary wind. The Tur states: provided that he was not under unavoidable compulsion, for example if he fell from a roof because of an unusual wind. The Bach writes: This is difficult, for the Gemara says that one is liable for an unavoidable accident as for an intentional act, and it further says that one who fell from a roof in an unusual wind and caused damage is liable for the damage, although exempt from the other four categories of payments. The Tur rules this way in siman 421, seif 13, and the Beit Yosef raised the same difficulty. It appears that here the Tur means only that it must not have been a complete accident, for in a complete accident one is exempt even for the damage itself, as the Maggid Mishneh writes in chapter 1 of Hilchot Chovel in the name of the commentators. His example of one who fell means that he fell under circumstances amounting to a complete accident. The Sema distinguished between damage to a person and damage to utensils, but this is untenable, for the Mishnah and Gemara expressly make no such distinction. Rather, the correct distinction is within the category of an unusual wind itself: a complete accident, such as an exceptionally violent storm capable of uprooting mountains and breaking rocks, differs from a wind that is unusual but not so powerful.
