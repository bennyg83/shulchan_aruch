Rosh says that when two courtyards are at different elevations, the upper owner must assist with the lower foundation because no wall can stand without a foundation. Yet for a ten handbreadth anti trespass barrier, Rosh and the Tur indicate that the lower owner supplies the ground up to the level of the upper courtyard and the upper owner need only contribute to the additional height needed to reach ten handbreadths. The distinction is that the four amot privacy wall gives the upper owner no saving from the difference in elevation. He still needs four full amot above his own courtyard, so he must help with the foundation below. A ten handbreadth barrier is different. The upper owner already benefits from the vertical difference. If the lower roof is six handbreadths below the courtyard, only four more are needed. Thus the elevation itself is counted toward his protection, and the lower owner bears the ground below the upper courtyard level. Ramban appears to disagree and to require the upper owner to assist from below even for the ten handbreadth barrier. He uses the rule of two courtyards at different heights to answer why the Gemara did not prove that visual intrusion is damage, saying the case could be established as a ten handbreadth partition. Since the source there says the upper owner assists from below, Ramban evidently applies that assistance even to the ten handbreadth case.
The roof owner builds from the ground of the courtyard. This requires explanation in light of the rule for two courtyards at different elevations. There the upper owner assists the lower owner in the foundation when the wall is required for privacy, because there is no stable wall without a foundation. For the ten handbreadth barrier, however, Rosh and the Tur indicate that the lower owner bears the lower portion and the upper owner contributes only to what is needed above his level. The reason is that a four amot privacy wall must rise four full amot above the upper courtyard, so the upper owner gains no benefit from the existing elevation and must participate in the foundation. A ten handbreadth anti trespass barrier, however, counts the existing elevation toward the required ten. The lower owner therefore provides the structure up to the upper courtyard level, and the upper owner joins only in the additional height needed to reach ten. Ramban seems to apply the upper owner's participation from below even to the ten handbreadth case, as explained above.
