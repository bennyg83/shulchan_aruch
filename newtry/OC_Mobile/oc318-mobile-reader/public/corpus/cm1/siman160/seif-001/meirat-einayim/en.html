A roof adjoining a courtyard, etc. In the preceding siman, seif 2, I wrote that this appears to mean adjacency in height, so that one is not much higher than the other. If the courtyard were four cubits above the roof, then according to one opinion even the roof owner is exempt, as the Mechaber concludes here and in the following seif. I also wrote there that when they are separated horizontally, that fact may not matter according to the Rambam's approach. Here, however, where the Mechaber rules like the other authorities who require the courtyard owner to contribute toward a ten handbreadth partition for the roof owner, the phrase "a roof adjoining a courtyard" can also be understood as meaning that they are physically close. Their proximity matters because if there is open space between them, the courtyard owner need not contribute merely so that someone crossing from courtyard to roof would be caught as a thief. Even without a partition, crossing an open gap already makes him appear as an intruder. Only where they are close enough for a person to step directly from one to the other can he plausibly say that he crossed accidentally. Then a ten handbreadth partition serves the purpose of establishing trespass.
And the courtyard owner does not cause harm, etc. The Beit Yosef explains that use of a courtyard is fixed and frequent. The roof owner therefore avoids using his roof when the courtyard owner is present and is not harmed by the courtyard owner's observation. The courtyard owner, however, is harmed by the roof owner, because the latter uses the roof only intermittently and can appear unexpectedly, so the courtyard owner does not constantly guard against him. This is similar to what I wrote in the preceding siman. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe conclude that according to the Rambam, who distinguishes only between roofs used for residence and those not used for residence, the present case must concern a roof not used for residence. If it were used for residence, both parties would have to build the four cubit wall together. The Rambam's language, however, does not appear so. He begins with two roofs used for residence and then immediately says, "A roof adjoining his fellow's courtyard requires a parapet four cubits high," which implies that the roof just mentioned is the one under discussion, yet the phrase "he makes for it" implies that the roof owner builds alone. Only afterward does the Rambam introduce the law of roofs not used for residence. The Beit Yosef's reading is therefore strained. It appears instead that even according to the Rambam, although the roof is used for residence, the courtyard owner need not contribute to the full four cubits because use of the roof is still less fixed and less frequent than use of the courtyard. The courtyard owner is therefore not treated as a significant source of visual harm toward the roof owner, while the roof owner is a source of harm to the courtyard owner. This is why the Rambam distinguished between roofs used for residence and those not used for residence: if the roof is used for residence, ten handbreadths are not enough and the roof owner must build four cubits; if it is not used for residence, a ten handbreadth partition suffices.
And the courtyard owner assists him only according to his share in a ten handbreadth partition. The Tur writes likewise. I have already explained that Tosafot and the Rosh hold that the courtyard owner must contribute toward ten handbreadths, while the Rambam holds that he need not contribute even to that amount. Although the Rambam states his exemption explicitly only where the roof is lower than the courtyard, the same reasoning applies to a roof adjoining a courtyard. The Gemara itself compares the baraita stating that when the courtyard is higher than the roof the upper owner is not obligated to the lower at all, with Rav Nachman's rule that adjoining roofs require a ten handbreadth partition. This proves that the questioner viewed a courtyard above a roof and two equal roofs as subject to the same principle. The answer likewise treats them comparably. The Rambam, who exempts the upper courtyard owner even from ten handbreadths, explains the Gemara differently, as I wrote in the Derishah. According to him, the Gemara's final requirement of ten handbreadths applies specifically to two adjoining roofs not used for residence. But between a courtyard and roof, because the roof owner is the source of visual harm, he alone must remove that harm by a full four cubit wall, and the upper owner is entirely exempt. The Mechaber states the Tur's rule here without qualification because the Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef left the Rambam's formulation difficult. In the following seif, where the courtyard is higher than the roof, the Mechaber explicitly records both the Rambam and those who disagree, because that is the precise case of the baraita over which they differ.
