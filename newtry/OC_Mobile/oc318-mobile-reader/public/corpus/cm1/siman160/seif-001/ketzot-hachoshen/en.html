Rambam rules that the owner of a roof adjacent to another's courtyard must erect the protective barrier. Raavad explains that this refers to roofs not ordinarily used for residence. Maggid Mishneh says that this is plainly Rambam's intent. Sema, however, understands the case even with roofs used for dwelling and says the courtyard owner suffers greater harm. That is difficult. Ramban explicitly says that a roof used as living space is like a house, and all agree that visual intrusion from such a roof is a legally cognizable harm. The more convincing reading is therefore that Rambam's case concerns roofs not ordinarily used for dwelling.
When a roof is lower than the neighboring courtyard, Rosh says the courtyard owner shares in the cost necessary to create a ten handbreadth anti trespass partition only to the extent that both parties need it. If the roof is already ten handbreadths below the courtyard, the courtyard owner contributes nothing. If the difference is six handbreadths, the parties share the four handbreadths needed to reach ten, with each effectively bearing two. This raises a question. Once the courtyard owner pays part of the ten handbreadth wall, he appears to become a partner in the wall and its ground. How then can the roof owner continue building the same wall up to four amot to prevent visual intrusion? Rosh elsewhere holds that one partner cannot unilaterally raise a jointly owned courtyard wall where doing so reduces the other's airspace or interferes with his use of the wall. The answer is that the roof owner may place the entire wall on his own ground. Rosh explains at the beginning of Bava Batra that the compulsory customary wall is required only when it is built on both parties' land. If one owner withdraws into his own property and builds a sufficient barrier there, the neighbor cannot force him to surrender land merely because the local wall is ordinarily thicker. Here, too, the roof owner can build on his own ground. The courtyard owner's payment toward the ten handbreadth portion does not necessarily give him ownership of the ground or wall in a way that blocks the additional height. His payment is reimbursement for the protective benefit.
