And if it was a makkat medinah. See Sema, seif katan 20. At first glance the rule is very difficult. Why should a regional calamity strengthen the worker more than an accident that occurs to the employer personally? If the employer suffers an unavoidable event, the worker ordinarily bears the loss because the employer was no more at fault than the worker. A makkat medinah likewise involves no fault of the employer. Indeed, elsewhere makkat medinah can weaken a worker or tenant. A cultivator may receive a rent reduction in a regional calamity where an ordinary private accident would not justify one. Likewise in some rental cases even those who require full rent after destruction of the house exempt in a regional disaster. Rama's intent here is therefore not that in every makkat medinah the worker receives his full wage. Had that been his ruling, he should have written plainly, "If it is a makkat medinah, the employer must pay the entire wage," especially since this siman is the central place for accidents affecting workers. Moreover, Rama immediately rules below that if a teacher must flee because of a change in the air, the teacher bears the loss, even though such environmental change can itself be a makkat medinah, as Shach explains. Rather, Rama is addressing the legal framework. Where an individual accident occurs, the ordinary rule is one of cancellation. For example, if a river changes so the worker's task becomes much harder or easier, and one side wishes to continue only with a wage adjustment while the other refuses, neither can necessarily compel the other to continue. The matter stands in the law of retraction. A makkat medinah is different. There the relationship can remain in force with an adjustment of the wage according to the changed burden. Each side can compel continuation subject to the proper reduction or increase, as Rama explains in siman 321 concerning deduction in a regional calamity. Thus the special effect of makkat medinah is to place the case under the law of adjustment rather than simple cancellation. It does not mean the worker automatically receives the entire original wage. This also explains why Rama cited Mordechai's teacher case in siman 321. Rama relied on Mordechai not necessarily for the conclusion that the teacher receives full pay, but for the principle that makkat medinah can govern future performance as well as past losses, contrary to Maharam Padua. Mordechai's own ruling that a teacher receives pay during a governmental prohibition can be explained on another ground. A teacher is prohibited from taking wages for Torah instruction itself and receives compensation for supervision and guarding, as Nedarim explains. A governmental decree against teaching prevents the instructional objective, but it does not necessarily make supervision of the child impossible. The accident therefore does not fall upon the actual paid service. This resembles one hired to bring cabbage or medicinal produce for a sick person who arrives and finds the patient dead. He performed the very service for which he was hired, even though the ultimate purpose, healing the patient, became impossible. Accordingly, if the student himself dies or becomes ill, or flees so that there is no child to supervise, the accident affects the very service for which the teacher is paid, and the employer is exempt. That is why Rama rules that way elsewhere. If the prohibition affected only the teacher personally, such as the teacher losing his voice, that would be an accident on the worker and the employer would certainly be exempt. A regional governmental decree is different because it prevents the intended instruction without making the paid supervision impossible. That is Mordechai's special case.
However, some disagree. See Shach, seif katan 2. According to the dissenting view, even the deduction for being idle may not apply because rental for a day is treated as a sale for that day. Rama concludes that if the worker already received the entire wage, he need not return even the amount corresponding to idle time. The earlier ruling in siman 312, that if a rented house falls the tenant need not continue paying, does not contradict this. There the defect lies in the rented house itself, which is like a defect in the subject of a sale and places the case under the law of cancellation, as Maharam Padua explains and Sema cites in siman 321. Here the defect lies in the person who hired the service or rented the property. From the worker or owner's perspective, the subject they undertook to provide remains fit. Therefore the sale-like character of daily rental can place the risk on the hirer.
