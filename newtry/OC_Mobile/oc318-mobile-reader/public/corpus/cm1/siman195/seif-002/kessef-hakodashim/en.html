After the time required for speech. In an acquisition involving the actual subject of the sale, once the brief period known as tokh kedei dibbur has passed the parties cannot ordinarily retract even while they are still discussing the same matter. If the transaction operated only through agency, however, one might argue that the agent can withdraw from the agency after that interval, because agency exists only by the agent's continuing consent and is not itself an acquisition in the body of the property. Although in ordinary agency one cannot retract after the act authorized by the agency has already been performed, perhaps in a sale one can say that the agent may retract from the agency component where that component never became an independent property right. If so, in a partnership purchase made through one partner as agent for the other, cancellation of the agency could potentially undermine the entire joint purchase. Yet the sources do not appear to rely on such a distinction. The fact that they do not say that an authorization is useful merely because agency could otherwise be revoked suggests that retraction is not available once the transaction has been performed. Reason also supports this. The transfer of the property itself is irrevocable, and ordinary agency is not revoked after the authorized act is complete. Combining two irrevocable elements should not somehow create a new power of retraction. Regarding acquisition by money, Sema writes that it is doubtful whether one can retract after tokh kedei dibbur while the parties are still engaged in the same subject. An agent who acquires for one party can also resemble one who seizes property in a manner detrimental to others, where even explicit agency does not always validate the seizure. On the other hand, the presumption of the original owner may matter if the alleged retraction is viewed as undoing the agency retroactively, because merchants sometimes conduct themselves in reliance on a limited right to retract. This requires careful study.
