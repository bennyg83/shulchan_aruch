Acquisition is effected only with a utensil, etc. For this reason the current custom is to write at the end of a document, "and we acquired from him with a utensil fit to effect acquisition." "With a utensil" excludes ordinary movable property that is not classified as a utensil. "Fit" excludes objects from which benefit is prohibited; some say it also excludes a utensil made from cattle dung. "To effect acquisition" means with a utensil belonging to the acquirer. "With it" excludes coin. This is the Gemara's formulation. Mahari, siman 16, writes that if this language was not included, money may not be extracted on the basis of that document.
And not with coin. Although one can use a coin as a weight or hang it around his daughter's neck, it is not designated for such uses. Its principal function is spending, by virtue of the image stamped upon it, and that image is subject to cancellation, since the king may invalidate that image and order another image to be minted. Therefore the transferor does not fully rely upon effecting an acquisition through the coin. It follows that if the coin has been invalidated and can no longer be spent, it may be used for chalipin, as Rama writes immediately below. See also the beginning of siman 203, where the source of this law will be explained.
An animal is considered a utensil. In the Derishah I explained that this is not limited to a cow or ox that performs labor such as plowing. Even a lamb and the like qualify, because people derive use from them in their present form: the lamb is raised for wool, it is shorn, and one takes its milk and offspring. They therefore resemble a utensil used for a functional purpose and can serve for chalipin. For this reason the Tur and Rama simply write "an animal," implying every animal.
But other movable property, etc. Hagahot Mordechai to Bava Metzia writes that anything made by human hands is included in the category of a utensil, whereas something formed naturally is produce. Baal HaItur writes likewise. See Maharik, shoresh 20, regarding which matters require a kinyan.
And acquisition is not effected with a utensil belonging to the transferor. This excludes the view in the Gemara that interprets the verse, "A man removed his shoe and gave it to his fellow," to mean that the transferor gives the utensil to the acquirer and says, "Acquire this utensil," and by means of that act the acquirer also acquires the specified sale, whether land or movable property.
