Although they committed a transgression. A question arises concerning sudar kinyan performed on Shabbat. Even if a transaction made in violation of a prohibition is generally effective, our customary sudar kinyan is often performed with the witnesses' utensil. They give their utensil to the transferor on behalf of the purchaser, and documents state, "we effected a kinyan from him." If this depended upon agency, there would be no agency for a transgression. Tosafot says that where a kohen appoints a Jew to betroth a divorced woman for him, the agency is void because one cannot appoint an agent to sin. Although Maharit suggested that agency may survive for a rabbinic prohibition, that is far from certain, and Mishneh LaMelech holds that even rabbinic transgression defeats agency. How, then, can a sudar kinyan on Shabbat remain effective when the witnesses who supply the sudar are apparently the purchaser's agents?
The answer is that the witnesses' sudar does not work through agency from the purchaser. If the purchaser's own sudar were delivered through an agent, ordinary agency would indeed be involved. But where the witnesses supply their own sudar, the purchaser never owned that sudar at all. The mechanism is instead analogous to the Canaanite slave principle. Kiddushin teaches that if one person says, "Here is a maneh, and let your field be acquired to so and so," the third party can acquire, because the transferor's receipt of value can create the acquisition for another. The same principle applies to money and, by extension, to sudar kinyan. Once the transferor receives the witnesses' sudar, that receipt can generate acquisition for the purchaser even though the purchaser himself never owned the sudar. The recipient's consent is still needed in an ordinary gift or sale because one cannot force a benefit upon someone who rejects it. That is not agency. It is merely the rule that a person does not acquire against his will. A Canaanite slave is exceptional in that emancipation can occur even against the slave's will because the master's receipt is decisive. Accordingly, on Shabbat the kinyan remains legally effective even though the participants acted improperly. There is no invalid agency to undo, because the witnesses are not acting as agents with the purchaser's property. Their own sudar operates through the Canaanite slave model. Baal HaIttur's language that the Sages instituted use of an utensil not belonging to the purchaser and that one may acquire for a person in his absence does not prove otherwise. His reference to acquisition in absence explains why we presume the purchaser desires the benefit. The actual transfer mechanism still rests on the transferor's receipt of another person's sudar, not on ordinary agency. This is the correct understanding.
