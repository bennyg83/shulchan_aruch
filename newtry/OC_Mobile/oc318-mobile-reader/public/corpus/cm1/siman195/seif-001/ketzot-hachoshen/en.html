How is acquisition effected through a kinyan? Tosafos, Kiddushin 4, write that a non-Jew also acquires through chalipin. Tosafos at the beginning of HaLokeach Ubar Parato likewise write this, but ask from the Gemara's derivation comparing a non-Jew to 'your fellow': just as acquisition from your fellow is through one mode, so too from a non-Jew through one mode. Yet if chalipin works for both, there would be two modes, money and chalipin, or meshichah and chalipin. Tosafos answer that the statement 'your fellow through one' refers only to the acquisition mode newly taught there, namely money or meshichah, whereas chalipin is common to both.
In my opinion the matter can be resolved according to Rif's responsum concerning whether ona'ah applies to chalipin. Rif writes that the Talmud indicates it does not. The Torah likewise supports this by saying, 'When you sell a sale,' meaning that the Torah warned against ona'ah specifically in a sale, but chalipin is not categorized as a sale. Shitah Mekubetzet in chapter HaZahav cites this. Kesef Mishneh, chapter 13 of Hilchot Mechirah, likewise explains Rambam's ruling that exchanging vessels for vessels has no ona'ah, citing the students of Rashba that the verse speaks of a sale, while chalipin is not a sale. Accordingly, the verse, 'when you sell a sale to your fellow,' can mean that with respect to sale proper there is one mode for a Jew and one for a non-Jew, namely money or meshichah. Chalipin is outside the category of 'sale.'
Shach in siman 123 disputes Tosafos' view that chalipin works for a non-Jew. He rejects Rabbeinu Tam's proof from 'the money of his purchase,' arguing that the exclusion of chalipin there teaches that even for a Jew a Hebrew servant cannot be acquired through chalipin. Although the verse speaks about a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew, it also teaches the law for a Jewish purchaser. Otherwise, from where do all the authorities derive that a Hebrew servant cannot be acquired through chalipin? Shach further suggests that the verse itself comes to reveal that a Hebrew servant cannot be acquired through chalipin. Rav Yosef in Kiddushin 8 interprets 'the money of his purchase' as excluding produce and utensils because their value is not fixed, even though according to Rav Yosef acquisition by ordinary objects also fails where the value is not fixed. Why then was a verse needed regarding a Hebrew servant? Shach concludes that the verse comes to teach the rule specifically here.
I do not see any difficulty here against Rabbeinu Tam. It is true that even a Jewish master cannot acquire a Hebrew servant through chalipin, as is implied by the Mishnah in Kiddushin 14, which says a Hebrew servant is acquired by money and document, excluding chalipin. But that can be because once the verse revealed regarding a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew that chalipin does not acquire him, the same rule applies to a Jewish purchaser. If one were to say, however, that chalipin does not exist for non-Jews at all, then what purpose would there be in excluding chalipin through 'the money of his purchase' specifically in the law of a sale to a non-Jew? If the Torah wished to exclude a Hebrew servant from chalipin as a category, it should have written the exclusion in the law of a Jewish purchaser. The better explanation is that a non-Jew ordinarily does have chalipin, but a Hebrew servant was specially excluded from acquisition through chalipin. Consequently a Jewish purchaser likewise cannot acquire a Hebrew servant through chalipin.
Shach's further suggestion that the verse itself comes to reveal that a Hebrew servant is not acquired through chalipin from a non-Jew because non-Jews lack chalipin also does not work. That would be meaningful if a Hebrew servant could be acquired through chalipin by a Jew. But once a Jew, who unquestionably has chalipin generally, also cannot acquire a Hebrew servant through chalipin, one can no longer say that the verse's exclusion is based on the non-Jew's general lack of chalipin. According to Rav Yosef, who explains the exclusion because the value is not fixed, the verse can indeed teach that something of unfixed value does not count as money. But one cannot explain that the verse excludes chalipin merely because the purchaser is a non-Jew, since even a Jewish purchaser who generally has chalipin cannot acquire a Hebrew servant that way. We must therefore say that non-Jews too are within the law of chalipin, and the verse specifically excludes a Hebrew servant from chalipin. Consequently the same exclusion applies for a Jewish purchaser. This is clear.
