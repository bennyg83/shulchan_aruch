To retract after the time of immediate speech. Responsa VeShav HaKohen 19 writes that the rule stated here, that one may retract from any kinyan within the time of immediate speech, is not universally accepted. Rambam, Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot 15:1, implies that with delivery and similar completed physical acts one cannot retract even within that short period. Rambam rules that in consecration one cannot retract within immediate speech once he has spoken. The reason must be that a declaration to Heaven has the force of delivery to an ordinary person. If the reason were merely a special stringency of consecrated property, then even an unspoken mental resolve would be irreversible. Tosafot in Menachot 81b states explicitly that one cannot retract from consecration within immediate speech because a declaration to Heaven is like delivery to a person. If so, actual delivery to an ordinary person should certainly be irreversible even within immediate speech, and the same would apply to meshichah or hagbahah. It is possible to read the Rosh in Bava Batra consistently with this. When the Rosh says that these acquisitions cannot be retracted "after" immediate speech, he may mean only that after that period none of the acquisitions, except kinyan sudar in its special context, may be retracted even while the parties are still discussing the same matter. Within immediate speech, however, some acquisitions may still be retracted, such as money, deed, and chazakah, while completed acts of meshichah, delivery, or hagbahah may be irreversible at once. VeShav HaKohen concludes that the matter requires very great study. See also Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer 27, end of seif katan 12.
After the time of immediate speech. See what I wrote in Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer 38, seif katan 5, in the name of Beit Meir: just as one may retract within immediate speech, one may certainly add a condition within that time, contrary to Beit Shmuel in the name of the Taz. Responsa Shlomo, Choshen Mishpat 11, asks that this seems difficult. The person first said, "I give you this as a gift," and then within immediate speech adds, "on condition that such-and-such." If the condition means that the gift will exist only if the condition is fulfilled, how can it work? The act appears to have preceded the condition, which ordinarily invalidates a condition according to most authorities, who require even verbal conditions to be stated before the act. He answers that the requirement that the condition precede the act concerns the wording of the conditional formulation itself. If one says, "the gift shall be effective if such-and-such occurs," placing the act before the condition within the conditional sentence, that language shows that he emphasizes the act's effectiveness, and the condition is defective. But where the condition itself is phrased properly, "if such-and-such occurs, it shall be a gift," the fact that he had uttered the unconditional gift moments earlier is not fatal. Either the later statement explains his first words, or it operates as a valid retraction and replacement. Since the conditional formula itself properly places the condition before the act, it is effective. He discusses this at length. See also my commentary to Seder Gittin, seif gadol resh-lamed-gimmel.
