This is specifically with a gift. Sema cites a suggestion attributed to Mahari Weil that even a gift may not always be considered an unqualified benefit, because "one who hates gifts shall live." He therefore suggests there may be no difference between gift and sale. I am astonished. The Mishnah expressly treats a gift as a benefit for which one may acquire on another's behalf. Bava Batra likewise says that when a gift was transferred through a third person, even a recipient who later protests may already have acquired, depending on when he protested. If a gift were inherently a detriment because of the verse concerning hating gifts, silence could not create acquisition. The correct distinction is that a person may refuse a gift. If he protests from the outset, we cannot force a benefit upon him against his will, and the verse explains why such a refusal is legally meaningful. But absent protest, ordinary people desire gifts and the transfer is treated as a benefit. Rashba says explicitly that although a gift can in one respect be called a detriment because a righteous person may not want gifts, natural human desire for property makes it sufficient benefit for acquisition in the recipient's absence. Moreover, the actual responsum of Mahari Weil does not make Sema's argument. It concerns a deathly ill man who sold his house to his son in law. The son in law was absent when a sudar kinyan was made. Mahari Weil questioned the acquisition because some authorities hold that sudar kinyan cannot be performed for an absent purchaser, and even those who permit it may distinguish a sale, where the purchaser must pay money and may not wish to spend it, from a gift. Darkei Moshe itself added the separate speculation that perhaps even a gift is not always a benefit because of "one who hates gifts shall live." That addition is not Mahari Weil's ruling. Thus Mahari Weil's actual concern is limited to sudar kinyan in the absence of the purchaser. Other modes of acquisition can certainly transfer a gift for an absent recipient. The Talmud is explicit that a gift is ordinarily a benefit so long as the recipient has not protested.
Who does not wish to buy. Mahari Weil says that even according to authorities who allow sudar kinyan in the beneficiary's absence, a sale may differ from a gift because the purchaser must spend money and perhaps does not want the purchase. Even if he later says he wanted it, the seller may not have had settled intent at the time because the purchaser's acceptance was then uncertain. In my view Tosafot indicates that sudar kinyan can work even in a sale when the purchaser is absent. Tosafot proves that sudar kinyan need not be performed in the purchaser's presence from the rule that acquisition documents can be written without the creditor present. They add that if presence were required, deeds would have to recite that the kinyan occurred before the purchaser or creditor, just as they recite other essential details. If a sale required the purchaser's presence, the same question would remain for sale documents. Rambam and other decisors also say that acquisition deeds of sale can be written without the purchaser present, which strongly implies that the acquisition itself can be effective in his absence. There is, however, a broader dispute. In siman 235 I explain that Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch allow even a purchase to be acquired for a person in his absence, while Rosh limits ordinary acquisition in absence to a gift. Still, Mahari Weil's claim that even Tosafot would reject an absent purchase appears difficult. At minimum, where the transaction is plainly favorable, such as merchandise sold substantially below market value, everyone should agree that it is a benefit. Tur cites Ramban that a person's courtyard can acquire unusually cheap merchandise for him, like a found object. Mordechai likewise treats bargain merchandise as a benefit comparable to a find. Authorities who question courtyard acquisition do so because the seller's continuing control can complicate the courtyard mechanism, not because the buyer may not want the bargain. The law that one may not take another person's property intending to repay him with something better likewise implies that if the superior replacement is immediately transferred to him in a valid manner, the transaction can be treated as a benefit. Therefore, even if ordinary sales in a purchaser's absence remain disputed, a clear bargain is an unqualified benefit and can be acquired for him. Rambam's ruling is especially strong: if a seller transfers land or movables to a purchaser without his knowledge, the purchaser may accept and the seller cannot retract, though the purchaser himself may reject. This directly contradicts Mahari Weil's argument that the seller's acquisition is invalid merely because the purchaser retained an option to refuse.
