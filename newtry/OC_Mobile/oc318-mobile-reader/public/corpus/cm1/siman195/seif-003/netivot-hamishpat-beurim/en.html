This applies to a gift. See Sema, seif katan 11, citing Mahari Weil that even with a gift one might say the recipient does not wish to acquire because "one who hates gifts shall live." These words seem astonishing, since the Gemara and poskim are full of cases where a gift is acquired for a person through another without his knowledge. Ketzot HaChoshen already raises this. The matter can be clarified through a broader distinction. Mechaber rules that in a sale one generally cannot acquire on behalf of an absent buyer, yet siman 235, seif 23, says that where the seller explicitly transfers through another, the seller cannot retract. Thus a sale can be beneficial when the seller says to the third person, "Acquire for so and so," because the buyer then retains the choice to accept or reject while the seller is bound. Likewise, siman 190, seif 4, where one says, "Here is a maneh and let your field be sold to so and so," requires that the absent buyer have appointed him agent or later consent, even though the payer is effectively giving money on the buyer's behalf. Ran explains that in monetary acquisition the agent ordinarily must derive authority from the owner of the property. The Yerushalmi similarly says that even a receiving agent does not automatically work for a gift. Thus a person who simply makes himself an agent for the recipient, without the donor saying "acquire," may lack power because his act harms the donor by preventing retraction. When the seller himself tells the third party, "Acquire for so and so," that third party becomes the property owner's agent to confer the benefit, and because the wording expressly invokes the beneficiary's advantage, the seller cannot retract while the buyer still can. But where the seller merely says to perform a kinyan for the absent buyer, without saying "acquire," the kinyan is not complete until the buyer consents. If the buyer later rejects the transaction, the donor of the purchase money may have to receive his money back, and a kinyan cannot be effective on one side only. This is why the seller can also retract before the buyer consents. The Mishnah in Chullin concerning the four special slaughtering periods supports this. The Gemara establishes the binding transaction there through acquisition by another, yet outside those four periods the seller may retract. The case must therefore be one where the seller told the third party merely to make a kinyan for the buyer, rather than explicitly saying "acquire." On those special days the buyer himself cannot retract, so the kinyan binds both sides; on ordinary days, because the buyer can retract, the seller likewise can, since the acquisition does not take effect one-sidedly. This can partly explain Sema. If Reuven wishes to give Shimon a maneh and hands it to Levi saying, "Here is a maneh so that Shimon may acquire the field," the field does not necessarily become Shimon's merely because the money was a gift. If Shimon later rejects the purchase, the seller may have to return the money. The acquisition therefore remains incomplete until Shimon accepts. The issue may depend on the dispute concerning one who was given a gift through another and, upon learning, immediately protests. According to Rashbam the gift becomes ownerless, while Rosh says it returns to the donor. If it returns to the donor, then in our case the purchase money returns when the beneficiary rejects, and therefore the sale cannot be final against the seller either. This is the sense in which concern for "one who hates gifts" can matter. Nevertheless, the primary rule follows Rama that a gift acquired by kinyan sudar can be effected for an absent recipient. Kinyan sudar is fundamentally a symbolic kinyan on condition to transfer, and once completed there is no continuing purchase money that must be returned. A second possible understanding of Sema is that if the seller takes a utensil belonging to the absent buyer, there is no one present with authority to transfer that utensil to the seller for the kinyan. Sema may think that if one fears the absent person may reject gifts, there is no clear presumption that he would have consented to transfer his utensil. But with the witnesses' own sudar, the better view is that the kinyan works for the recipient's benefit. As Shach writes in siman 358, where there is a compelling presumption that the owner would have agreed to a gift, the gift can take effect even without his knowledge. Here the large benefit exchanged for the sudar supplies that presumption. Therefore, the halachah follows Rama that a gift through kinyan sudar can be effected in the recipient's absence.
