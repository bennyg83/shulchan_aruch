But if he was engaged in work, etc., he should not say, "Give me a dinar," etc. The Mechaber's wording here is somewhat awkward. He should first have written that if the finder is engaged in paid work, he is not obligated to abandon it and return the lost item without compensation, because of the verse "there shall be no pauper among you," as explained at the beginning of the preceding siman. Then he should have added that he nevertheless need not receive his full wage. Even if the Mechaber considered the first point obvious from the preceding siman, he should not have introduced this law with the word "but." Rambam, chapter 12 of Hilchot Gezeilah, law 4, from whom the Mechaber copied it, arranges the law differently and more clearly.
Like an idle worker who would stop that work in which he was engaged, etc. Rambam and the Mechaber understand "an idle worker" here as in the law of interest concerning two partners, Yoreh Deah 177:1. According to them, he receives no wage for returning the object itself. He receives only the amount this particular worker would accept to stop his current work and sit entirely idle. The Tur, whose view is cited by Rama, holds that since the mitzvah of returning the object does not require this worker to suffer a loss, he must also be paid for the return itself. Although regarding interest in Yoreh Deah 177 the Tur explains "idle worker" differently, meaning how much a completely idle person would accept to engage in the partnership venture, I explained his reasoning there and in the Perishah. With interest, the recipient partner is pleased to perform the work because he receives half the profit, and the only problem is the prohibition of interest, so the lesser payment suffices. This worker, by contrast, does not want to stop his own work merely to perform the return.
Up to the amount of his lost wages. Since he suffers no monetary loss, even if the return involves greater exertion than his regular work, he is already commanded to perform the mitzvah of returning lost property and may not charge for the mitzvah itself.
Only as a completely idle worker. Meaning, according to the Mechaber's interpretation at the beginning of the seif, how much this worker would accept to sit completely idle. The Tur writes this explicitly at the end of the siman. In the Derishah I explained why here, where he should have stipulated and did not, he receives no wage for the return itself but only compensation for lost work, whereas in the preceding siman, seif 3, one who voluntarily saved another person's donkey while allowing his own to be swept away receives the wage for saving the other's donkey and not the value of his own loss. Strictly speaking, there the owner of the lost donkey should owe nothing, because he was present and the rescuer did not stipulate with him. The owner can say, "Had you not gone down on your own to save it, I would have gone down and saved my own donkey." Nevertheless, the Sages assessed ordinary intent and assumed that a person wants whoever first saves his property in such a situation to be paid. Since that liability rests only on presumed intent rather than strict law, they award only the smaller amount. Here, the loss from stopping work is less than or equal to the ordinary return wage, while in the preceding siman the rescuer's lost donkey is worth more than the wage for returning the other's donkey. In each case the smaller amount is paid.
That he take what he will lose by stopping his work. For he does not wish to sit idle; he needs to support his household and prefers to work hard and earn his full wage.
He may take it. Meaning, he is permitted to take that amount and the other party is obligated to pay it. There is no prohibition in accepting payment for the return, because the Torah did not obligate him to suffer a loss. Ordinarily, however, the Sages assume that a person prefers some rest from hard work and would accept less than his full wage to sit idle. Here he revealed that he does not.
Even if they fixed for him more than is ordinarily fitting. This is also the Tur's wording. In the Perishah I proved that it means more than another person would ordinarily charge for sitting idle, but still not more than the value of his own work. If they promised him more than the value of his work itself, they need not pay the excess, for they may say that the agreement was made merely to induce him, even in an ordinary optional matter, all the more so where a mitzvah of returning lost property rests upon him.
If the owners are not there, etc. This is not comparable to Rama at the end of seif 3 of the preceding siman, from the Rosh and Tur, that where the owners are absent it is as though they stipulated. Here, ordinarily a person prefers to rest from hard work and perform the return for less than his normal wage. The particular worker discussed by the Tur and Mechaber does not want rest but wants to continue working hard for full earnings. If he returns the object without stipulating, the owner will not believe that he is exceptional but will say he is like ordinary workers, and will pay only the amount of an idle worker. The Sages therefore allowed him to continue his own work and leave the return where he cannot make a condition. In the preceding siman, however, where a river swept away both his donkey and his fellow's, and he abandoned his own to save the other's, everyone knows that the effort to save the two donkeys was the same. It is obvious that a person ordinarily prefers to save his own animal rather than his fellow's unless he acts because he expects compensation at least equal to his own animal's value. Therefore the Sages treat it as though he had stipulated, unless the owner was present and he could have stipulated but failed to do so. Examine this carefully.
