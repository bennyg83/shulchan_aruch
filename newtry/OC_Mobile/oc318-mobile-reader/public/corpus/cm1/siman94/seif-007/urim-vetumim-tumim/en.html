And there is an opinion that there is no gilgul shevuah here, etc. The Sema asks how this can be presented as a dispute in which the Rosh, who is the author of the latter opinion, disagrees with the first opinion of the Baal HaTerumot. The Tur cites the Baal HaTerumot's rule in siman 75, then cites the Ramah as disagreeing, and adds that his father the Rosh agreed with the first view. The Sema therefore explains that the Baal HaTerumot speaks where, even without gilgul, the defendant must take hesset because the principal claim is definite. Although the principal Torah oath has fallen away when he says "I do not know," the question remains whether the rolled matters should be treated under the rule that one who cannot swear must pay, and the Baal HaTerumot says they are not. The Rosh, by contrast, discusses a case where, without gilgul, there would be no hesset at all, and therefore exempts the defendant entirely because there is no principal oath onto which anything can be rolled. A study of the Baal HaTerumot at the beginning of gate 38, however, shows that he discusses at length whether the payment rule applies to gilgul. This indicates that he does not simply accept the Rosh's premise that the principal oath disappears as soon as the defendant says "I do not know." Otherwise he could have answered briefly that there is no principal oath at all. The Sema himself, in the Perishah, explains the Tur's statement that the Rosh agreed with the first view as referring only to the particular point on which the Ramah disagreed. The Ramah's disagreement there concerns the later case, where the principal oath is definite but the defendant says "I do not know" concerning the rolled allegation. It does not necessarily address the earlier case in which the defendant says "I do not know" concerning the principal claim itself. That earlier issue belongs here in siman 94 rather than in siman 75. Even if one assumes that the Baal HaTerumot does not disagree with the Rosh on this point, Ramban appears to disagree. Ramban explains the Mishnah's case, "I bought the large slave" and the other says, "I do not know," by saying that the claim includes the slave and its clothing. The clothing creates a partial admission oath, and the slave is included by gilgul. If the defendant cannot answer the slave claim, the rule that one who cannot swear must pay is applied through gilgul. But surely the defendant is also uncertain regarding the clothing. If he knew that the clothing was that of the small slave, how could he remain uncertain whether the slave itself was large or small when the claim was a slave together with its clothing? The Mishnah's statement that the claimant receives the large item therefore implies uncertainty concerning both the clothing and slave. According to the Rosh, once the defendant says "I do not know" on the principal clothing claim, no oath remains and there is no gilgul at all. Ramban must therefore hold that even though inability to swear produces monetary liability, the underlying oath status does not vanish and can still serve as the basis for gilgul. Although we do not follow Ramban regarding the payment consequence of gilgul, his underlying conception may still be correct: an oath obligation can remain legally present even when inability to swear already produces payment. Thus there is a genuine dispute among the authorities whether the oath has disappeared or remains for purposes of gilgul. In my view the dispute follows their respective positions elsewhere. At the beginning of Bava Metzia the Rosh asks why, in the case of a bailee who transferred the deposit to another bailee and thereby becomes liable because he cannot take the required oath, the second bailee's testimony as one witness does not assist the first and exempt him from the oath. The Rosh answers that once the first bailee cannot swear, the matter is no longer one of oath at all but becomes a pure monetary liability, and one witness cannot defeat a monetary obligation. This is exactly his position here: once the defendant says "I do not know" in a case where inability to swear requires payment, the oath has fallen away, leaving money only, and there is no oath upon which to build gilgul. Ramban, in Milchamot, uses that very case to argue that the second bailee should function as a supporting witness. He necessarily holds that the oath obligation has not disappeared merely because inability to swear creates payment; the oath remains the legal source of the liability and a supporting witness can still remove it. If the oath remains, gilgul can likewise still attach. Ramban therefore follows his own approach consistently. The Mechaber was consequently justified in recording a dispute here. Practically, however, above in siman 87 he rules concerning a supporting witness in accordance with the Rosh. That implies acceptance of the Rosh's underlying principle that once the defendant cannot swear, the oath obligation has ended and become a monetary obligation. On that approach there is likewise no gilgul. Thus the practical result should follow the Rosh.
