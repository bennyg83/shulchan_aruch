And there is an opinion that there is no gilgul here. Sema writes that these two opinions do not disagree. According to everyone, if the defendant says 'I do not know' regarding the very matter of the principal oath, there is no gilgul. The first opinion concerns a case where the claimant makes a definite claim and the defendant gives a definite answer. Even without gilgul, therefore, the claimant could impose hesset concerning the added matter. The ruling teaches only that we do not apply the principle that one who cannot swear must pay. But where there is no definite claim and no oath could be imposed except through gilgul, all agree that there is no oath. See Tumim, where I wrote that there is in fact a dispute. Ramban holds that the original oath obligation has not disappeared and therefore gilgul still applies. As a matter of practical law, however, the view introduced as 'there is an opinion' is correct, and this is also compelled by the ruling above in siman 87. See there.
For he was never obligated in an oath. This means that from the outset he said, 'I do not know,' and therefore no oath obligation ever took effect. But if he initially denied the claim, became obligated to swear, and gilgul was imposed, and only afterward said, 'I do not know' concerning the principal oath and wished to pay instead, even Rosh agrees that he remains obligated in the gilgul oath because the oath obligation had already taken effect. This is the case stated above in seif 3 and is learned from the Yerushalmi, which equates paying with saying, 'I do not know.'
