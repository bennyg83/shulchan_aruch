One who became obligated, etc. This is stated in the Yerushalmi at the end of Shevuot and cited by Rif.
From certain claims, etc.; but if, etc. The wording appears to refer to uncertainty in the appended claim. Nimukei Yosef, however, explains that where the principal claim itself is uncertain, the rule of 'obligated to swear but unable' does not apply. He further writes that one may avoid the ancillary oaths by paying the principal claim. The Yerushalmi concerning a bailee who first agreed to swear and then chose to pay after seeing further oaths supports this distinction, though Rif interprets that passage differently. Rabbi Yose's language suggests that the oath was imposed to ease the bailee's position, allowing him either to pay or to swear. See citations 31, 61, 81, 450.
And even, etc. At the beginning of HaMafkid the Gemara imposes additional oaths because of concern that the bailee used the deposit. Rosh explains that the three oaths at the beginning of Bava Metzia are all rolled into the oath that he was not negligent, and Tosafot agree. Therefore, even if he wishes to pay, he may still be required to swear once the ancillary obligations arose. The Yerushalmi may likewise refer to additional oaths about the deposit itself, such as that he did not use it and that it is not in his possession.
