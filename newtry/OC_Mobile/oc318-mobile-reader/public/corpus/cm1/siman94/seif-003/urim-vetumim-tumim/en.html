Who became obligated to take an oath, etc. See Urim, seif katan 11. The Shach holds that where the halachah believes a person who says, "I had a prozbul and it was lost," or, "I took the ribbit in a permitted manner," so that no hesset is required because we presume a person does not abandon a permitted route and choose a prohibited one, that presumption nevertheless does not exempt him from gilgul shevuah. I am uncertain about this. If the chazakah is strong enough to defeat hesset, why should it not also defeat gilgul? At first glance the Shach has a proof according to the Rosh and those who hold that an oral declaration before beit din, "I hereby transfer my debts to you," creates a prozbul even without any written instrument, so that the writing serves only as proof. The judges or witnesses sign beneath merely to attest that the prozbul was made. Why is such written proof needed if the lender is independently believed by the chazakah that he would not abandon the permitted procedure and collect unlawfully? It must have practical value where he would otherwise face gilgul shevuah, and the written prozbul removes even that possibility. This proof can be rejected. The principal prozbul enactment was designed for oral loans, as the Ran writes, because a written debt can in some circumstances be transferred directly to beit din. In an oral loan, the borrower may have a migo to deny the existence of the prozbul by saying instead that no loan ever existed or that it was paid. A written prozbul can remove complications of that kind and is therefore not superfluous. Compare Yoreh Deah siman 169, seif 25. Therefore, it appears that a distinction should be made. If the defendant is already obligated in a Torah oath and the claimant rolls onto it an allegation that he took ribbit, the gilgul itself is d'oraisa. The chazakah that a person does not abandon a permitted path may not be strong enough to eliminate a Torah oath. It is sufficient that the chazakah eliminates hesset, which is rabbinic. But where the only base oath is hesset and the proposed gilgul is therefore entirely rabbinic, there is strong reason to say that the same chazakah that defeats hesset should defeat the gilgul as well. From definite claims. If the claimant's additional claims are uncertain and the defendant offers to pay the principal claim instead of swearing, the principal ruling is that he need not swear upon the matters that would have been included by gilgul. The Maggid Mishneh writes this in explanation of Rambam and says that Ramban and Rashba likewise wrote so in Bava Metzia. I am troubled, because the entire source of this law is a Yerushalmi. A man claimed two dinars before Rabbi Zeira and Rabbi Zeira obligated the defendant to swear. The defendant said, "Are these not the two dinars I owe you? Here they are." The claimant then added other matters. Rabbi Zeira ruled: either pay everything he claims or swear concerning everything he has rolled upon you. According to the Maggid Mishneh, one must say that the additional matters were definite claims. But if so, how could the Rif have initially thought that the Yerushalmi concerned a hesset oath? Even without gilgul, a definite claim would independently support hesset. It is difficult to force the Yerushalmi into the special factual constructions later authorities use to explain Rambam. Moreover, if the Yerushalmi concerns definite additional claims, why does the Rif treat it as conflicting with the Bavli in HaMafkid, where a person says, "I will swear," but after seeing that additional matters are being rolled upon him says, "I will pay," and the Gemara explains that the Torah gave gilgul to ease matters, not to make them more severe? The Bavli there concerns uncertain allegations like the ordinary bailee's oath. There would be no contradiction: the Yerushalmi could concern definite gilgul while the Bavli concerns uncertain gilgul. The wording of the Yerushalmi itself indicates that the additional matters are suspicions, not definite claims. It comments on the Mishnah, "These swear without a claim," and Rabbi Yose says that this applies where one conducts the household business without rendering a detailed account; if he renders an account, there is no further oath. The Yerushalmi asks which household member is meant, since an ordinary manager ultimately gives some account. It answers that even one who accounts for large matters may be trusted regarding small expenditures that cannot practically be itemized. On those small matters an uncertain suspicion can support the special oath. Once such an oath exists, other matters can be included through gilgul. The Yerushalmi then gives Rabbi Zeira's case: a defendant became obligated regarding two dinars, offered to pay them rather than swear, and the claimant mentioned other uncertain matters. Rabbi Zeira said he must either pay everything claimed or swear upon all the matters rolled onto him. This is exactly the natural continuation of the Mishnah's uncertain partner or household oath. The Ran expressly cites students of the Rashba that the phrase "such and such matters" refers to uncertain allegations. Thus Rambam's ruling requires explanation. There is, however, a proof for Rambam from Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Chiya bar Abba, who hold that the oath of bailees requires a denial and partial admission, represented by three animals. Why is a special verse for the bailee's oath necessary? An ordinary partial admission already creates a Torah oath. One could have required the bailee's additional oath through gilgul. The Raavad and Ramban ask this. They answer that if the defendant says "I do not know," the rule that one who cannot swear must pay is not applied to a mere gilgul, whereas when the bailee's oath is primary the rule does apply. That answer works according to the opinion that one who cannot swear pays, but what of an authority who rejects that principle? It is unlikely that whether the special verse is necessary should depend entirely upon that separate dispute. The Or Zarua and Ritva in the beginning of Bava Metzia provide another answer: the verse is needed for a case in which, after the claim, the claimant waives the animal concerning which there was the partial denial. Even after that waiver the independent bailee's oath remains. This necessarily implies that if the oath existed only by gilgul, waiver of or payment of the principal claim would eliminate the gilgul. Otherwise the question would remain, because the gilgul would survive. Payment should be no different from an express waiver, which is the most complete form of relinquishing the principal claim. It therefore follows that Rabbi Yochanan and Rabbi Chiya bar Abba assume that when the principal matter is waived or paid, the gilgul obligation falls away. Even though we do not follow them regarding the requirement that every bailee's oath contain ordinary denial and partial admission, there is no reason to assume the later authorities disagree with this collateral principle. Rambam's ruling therefore has a strong basis. This proof disappears, however, if one adopts the view that there is no gilgul at all when the claimant's additional allegation is uncertain and there are no independent grounds for suspicion. On that approach, the special bailee's oath is needed simply because an uncertain allegation could not otherwise be rolled onto the ordinary partial admission oath. Rambam apparently does not adopt that limitation and instead follows the first answer of Tosafot, under which uncertain matters can be rolled onto an oath of the same class. The Mechaber, however, appears elsewhere to treat the opinion restricting uncertain gilgul as primary. His adoption here of Rambam's rule therefore requires study, because once that restriction is accepted, the stated proof no longer establishes that payment of the principal claim always eliminates uncertain gilgul, while the Yerushalmi itself points in the opposite direction.
