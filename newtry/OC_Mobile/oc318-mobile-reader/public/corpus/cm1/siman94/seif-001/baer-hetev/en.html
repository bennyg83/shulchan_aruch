To roll over. See the Ritva at the beginning of Kiddushin, who discusses the laws of gilgul shevuah at length and concludes that any oath may have additional claims rolled into it, even an uncertain claim upon an uncertain claim. This follows the Ran's view, except with a hired worker and with one upon whom the oath was reversed. Shach.
Its reversal. So states Terumat HaDeshen. The reason is that the Sages do not enact one safeguard upon another. The Taz writes that this ruling requires study, discusses it at length, and concludes that one should not act upon this ruling to extract money. See the Shach.
Money. The Tur gives the example: One says, "You were my partner and therefore you owe me the partners' oath," and the other says, "I was never your partner." We do not roll into the oath the claim that he was not his partner. Rather, we may roll in a claim that he earned no profit at all during the period when the claimant says they were partners. There is a practical difference between these formulations, as explained in siman 93, seifim 9 and 10. Shach.
Hired worker. The Sema explains that the Sages were lenient because the worker risks himself for these wages, and the worker's law has already been explained in the Gemara and in siman 89. This siman too implies that gilgul is excluded only for a hired worker, but that it applies to other claimants who swear and collect. The Taz writes that Rashi and Ri explain the reason differently, as noted above: this oath is not imposed by strict law, but only to appease the employer, and the Sema's explanation is therefore difficult.
They roll in. The Sema writes that claims may be rolled into a severe oath even where the added claim would independently require only a shevuat heset, or no independent oath at all. Through gilgul, he swears on that claim while holding a sacred object. This applies even to uncertain claims by the claimant, as the Tur states in this siman and as explained at the end of siman 75.
