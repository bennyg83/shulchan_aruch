Anyone who became obligated, etc. Where the defendant is disqualified from swearing and the opposing party therefore swears and collects, it requires study whether the disqualified defendant can impose gilgul shevuah upon the claimant's oath. The Shach cites the Ritva in Kiddushin that he cannot, but writes that the general view of the poskim appears to permit gilgul. He also seeks support from a manuscript ruling cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 87. That ruling says that if a suspect person is obligated only in a rabbinic oath concerning land, the opposing party does not swear and collect, because we do not make one enactment upon another. But if the suspect defendant is also obligated in a Torah oath, so that his opponent swears and collects, the opponent may through gilgul also swear concerning the land, since the Mishnah teaches that matters are joined by gilgul. In my view that manuscript is not proof that the suspect defendant himself can add new matters onto the claimant's oath. Its meaning is different. If the suspect defendant's only obligation concerns land, the claimant cannot collect through the special oath. But if the defendant is already obligated in a Torah oath and the claimant also asserted a land claim, then had the defendant been fit to swear, that land matter would itself have been included in his Torah oath by gilgul. Since the defendant is suspect, the opposing party takes the very oath that would otherwise have rested on the defendant, including the attached land matter, and then collects. Gilgul itself is d'oraisa, so the claimant's oath follows the full scope of the transferred obligation. This is also indicated by Rambam above, siman 92, seif 8, regarding a suspect bailee, where the opposing party swears that the bailee misappropriated the deposit even though that element is reached only through gilgul. It is also possible that the manuscript authority holds that the rule of one who cannot swear and therefore pays applies to gilgul itself. If so, the result is obvious and tells us nothing about an independent right of the suspect defendant to initiate gilgul against the claimant. The question may depend upon Tosafot's explanation of why the Sages required the opposing party of a suspect defendant to swear. One answer is that this oath was instituted merely for the benefit of the suspect person, so that otherwise anyone could sue a known suspect and collect without restraint. If the claimant's oath exists only to quiet the defendant's mind, it resembles the employee's oath. The Ran explains that the employee's oath does not support gilgul because the oath is merely for reassurance. Even though I concluded above in siman 92 that the suspect rule itself is rabbinic, treating the defendant as though he were unable to swear and had to pay, the same is true of the employee in another form: the Gemara's proposal to pay him without an oath is itself based on the rabbinic protection of his livelihood, and yet the final oath is regarded as merely reassuring. Thus it is plausible that the claimant's oath against a suspect defendant likewise cannot carry gilgul. This also explains why Rav Huna said "except for an employee" and did not add "except for a suspect defendant." Rav Huna did not wish to enter the dispute of Rabbi Abba against Rav and Shmuel concerning whether inability to swear causes payment. Rav Huna was Rav's student, and Rav does not accept the relevant payment rule in the same form. According to one Tosafot answer explained above in siman 92, the authority who holds that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain claim uses the verse "the oath of Hashem shall be between them" for Rabbi Tarfon rather than for the inability rule. Rav Huna himself holds that a definite claim prevails. Thus he could not state the suspect case as an undisputed exception. This makes the Ritva's statement about an inverted oath more difficult. The Shach cites him that when an oath is reversed onto the other party, gilgul likewise cannot be added, and the Terumat HaDeshen, cited by the Rama, says the same. What reason is there for this? In an ordinary reversed hesset oath there is no special element of mere reassurance. The law itself permits the defendant to say, "You swear and take." The Sages instituted hesset as a corrective measure, and if the defendant chooses to reverse it, the claimant's oath is the actual substitute for the oath that would otherwise exempt the defendant. Why should the claimant not bear gilgul just as the defendant would have? It may be that the Ritva follows the authorities who rule like Mar bar Rav Ashi that even a Torah oath may be reversed. According to them, the Torah baseline may still be "swear or pay," while the possibility of reversal was granted only to quiet the opposing party's mind. If so, a reversed Torah oath does not inherit the full legal incidents of the original oath, including gilgul. But with hesset the power of reversal is intrinsic to the rabbinic institution. Since the Sages could choose which party should swear, the defendant may say, "You swear and take," and there is no reason to prevent gilgul. If gilgul applies when he swears to be exempt, why should it disappear when the same oath has been shifted to the claimant for collection? This reading also clarifies Rashi. Tosafot understand Rashi as ruling like Mar bar Rav Ashi that even a Torah oath can be reversed. Rashi nevertheless explains in HaKotev that Rabbi Chiya bar Avin's suggestion that the oath was d'oraisa matters for reversal. At first glance, if Torah oaths can also be reversed, the distinction has no practical effect. According to what has been explained, there remains a difference. A rabbinic oath that is reversible by its own legal structure carries gilgul after reversal. A Torah oath may be reversed only as a special concession for reassurance and therefore may not carry gilgul. Rashi's statement that the difference is whether the oath can be reversed refers to the authorities who disagree with Mar bar Rav Ashi; for Mar bar Rav Ashi, the practical difference is whether reversal is intrinsic or merely a concession, with gilgul depending on that distinction. This also answers the Maharam of Rothenburg and Maharsha, who ask why Rashi elsewhere, on "if he is prudent," gives a different practical consequence rather than reversal. Rashi may have had the reading of Tosafot in Shevuot 41 that attributes "if he is prudent" to Rav Nachman. Rashi follows the Rif that Rav Nachman, who rules that two suspect litigants divide, does not accept Rabbi Abba's general rule of automatic payment for inability to swear. For Rav Nachman, a Torah oath can therefore be reversed as part of its ordinary structure, since refusal to swear does not itself create an immediate monetary obligation. Reversal no longer distinguishes Torah from rabbinic in that context, so Rashi gives another consequence. Tosafot themselves appear to support the sharper distinction. In Shevuot they cite the Seder Tannaim VeAmoraim that the halachah does not follow Mar bar Rav Ashi regarding reversal of a Torah oath, and reject an explanation that Mar bar Rav Ashi's case concerned a suspect defendant. Their objection cannot merely be that the Mishnah already says the opponent of a suspect person swears and collects, for then there would be no dispute at all. Rather, the issue is whether the Sages truly transferred the original oath, giving the claimant all the legal characteristics of the defendant's oath, or merely instituted an oath of reassurance before collection. If Mar bar Rav Ashi understood it as a genuine reversal, gilgul would accompany it. According to the opposing view, the suspect claimant's oath is only for reassurance and carries no gilgul. Tosafot object only that the passage does not yet concern a suspect litigant. The responsum of Maharam cited in Hagahot Maimoniyot can be understood similarly. He derives from Rabbenu Tam that whenever one who cannot swear must pay, such as one who says, "I know fifty and do not know fifty," the opposing party nevertheless swears before collecting, whereas in the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba the claimant does not swear because the single witness supports him. One might ask how the Gemara can say that a Torah oath is not reversible and tell the defendant "swear or pay." If a defendant who cannot swear always causes the claimant to swear, this appears to be reversal. The answer is that the claimant's oath in the inability case is only for reassurance. Torah law already imposes payment; the additional oath does not receive the full status of the defendant's oath and therefore carries no gilgul. Mar bar Rav Ashi, by contrast, treats true reversal as transferring the oath, with all its incidents. Thus the dispute is coherent. It follows that an ordinary reversed oath does support gilgul, contrary to the Terumat HaDeshen, as the Shach and Taz indeed conclude, though they do not give this proof. Tosafot also reason in one approach that if a borrower can require a document holder to swear before collecting an otherwise valid debt, all the more can a litigant shift an oath obligation. If the Terumat HaDeshen were right that a reversed oath cannot support gilgul, then certainly the special oath demanded from a document holder should not support gilgul. Yet in Ketubot, Rabbi Shimon rules that a husband cannot make his wife swear about her household management or her spinning unless she claims her ketubah, while once she claims the ketubah and he says, "Swear to me," he can include even those matters by gilgul. Even HaEzer simanim 97 and 98 and the early and later authorities make this explicit. Thus a genuinely transferred or collection oath can support gilgul. The special case of a suspect defendant remains disputed. We explained why an employee's oath carries no gilgul: the Sages enhanced the employee's position and required only a minimal oath for the employer's reassurance. One might ask why the shopkeeper on his ledger, where the employer is uncertain whether the workers were paid, is not likewise excluded from gilgul, since his oath also reassures the employer. Tosafot's answer is that the employer was not expected to know whether the shopkeeper actually paid, since employers commonly direct workers to a shopkeeper. Even according to Rav Huna that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one, Torah law would not necessarily allow the shopkeeper to collect merely from the employer's ignorance where the employer could not have known. Both the shopkeeper and workers therefore collect by special rabbinic oaths, and the Sages did not enhance their position by removing an otherwise required oath. Rather, they imposed an oath as a condition of a rabbinic collection right. Such an oath can still carry gilgul. The employee's oath is different. The Gemara itself considered allowing the employee to collect without swearing because of the special concern for his livelihood. The final oath is therefore an added reassurance that limits a rabbinic advantage granted to him. The same is true of the oath required when one witness says a document has been paid: the document holder would otherwise collect under the ordinary law, and the added oath merely reassures the debtor. In those settings the Sages did not create a full transferred oath with new extensions. And by this the Sema's words in seif katan 4 can be understood. He says that an employee has no gilgul because "to it he bears his life," meaning the Sages were exceptionally solicitous for the employee's livelihood. At first glance this rationale appears invented rather than stated by Tosafot or Ran. According to what has been explained, it captures the Ran's point precisely. The Gemara contemplated removing even the basic oath for the employee's benefit, and retained it only to quiet the employer's mind. It would contradict that policy to burden the employee with additional gilgul claims. A suspect defendant, by contrast, receives the claimant's oath as an enactment for the suspect's own protection. The claimant was not granted an enhanced collection right because of his livelihood. There is therefore substantial room to say that gilgul should apply. The practical law in the suspect case consequently requires further study.
