That there be grounds supporting the matter. See Baer Heitev, and the Shach's objection there. Responsa Mayim Chayim, siman 15, discusses Reuven, who sent merchandise with a gentile wagoner to Shimon. Shimon settled with Reuven according to the account book, while Reuven claimed that he had sent more than was recorded in the book given to the wagoner and presumed that the wagoner had delivered it. Shimon said he knew only what appeared in the book. Shimon later became obligated to take a hesset oath to Reuven on another matter, and the question arose whether Reuven could include by gilgul an oath that Shimon did not know whether more had been delivered than was recorded. The responsum answers that this cannot be derived from the Sema in siman 93, seif 13, seif katan 26, with which Urim VeTummim agrees against the Shach. There the case concerns partners, who may rationalize taking partnership property for themselves, and there are therefore grounds supporting suspicion. Here such rationalization does not apply, so supporting grounds are required. Although the Shach in siman 34, seif katan 7, challenges the Shulchan Aruch from the end of siman 344 and leaves the matter requiring study, in a case of doubt one certainly cannot impose this additional oath upon Shimon. Nevertheless, if the wagoners state with certainty that they delivered more to Shimon than appears in the book, their statement should at least count as grounds supporting the matter. The Shach in siman 149, seif katan 13, writes that according to those who regard 'my father told me so' as a definite claim sufficient to impose an oath, the same applies where the claimant says that a gentile told him so. Although the Shach adds that where the gentile is interested in the matter his statement cannot itself impose an oath, that limitation concerns creation of an oath as upon a definite claim. For gilgul, an oath may be added even upon the claimant's doubtful claim, except that the Shulchan Aruch in siman 94 requires supporting grounds. The interested wagoner's statement may therefore qualify at least as such grounds. One may also combine the fact that in siman 344 the Shulchan Aruch rules that an oath may be added even without such grounds, which is why the Shach in siman 94 leaves the matter requiring study; see there.
