He may impose an oath through gilgul, etc. All laws of gilgul shevuah are derived from sotah. We expound the repeated 'amen, amen' as one amen concerning the man about whom her husband warned her and with whom she secluded herself, and another concerning a different man who was not included in the husband's suspicion. This is gilgul shevuah based on an uncertain claim. Likewise, she swears that she did not commit adultery while betrothed, even though a betrothed woman does not drink the sotah water. This shows that through gilgul one may impose an oath even concerning a matter that independently would not generate an oath. Therefore, although one does not swear independently concerning land, one may be required to swear concerning land through gilgul. See what I write in this siman, that all such cases must resemble sotah in that there are grounds supporting the suspicion. See the Gemara and Sema.
Through shifting the oath. The reason given is that shifting an oath is itself a rabbinic enactment, and gilgul is likewise an enactment, and we do not institute one enactment upon another. Shach and Taz challenge this ruling. See Tumim, where I wrote at length, with many proofs, that their position is correct and that gilgul may be imposed even after an oath has been shifted. See there.
Only an oath. For example, if he seeks to impose through gilgul, 'Swear to me that you were my partner and that we have not yet divided the partnership,' so that if the other admits this he will become obligated in the partners' oath, he cannot impose that oath through gilgul. He may, however, impose through gilgul, 'Swear to me that you did not retain anything of mine from the partnership dealings.' The practical difference concerns an oath while holding a sacred object and similar matters. See what I wrote above in siman 93.
But a hired worker, etc. Sema explains that because the worker risks his life for his wages, the Sages did not wish to burden him with additional oaths through gilgul. Tosafos and Ran mention only that the Gemara asks why the worker is not paid without any oath, and answers that the oath is merely to ease the employer's mind. If so, on what basis should additional oaths be imposed upon him? See Tumim, where I discussed this at length. I wrote that Sema's words can be reconciled with Ran, but not with Tosafos. I also concluded that where the defendant is disqualified from swearing and the opposing party swears and collects, whether the disqualified defendant may impose gilgul upon the claimant is disputed by the authorities and is certainly a matter of unresolved law. The indications favor the view that he cannot impose gilgul. See there.
Beit din on its own initiative, etc. This is like sotah, where the kohen imposes the additional matters even without the husband's knowledge. Nevertheless, beit din must know that the opposing party has claims or suspicions that would support gilgul, even though he himself did not ask to impose them. Otherwise it is forbidden for the judges to suggest such claims, for that would make them like advocates for a litigant. See Beit Yosef and Shach.
