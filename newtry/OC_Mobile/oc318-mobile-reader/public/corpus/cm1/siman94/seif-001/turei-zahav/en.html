(Shulchan Aruch, seif 1, and some say this law) This requires study, for the opposite conclusion can be established. We should first set out the sugya at the end of Kol HaNishba’in. Rav Huna said: one may extend every oath to additional claims except the oath of a hired worker. Rashi explains that additional claims may be attached to every rabbinic oath except the worker’s oath, which itself is not imposed by ordinary law but only to appease the employer. Tosafot likewise cite Rabbenu Yitzhak: the worker is excluded because his oath is not imposed by ordinary law. Although a single witness who testifies that a ketubah was paid causes the wife to swear in order to appease her husband, extension still applies there, because she is exempt from an oath only by a Scriptural decree that no oath is imposed concerning a lien upon land. Before this, Tosafot cite Ritzba’s view that Rav Huna did not mean the worker alone. The same rule applies to the other persons listed with him in the Mishnah who swear and collect. Rav Huna mentions the first example only. The reason is that, since the oath was enacted for their benefit, additional claims may not be attached. The Ri disagrees. Terumat HaDeshen, responsum 324, discusses one upon whom an oath was reversed, where the party reversing it seeks to attach additional claims. He rules that extension does not apply, because the Sages do not make one enactment upon another. Reversal of an oath is itself a rabbinic enactment, as Mordechai in Shevuat HaDayanim implies. Semag, however, writes in Rashi’s name that since the reversal occurs with the defendant’s consent, it is not considered one enactment upon another. Terumat HaDeshen then adduces support from Ritzba: even where the Sages themselves established an oath for those who swear and collect, because the oath allows them to collect, no extension applies. He adds that even according to the Ri, reversal can be viewed as contrary to ordinary law, because by strict law the defendant should swear or pay. The first proof plainly has no force, because Semag in Rashi’s name holds that extension does apply. One cannot rule against them, especially to extract money. The proof from the Ri is stated by Terumat HaDeshen only tentatively, “it somewhat implies.” In my opinion there is no comparison. How can reversal be called “contrary to law” because the defendant should have sworn or paid? By Torah law he was entirely exempt; the Sages imposed hesset upon him. Their allowing him to reverse that oath is not an enactment upon an enactment, as Semag wrote. Nor did the Sages need a separate enactment to permit extension to hesset. Every rabbinic enactment is modeled after Torah law, as Rif writes there, and extension therefore follows automatically just as with a Torah oath. There is another difficulty. If reversed oaths were excluded because they are contrary to ordinary law, Rav Huna should have said, “except a hired worker and a reversed oath.” Tosafot were troubled that he did not mention the case of one witness testifying that a ketubah was paid and gave a strained answer. They did not say that “hired worker” includes it merely because both oaths serve to appease the opposing party. This shows that absent the “first example” explanation of Ritzba, Rav Huna should have stated every distinct exclusion expressly. Thus Terumat HaDeshen’s real support can only come from Ritzba, who applies the worker’s exception to all who swear and collect. But we do not rule like Ritzba in that respect. The authorities state expressly that only the hired worker is excluded from extension. This also appears to be Beit Yosef’s intent when, after citing Terumat HaDeshen, he writes that Semag holds extension applies. Apart from all this, the concept of “one enactment upon another” is inapplicable. Hesset itself is an enactment, yet the Sages allowed extension to it. Necessarily, extension did not require a new enactment but arose automatically from the character of the oath. Rama’s own wording, “some say,” indicates that he did not decide the point conclusively. It therefore appears that one should not act upon this ruling to extract money.
