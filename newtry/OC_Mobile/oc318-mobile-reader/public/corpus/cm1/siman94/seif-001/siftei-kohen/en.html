Anyone who became obligated to take an oath. See the Ritva at the beginning of Kiddushin, who discusses the laws of gilgul shevuah at length and concludes that gilgul applies to every oath, even an uncertain claim appended to another uncertain claim. This accords with the Ran. See there, except regarding a hired worker and one upon whom an oath was reversed. See there.
And there is one who wrote, etc. So writes Terumas HaDeshen, siman 327. His reason is that we do not make an enactment upon an enactment, because reversal of an oath is an enactment and applying gilgul to a rabbinic oath is likewise an enactment. He further writes that at the end of Perek Kol HaNishba'in the Gemara concludes that gilgul applies to everyone except a hired worker. Rivan, as cited by Tosafos there, explains that the hired worker is not unique and the same applies to all others who swear and collect. Even according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who explains that only a hired worker is excluded, one could still say that reversal of an oath is itself considered outside the strict law, since by strict law the defendant should either swear or pay. These are his words. They do not appear correct to me. On the contrary, this is not an enactment upon an enactment. By strict law the defendant would have been entirely exempt. Rav Nachman enacted heset and also enacted that the defendant could reverse the oath onto the opposing party, who would then swear and collect. Thus, by strict law the claimant could not collect even by swearing. This is unlike a hired worker, or even the other categories who swear and collect according to Rivan, where the Sages of the Mishna enacted that they collect with an oath, after which gilgul is not imposed upon them. Here, even according to the Sages of the Mishna, the claimant could not collect. Only under Rav Nachman's enactment can he collect by accepting the reversed oath. The defendant can therefore say: since you seek to swear and collect, also swear to me regarding the gilgul. If you refuse to swear regarding the gilgul, then do not swear and collect from me either, and I remain exempt from heset. This is certainly so according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak's interpretation, which we follow, that gilgul applies to all who swear and collect. Examine carefully. Nevertheless, the Ritva at the beginning of Kiddushin appears to agree with Terumas HaDeshen. He writes that whenever an oath is reversed onto the opposing party, whether a rabbinic oath or because the original oath taker is disqualified and the opposing party therefore swears and collects, no gilgul is imposed at all. He swears only concerning that which was reversed upon him. This, the Ritva says, is the view of the leading authorities. He concludes that gilgul applies to every oath, whether Torah or rabbinic, except a hired worker and one upon whom an oath was reversed, and that this was also his teacher's view. Even so, the practical law requires study, especially according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak's interpretation, which we follow. Moreover, it appears that regarding a disqualified oath taker we do not follow the Ritva, because all the poskim seem to hold that gilgul can be imposed. The Beit Yosef explicitly writes so above in siman 87, end of seif 17, in the name of the Mordechai commentary. If so, the same may apply to other reversals, since the Ritva equates them. Examine carefully.
And he would not become monetarily liable except, etc. The Tur gives the following example: one says to the other, you were my partner and therefore owe me an oath, and the other says, I was never your partner. We do not impose through gilgul an oath that he was never his partner. Rather, we impose an oath that he earned no profit during the period in which the claimant says they were partners. There is a practical difference between these formulations of the oath, as I wrote above in siman 93, seif 9 and seif 10, seif katan 10 and 14.
Beit din applies gilgul on its own initiative. This means where beit din itself knows that the claimant has a right to impose a gilgul oath. Otherwise it is forbidden for beit din to suggest it, because that would make the judges into advisers for litigants. So writes the Beit Yosef in the name of Rabbi Yehuda ben Mizir.
