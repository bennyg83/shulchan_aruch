(Seif 6) “Or swear concerning all these matters.” This requires study. The claimant plainly asserts the added claims with certainty, because the ruling concludes that he may swear concerning all of them and collect. Why is extension needed to impose hesset? A separate definite claim would itself obligate hesset. We know of no case in which a person is exempt from hesset except where the claimant is uncertain, as stated at the end of siman 75, while here the claim is definite. It is strained to say that the defendant reversed upon the claimant an uncertain oath, requiring the claimant to swear that he truly suspects him, as in siman 87, seif 11. The phrase “swear and collect” implies that after swearing he is substantively entitled to payment. The answer appears from Kiddushin 28a, where the Gemara asks how far extension reaches and gives the example that the master may say, “Swear to me that you were not sold to me as a Hebrew slave.” One might have thought that if he had actually been sold as a Hebrew slave, the matter would have become public, so the claim should not be extendable. The Gemara teaches that it may nevertheless be attached. Raavad, who is the source of the present ruling, explains that although extension applies, if the master asserted this claim independently the defendant could rely upon the absence of publicity and would not be required to take hesset. This resembles the rule at the end of HaMekabel: “The oath of Hashem shall be between the two of them,” only where no one sees; if someone sees, the bailee must bring proof and is exempt from swearing. A matter that would ordinarily become public is treated like testimony. Indeed, if the inference from publicity is strong enough to extract money, as in siman 187, it certainly suffices here to exempt from an oath and restore Torah law. There is additional proof. Rav Nahman enacted hesset because of the presumption that a person does not ordinarily bring a claim unless he has some basis. Yet at the beginning of the second chapter of Ketubot the majority that women marry as virgins is weakened by the fact that a virgin marriage normally has publicity; absence of publicity undermines the majority. If publicity can weaken a majority, it can certainly weaken the lesser presumption that a person does not bring a baseless claim. One cannot object from those authorities who require hesset in the virgin-marriage case, because there the woman also has the supporting majority that women ordinarily marry as virgins. The two majorities offset one another, leaving Rav Nahman’s presumption to support hesset. Thus the Mechaber properly rules that the defendant cannot avoid the attached claim merely by reversing the oath on the principal claim. He must either swear concerning the attached matter as well or reverse all of it upon the claimant. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
