And concerning the rolled matters she swears and collects. The Shach cites proof from the Ritva and a responsum of the Rashba that the claimant cannot reverse this rolled oath. He reasons that gilgul is no different from the principal Torah oath, and a Torah oath cannot ordinarily be reversed. I also found in the Ritva at the end of the Bava Metzia sugya of borrowed and rented animals that the Baal HaMaor and Raavad dispute this regarding one who is obligated in the partners' oath and has additional matters rolled upon him. The Raavad holds that the rolled oath cannot be reversed because whenever the Sages instituted Mishnah oaths they modeled them on Torah oaths. These sources are not decisive for our law. Those authorities hold that even on gilgul we apply the principle that one who cannot swear must pay. Their proof comes from the Mishnah concerning a slave and its clothing, and they follow Ramban's approach. The Ritva in Bava Metzia expressly adopts Ramban, and the Shitah Mekubetzet cites the Rashba similarly; the Nimukei Yosef says the Raavad, after reconsideration, also holds that inability to swear causes payment on gilgul. The Ritva appears even to extend this to a Mishnah oath because the Sages modeled it on Torah law. If gilgul has the full force of the principal oath even to create payment, it is understandable that it cannot be reversed. But our accepted law is that inability to swear does not cause payment on gilgul. We therefore already see that gilgul does not possess every legal force of the principal oath. Moreover, I do not understand why reversal should be impossible under our ruling. Regarding the principal Torah oath we tell the defendant: swear, and if you refuse or cannot swear, pay. But regarding the rolled matter he says, "I do not wish to swear because my heart is uncertain and I am no longer sure of the facts." What can beit din tell him? If they say, "Then pay," that contradicts the accepted rule that even an explicit "I do not know" regarding the gilgul does not produce payment. It is impossible that one who says honestly, "I am no longer certain enough to swear," should be forced to pay more than one who says from the outset, "I do not know." As I wrote above, any authority who rejects the payment rule in this context should logically allow the oath to be reversed. There is also support from Maharam in Hagahot Maimoniyot, Laws of Oaths. He explains Rashi's practical consequence of "if he is prudent" in terms other than reversal because a supporting witness prevents the relevant oath. Yet if the litigant has many other rolled claims against the woman, there would still be an obvious difference if those rolled claims could not be reversed when attached to a Torah oath but could be reversed when they stand independently. Maharam does not mention such a distinction. This suggests that the rolled claims themselves can be reversed. Admittedly, according to what I explained above in seif katan 1, Maharam may hold that the entire reason Torah oaths are not reversed is precisely because of the legal force of gilgul, which weakens this proof. Still, the simple implication of his words supports reversal. Therefore one should not depart from the Tur and Mechaber, who rule that the rolled matters may be reversed. The Shach's assertion that the same law remains true even according to those who reject the payment consequence of inability to swear on gilgul is difficult to understand. Once the defendant can say "I do not know" without paying, he can likewise say that he is unwilling to swear because he is uncertain and ask the other party to swear and take. The legal basis for forbidding reversal has disappeared.
