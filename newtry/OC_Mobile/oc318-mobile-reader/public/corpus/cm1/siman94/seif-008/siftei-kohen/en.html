Regarding the additional claims, she shall swear and collect. The Tur and Mechaber rule this way, but I labored to find its source and did not find one. It is also difficult. An oath imposed by extension is a Torah oath. The Torah placed that oath upon the defendant, and a Torah oath cannot be transferred to the opposing party. Rav Nachman enacted hesset only where no Torah extension existed. He did not come to uproot Torah law. This is especially clear according to the authorities who hold that if one answers, “I do not know,” regarding the additional claim, he must pay because he is obligated to swear and cannot do so. If transfer were possible, he should transfer the additional claim and require the claimant to swear that the debt is known to him, withholding payment until then. Even the Rosh and the dissenting view there disagree only because they hold that no oath is imposed by extension upon an uncertain response. Had it been imposed, they too would treat it exactly like the principal oath. Although the claimant may later be compelled to take a hesset oath after receiving payment, there is a substantial practical difference. The Tur's phrase means that the claimant must swear before collecting. In truth, if the Torah oath cannot be transferred, the defendant must first pay, and only afterward may bring a separate hesset claim. One cannot say the Tur merely reversed the procedural order, for then the same would be true even where the proposed extension is itself a Torah oath, and the ruling would teach nothing. I later found Responsa Rashba, siman 944, where both the questioner and Rashba assume this principle. I rejoiced over it as one who finds great spoil. Rashba further considers a defendant who hurried and took the principal Torah oath before the additional claim was imposed. If the parties have not yet withdrawn from that matter, his haste accomplishes nothing. The law of extension still applies, the additional oath is Torah law, it must be taken while holding a sacred object, and it cannot be transferred. If the parties already withdrew and only later the claimant returned with the additional claims, this is a separate oath and no longer an extension of the first. Beit Yosef cites this responsum briefly at the end of the siman. I also found the same ruling in Ritva on Kiddushin: a Torah oath imposed by extension cannot be transferred, while a rabbinic oath may be transferred, because the accepted law is that a Torah oath is not transferred and a rabbinic oath is. Therefore the primary law is contrary to the Tur and Mechaber. Baal HaIttur appears to support the Tur and Mechaber, and the Tur may have derived the ruling from him. Yet Baal HaIttur elsewhere cites many Geonim who hold that even a Torah oath may be transferred and leaves that issue doubtful. For that reason he did not treat an oath by extension more stringently. According to our settled law, however, that a Torah oath cannot be transferred, a true oath by extension has the full law of a Torah oath. Rashba and Ritva therefore remain primary. Lechem Mishneh's discussion also supports this, since he explains that where the principal oath is stringent and taken while holding a sacred object, a person has reason to avoid it, implying that the extended oath shares that character.
