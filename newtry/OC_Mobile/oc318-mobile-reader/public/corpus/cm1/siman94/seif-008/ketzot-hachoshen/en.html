And concerning the matters added through gilgul shevuah she shall swear. The Shach concludes that even an oath arising through gilgul cannot be reversed onto the other party. Tumim objects: regarding the principal oath we tell him, 'Go and swear, and if you refuse or do not know, pay.' But here he says, 'I do not wish to swear because my heart troubles me and I am uncertain.' What can we tell him? If his claim is explicitly uncertain, the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear does not apply to the gilgul matter. Why then should we compel payment merely because he refuses the oath? Tumim therefore says that according to the view that does not apply the rule of compelled payment, a Torah oath can certainly be reversed. His objection is not compelling. According to Rav and Shmuel, who do not apply 'one who must swear and cannot swear must pay,' one who says, 'I know fifty and do not know fifty,' is exempt without taking any oath. As I wrote in siman 75, seif katan 17, according to them a partial-admission defendant swears only where his denial is definite. If he makes himself uncertain, he is exempt even without an oath and has no reason to reverse anything. Tosafot in chapter HaShoel already asks why a partial-admission defendant is not believed through a migo that he could have said, 'I do not know about the fifty.' They answer that a person does not willingly make himself appear uncertain. Thus when he does not make himself uncertain, he must swear. The same applies to gilgul. If he keeps a definite claim, he must swear and has no migo to convert himself into an uncertain claimant because people do not willingly do so. If he truly says he is uncertain, he is exempt from the oath and need not reverse it at all. Tumim's objection therefore has no basis. More fundamentally, Tumim's suggestion that he can simply say, 'My heart troubles me,' is also difficult according to the Shach's view that one who answers 'I do not know' to a gilgul matter does not swear that he does not know. He then cannot retract from a definite claim and switch to an uncertain one merely to escape the oath. Although siman 75, seif 9, allows one to reduce the severity of his oath by changing a definite claim to an uncertain one, that is because he still swears according to his new words that he does not know. In a gilgul matter, if 'I do not know' removes the oath altogether, he would be retracting from a position that obligated an oath to one that exempts him. The Shach in siman 80, seif katan 5, writes that even where the only consequence of the original claim was an oath, the litigant cannot retract to a claim that exempts him from that oath. Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 7 of Hilchot To'en says the same.
