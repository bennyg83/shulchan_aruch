But if he makes an uncertain claim, etc. This means that the defendant says that he does not know concerning the matters sought to be added through gilgul. He need not include within the stringent oath a statement that he does not know those additional matters. The claimant may merely impose hesset upon him that he does not know. As for Shulchan Aruch above, siman 93 seif 13, which says that he includes in his oath that he does not know the amount of the loss, see Tumim. I wrote there that this is itself included in the partners' oath. There is no essential difference between swearing that he does not possess money belonging to his partner and is withholding it, and swearing that he does not know that he owes his fellow a partnership loss. Both are forms of the partners' oath. See Tumim, where I clarified the matter thoroughly.
We do not impose gilgul. See Tumim. I concluded that this applies specifically where the claimant makes a definite claim and the defendant answers that he is uncertain. In that case we do not require the uncertain matter to be included in the primary oath, although the claimant may later impose hesset. If, however, the claimant also makes only an uncertain claim, in a circumstance where gilgul is otherwise available, then even though the defendant likewise says he does not know, he must include within the primary oath that he does not know. This resolves the apparent difficulty from the Mechaber below, siman 344 seif 4. See Tumim at length.
Grounds supporting the matter. This means that beit din itself recognizes that there are grounds supporting the suspicion of the person imposing the oath. It is not sufficient that he merely asserts that such grounds exist. See Tumim, where I showed that this follows from Tosafos.
Concerning the matter. See Tumim, where I resolved Shach's question from the end of siman 344. There the ruling is that if a custodian does not know whether the animal died while rented or borrowed, since he must in any event take a custodial oath, he may include that it died while rented. I explained that where the primary oath arises from a definite claim, such as an admission to part of a claim or a single witness, gilgul may be imposed only on a definite claim, or on an uncertain claim supported by grounds that render it like a definite claim. But where the primary oath itself is based on uncertainty, as with the custodial oath, the gilgul matter may likewise be uncertain even without any supporting grounds. See there at length.
As with the partners' oath. In a partnership there are grounds for gilgul because partners sometimes rationalize withholding money from one another. If one claims, 'You have one hundred of mine,' and the defendant says, 'I do not know,' so that he must take hesset that he does not know, see Shach. Bach appears to hold that the claimant cannot impose gilgul, while Shach holds that he can. In Tumim I wrote that this requires study, for the Geonim appear to agree with Bach that gilgul cannot be imposed. The practical ruling therefore requires study.
