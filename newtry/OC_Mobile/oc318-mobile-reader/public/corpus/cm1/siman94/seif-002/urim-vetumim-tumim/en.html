But if he makes an uncertain plea, etc. This means that the defendant answers, "I do not know." The source is Tosafot and the Rosh in Bava Metzia 94. In the Mishnah one party says, "The borrowed animal died," and the other says, "I do not know," and liability is imposed. According to Rav Nachman, who holds that in an ordinary definite claim against an uncertain response the burden of proof remains on the claimant, the Gemara establishes the Mishnah as a case involving another oath obligation, such as an additional animal. Tosafot ask that the defendant is already subject to the bailee's oath that the animal died naturally, and by gilgul the claimant could include whether the animal that died was rented rather than borrowed. If the defendant says he does not know, he should be one who cannot swear and therefore pays. Tosafot and the Rosh answer that when the defendant's response to the rolled claim is uncertain, gilgul is not imposed. The Rosh adds that this can be rejected: perhaps gilgul does exist, but the rule that one who cannot swear must pay applies only to the principal oath, as in the case of "I know fifty and do not know fifty," and not to a rolled oath. At the end, however, the Rosh says, "We learn from here that one does not impose gilgul at all when the defendant answers that he does not know," returning to the first answer. In his abbreviated rulings the Tur records both propositions as law: gilgul ordinarily requires a definite response, and even where gilgul exists we do not apply the payment rule merely because the defendant cannot answer the rolled matter. There is a practical difference. Under the second explanation, the defendant must at least include in his severe oath that he does not know the answer to the additional allegation. The only point is that his inability to give a definite denial does not make him pay. Under the first explanation, the uncertain allegation is not included in the severe oath at all; there is no gilgul. Conversely, suppose one witness says the defendant seized a document or land and the defendant says, "I did not seize it; what I seized was mine." The additional matter can create a Torah oath through gilgul because the factual denial is definite, even though without the principal oath there would be no Torah oath concerning that property. Yet if the rule that inability to swear does not cause payment on gilgul is independently true, the claimant still cannot convert uncertainty on that rolled matter into automatic payment. The Tur therefore had reason to preserve both rules. In siman 75 the Tur likewise places them together. He cites the view that one who cannot swear and therefore pays does not apply to gilgul, and then gives the example of one who admits fifty of a hundred, denies fifty, and says regarding additional rolled allegations, "I do not know." He takes the Torah oath on the denied fifty, while regarding the rolled matters he is not forced to pay. The Maadanei Melech asks why the Tur adopts both answers when the Rosh's concluding "we learn from here" appears to accept only the first. The Gidulei Terumah similarly asks how the Tur knows that the Rosh rules that the payment principle does not apply to gilgul. The answer can be that once the Rosh records both explanations, money cannot be extracted where one explanation would exempt. Nor can we necessarily require inclusion of the uncertain matter in the oath where the first explanation says there is no gilgul at all. More fundamentally, the two conclusions are connected. The principle that one who cannot swear must pay rests on the idea that where the Torah imposed an oath, the baseline monetary liability is present and the oath serves as the defendant's means of exemption. With one witness, the testimony would ordinarily create monetary force akin to two witnesses, except that the Torah allows the defendant to defeat it by oath. Therefore if he cannot swear, the monetary consequence returns. But if an allegation becomes attached only by gilgul and the defendant could have answered "I do not know" before any gilgul existed without owing money or even an oath, there is no underlying monetary liability waiting to reappear. It is therefore all the more obvious that inability to answer a mere gilgul cannot itself create payment. The two rules reinforce each other. This is especially clear according to what I wrote above in siman 75 concerning the Tosafot in Shevuot: the law of Rabbi Abba's silver ingot depends upon the fact that the defendant does not have a migo to say "I do not know" and be exempt. Where such an uncertain plea would indeed exempt him, it remains a migo against converting the gilgul into monetary liability. The Taz objects from an earlier ruling of the Tur in siman 92, seif 13. There, if Reuven says that Shimon certainly knows the amount of the partnership loss, Shimon must include in the partners' oath that he does not know the amount. Why is an uncertain response included if the Tur rules here that gilgul does not apply when the defendant says "I do not know"? The Taz therefore interprets the present rule narrowly: "we do not impose gilgul" means only that we do not apply the principle of one who cannot swear and therefore pays; the defendant still swears that he does not know. This cannot be accepted. The Tur's language here and in siman 75 expressly says that the uncertain matter need not be included in the severe oath, and the Rosh's conclusion states the same. One possible distinction is that when the claimant definitely says, "You know the fact," and the defendant says he does not know, the contradiction itself is definite. This is still somewhat strained because the substance of the rolled matter remains unlike Sotah. The Shach suggests that a declaration "I do not know" is not a Torah oath at all, whereas if the claimant definitely asserts that the defendant knows, the Ran's reasoning regarding heirs may require a Torah oath that the defendant truly does not know. But this too does not fully answer the analogy to Sotah. A better explanation is that the oath in siman 92 is itself the partners' oath. The Sages imposed that oath on uncertain suspicions because partners rationalize withholding property. Whether the suspicion is that Shimon actually took partnership assets or that he knows of a loss and is withholding what he should pay, the underlying concern is the same: he may rationalize keeping money because of his labor or another self justification. Thus the statement "You certainly know the amount of the loss" does not create a foreign gilgul. It particularizes the very partners' oath. The Beit Yosef similarly explains that Rambam's requirement of a definite claim that the partner knows the amount is relevant to hesset in the later clause, whereas the basic partners' oath can arise on suspicion. The later authorities ask how that can be if uncertain matters need objective grounds for gilgul. The answer is that no separate gilgul is involved; this is part of the original uncertain partnership oath. In truth, the Shach raises a more serious contradiction from siman 344, seif 4. There, where the lender says, "I do not know whether the animal that died was borrowed or rented," and the borrower says it was rented, the Mechaber rules that the borrower swears the bailee's oath that it died naturally and, through gilgul, swears that the dead animal was rented or that he does not know. This appears directly contrary to the present ruling, and the very sugya from which the present rule was derived is the source there. The answer requires distinguishing the character of the principal oath. Tosafot's first answer is that where the principal oath rests on a definite claim such as ordinary partial admission, an unrelated uncertain allegation cannot be attached unless there are independent grounds analogous to Sotah. But a bailee's oath itself arises on uncertainty. The Rashba explains that the entrustment of the property to the bailee creates objective grounds and makes the oath resemble a definite claim. According to Tosafot's second approach, those grounds are needed for any uncertain gilgul. According to their first approach, however, an uncertain allegation that is of the same type as the principal bailee's oath may be attached even without a separate evidentiary indication, because it resembles the very oath already being taken. This explains why the bailee can include "I do not know whether it was rented" when the principal oath is itself a bailee's oath, while an uncertain foreign allegation cannot be rolled onto an ordinary partial admission oath. This also resolves the earlier question concerning the oath that the bailee did not misappropriate the deposit. Rabbenu Tam asks how that can be included if uncertain gilgul requires grounds, since there is no specific reason to suspect misappropriation. The Tur and Mechaber nevertheless follow Rashi and the general authorities that even when the animal died before us, or witnesses establish the theft, the bailee includes that he did not misappropriate it. Under the first Tosafot approach, this is no difficulty: because the principal oath is a bailee's oath arising from uncertainty, additional ordinary bailee matters may be included without separate grounds. The Nimukei Yosef and Rashba in Kiddushin also answer the Mishnah by saying that it is known that the animal died naturally, so there is no bailee's oath onto which to roll the rented or borrowed issue. This does not necessarily contradict the Gemara's later statement that the renter swears through gilgul. The Mishnah's word "and it died" can be understood, where needed, as death established by witnesses, so the basic cause of death is known. For the view that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one and no additional animals are needed, Tosafot can instead use the answer that the claimant trusts the bailee regarding natural death. For Rav Nachman, who requires an additional oath framework and therefore additional animals, the presence of a separate denied animal may destroy that trust, so the explanation using witnesses is preferable. Thus the approaches of Tosafot, Rashba, and Nimukei Yosef can be harmonized. There remain technical questions under Rabbi Chiya bar Yosef, who requires the classical structure of three animals for the bailee's oath. The most coherent resolution is that the reason some authorities do not treat "the borrowed animal died" as a bailee's oath is that normally both owner and bailee should know whether the animal was borrowed or rented, whereas the Torah imposed the ordinary bailee's oath where the owner cannot know whether the loss was accidental or negligent. But Rabbi Chiya bar Yosef holds that the Torah actually lightened the bailee's obligation so that even his special oath requires denial and admission in the full structure. Under his view the claim that the borrowed rather than rented animal died can itself participate in the bailee oath structure, as Rabbenu Tam maintains. The three required animals are then the admitted one, the denied one, and the one concerning which the borrower invokes the bailee defense. This explains why the Gemara need not separately count the natural death allegation. Finally, the cases teach an important limit on "grounds for suspicion." It is not enough for the claimant merely to shout that he has strong grounds and that the defendant is certainly lying. The analogy is to Sotah, where warning and seclusion are established by witnesses. The evidentiary grounds must be objectively apparent to beit din. Otherwise every definite claimant could manufacture the required grounds merely by insisting on the truth of his own accusation, and Tosafot's distinction would have no content.
Provided there are grounds for the matter, etc. The source is again the Mishnah concerning a borrowed and rented animal. The claimant says that the rented animal died, while the other says, "I do not know," and the Mishnah exempts him. Tosafot ask why he should not swear through gilgul. He must already take the bailee's oath that the animal died naturally, and could therefore include that the animal that died was rented. Tosafot answer first that in the middle case the owner does not see the bailee as an obvious liar and therefore trusts his report concerning the bailee's oath, leaving no principal oath for gilgul. They also answer that where the claimant's added allegation is uncertain, gilgul is imposed only when there are objective grounds, as in Sotah and in the partners' oath. Here there are no grounds indicating whether the borrowed or rented animal died. The Rosh treats this answer as significant for practical law, and it is the source of the ruling here. The Shach asks from siman 344, where the Mechaber rules that if the lender himself says he does not know and the borrower says the rented animal died, the borrower swears that it died naturally and by gilgul also swears that the rented animal died or that he does not know. That appears to permit uncertain gilgul without independent grounds. There is an additional difficulty. Rabbenu Tam, in Bava Metzia, asks according to the rule that gilgul requires similarity or grounds how a bailee can ever be required to include the oath that he did not misappropriate the deposit. There may be no particular reason to suspect misappropriation. Rabbenu Tam therefore gives a different interpretation of that oath. Yet later, siman 294, the Tur and Mechaber clearly follow the ordinary Rashi interpretation: even where the animal died before us or witnesses establish that it was stolen, the bailee swears that he was not negligent and includes that he did not misappropriate it. The question of grounds remains. The Nimukei Yosef and Rashba in Kiddushin ask Tosafot's question and answer that the Mishnah is a case where it is known that the animal died naturally, so there is no bailee's oath onto which the rented versus borrowed issue can be rolled. The Gidulei Terumah objects that the Gemara itself says in the case "one says borrowed and one says rented" that the borrower swears through gilgul, asking how he can swear when the admission and claim concern different matters. This seems to prove that no witnesses established the natural death and that a bailee's oath remains. The answer is that the Mishnah's statement "and it died" can, where the sugya requires it, mean that the death itself was witnessed, even if the precise legal status of the animal remains disputed. The baraita need not spell out every evidentiary circumstance. For the authority who holds that a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one, Tosafot can say the owner simply trusts the bailee about natural death. For Rav Nachman, who requires an independent oath framework and therefore expands the case to multiple animals, the additional denial makes it harder to say the owner trusts him altogether. Rashba and Nimukei Yosef can then establish the natural death by witnesses instead. Thus the answers are complementary rather than contradictory. There is still a technical problem. If witnesses establish natural death, how does the case supply the three animals required according to Rabbi Chiya bar Yosef for a bailee's oath? One cannot simply count the animal whose natural death is known as the bailee-defense animal. Nor is it satisfactory to say that the claim "the borrowed animal died" itself is a bailee's defense under every approach, because some authorities distinguish ordinary bailee uncertainty from the borrowed versus rented status, which both parties should ordinarily know. The better explanation is that this distinction applies according to the view that the Torah made the bailee's oath especially stringent even without ordinary admission and denial. The Torah requires an oath about accidental loss precisely because the owner cannot know how the loss occurred and the bailee may speak imprecisely. But Rabbi Chiya bar Yosef holds that the Torah instead lightened the bailee's position so that he does not swear without the full combination of denial, admission, and a bailee defense. Under that view there is no reason to exclude the claim that the borrowed animal died from the category of the bailee defense. The denied animal, admitted animal, and disputed borrowed animal together supply the required three, in accordance with Rabbenu Tam. The natural death itself need not be counted separately. However, Tosafot in Bava Kamma 107 and at the beginning of Kol HaNishba'in establish from several Mishnayot that Rabbi Chiya bar Yosef's requirement of three animals concerns the special bailee's oath. An ordinary partial admission requires only two items, one admitted and one denied. If we disregard the bailee's oath because witnesses established natural death, there still remains an ordinary partial admission oath on the additional animals. Why then can the claimant not roll onto that Torah oath the uncertain question whether the dead animal was rented? The defendant should not be entirely exempt. The answer is that Tosafot's second principle, requiring objective grounds for an uncertain gilgul, remains valid where the principal oath is an ordinary definite oath such as partial admission. In that setting the additional uncertain claim does not resemble the principal oath at all. It therefore needs independent grounds, and here there are none. The Maharsha asks that if Tosafot allow a definite added claim even when it is factually dissimilar because it resembles the definite character of the principal oath, then when the principal oath is a bailee's oath based on uncertainty, an uncertain added claim should similarly be attachable even without independent grounds. That is exactly the distinction. According to Tosafot's first answer, an uncertain claim can be rolled onto a principal bailee's oath because both arise from uncertainty. According to their second answer, a bailee's oath itself provides objective grounds because the property was certainly entrusted to the bailee, as the Rashba explains. Either way, the rule requiring grounds is most restrictive when one seeks to attach an uncertain foreign claim to a definite oath of partial admission. This explains the Tur and Mechaber. Concerning the oath that the bailee did not misappropriate the deposit, they follow Rashi and the first Tosafot approach: because the principal oath is itself an uncertain bailee's oath, another ordinary bailee issue may be attached even without separate grounds. The Rosh cites both answers concerning trust and grounds. They can both be true in their respective contexts. Here, where the discussion concerns a principal oath arising from a definite partial admission or comparable definite claim, the Tur and Mechaber state simply that an uncertain additional allegation requires grounds. Later, in siman 344, where the principal oath is itself the bailee's oath, they permit the uncertain rented versus borrowed matter to be included even without a separate indication, because it is of the same class as the principal oath. The same distinction resolves the Beit Yosef's earlier question in siman 93 concerning uncertain gilgul. There the principal oath is the partners' oath, which itself arises from suspicion and is comparable to a bailee's oath. An added uncertain partnership matter can therefore be included without a separate indication beyond the relationship that generates the principal oath. One may still ask how Tosafot's "he trusts him" answer establishes the broader rule that an uncertain response cannot be rolled at all. If the claimant in the Mishnah trusts the bailee concerning natural death, there is simply no principal oath. The answer is that Tosafot continue to test the alternative precisely because if gilgul on uncertainty carried the payment rule, the Mishnah would have been better constructed in a straightforward case where the owner knows natural death and withholds only the borrowed versus rented fact to force the oath, rather than requiring added animals and an artificial oath framework. Their analysis shows that where the defendant's uncertainty would otherwise be harmless, one cannot turn it into automatic payment through gilgul. Another way to express the distinction is through strategic pleading. If both parties are uncertain about a fact that each ordinarily could have known, a defendant who answers "I do not know" may be suspected of choosing that plea to avoid an oath. In such a case the court need not reward the maneuver and can require inclusion in a comparable uncertain oath. If the claimant says definitely, "You know," while the defendant says he does not know, the defendant gains no obvious strategic advantage because the definite assertion itself exposes him to contradiction, and Tosafot's limitation can operate more fully. This helps reconcile the cases without multiplying disputes among the poskim. Finally, the requirement of grounds means grounds recognized by beit din, not merely the claimant's assertion that there are grounds. We learn gilgul from Sotah, where the warning and seclusion are established through legally cognizable facts. If the claimant's own insistence were enough, every definite claimant could say, "I am certain the defendant lies, therefore there are grounds," and the limitation would be meaningless. The circumstances themselves must provide the evidentiary basis.
As with the partners' oath. See Shach, seif katan 5, citing the Bach that if one claims a maneh and the other says, "I do not know," the defendant is exempt but must take hesset that he does not know, and the claimant cannot use gilgul shevuah upon that oath. The Shach holds that gilgul is available, and the unqualified language of the poskim appears to support him. Nevertheless, the early authorities cited by the Rashba in Kiddushin, and the Ran at the end of Shevuot, hold that one does not impose gilgul upon an uncertain plea. They say that Sotah is considered a definite basis because the husband warned her and she secluded herself. This position goes back to Rav Hai Gaon in Mishpetei Shevuot. The Rashba and Ran object from the partners' oath, the oath of bailees, and the oath that the object is not presently in one's possession, all of which can involve uncertainty and yet support gilgul. It seems those Geonim consider a bailee's oath effectively definite for this purpose, because it is certain that the property entered the bailee's possession. The same applies to the oath that the object is not in his possession. The special Mishnah oaths imposed on an uncertain claim likewise arise only where there are substantial grounds for suspicion. They are therefore analogous to a definite claim, much as the Rosh explains that these uncertain oaths were modeled upon the bailee's oath. There are objective grounds, so the case resembles Sotah. The Geonim's limitation can therefore concern an ordinary hesset oath in which the defendant swears merely that he does not know. Whether he truly knows is itself entirely uncertain, and there are no objective grounds of suspicion. In such a case the oath does not resemble Sotah and no gilgul is imposed. This answers the objections from the Mishnah oaths, because there the Sages required the oath only in circumstances containing evidentiary grounds that elevate the uncertainty. Accordingly, the practical ruling requires study. There is a coherent basis for the Geonic position, and their words are received tradition. Moreover, hesset itself is rabbinic, and we ordinarily do not create one enactment upon another. One should therefore hesitate before using a bare hesset oath based on "I do not know" as a vehicle for additional uncertain claims.
