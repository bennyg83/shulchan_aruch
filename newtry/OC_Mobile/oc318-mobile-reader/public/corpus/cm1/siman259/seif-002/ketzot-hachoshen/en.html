And it has an identifying sign on its body. Bava Metzia asks whether the Torah's reliance on identifying signs is biblical or rabbinic. One practical difference is returning a woman's get through signs. If signs are biblical, they can suffice; if only rabbinic, the Sages would not rely on their enactment for a serious prohibition. The matter remains unresolved, and the decisors therefore do not permit a woman based merely on ordinary signs. Some asked from Chullin, where the Gemara says, "Learn from here that signs are biblical," in identifying animals that resemble one another. Rabbi Ozer reportedly inferred that two signs together are biblical because together they are like an especially distinctive sign. There is no difficulty. Ramban, cited by Ran, explains that the Chullin passage is not discussing the general evidentiary law of returning a lost object by signs. It means that the particular physical characteristics used there are reliable identifying markers by received tradition, like the Torah signs of animals and birds. That does not resolve whether ordinary signs prove ownership of a lost item. This also suggests why the Bava Metzia question is framed as returning a get rather than identifying a dead husband by bodily signs. Identification of a person by true physical signs can be inherently reliable by Torah law. The special problem of a lost get is different. The person offering the sign may have seen the get previously, or may be guessing from circumstances. Therefore the Gemara asks whether the general lost property rule of signs is strong enough to return the get. In testimony that a husband died, a single witness is already believed and is not ordinarily suspected of lying. Once the witness gives genuine identifying characteristics, those signs can be reliable on a Torah level. The special uncertainty about lost property signs need not apply in the same manner.
Accordingly, when the Gemara asks about returning a lost get through signs, the concern can be that the messenger himself is not fully trusted. Rashba and Ran explain that he may fear the husband's complaint or have a financial interest in his fee. Even a true sign can therefore be insufficient if he merely saw the object or is guessing. This explains the Gemara's proof from the verse concerning the garment, which teaches return of objects having signs. If signs in lost property could always be defeated by the concern that the claimant had seen the object, the verse would not establish the rule. The Gemara answers that the reference to signs may not be essential, and later seeks proof from the donkey identified by its saddle, only to answer that witnesses to the saddle may be intended. Commentators ask why the Gemara did not simply establish the case with an especially distinctive sign. On the present analysis, that would not solve the lost get problem if the claimant is suspected of having seen the object. Whether the sign is ordinary or extremely distinctive, a liar who saw it can repeat it. The Gemara then compares the issue with the dispute whether one may testify to a man's death based on a mole. If the Sages reject the mole because signs are only rabbinic, that is strong evidence even in a death identification context where a single witness is otherwise trusted and there is no ordinary fear of lying. The Gemara ultimately says the dispute can instead concern whether such moles are common among people of the same age or whether they change. Another answer says everyone agrees ordinary signs are rabbinic and the dispute concerns an especially distinctive mole. Such a distinctive sign can certainly work in death testimony because the witness is not suspected of lying. Yet it can still fail for returning a lost get if the claimant himself may simply have seen the sign. This framework also explains Rambam. Rambam rules that if a man was killed and his face was unrecognizable, but witnesses had clear bodily signs, his heirs may descend to the estate even though the wife is not permitted to remarry. Rambam says the extra stringency exists only because of the severe prohibition. At first this seems impossible if ordinary signs are only rabbinic by essence. But the Talmudic uncertainty about the Torah status of signs concerns the lost property context and the danger of false matching. Where the signs themselves are truthful and the witness is not suspected of lying, Rambam can treat them as inherently reliable by Torah law. Rav Ashi's later hesitation can then be understood as whether the Sages imposed an additional stringency in matters of severe marital prohibition. For inheritance they did not. This also explains Rambam in the laws of divorce, where he requires witnesses to identify a get by an especially distinctive sign such as a hole beside a particular letter. Raavad says even the messenger should suffice. According to Rambam, the messenger remains subject to the lost get concern that he may be fabricating or relying on what he saw, whereas independent witnesses do not. Thus Rambam's wording, "the witnesses had a distinctive sign in the get," is deliberate.
The Bava Metzia question therefore remains a Torah question as to the lost property mechanism, but where the sign itself is unquestionably genuine, signs can be Torah evidence in other contexts. This is why the Gemara asks how lost objects can be returned by signs and attempts a proof from the donkey's saddle. The separate reason we do not permit a wife based on an ordinary bodily sign derives from Rav Ashi's statement in the first chapter of Bava Metzia. He says that a hole beside a particular letter is a sufficiently distinctive sign, but a general hole is not, because he remains uncertain concerning signs. That passage cannot be explained merely by fear that the claimant saw the sign. If so, even the most distinctive sign would be useless. It deals with the intrinsic strength of the sign where there is no lying concern. Rambam can therefore understand Rav Ashi's uncertainty not as a doubt whether signs have any Torah evidentiary force, but whether the Sages imposed extra stringency for the severe marital prohibition. By Torah law, truthful identifying signs are sufficient. For ordinary monetary inheritance the Sages did not add that stringency, so heirs can inherit on the basis of such signs. For permitting a wife, only an especially distinctive sign is used. This also explains Rambam's refusal to accept even a messenger's extremely distinctive sign in the lost get case. The messenger is not merely subject to the intrinsic sign stringency. He also has the separate credibility problem described by Rashba and Ran. An independent witness with an especially distinctive sign has no such problem. I then considered whether signs can be used not merely to return lost property but to extract money from a possessor. Yevamot concerning deposited sesame appears to imply that an especially distinctive sign could suffice. Shach in siman 297 likewise assumes that an especially distinctive sign can defeat even a migo, treating it almost like witnesses.
Yet the general law of signs appears to point the other way. Chullin says that visual recognition is stronger than signs because if two witnesses testified that a man bearing particular signs killed someone, beit din would not execute on that basis, whereas visual recognition would suffice. Tosafot asks why documents can be returned by signs if signs are so weak. They answer that a document is not currently possessed by someone claiming ownership, while money or an object in a person's hand cannot be extracted merely by signs. This appears to mean that no ordinary sign, even in property commonly lent or rented, can extract a movable object from a present possessor. If witnesses see an object with certain signs in Shimon's hand and say that an object with those signs belongs to Reuven, Shimon can deny it and say another object happens to have the same signs. Signs are not sufficient to remove property from possession. The sesame case and the other deposit cases are different because witnesses had previously observed the deposit with those very signs. When they later see an item in the custodian's hand bearing the same distinctive characteristics, the most reasonable conclusion is that it is the same deposited item. The custodian cannot say, "I returned yours and this is another identical object." Thus the signs operate together with the prior witnessed deposit, not as standalone proof against present possession. A passage in Bava Batra concerning a witness who later became blind seems at first to prove that signs can establish ownership. One view allows him to identify land by its boundaries, another even a garment by length and width, and another an ingot by weight. Although those Amoraim are ultimately rejected, the rejection can be because the witness's legal status changed from qualified to disqualified, not because signs themselves are useless. One can answer that land differs because no one physically holds it in the same way as movables, and signs can sometimes establish identity against prior title. The garment and ingot cases can be understood as lost property, where there is no competing possessor. Nimukei Yosef indeed distinguishes. Measurements and weight can be useful signs for lost property, but to extract money from a present owner one would need a truly exceptional sign. Rabbeinu Tam, however, writes that testimony in monetary law fundamentally requires visual recognition, not mere comparison of signs. He derives this from the very blind witness passage. I previously found his proof difficult because the blind person's rejection could be due to his status as blind. Yet Rishonim in Gittin explain that a blind person is not necessarily intrinsically disqualified for every relevant statement. Their explanation of the blind witness's inability can therefore support Rabbeinu Tam's deeper point: ordinary signs do not create full monetary testimony. Other Tosafot passages treat blindness as a bodily witness disqualification from the verse "or saw," which weakens that proof. Still, the explicit Tosafot in Chullin remains decisive that money in a person's possession is not extracted merely by signs. This is the practical conclusion.
