And most butchers are Jews. Meaning, most of those who sell meat to the townspeople, even to gentiles, are Jews. Therefore the meat is permitted for eating whether it was lost by a gentile or by a Jew. If a Jew lost it, he has certainly despaired because most inhabitants are gentiles. There is also no concern that the meat was tereifah, because the case is in a city where whenever a tereifah is discovered it is publicly announced. More likely, the Mechaber deliberately mentions an entire animal or bird, meaning it was found whole. Then there is no concern about tereifah, because it can be examined, and no concern about improper slaughter, since most Jews involved in slaughter are competent. The case must also be one in which there is no prohibition of meat that was hidden from view. This can occur in either of two ways. The animal or bird is recognizable as belonging to one of the residents of that city, and most butchers there are Jews; or the finder saw from a distance that it fell from a person's hand, though he does not know whether that person was Jew or gentile. Even if meat is found in the hand of a gentile, where most butchers are Jews we do not prohibit it merely because it was hidden from view. As I explained in Yoreh Deah siman 63, substitution is a concern primarily where meat is found abandoned in a corner, not where it is in someone's hand. The Rashbam, followed by the Rosh, likewise holds that the prohibition of meat hidden from view applies where there is some present defect in the chain of custody, such as meat that was lost and later found, or was carried off by a bird and later returned. If the meat was never lost but is simply found in someone's hand, or one saw it fall from that person's hand, there is no such defect and we assume it is the same meat that person had previously obtained from Jewish butchers.
