(Seif 7, and if it is an article that can be saved.) We must first clarify the Tur’s words, for there is considerable difficulty in his statement, “if the owners were pursuing it.” He then adds, “provided the circumstances were such that if they stood there,” which implies that they were not actually standing there. It is also difficult that concerning the second case, where the object cannot be rescued at the place of the flood, he writes, “if they stood there,” for even if they pursued it, there is despair where rescue is impossible. It appears to me that the Tur is discussing a river containing obstructions, mentioned immediately before. Where the article bears an identifying mark, the existence of the obstructions prevents us from presuming despair. He then explains that for an article without an identifying mark, the obstructions help only if the article could be rescued immediately at the place where it was swept away through some active effort. The law is that so long as the owner is engaged in those rescue efforts and pursuing the article, another who arrives first and saves it does not acquire it. We are witnesses, as it were, that the owner does not despair merely because someone else may precede him in rescuing it. Although he cannot provide an identifying mark, he relies on witnesses who will testify that it was swept from him and that he immediately undertook efforts to save it, and there is no greater identification than that. The Tur’s words, “or even if they are not there,” teach the second case. Since rescue was possible, but the owners did not yet know of the loss, their despair would be despair without knowledge, and another’s prior rescue does not give him title. Regarding both cases already mentioned, the Tur then writes that if another came and rescued it, he did not acquire it. He next states that if it could not be rescued immediately, but only by means of distant obstructions, those obstructions are ineffective. Even if witnesses know that the beams belonged to him, we say that he despaired because immediate rescue was impossible. His pursuit is ineffective regarding rescue through distant obstructions, since the obstruction is far away and the beam may reach it before he does. Since the article lacks an identifying mark, he certainly despairs. The proper reading is, “but only after it travels far away, there his pursuit is ineffective,” without the conjunction before “there.” The Tur means that the rescue which is effective is immediate rescue through his pursuit and efforts, not rescue through distant obstructions, for the reason stated. He then discusses the form of rescue that is effective, namely, immediate rescue: “If he stood there when it was swept away and despaired,” we see that he did not desire even that available rescue. If he instead relied on the obstructions, this is certainly despair. It appears that a conjunction should be added and the text should read, “and if they stood there.” The Tur’s later phrase, “and if the matter is unspecified,” would at first glance mean that the owners were not present, similar to Tosafot’s explanation cited by Beit Yosef. That cannot be correct, for the Tur already said, “or even if he is not there.” Rather, it means that it is unknown whether the circumstances were such that the owner despaired, because rescue was impossible, or whether rescue was possible. In an unspecified case we do not presume despair. The Rama, however, appears to understand the Tur’s phrase “if they stood there” as referring to a case where rescue was impossible. This is astonishing, for where rescue is impossible, even pursuit constitutes despair. The Sema repairs the Rama’s wording by explaining that “and he stands there” means “or he stands there,” in a case where rescue is possible. Yet what will he do with the Tur’s explicit words “if he stood there,” which cannot be explained that way? The Rama’s statement that an unspecified case constitutes despair is likewise astonishing, for the Tur says that it does not constitute despair. The Sema writes that the Rama’s text should also read “does not constitute despair,” and that he saw this in a manuscript of the Rama. Nevertheless, the Rama’s overall explanation of the Tur remains concealed from me. I have written what appears correct.
