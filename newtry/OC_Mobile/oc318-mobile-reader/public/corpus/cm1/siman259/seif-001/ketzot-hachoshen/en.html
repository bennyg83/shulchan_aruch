Even if he returns it afterward. Bava Metzia says that one who finds a coin, takes it intending to keep it, and later returns it after the owner despairs has merely given a gift. Rambam says the finder has already violated all the relevant prohibitions. Baal HaMaor asked why returning the item does not at least repair the prohibition of theft, since even a robber who acquires the stolen item after despair remains obligated to pay value. Ramban answers that the case combines the laws of loss and robbery. A lost object can be acquired through despair if it was not first taken into a custodial relationship with the owner. If the finder took it intending not to return it, the object remains like a lost object lying on the ground from the owner's perspective and can be fully acquired by later despair. Since the acquisition is complete, no monetary obligation remains to fulfill restitution. By contrast, if he took the lost object in order to return it before the owner despaired, he became a paid custodian for the owner. His hand is then legally the owner's hand, and later despair cannot acquire. Ritva says this is what the Gemara means by, "it came into his hand in prohibition." It does not mean an actual act of theft, since he was obligated to lift an identifiable lost object. It means that when he received it, the owner's legal right was still intact and the finder became the owner's custodian. His hand therefore remained like the owner's. This distinction explains why later return after a full despair acquisition is only a gift, while a lost object taken for return before despair can never be acquired merely by the owner's later despair.
Ramban's statement that despair does not acquire while the object remains "in the owner's domain" seems at first to imply that despair works only when the item is physically outside the owner's premises. That cannot be literal. A sentenced ox whose witnesses were later discredited can become ownerless even if still physically in the owner's premises because the owner had abandoned it. True ownerless designation certainly works even in one's own premises where the courtyard has not acquired it back. More importantly, Bava Metzia treats fallen figs beneath a tree leaning toward the road as acquired through despair even though they can still lie within the owner's land. Money found in synagogues and study halls can likewise be acquired through despair even though a synagogue can be a jointly owned courtyard. A slave can also be the subject of despair language even though a slave's hand is ordinarily his master's. Ramban therefore does not mean that physical presence on the owner's land prevents despair. His point is that where another person holds the object as the owner's custodian, that person's hand is legally the owner's hand, and the custodian himself is not despairing. The owner's separate despair cannot dislodge an object already held in the owner's own legal hand through his custodian. But if the object lies in the owner's courtyard in circumstances where the courtyard itself does not acquire for him, as Rama discusses in siman 268, despair can operate. The decisive point is not the physical location but whether the item is presently held in a legal hand acting for the owner.
The sugya of unknowing despair now requires analysis. Abaye says unknowing despair is not despair. Regarding an object with an identifying sign, everyone agrees that later despair does not help because when the object first came to the finder the owner had not yet despaired. Regarding an object without a sign, Rava says the inevitable later despair can count from the outset. According to Ramban and the Geonim, if the finder took the object intending not to return it, later despair should acquire even when the object has a sign, because he never became the owner's custodian. If he took it intending to return it, then even if the owner had already despaired before it came to him, he did not acquire at that moment because he lacked intent to acquire. The answer is that a finder who lifts after the owner's despair but merely intends to return the item can later decide to acquire it. Since the owner's despair had already occurred before the lifting, the finder never became a custodian obligated in restoration. His initial lack of acquisitive intent postpones acquisition but does not create the owner's continuing legal hand. By contrast, where the finder lifts before legally effective despair with the intent to return, he becomes a custodian immediately. His hand becomes the owner's hand, and no later despair can help. Thus the dispute of Abaye and Rava concerning unknowing despair can be established where the finder lifts intending to return. According to Abaye, since there was no effective despair at the time, he became a custodian and must return forever. According to Rava, unknowing despair counts, so he never entered the status of custodian and can later acquire when he chooses. The Gemara cannot be speaking of one who lifted with intent to steal for himself, because then even an object with an identifying sign could be acquired if the owner later actually despaired. The phrase "it came into his hand in prohibition" means it came to him at a time when the owner's right still stood and he became the owner's custodian, as Ritva explains.
