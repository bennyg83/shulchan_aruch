He has already violated "You may not hide yourself." See Shach, seif katan 1, who concludes that the primary ruling follows Ramban. Ramban's basic position is this. Although despair does not ordinarily acquire stolen property because it came into the robber's hand through prohibition, and even according to the view that despair can acquire the robber must still pay value, that is specifically where the object was stolen directly from the owner's possession. If a person took a lost object before the owner's despair with intent to steal it and the owner afterward despaired, the taker acquires through that despair and need not even pay value. Despair can acquire in a stolen lost object because the object had already left the owner's possession before the robbery. Only theft directly from the owner's domain remains resistant to despair. But if one took the lost object with intent to return it, he became the owner's custodian. His hand is like the owner's hand, and the object did not leave the owner's legal possession. Despair therefore does not help. See Ramban. It follows from Ramban that if an object is lost but remains lying inside the owner's house, even if we hear the owner despair, the despair does not transfer ownership because the object has not left his domain. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 26, however, imply that if we actually hear the owner despair even in a building, the despair can work. Ketzot HaChoshen also challenges Ramban from the rule of a fig tree leaning over the road, from found property in a synagogue, even though a synagogue courtyard can be jointly held, and from the rule "I have despaired of so-and-so, my slave," which shows that despair can work even where the slave's hand is legally like the master's. In my view the answer is that both theft and lost property are treated as outside the owner's domain only when the object is located in an unguarded place, exposed to passersby. Then it is truly an aveidah that has left his possession and despair can work. But if it lies in a guarded place, it is still legally within his possession, and despair does not transfer it. The Torah gave legal force to despair specifically concerning a lost object, and something lying in a guarded place is not legally called an aveidah, as siman 260 makes clear. This explains Ramban's rule. Once the finder took the object in order to return it, he became the owner's custodian. The object is then in a place guarded for the owner and is no longer outside his possession. Despair therefore does not work. Likewise, a lost object lying in a guarded location is no worse than property guarded by the owner's agent. It is as though the agent is guarding it, and the legal category of aveidah does not apply. The slave case is no proof. A slave who steals himself away and flees is like property in the hands of a robber. Even though ordinarily the slave's hand is the master's hand, once he runs away he is outside the master's effective possession. Nor is there proof from Tosafot's case. There the object was in an unguarded courtyard. Certainly with three non Jews it is unguarded, but even with three Jews it can be unguarded in the relevant sense. Despair can therefore operate there. Ketzot also asks from the law of despair without knowledge concerning an object with an identifying sign. If the finder took it intending to steal, then according to Ramban later despair should acquire it. If he took it intending to return it, then even if the owner had already despaired before the lifting, the owner's custodian should hold it for him like one who lifts a found object for another. There is no difficulty. The case can be where he lifted it without present intent to steal and with the following lawful intent: if he later learns the owner had already despaired, he will keep it under the law; if he learns the owner had not despaired, he will return it as required. That is a perfectly coherent intermediate intent. Ketzot's answer, that a person who intended only to return it did not lift it as an act of acquisition for the owner, has no basis. He nevertheless lifted it on the owner's behalf, and the owner acquires immediately through his act. There are also textual errors in Sema. In seif katan 1 it should read, "You may not hide yourself, and this is a prohibition from the moment he sees it." The printed phrase referring to its coming into his hand is erroneous. In seif katan 16, the text should likewise read, "even if it came into his hand," rather than the corrupted wording in print.
