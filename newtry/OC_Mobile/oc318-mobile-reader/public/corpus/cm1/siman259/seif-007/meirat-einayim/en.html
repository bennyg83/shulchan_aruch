The surge of the sea, etc. Rashi explains in the chapter Elu Metziot that there are places along the seashore where the sea regularly retreats ten or fifteen parsangs and then returns, sweeping away whatever is found there. The term refers to the swelling and spreading movement of the water. Likewise, "the washout of a river" refers to a river that rises beyond its banks and sweeps away property.
It belongs to the finder. This is so even if it came into his hand before the owner's despair. The Gemara derives it from the verse, "every lost thing of your brother which is lost from him and you find." The extra phrase "from him" teaches that only an object lost from the owner but available to other people remains within the law of return. An object lost from him and not ordinarily available to anyone else is excluded. Even if the owner declares that he does not despair, or runs after the flooding waters, his personal attitude is disregarded as against the ordinary attitude of people. It is like one crying over a house that has collapsed.
It is necessary to return from the power of Dana D. D.C., which is written in the book of Dovgenova to return even after despair and a change of authority:
If there are obstacles in the river. Trees or stones set in the river and inhibit this from going to the distance:
But if it is something he cannot save immediately, and he stands, etc., until "ordinarily it is despair." The printed text stating "ordinarily it is despair" requires correction. The law should be: If the object cannot be saved immediately, or if it could be saved immediately but the owner stands there and does not pursue it, there is definite despair. But if the facts are unknown, there is no presumed despair. The conjunction in "and he stands" should be read disjunctively, meaning "or he stands." Otherwise Rama's words contradict one another. At first he says that when the owners can save and are pursuing, the finder does not acquire, implying that where they cannot save, even pursuit does not help. Tosafot explicitly say that where immediate rescue is impossible, even pursuit does not prevent legal despair. The Tur also rules that in an unknown case we do not presume despair. I found in Rama's own handwritten gloss that the word "not" is explicitly present. It was omitted in printing. Read in this way, Rama accords with the Tur and Tosafot. The Ir Shushan copied the faulty printed wording without noticing the contradictions.
