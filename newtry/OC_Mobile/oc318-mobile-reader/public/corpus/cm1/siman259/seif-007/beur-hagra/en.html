And if it is an item, etc. The text here and in the Tur must be emended in accordance with Tosafos 22b, s.v. "washed away, etc." The Gemara answers there that the case is where he can save it. This is difficult, for if the initial assumption was that he could not save it, what was the challenge against Rava? Moreover, even if the owner pursues it, it would belong to the finder, like property lost in the sea. See Bava Kamma 116b. Rather, even the initial assumption concerns a case where it can easily be saved, but the Gemara initially understood the case as an item without an identifying mark, and ordinarily the owner is absent. We therefore have three categories. If the owner pursues it, it must be returned. If he does not pursue it, it belongs to the finder. In an ordinary case, according to Abaye, it must be returned, and that is our ruling, but this is difficult according to Rava. The Gemara therefore answers that the case concerns an item with an identifying mark. It then asks, if so, etc. The sugya concludes that where there is an identifying mark, in all three situations it must be returned. It then establishes the case differently, showing that even where there is an identifying mark, the same three categories apply as where it can easily be saved but lacks an identifying mark according to Abaye. The rule is: if it cannot be saved, it always belongs to the finder. If it can be saved and has an identifying mark, it always must be returned. If it lacks an identifying mark, or can be saved only with difficulty after being swept away even though it has an identifying mark, then if the owner pursues it, or in an ordinary case, it must be returned; if he does not pursue it, it belongs to the finder.
Geese, etc. Tosafos to Chullin 139a, s.v. "keivan, etc." Although we do not rule like Rav regarding consecrated property, that is specifically because wherever, etc. Nevertheless it leaves the owner's possession, as in the caravan case in Bava Kamma 116b. This is the meaning of "specifically, etc."
