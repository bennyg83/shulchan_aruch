One who rescues property from a lion or bear, from the sweep of the sea, or from the inundation of a river acquires it, even if the owner stands and protests. Nevertheless, it is proper and upright to return it, as explained in seif 5. Although the strict law does not require return in these cases, if the king or beit din decrees its return, one must return it by virtue of the law of the kingdom or the authority of beit din to declare property ownerless. Thus, where a ship sank and the ruler and communities decreed that anyone buying goods from non-Jews who rescued them must restore them, the buyer must return them and receives from the owner only what he paid. If obstructions in a river cause a floating object to remain lodged, an object bearing a distinguishing mark is ordinarily not considered abandoned. Rama: If it lacks a mark, the finder does not acquire it when the owners can save it and are pursuing it, or when they are absent but would likely save it were they present. If it cannot be saved immediately and the owner stands by without pursuing it, he certainly despairs, and ordinarily this is abandonment. Geese and chickens that escaped their owners are ownerless, and whoever takes possession acquires them, but only when the owners cannot recover them.
