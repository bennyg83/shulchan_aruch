But if it is something he cannot save. See Sema, seif katan 19, who reads the letter vav in "and he stands" as disjunctive. According to him, where immediate rescue is impossible, even pursuit does not help because the case is like property swept away by the sea. This follows the second interpretation Perishah gives Tur. In my view the interpretation is not viable. If inability to save made the property like sea-loss, then Rama's opening phrase, "or he is not there," would have to mean he did not know of the flood at all. If he knew, he would certainly despair because the item has no identifying sign. The principal qualification would be missing from the text. Further, Rama's later statement that an unexplained case is not despair would then have to refer only to the clause, "he stands and does not pursue," in a case where rescue is possible. But where rescue is impossible, even pursuit would not help. Yet a case where rescue is possible but he is not present was already covered earlier. Perishah's reading of Tur is likewise difficult. It would require rearranging Tur's clauses. Moreover, if inability to save means the case is like something swept away by the sea, then even an identifying sign would not help. Tur, however, explains the problem by saying the people in the distant place will not know the item belongs to him merely because he pursued it. This implies that if the object had an identifying sign it would be returned. In my view Tur has a straightforward meaning. Tur says that if the owners pursue the items, that is evidence that the items are theirs and they are returned to them, provided the situation is one in which, had the owners been present and seen the flood, they could have rescued the property immediately. Then they certainly did not despair. Even if they were not present when the flood occurred and another person rescued the objects, the finder does not acquire them, even if the owners later despair, because at the time the item came into his hand there had not yet been a proper basis for despair. But if the flood was such that even had the owners been present they could not have rescued the object immediately where it was swept away, and rescue would require following it to a distant place, then pursuit may no longer be sufficient proof for people at the distant location. Before the owner arrives there, the beams may already reach another person's hand, and those people will not know whose property they are merely because he pursued after them. In that case, if the owners stood by during the flood and did not pursue, we treat that as actual despair. If we simply do not know whether they despaired, an unexplained case is not treated as despair. Tur cannot mean that witnesses must have seen the owner pursuing from the exact moment of the flood. If so, there would be no difference between a near and distant location once he has witnesses to the pursuit. Nor would Tur's statement, that people in the distant place will not know the item belongs to him because of his pursuit, make sense. Why would nearby people know from the pursuit while distant people would not? Moreover, Tur had just said that even if the owner was absent during the flood the property can still be returned. If he was absent, there could be no witnesses to an immediate pursuit. We would then be forced to say that Tur suddenly switched to a case of witnesses who recognize the object visually, which would be very strained. Rather, no witnesses to pursuit at the moment of the flood are required. If people later see the owner pursuing the beams, even after the beams have reached another person's hand, he can be believed to say they are his in an appropriate nearby case. This resembles Bava Kamma 114 regarding a swarm of bees, where the owner pursues them. Rashi explains that the pursuit before the child's statement creates grounds to believe the claim. Shitah Mekubetzet says explicitly that where the items are still close to the owner's location, people identify them as that person's beams, and if he pursues he can recover them. But once the items have traveled far away, there are no local persons who recognize them and he has no identifying sign to provide. This is exactly Tur's distinction. The statement there that people say, "These are so-and-so's beams," does not mean legal visual recognition by witnesses. It means a general local attribution based on the fact that the beams were found close to the known place of the flood and there was no report of another person's beams being swept away nearby. A flood of beams would create publicity, as Rashi explains. Thus in a nearby location the owner's later pursuit can be enough. In a distant place it cannot. Tur's text therefore requires no major emendation, except that one word should read "then" rather than "or." He first teaches that when the owners pursue, even after the property has reached another person's hand, the pursuit can prove ownership in the nearby setting. He then qualifies that even if pursuit identifies the objects, there is no obligation to return after actual despair. Therefore the case must be one in which, had the owners been present, they could have rescued immediately. In that setting they certainly did not despair. Even if they were absent but knew of the flood, we presume they did not despair because the items were likely to reach Jews nearby who would return them based on the later pursuit. If they did not know at all, later despair after the item came into the finder’s hand is despair without prior knowledge and does not help. Tur next speaks of a case where the object cannot be rescued until it reaches a distant place. In that setting later pursuit will no longer establish ownership in the eyes of distant holders. There he distinguishes. If the owners were present during the flood and did not pursue, they certainly despaired. If they did pursue, there is no despair, because they may expect to catch the property before it reaches someone else's hand. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 114 say that even according to the opinion that an ordinary theft produces presumed despair, active pursuit shows that the owner has not despaired. The same applies here. Rosh likewise indicates that all this concerns a place where many people are found. Therefore Tur says despair is established only when the owners were present and did not pursue. When the facts are unknown, there is no presumed despair. If witnesses can identify that the owners pursued and declared the beams theirs before the objects reached another person's possession, that declaration can operate as ordinary testimony to ownership. An ordinary person who claims ownership before the object comes into someone else's possession can be believed in this kind of dispute. A proof appears from the case of the ship where two people each say, "It is mine," and if a third person seizes it, he can be required to pay each claimant. Although neither has independent proof, the third cannot treat the property as though it belonged to neither. Before his seizure, each claimant's assertion creates a claim against him. But once the property has already reached another person's possession, a later claimant generally needs an identifying sign. Therefore in the distant-location case, later pursuit alone does not suffice. This also explains Rama. Where the item lacks an identifying sign, if the owners can save it, meaning they can rescue before it reaches a distant place because obstacles will cause it to come into the hands of nearby people who will return it based on the owners' later pursuit, there is no despair. Rama's phrase "and they pursue" refers to pursuit after the object has already reached another person's hand. His clause "or he is not there" means that even if the owner knew of the flood but did not pursue immediately, this still need not be despair because he can rely on rescue in the nearby place and on later pursuit. But if the owner stands by and does not pursue in a case where the property will otherwise reach a distant place, that is despair. An unexplained case is not despair because perhaps the owner did not know of the flood, and that would be despair without knowledge. This also resolves Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia 102 on Rashi's version, "because the owners despaired." Tosafot ask why the Gemara needed to challenge from the later clause concerning pursuit rather than from the opening clause itself. According to this explanation, under the initial assumption the Gemara thought the case concerned an object that could not be saved before reaching a distant place and had no identifying sign. There, if the owner knows of the flood and does not pursue, ordinary circumstances can indicate despair. Yet an unexplained case remains only despair without knowledge and is ineffective. The later clause teaches that if the owner actively pursues, even in a case where immediate rescue is impossible, there is no despair. The Gemara therefore challenges specifically from that clause. The answer establishes the case where rescue is possible, and the questioner then understands it as involving an identifying sign. The opening clause remains coherent: if rescue was easy and the owner did not pursue, that can indicate despair, while an unexplained case is not despair because even Rava agrees that despair without knowledge does not work when the item has an identifying sign. The Gemara then challenges from the later pursuit clause, because with an identifying sign even absence of pursuit should not matter. It answers that "can save" means save only with difficulty, such as by pursuing to a nearby location. Failure to pursue then indicates despair, while active pursuit preserves ownership. The baraita's formulation teaches an additional point: if the owner pursued and reached the object while it was still nearby, pursuit can establish the claim even without an identifying sign.
