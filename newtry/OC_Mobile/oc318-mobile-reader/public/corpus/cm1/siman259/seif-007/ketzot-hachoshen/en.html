Or by beit din's declaration of ownerlessness. Shach writes that contemporary practice requiring return of lost property even after despair is not necessarily an improper custom against Torah law. The generation's sages can enact such a rule, and the law of the kingdom can also support it. In my view the custom is not merely tolerable but upright. The Gemara says that even after despair one should return a lost object beyond the strict law, based on the duty to do what is right and good. Likewise, after despair plus change of possession, one who fears Heaven should return the value to the original owner. Therefore, when communal authorities enact compulsory return after despair, they are not creating a corrupt custom contrary to Torah. They are enforcing conduct the Torah itself commends beyond the strict measure of law. Mordechai cites authorities who even compel performance beyond the strict law where the person has the ability. This also explains Rama's ruling that if the finder is poor and the loser rich, the finder need not return after despair. If the obligation rested solely on law of the kingdom or beit din confiscation, rich and poor should be treated alike. The exemption shows that the core is enforcement of upright conduct beyond the strict law, which is demanded only according to a person's capacity. A poor person may rely on the strict written law.
