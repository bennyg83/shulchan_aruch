It is proper and upright to return it. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat 58, concerning merchandise confiscated because customs duties were evaded, where another person preceded the owner and bought it cheaply from the customs official and wished to keep it for himself. He writes that if the original owner was attempting to repurchase it, there is no doubt that the buyer must return it. Even if it had truly belonged to the customs authority, this would fall under the rule of one who encroaches upon a poor man who is pursuing a particular opportunity, and according to Rabbeinu Tam the purchaser must restore it. In the present case, even if the buyer did not actually know that the owner was trying to redeem it, it is customary for an owner to seek to redeem his confiscated merchandise, and this is treated as though the buyer knew. Although Shulchan Aruch, siman 237, states that purchasing something at an unusually low price resembles finding an ownerless object, so that according to the first view the rule of the poor man pursuing the opportunity does not apply, the Shach there cites Ramban in disagreement. Moreover, here all would agree, because the reason a bargain purchase resembles a found object is that everyone pursues it and no one person has a greater connection to it than another. Here, however, the former owner plainly has a greater claim and reason to pursue this particular merchandise than others. For the same reason, the distinction there between a price already fixed and one not yet fixed does not apply. There, until the price is fixed both parties stand equally with respect to the item, but not here. Independently of this, even if one wished to distinguish the cases because a bargain purchase resembles a found object or because the price had not yet been fixed, here the goods were originally his. Even though he certainly despaired of recovering them, the case is no better than property rescued from the surge of the sea, concerning which Rama, siman 259:7, writes that it is proper and upright to return it. The Shach there explains that where the finder is wealthy, beit din may compel return by virtue of communal enactment and local custom. In the present case, it appears that one need not distinguish between rich and poor. Rama likewise writes in simanim 356 and 368 that the current custom is to return stolen property even after despair and a subsequent change of possession. That enactment applies here as well. One cannot distinguish by saying that the enactment applies only to theft or robbery, where the property came into the taker's hands unlawfully, whereas here the owner violated the law of the sovereign and the goods were lawfully confiscated. The sovereign's intent was plainly that the goods enter the royal treasury. The customs official's taking and concealing them for himself was not part of that law, and the matter therefore depends only upon despair. Accordingly, the buyer must return them under the enactment of the generation, as in siman 259. Indeed, the practice of upright people is that even when confiscated property is auctioned by public announcement, if the owners are known, purchasers buy it on their behalf and restore it to them. If the purchaser seeks compensation for his effort, beit din should assess an appropriate amount under siman 264:5, Rama and Shach there. Thus the purchaser should voluntarily act in this manner to fulfill, "You shall do what is upright and good"; if he refuses, he may be judged under the communal enactment. Beit Ephraim 59 further discusses a case where the original owner demanded that the purchaser accept a ban concerning how much he had paid. He concludes that the matter depends upon the judgment of beit din. If the amount claimed is customary and there is no reason to suspect that he is inflating the purchase price merely because the other says, "I do not believe you," beit din may act beyond the strict line of law, accept his truthful assertion, and dispense with a direct ban. Nevertheless, if the owner wishes to pronounce a general ban in beit din, not in the purchaser's presence, upon anyone who seeks to take his property without payment, or after paying wishes to pronounce a ban upon whoever robbed him and does not return it, the purchaser cannot prevent him, as stated in siman 71:6, where even an express clause of credibility does not prevent such a general ban. If, however, there is genuine grounds for suspicion, such as a plausible claim of permitted self-interest, beit din may require the purchaser to accept the ban. He has no cause for complaint, for just as he fulfills "You shall do what is upright and good," he should also fulfill "You shall be clean before Hashem and Israel."
