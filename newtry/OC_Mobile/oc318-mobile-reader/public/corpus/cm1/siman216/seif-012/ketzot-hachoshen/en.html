Likewise, if he retained two trees. The Gemara says that even according to Rabbi Akiva, who holds that a seller ordinarily sells generously, if a seller sells the land but retains two trees, he retains land beneath them. The reason is that the trees weaken the land. If no land had been retained for them, the purchaser could tell him to remove his trees and leave. Tosafot asks why this proves a continuing land right. Rashi explains the purchaser's statement as applying when the trees eventually dry out. But perhaps that is all the purchaser can demand, while during the trees' lifetime the seller may leave them standing even without having retained land.
Ramban records two explanations of the phrase, "Remove your trees." One view says it is not literal during their lifetime. A purchaser of two trees does not ordinarily acquire the soil, and when those trees die he has no land in which to plant replacements. Rashbam explains similarly. Another view distinguishes between an express sale of trees and a seller who sells the land while simply leaving trees behind. If one expressly sells "this tree," he sold a living tree, not timber, so the purchaser cannot demand removal during its life. But where the seller sold the entire land and merely retained the trees by implication, if no soil was retained with them then he can be understood as having retained only timber. In that case the purchaser could demand removal even during their life. There is still a question. The argument proves only that the seller retained the trees as trees and not merely as wood. How does it prove that he retained enough land to plant replacements after they die? The answer follows the unresolved Talmudic law of one who buys two trees. The Gemara leaves doubt whether two purchased trees include land. A purchaser cannot extract land from the original owner because of that doubt. But where the seller retained two trees, he remains the original possessor of the soil. The same doubt therefore works in his favor. Since three trees certainly include land and two are doubtful, the seller retains the relevant land through his prior possession. Thus the statement, "Otherwise he could tell him to remove your trees," establishes that the seller retained them with the status of trees rather than mere timber. Once that is established, the ordinary doubt concerning two trees applies, and the original owner's possession gives him the soil as well. This also explains why the Gemara speaks specifically about retaining two trees. With one tree, even if it is retained as a living tree, there is no corresponding doubtful right to land for replacement.
The Gemara challenges the rule from Rabbi Shimon, who says that one who consecrates a field consecrates only the grafted carob and the trunk of the sycamore, and the baraita explains that because they draw nourishment from consecrated land. If the owner had truly retained land for himself, the trees would be drawing nourishment from his own land. Tosafot asks that even without Rav Huna's rule, the seller of trees retains land beneath them for the duration of their life, so the trees should still be considered to draw from his own land. According to Ramban, the question is resolved. Without Rav Huna, one could say that the seller retained the trees merely as timber and retained only the limited right needed while they lived. Rav Huna teaches, however, that when two trees are retained, the seller did not retain them merely as timber. He retained them as trees. Once that is so, the law of retained trees is analogous to one who retained three trees, and land is retained with them. Accordingly, even with one tree, at minimum he retains the land needed for the tree throughout its lifetime. This resolves Tosafot's difficulty.
