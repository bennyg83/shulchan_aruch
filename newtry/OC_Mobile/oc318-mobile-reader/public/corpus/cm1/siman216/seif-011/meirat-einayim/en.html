He has half of all the land. The Maggid Mishneh explains the Rambam's reason both here and in the following law concerning reservation of two trees. Whenever a seller reserves property for himself, he is presumed to reserve generously, so we elevate his retained right by one level. If he sold three trees to another in his field, the buyer would acquire only the land beneath and between them and around them to the extent needed for harvesting. If the seller reserves the trees for himself, the Rambam elevates his reservation and gives him half the field in which they stand. If he sold two trees to another, the buyer would acquire no land at all. If he reserves two trees for himself, we elevate his right one level and say that he retained the land necessary for them, beneath and between them. The Rambam's added reason, that otherwise the purchaser of the land could say, “Uproot your trees and go,” does not explain why he has half the field. The minimum land necessary for the trees would suffice for that. Rather, the Rambam uses the Gemara's wording to reject Rav Zevid, who would give him no land at all, not even the right to plant beneath them or replace them after they dry. Rav Pappa answers that if so the landowner could demand removal of the trees even before they dry. The alternative opinion disagrees with the Rambam's extension to half the field, but agrees that even when the seller reserves only two trees, he retains all land necessary for them, beneath and between them, because he reserved for himself. See the Perishah for another explanation of the Rambam.
