And this one took possession of the trees, etc., with half the land. Above, at the end of siman 141, the Mechaber rules that where one person possessed the trees and another possessed the land, each claiming the whole, the tree possessor receives the trees together with the land beneath, between, and around them sufficient for harvesting. That too is from the Rambam. Here, however, the Rambam and Mechaber give the tree owner half the land. It is difficult to say that “half the land” is imprecise and merely repeats the Gemara's expression, which Rashbam interprets as the land beneath, between, and around the trees, because above in seif 11 the Mechaber already followed the Rambam's understanding that “half” means literally half. The Maggid Mishneh also questions the Rambam on this. One can reconcile with difficulty. Here it is certain that the seller sold the trees to one purchaser just as he sold the land to the other. The tree purchaser may therefore say: Since you sold me the trees in a manner that necessarily gives me some land for their needs, just as you reserved land for me you reserved half. Above, by contrast, the two litigants come only with claims of possession and each says the whole belongs to him. It is possible that the land possessor tells the truth and owns everything, trees included. Since the tree possessor has witnesses only to his consumption of the tree fruit, we give him at least the trees, but no more land than what his possession necessarily supports. The remainder should presumptively go to the person who possessed the land. Rashbam holds that even here the tree owner receives only the land beneath, between, and around the trees. Ri and the Rosh hold that here he does not even receive those broader land rights. Since the seller conveyed to two different buyers, we say he sold generously to each what he explicitly named. The land purchaser therefore receives the entire land, while the tree purchaser gets only the space necessary for the trees themselves, enough that the landowner cannot say, “Uproot your trees and go.” He must also allow space to gather fruit and replacement planting when the trees dry, but the tree owner has no independent right to sow beneath or between them as in an ordinary sale of three trees. The reason is that when the seller conveys to two different people, there is no basis to strengthen one at the expense of the other. This can even make the tree purchaser stronger than one who purchased only two trees from a seller who retained the land, because there the purchaser of only two trees acquires no land right to replant after they dry. Here, where the seller sold the land to one person and the two trees to another, he is presumed to have sold generously to both, so the tree owner may replace them when they dry. It is surprising that Rama cites only Ri's opinion here and not Rashbam's, and above at the end of siman 141 he does not cite Ri's opinion as he does here. Tosafot, the Rosh, and Tur present Ri's view in both places. Perhaps Rama distinguishes: here, where the land was certainly sold to one purchaser, it is more reasonable to say the land purchaser receives all of it except what is strictly necessary for the trees. Above, where the truth of the competing claims is unknown and it is possible the land possessor never purchased anything, Rashbam or the Rambam's broader allocation to the tree possessor is more plausible.
