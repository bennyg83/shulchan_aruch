If there are only palm trees, etc. The novelty is that even palm trees and vines are included under the general term "trees" when there are no other trees in the field. But where ordinary trees are also present in the field, we say he reserved only the ordinary trees. If the field contains only palm trees and vines, vines fall more readily under the ordinary term "trees" than palm trees do. Therefore we say that he reserved the vines for himself, but not the palm trees.
Likewise other trees. This is somewhat difficult, for it appears to follow all the more so. It is strained to say that it was written because of the concluding clause, to teach that even with ordinary trees he reserved only those not bent down by the yoke. It therefore appeared to me that "likewise other trees" means other important trees, such as fig, pomegranate, or olive trees. The ruling would be that if such ordinary trees were present together with the palm trees and vines, we would not say that he reserved the palm trees and vines, just as with ordinary trees. But now that no such other trees are present, his intent certainly included these and he reserved them. In the Perishah I quoted the Gemara and Tur, whose wording somewhat resists this explanation.
Likewise with palm trees and other trees. Meaning, just as where there are palm trees and vines we say he sold the palm trees and reserved the vines, so if there are ordinary trees and palm trees we say he sold the palm trees. We do not say that he reserved both the inferior ordinary trees and, all the more so, the superior palm trees, as explained above.
Because a seller sells generously. Meaning, therefore we do not say he reserved both the vines and palm trees. Wherever we can say that he did not reserve something, we presume that he certainly sold it.
He said, "except for this carob tree." Meaning, a grafted carob. This rule applies specifically to that case, because even if he had not reserved it, a grafted carob would ordinarily not be included in the sale of the field, as the Tur and Mechaber write in the preceding siman. Therefore his expressly reserving it is superfluous and is interpreted as reserving also a right of passage to that carob. Ordinarily he would not have such a right of passage, as the Tur and Mechaber write there at the end of seif 5. But if he said regarding an ungrafted carob, "except for this carob," there is no superfluous language. He has no right of passage to it, and the other ungrafted carobs are not reserved.
That one ascends with a rope. A palm tree is tall, its fruit is near the top, and it has no side branches by which one can climb to gather the fruit. One must ascend with a rope to collect it. The ruling is that if it is not so tall, it is not included in the ordinary term "palm tree" for purposes of the reservation and is sold. The Tur places this law in the case where the seller says, "except for my trees," and the field contains only palm trees, so that we say he reserved them for himself. The Tur states as an additional novelty that even then, any tree that does not require climbing by rope is sold. Even though he did not reserve them using the term "palm tree" but rather the general term "tree," an ordinary tree is not so tall. See the Perishah.
That the yoke does not bend it. Their practice was to plow around the trees. If the yoke bends the tree and the ox can continue on its path without the tree obstructing it, the tree is not considered significant. With ordinary trees it is not appropriate to use the criterion of climbing with a rope, because they have side branches by which one climbs. With a palm tree, by contrast, even if the yoke bends it, so long as it requires climbing by rope it is significant, and we say his intention included it when he wrote "except for the palm tree."
