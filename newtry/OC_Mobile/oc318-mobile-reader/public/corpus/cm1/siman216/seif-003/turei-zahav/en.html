(Seif 8, in the gloss.) The buyer must bring proof. It appears to me that this is certainly so where the parties disagree whether the trees were sold at one time or successively. If the seller says, “I sold them to you one after another,” and the buyer says, “I bought them together,” the buyer must bring proof. This resolves the objection of the Rashba and Ritva against the Ramban. They asked why the buyer must bring proof, since it is known that trees were sold to him and we now see branches extending over the ground, which should testify to the sale of the soil under the rule, “here they are found and here they were.” According to what I have written, the matter is clear. The seller is believed according to the Ramban because he has a migo: he could have said that he sold the trees one after another. In that case even the Rashba and Ritva would agree that the buyer must bring proof, because where the uncertainty concerns the essence of the sale, the rule “here they are found and here they were” does not apply. Nor is this a migo used to extract property, for land remains in the possession of its owner, and it is the buyer who seeks to take it from the seller. This also resolves the Sema's question in seif katan 32 why the buyer must cut the branches. Since the seller will be believed if a dispute later arises, why require cutting now? The Sema's first answer follows the Nimukei Yosef. His second answer is that there may be the uncertainty described in seif 9, where the seller says, “I sold you two trees,” but roots later emerged under the soil, in which case the buyer is believed because the matter is uncommon. The Nimukei Yosef, however, writes explicitly that even there the seller is believed. Yet the Nimukei Yosef's own reason, that the seller does not wish to descend into litigation, is also difficult. At a minimum, he cannot avoid litigation entirely, because the buyer who possesses the tree may claim that he bought it together with the soil, as stated at the end of siman 141. Although the seller can protest, he still cannot avoid litigation, and it is strained to distinguish one type of litigation from another. According to my explanation, however, the matter is clear. The seller's principal credibility rests on his migo, and he does not wish to rely on that migo from the outset, lest witnesses appear who know that he had dealings with this buyer only once. He would then be unable to claim that he sold the trees successively. The buyer must therefore cut the branches. This appears correct in my humble opinion.
