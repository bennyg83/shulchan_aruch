See Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 26, who writes that even if the purchaser wishes to obtain substitute palm trees elsewhere and the seller does not wish to provide them, the purchaser cannot compel the seller to supply trees elsewhere. This is clear from siman 223, seif 4 in the gloss, concerning ona'ah. So long as the purchaser can retract, the seller likewise can retract because the sale was never finally completed. This is true even if the purchaser wishes to uphold the transaction as it stands despite the ona'ah, and all the more so if he wishes merely to recover the amount of the ona'ah, in which case the seller can also annul the sale. Avodat HaGershuni further discusses a deed of sale from Reuven to Shimon stating: "I sold him such and such house and the other structures built in the courtyard until the inner wooden house. That inner house I did not sell him, but it remains mine. The large animal stall in the courtyard, situated beneath the inner house toward such and such side, likewise remains in my possession from the depth of the earth," and so forth. Beneath the inner house there was also a small stall adjacent to the large one. Shimon claimed that the small stall was included in the sale. He argued, first, that because the deed states that the large stall remains with Reuven, it implies that only the large stall was reserved and not the small one. Second, the deed says "and the other structures built in the courtyard," although there were no other structures in the courtyard, so the phrase must refer to the small stall. Third, the small stall lies within the boundaries described in the deed. Reuven claimed that he had not sold any stall. Avodat HaGershuni replied that Shimon's arguments have no substance. The first argument fails because Bava Batra 70a asks concerning one who says, "except for such and such carob tree," and it is clear that he does not thereby transfer the other carob trees. The proof is that if one says, "My field is sold to you except for such and such other field," the expressly excluded field does not imply that all other separately excluded property is included. Explicitly naming one item outside the sale does not surrender one's rights in other items also outside it. As to Shimon's second argument, that the clause "and the other structures built in the courtyard" must refer to the small stall because no other structure exists, this also has no force, because the deed immediately limits the clause by saying "until the inner wooden house." In legal documents, "until" generally excludes the endpoint itself. This resembles one who says, "I sell you land with palm trees." If there are no palm trees, the sale may be mistaken. Yet here the purchaser knew and saw that there were no additional structures in the courtyard, so even a claim of mistaken sale is unavailable, as Shulchan Aruch, siman 218, seif 16, rules concerning one who sells a field that the purchaser knows and says it measures two hundred units when it actually measures only one hundred fifty. The purchaser accepts it because he knew its true extent, and the seller's larger figure is understood as praise of the property, meaning it is worth as much as another field of two hundred. Rashbam explains likewise, and Tur agrees. Thus, where the purchaser knew that there were no other structures, he accepted the known facts and understood the phrase as sales language, unless one can establish some special distinction between oral wording and written wording, for which there is no source. The third argument concerning the boundaries also fails automatically. Once we have established that the wording of the sale does not include any stall, the inclusion of the stall within the outer boundaries is treated as an expansion of the boundary description, not as a transfer of the stall. Although where the deed says that nothing was reserved we sometimes do not say that the boundaries were merely expanded, that is only where some people use the same name for the disputed tract.
