(Seif 5.) One who says to another. According to the Rambam, a sale described as “land and palms” is not two separate sales, contrary to the Sema in seif katan 14. The Rambam disagrees with the Rashbam, who regards them as two sales, so that even if one component fails, such as where the seller owns no palms, the sale of the land remains valid. According to the Rambam, the Gemara is not discussing that issue here at all. It follows that whenever one sells two items together for a single aggregate price and it is discovered that one was not in his possession, reason dictates that the sale of the other is also void. The buyer can say: Since I combined them for one price, I regarded them as one transaction, and their combined value was important to me; I am unwilling to take one item at the valuation others would assign it. This is especially true of land, to which the law of overreaching does not apply. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes here, according to the Rambam, that the buyer may say, “I would not have bought one without the other.” Rabbeinu the Tur similarly writes, according to the Rambam, that if the seller does not provide two palms, the purchase of the land is also void. One may ask why it suffices for the seller to purchase two palms elsewhere. At the time of sale they were at least not in his possession, so the buyer should be able to withdraw from the purchase of the land as well. One cannot answer that because palms exist in the world they are considered in his possession, for then, whenever he owns no palms, the sale should remain valid and the buyer should compel him to buy palms elsewhere. Why should the choice rest with the seller? The Kesef Mishneh answers that insofar as palms exist in the world, the transaction is not treated as concerning something that has not yet come into existence, but insofar as they are not in the seller's possession, it is treated as a sale of something not yet in his possession. Therefore, when the seller says, “I will fulfill what I promised, and you must uphold the sale,” the law is with him. We must add that the case concerns palms that are reasonably available for purchase. Otherwise there is no sale concerning them at all, as stated at the beginning of siman 211 and in siman 209 regarding things not in one's possession. Nor can one object that the original question returns, and the seller should be compelled to purchase the palms, as the Rambam himself rules in siman 209, seif 3, that he must provide the item at the market price. The answer is that palms are not very readily available. The seller may therefore purchase them in order to uphold the land sale, but if he does not wish to provide palms at all, the buyer certainly cannot be compelled to accept the land alone. All this follows the Rambam, who views the transaction as one sale. The Rashbam, however, views it as two sales. A case came before us in which one sold another two seats in a synagogue for one sum, and it later became clear that he owned only one seat. We ruled that this is the dispute between the Rambam and the Rashbam just described. Since we lack authority to extract money contrary to the Rambam, the entire sale was void. This also follows from siman 182, seif 8, which states that the buyer may withdraw and say, “I wished to purchase the two se'ah together.” This appears correct in my humble opinion.
