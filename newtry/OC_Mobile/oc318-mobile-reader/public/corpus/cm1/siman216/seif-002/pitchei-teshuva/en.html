Land and palm trees. See Baer Heitev, through his statement that if the seller does not provide two additional palm trees, the purchaser can annul the entire sale. See Kesef Mishneh, Hilchot Mechirah, chapter 24, halachah 13, cited in part by Taz with altered wording. Kesef Mishneh asks: if the palm trees are considered existing property even though they were not in the seller's possession, then the sale should stand and the seller should be obligated to purchase the trees for the buyer. If they are considered something not yet acquired because they were not in his possession, then what helps when he later acquires them, since at the time of sale they were beyond his ownership? He answers that the case combines both features. Because the trees were not in the seller's possession they resemble future property, but because they already existed in the world they also resemble existing property. Therefore, as against the purchaser they are treated as existing property, while as against the seller they are treated as something not yet acquired. See there. Shaar Mishpat cites this and strongly questions it. In his view the original difficulty of Kesef Mishneh does not arise. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, in stating that if the seller wishes to acquire two palm trees he may preserve the sale, do not mean that he can compel the purchaser to take the trees. Rather, all of these rulings concern the land. Since the seller can make good the palm tree component, the land sale remains effective. He concludes that this is clear. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 134, writes similarly and suggests that perhaps this was Kesef Mishneh's own intent. See the end of his responsum. Regarding Baer Heitev's citation from Taz, concerning a practical case in which they ruled that the entire sale was void because they lacked power to extract money against Rambam, Taz himself concludes that this is also implied by siman 182, seif 8, where the purchaser may say, "I wished to purchase only both seah together." Shaar Mishpat questions Taz's proof, because the rule in siman 182 is from the Rosh, whereas here the Rosh agrees with Rashbam, as the Tur cites him. The Rosh would otherwise contradict himself. Rather, one must distinguish between the sale of two separate items and the sale of one larger parcel of land, where size may be material. Still, two seats in a synagogue are comparable to two separate items, as stated above, siman 175, seif katan 23, and the practical ruling indeed depends upon this dispute. See there.
