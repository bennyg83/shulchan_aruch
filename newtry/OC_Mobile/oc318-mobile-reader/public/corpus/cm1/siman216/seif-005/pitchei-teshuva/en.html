Land and palm trees. See Baer Heitev, which states that if the seller does not provide the additional two palm trees, the buyer may cancel the entire transaction. Kesef Mishneh, Hilchot Mechirah 24:13, asks: if the palm trees are considered something already in existence despite not being in the seller's possession, the sale should remain valid and the seller should be obligated to acquire the palm trees for the buyer. If, however, because they were not in his possession they are treated as something not yet in existence, how can the seller's later acquisition of palm trees cure the original defect? Kesef Mishneh answers that the status is mixed. Because the palm trees were not in the seller's possession, the transaction has one aspect of property not yet available; because the palm trees themselves already existed in the world, it also has the character of existing property. Therefore, as against the buyer they are treated as existing, while as against the seller they have the limitation of property not presently in his possession. Shaar Mishpat challenges this explanation and writes that the original question does not arise. When Rambam and Shulchan Aruch say that if the seller wishes to acquire two palm trees the sale stands, the meaning is not that he can compel the buyer to take the palm trees. Rather, all these rules concern whether the sale of the land remains in force. If the seller supplies the missing palm trees, the land transaction is upheld. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 134, writes similarly and suggests that Kesef Mishneh may ultimately have intended this point. Baer Heitev also cites the Taz's practical ruling that where the promised items are not supplied, the entire sale may be void, and the Taz points to siman 182:8, where a buyer can say that he wished to buy both measures together or not at all. Shaar Mishpat questions the comparison, because the rule in siman 182 derives from the Rosh, whereas here the Rosh follows Rashbam's approach. He therefore distinguishes between a sale of two separate items and a sale of one large parcel where size itself is material. Nevertheless, two synagogue seats are considered two distinct items, and in such a case the ruling truly depends on the dispute among the authorities. See Shaar Mishpat, Netivot HaMishpat to siman 182:8, Divrei Mishpat here, Responsa R. Moshe Rothenburg, Orach Chaim, siman 10, and Mishneh LaMelech at the end of Hilchot Zechiyah U'Matanah.
And if not, it is a mistaken transaction and is rescinded. See Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 26. Even if the purchaser wishes to accept palm trees elsewhere and the seller refuses, the purchaser cannot compel the seller to provide palm trees in another place, as explained in siman 223. Regarding onaah, so long as the purchaser retains a right to rescind, the seller likewise may rescind because the transaction has not been finally concluded, even where the purchaser wishes to uphold the sale despite the onaah, and certainly where the purchaser demands reimbursement of the overcharge. The responsum also discusses a deed by which Reuven sold Shimon specified houses and "the other buildings in the courtyard up to the inner wooden house," expressly reserving that inner house and the large pen beneath it. A smaller pen stood beside the large one. Shimon claimed the smaller pen was included, arguing that only the large pen was expressly reserved, that the deed mentioned "other buildings" although no other building existed, and that the smaller pen lay within the stated boundaries. Reuven denied selling any pen. The responsum rejects Shimon's claims. Expressly excluding one item that would not otherwise be included does not imply that all similar unmentioned items are conveyed, as is evident from Bava Batra 70a concerning "except for this carob." The phrase "other buildings" is limited by the deed's immediately following words "up to the inner wooden house," and in deeds "up to" ordinarily excludes the endpoint. Although a promise to sell "land with palm trees" where no palm trees exist may create a mistaken sale, here the purchaser knew that no such buildings stood in the courtyard. This is analogous to siman 218:16, where a purchaser who knows the field accepts it despite an inaccurate statement of its measure, because the statement may have been mere sales praise. There is no basis to distinguish speech from writing. Finally, stated boundaries cannot enlarge the sale where the property in question is not included by the operative language itself. Thus Shimon's arguments fail. See there.
