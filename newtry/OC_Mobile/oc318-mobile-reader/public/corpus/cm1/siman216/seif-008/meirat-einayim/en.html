If they grew and produced shoots, etc. The Tur writes: if the branches grew and spread beyond the area of land belonging to him, he must cut them. This comes from the Mishnah in chapter HaMokher Et HaSefinah, which the Maggid Mishneh cites on Rambam in chapter 27 of Hilchot Mechirah. The Tur concludes: Rabbi Yonah writes that even if the branches grew only within the space needed for one who harvests with his basket, he must cut them, because if he does not prune them he may eventually establish a claim, by virtue of the additional branches, to another harvesting space outside the branches. This can also explain Rambam and the Mechaber. Kesef Mishneh, however, writes that the case is where they grew and spread beyond the harvesting space, for otherwise he should not cut them, since he is entitled to ascend the tree and gather its fruit. Rambam's wording does not compel that interpretation. Although Rambam writes that he must not reduce the owner of the field's path, the Tur likewise says that the branches grew beyond the amount of land belonging to him, and nevertheless cites Rabbi Yonah that the case can include branches still within the harvesting space. This requirement to prune applies specifically to one who purchased three trees. One who purchased only two need not cut them, as the Tur and Rama write in the next seif. There I will explain the reason.
The purchaser must bring proof. The seller is the possessor of the land. We do not say that if the trees had grown the seller would immediately have compelled the purchaser to cut them, because perhaps the seller did not notice the growth at that time.
Even from the roots. Meaning, growth from beneath the ground. This is the Tur's wording.
For he owns land. Meaning, everything that grows from that land belongs to him. This excludes a case where he purchased only two trees, as explained below.
