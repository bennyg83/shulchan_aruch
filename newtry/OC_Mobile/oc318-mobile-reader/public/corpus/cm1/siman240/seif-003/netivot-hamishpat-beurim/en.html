Wherever their judgment inclines. If it becomes established that the documents were not delivered on the date they were written but on later dates, then according to the authorities cited by Sema and Shach who hold that witnesses acquire through their signatures, both recipients can acquire retroactively at the end of the document date. Tosafot explain that the effect occurs only at the end of the day. Ramban likewise indicates this. If both deeds bear the same date, they therefore acquire equally and divide. According to authorities who reject the rule that witnesses acquire through their signatures, and we do not know which delivery came first, the rule is shuda, judicial discretion, where the priority cannot normally be clarified. But if we know the two documents were delivered on two different days and simply do not know which came first, the rule changes. Bava Batra 35 distinguishes shuda from kol d'alim gavar on the basis of whether the matter can realistically be clarified. Ramban explains that even though, in theory, witnesses could testify which deed was handed over first, where both deliveries occurred in the same day witnesses do not ordinarily focus on precise hours. Therefore the matter is treated as incapable of clarification. If the dispute is over different days, however, it is the kind of matter witnesses ordinarily remember and can clarify. When neither party is in possession and both make definite claims, the rule is kol d'alim gavar. If both claims are uncertain, the property is divided. Tur appears to say that where there were two kinyan sudar transactions before the same set of witnesses and we do not know which was first, the rule is shuda. This is difficult. Even if witnesses do not ordinarily focus on hours, they certainly remember which person received the first kinyan and which the second. That should be capable of clarification and therefore not a shuda case. Perhaps Tur speaks where it is already known that the witnesses died and therefore no clarification is now possible.
