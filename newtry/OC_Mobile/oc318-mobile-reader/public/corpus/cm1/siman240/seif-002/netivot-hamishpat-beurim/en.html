All the produce he consumed. See Sema, who asks: Rambam follows Rafram in Ketubot 44 and treats the second deed as an admission that the first is defective. Why then does he not also state the additional consequence mentioned there, that all other documents in the holder's possession bearing those same witnesses are invalid? In my view the answer is as follows. Whenever the admission can be explained by a defect in the deed while preserving the witnesses' personal credibility, we uphold the witnesses. We seek any reasonable explanation that keeps witnesses within their chazakah of fitness. Here, however, if the first deed was genuinely written and properly delivered, it would be valid. Since the holder's conduct is treated as an admission that he did not acquire at the first date, it seems to follow that the witnesses wrote falsely. One might suggest another possibility. Witnesses may write a deed for the seller even when the buyer is not present. Perhaps they wrote the first deed in Nisan and delivered it to the seller, but the seller did not actually sell until Tishrei, when the second deed was written. He later handed the first deed to the buyer for some improper purpose. The witnesses would then be innocent. That explanation does not work according to Abaye, who holds that witnesses acquire for the recipient through their signatures. Under that view a deed written in Nisan and later delivered would retroactively acquire from the deed date. If there were a normal possibility that such a deed later became an invalid antedated document, witnesses would be forbidden to write it initially. Therefore, once the holder is treated as admitting the first deed is invalid, the only explanation under Abaye is that it was false from the outset, which would disqualify the witnesses. Rif and Rambam, however, do not rule like Abaye. They follow Rav Asi and do not say that the witnesses acquire through their signatures. As Ran explains, even though witnesses ordinarily should not write a loan deed without the lender present, if they mistakenly wrote one contrary to Rav Asi they do not become invalid witnesses. Accordingly, here too the holder's admission that the first deed is invalid need not disqualify the witnesses. Perhaps they wrote the first deed for the seller without the buyer, mistakenly relying on the wrong rule, and the deed later became improperly antedated while the witnesses themselves remained kosher. This explains why Rif and Rambam omit the consequence that the witnesses are disqualified.
He returns them. According to Rambam, as explained above, the reason is that producing the second deed constitutes an admission that the first was not legally effective. One might ask why, with a gift deed, we do not say that the first deed was lost and a second deed was later written from the later date. Siman 239 allows a second gift deed in appropriate circumstances. Perhaps he later found the first deed and therefore now has both, with both truthful. Even though siman 239 ordinarily restricts writing a second deed from a later date out of concern that the recipient may have sold the property in the interim, that concern disappears where the donor knowingly participates. Siman 43, Shach seif katan 30, says even a later-dated deed can be written with the transferor's knowledge. The answer is that once the first deed has now been found and lies in the holder's hand, why does he bring the second deed to beit din at all? Producing the later deed despite holding the first functions as an admission that he lacks valid proof from the earlier date. Still, perhaps he sold the property back to the seller after the first date and then repurchased it at the second date. Then all produce he consumed might have been earned before the resale. We must say that because he does not make that claim and instead says the property never left his ownership from the first date onward, he is treated as admitting that he never resold and repurchased. One who says, "I never borrowed," is treated as saying, "I did not repay." Likewise, if we ourselves were to posit a resale, his own statement would imply he never reacquired, and he would lose even the land. We therefore limit the damaging effect of his admission to invalidating the first transaction date. According to Tur, Sema says only produce still extant is recovered, not produce already consumed. Tur follows the explanation that by taking the second deed the holder waived rights under the first. Tosafot hold that such waiver can affect even the body of the field, and Tur follows them. Waiver can therefore remove his claim to produce still existing, but it does not create a personal debt to repay produce already consumed. This law assumes that both deeds had already been established in beit din before the holder gave his explanation for having both. If he produces both at once together with a plausible explanation, he can be believed through a migo. If the seller admits that the first deed is genuine, the purchaser certainly keeps even produce still extant. This is not like one who claims wheat and the defendant admits barley, because there the barley owner is the defendant and possessor and the claimant's admission harms his claim. Here the produce lies in the purchaser's field and the purchaser is possessor, so we follow the seller's admission. If the purchaser dies and the heirs produce both deeds, it appears we presume that the property was sold back and then repurchased rather than automatically invalidating either deed.
The donor or seller. At first glance this implies that even if the seller or donor says the first deed is genuine while the purchaser says the opposite, the purchaser is believed and the seller must pay the land tax. This is difficult. What proof do we have from the seller's conduct that the first deed was false rather than the second? Even if accepting a new kinyan were itself an admission, the seller can presumably offer an explanation why he accepted a new kinyan. Why should he not be believed through a migo that he could say the second deed was false, especially where both are authenticated? It is strained to explain that the case concerns only a seller or donor who is actually taking the produce from the first date to the second, so he obviously bears the tax. That would teach little. The ruling must include a case with no produce, where the tax itself is disputed. It therefore appears that the case concerns the king demanding tax from the person named in the first deed for all prior years. The seller then seeks reimbursement from the purchaser. Since there is uncertainty which deed reflects the true period of ownership, the person seeking to extract money must prove his claim. According to Tur, who follows the view that the second deed represents waiver while both deeds remain substantively true, the purchaser should bear the tax. The waiver operates only against the purchaser's own property claims. Land tax is not something he can retroactively shift to the seller merely through waiver, just as produce already consumed is treated differently. A further question arises where both parties admit that the land was sold, later resold to the original seller, and then sold again, but they dispute the date of the intermediate resale. For example, the first deed is from 1 Nisan and the second from 3 Tishrei. The seller says the purchaser sold it back on 2 Nisan, while the purchaser says he did not resell until 29 Elul. Who owns the produce of the intervening months? We follow possession, like the law of exchanging a cow for a donkey in siman 223. Even there, the birth time can be known while the sale time is uncertain, and we still ask in whose possession the disputed value stands. Here likewise the dispute is whether the produce arose before or after the resale. The seller cannot say he has a migo that he could deny the first deed, because ordinarily we presume a sale followed by a resale, as explained above. If the produce is lying in a neutral place, it should remain with the purchaser as the prior certain owner of the field, comparable to the original owner of the cow who is considered the prior owner with respect to the offspring. As for land tax, whoever is presently assessed by the king must pay it. If he seeks reimbursement from the other party, the burden of proof rests on him.
