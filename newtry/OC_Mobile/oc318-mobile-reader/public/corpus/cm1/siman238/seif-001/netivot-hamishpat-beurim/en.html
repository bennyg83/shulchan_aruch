He transferred the field to him through a kinyan. This means that he says he wishes to transfer it to him through a deed, and therefore a kinyan is needed. If he merely admitted that he had already sold it, no new kinyan is needed, as Mechaber concludes. At first glance this is difficult from siman 198, seif 3, in the gloss, that a kinyan made in the buyer's absence does not help in a sale because a sale is not automatically a benefit to the buyer. If the seller can retract, we should fear that he will sell to another in the interim and only later deliver the deed to the first buyer. Taz in siman 184 and siman 190 goes even further, holding that later consent may not revive the kinyan because the sudar has already returned to its owner. As I wrote in siman 195, the case must therefore be where the seller told the witnesses, "Acquire for the buyer." This is analogous to siman 235, seif 23: when he tells witnesses to acquire on the buyer's behalf, the option lies with the buyer and not with the seller. That is certainly a benefit for the buyer, and the kinyan works according to all opinions. Sema, seif katan 12, writes that according to authorities who hold that a deed with an acquisition clause may be written even without a separate kinyan, it can be written here as well. Although sale is not ordinarily a pure benefit, the present case involves an express instruction to the witnesses to acquire on the buyer's behalf, and therefore it is considered beneficial.
Or he testified concerning himself. This does not mean only that he testified that the land had been acquired through a particular valid mode. Even if he simply admitted, "I already sold it," we presume he sold it in an effective manner, as explained in siman 60, seif 10, in the gloss. Sema, seif katan 3, says the admission also includes receipt of the purchase money. In my view, if land was already transferred through another valid mode while the money remained unpaid, even if the money was paid only much later, the purchaser can collect against encumbered property from the original sale date where appropriate, because a sale before witnesses creates public notice and the lien attaches immediately; the purchase price is simply an oral debt owed by the buyer. The same applies where the seller admits that the field had already been acquired through a valid mode. If the buyer later accepts that admission, he acquires retroactively through hoda'ah. Because that retroactive acquisition is beneficial to the buyer and the choice rests with him, the transaction is like a sale made before witnesses and can carry the usual consequences even before actual payment. Shach, seif katan 1, rejects Rambam's view that acquisition deeds are unnecessary in sale. In my view Rambam follows his teacher Ri Migash, who holds that a deed should not be written for the seller until he admits that he already received the money. He speaks in a place where the custom is to write only an evidentiary deed. Once that evidentiary deed reaches the buyer, the prior money acquisition takes effect retroactively, as explained in siman 190. The deed is therefore only like fulfillment of a condition in the original sale. Since fulfillment of the condition validates the transaction retroactively, the witnesses may write it. A loan deed is different, because without delivery of the deed no prior lien arose at all.
