The names in the deed, that this is so and so son of so and so, and that this is so and so son of so and so. In the Perishah and Derishah I questioned why the witnesses must recognize the purchaser. The reason to identify the seller is clear: otherwise someone could falsely assume another person's name and state that he is selling his field to a named buyer, creating a deed that could later harm the true person bearing that name. Once the seller is properly identified, that concern disappears. Nor need we fear that the seller wrote a deed but never delivered it, because the present case concerns a deed of kinyan or acknowledgment. I can explain the Mechaber's explicit requirement to know both parties only where the purchaser too appears before the witnesses and asks them to write the deed. Then the rationale cited by the Tur in the Ramah's name applies. Even where the seller acknowledges receiving the price, if the document implies that both parties knowingly agreed to the sale, the witnesses may not write it unless the purchaser's identity is known, because sometimes the purchaser does not want public knowledge that he owns the property. Such publicity may cause him loss from other people. Therefore, when both appear, the witnesses must identify both before writing that each agreed to the transaction. When only the seller appears, the witnesses merely record what the seller stated, and that publicity does not establish that the purchaser actually agreed. The Tur and Mechaber mention that the deed may be written even when the purchaser is absent as a greater novelty. Their requirement of recognizing both names applies to the case where both are present. The Ir Shushan begins by explaining only why the seller must be identified, yet his language in the middle seems to require recognizing both names. He apparently followed the Tur and Shulchan Aruch but did not have a reason for identifying the purchaser.
But one does not write a deed for the purchaser, etc. This is the Mishnah's language. The Tur cites Ramban, who says that the rule appears obvious. How could witnesses believe a purchaser who comes alone and asks them to write that he bought a certain field? Ramban therefore explains that witnesses had already made a kinyan from the seller transferring the property to the purchaser while the purchaser was absent, and afterward the purchaser comes and asks them to write the deed. They still may not do so without the seller's instruction. One might ask why the Mechaber cites this as "some say" if the Mishnah otherwise seems obvious. There are other answers. The Maggid Mishneh says the novelty may be that even if the purchaser says, "Write the deed and keep it with you until the seller comes and confirms the sale or makes a kinyan before you," they may not write it because at the time of writing it appears false. Another possible explanation is that even if the purchaser merely asks the witnesses to write that he stated before them that he bought a certain field, they should not do so, because although such a paper would not allow him to take the land from the seller, it could create harmful public rumor that the seller disposed of his field. This parallels the preceding rule that witnesses may write the seller's declaration of sale because such publicity generally benefits the purchaser and one may confer a benefit upon a person in his absence, whereas the purchaser's unilateral declaration generally has no apparent benefit for the seller.
