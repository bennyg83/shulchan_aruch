Mordechai, at the end of HaMokher Peirot, writes that witnesses need not investigate whether the donor is telling the truth about his ownership, because perhaps the property belongs to some person at the far end of the world and the witnesses are testifying only to the donor's act of gift. It appears to me that this applies only where the property is not known to have belonged to someone else. If the witnesses know that the house was previously possessed by another person and the putative donor tells them that he purchased it or received it as a gift, they should not write the deed until they see a deed of acquisition or evidence of chazakah. This is included in the rule that one may not write a deed for a purchaser without the seller's consent. Sema adds that even if the document merely states, in the putative purchaser's own words, “I bought such-and-such field from so-and-so,” witnesses should not write it. Although such a document cannot itself remove the field from the seller, it may create a public rumor that depresses the value of the seller's property. Panim Meirot, II, siman 2, discusses a deed in which Reuven appeared before witnesses and instructed them to record that a half-house formerly belonging to his brother-in-law Levi had been acquired from Levi according to Torah law and was now transferred to Shimon. Levi's creditor later sought to collect from that half-house, claiming it had never been validly transferred to Reuven. Reuven had no deed from Levi and no witnesses to a chazakah; the recital in the later deed rested only on Reuven's own statement. Panim Meirot rules that Reuven must prove his acquisition by deed or chazakah. If he cannot, the half-house remains legally Levi's property, and Levi's admission cannot prejudice his creditors. The creditor may therefore collect from it. This is not like a deed that identifies a borrower as “Yosef ben Shimon who is alive” or “the kohen,” where the wording itself naturally indicates that the witnesses knew the identifying fact. Here the deed expressly says that Reuven told them to transfer the half-house which he said he had acquired from Levi. The witnesses may have seen no prior deed or judicial transfer and merely recorded his declaration. A clear analogy is a moda'ah: if a party tells witnesses that he is compelled to sell, the moda'ah is ineffective unless the witnesses themselves know of the compulsion, and we do not infer from their signatures that they must have known it. Another proof is the rule in siman 255 that witnesses may record a deathly ill person's statement that he owns a hundred in another's hands even though they do not know whether the claim is true, because a later beit din must examine the underlying facts. Likewise here, the later beit din must investigate how Reuven acquired the half-house. This also accords with the Sema's statement that such a recital cannot by itself remove property from the known owner. The fact that witnesses nevertheless wrote it does not create a presumption that they acted only after verifying ownership.
