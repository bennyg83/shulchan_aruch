And they do not grant him any further time. This means only that he is not given additional time to prevent forfeiture of his right. His right is lost immediately and he immediately becomes liable for everything he undertook. If, however, he requests time to pay what he now owes, time is granted as stated below in siman 100. This is explicit in Responsa Rashba, siman 933, which concludes by comparing it to the Mishnah in the chapter Get Pashut: one who paid part of his debt, deposited the document with a third party, and said, “If I do not pay by such-and-such date, return the document to the creditor,” and the date arrived without payment, the document is returned. Even there, he is granted thirty days to pay, like anyone whom beit din has obligated to repay a creditor. See further there. Beit Yosef cites the responsum only briefly, but the printed responsum explains the matter correctly.
Because he was under compulsion, etc. See below, siman 55, seif 1, what I wrote concerning this.
He is exempt without a kinyan. The Bach labors to reconcile the Jerusalem Talmud in Kiddushin with the Rambam and the Mechaber. In my opinion, this effort is unnecessary, because the Babylonian Talmud in Nedarim 27a-b expressly supports the Rambam. There, a man deposited his legal rights and stipulated that if he did not return within thirty days they would be void. The Gemara concludes that they were not void because monetary liability arising from unavoidable compulsion is excused, even though a kinyan had been made before an eminent beit din. This is accepted by all authorities, as stated below in siman 55. Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh also imply incorrectly that the Rambam derived this from the Jerusalem Talmud. The same difficulty applies to Semag and Hagahot Maimoniyot, especially according to the Bach's emendation of Semag from “and so I saw in the Jerusalem Talmud” to “however, I saw in the Jerusalem Talmud,” thereby presenting it as contrary to the Rambam. How could the Jerusalem Talmud override an explicit passage in Nedarim supporting him? Some wished to emend the Jerusalem Talmud's phrase so that “compulsion is as though he did not act” would read “as though he did act.” This is incorrect. The reading with “did” is a scribal error in Beit Yosef. Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Kesef Mishneh, the Ran, the Jerusalem Talmud in Kiddushin and Gittin, and the Rashba's novellae all read that Rabbi Yochanan says compulsion is treated as nonperformance, meaning that compulsion is not a valid claim, while Reish Lakish treats it as performance, meaning that compulsion is a valid claim. The Jerusalem Talmud further states that Rabbi Yochanan instructed his sons to conduct themselves according to Reish Lakish lest another authority agree with Reish Lakish and they cause a concern of illegitimacy. It follows that according to Rabbi Yochanan she is not betrothed. Even were the Jerusalem Talmud to disagree, it would not challenge the Rambam against the explicit Babylonian Talmud. In truth, however, the Jerusalem Talmud agrees, and its case is wholly different. It concerns a man who said, “I betroth you on condition that I marry you on a certain day, and if that day arrives and I do not marry you, the betrothal shall be nothing,” and he was then prevented by compulsion. Rabbi Yochanan says the compulsion is treated as nonperformance. Rabbi Yochanan agrees that when a person conditionally undertakes a new obligation toward another and is then unavoidably prevented from fulfilling the condition, he is exempt and matters remain as before, because the Torah exempts one who is compelled. But where one undertakes a new obligation only on condition that the other party perform a certain act by a fixed date, and the other party is prevented by compulsion, the obligor does not become liable because of the other's compulsion. We say that the Torah exempts the compelled party, not that the Torah imposes liability on someone else because of that compulsion. The obligor accepted liability only if the condition was fulfilled. Since the other party did not fulfill it, albeit through compulsion, why should the obligor become liable? This is the Ran's intent in Kiddushin. He explains that betrothal differs from divorce. In divorce, the matter depends solely on the husband, and he intends to divorce only if he voluntarily violates the condition, not if he is compelled. In betrothal, however, the woman becomes betrothed only with her consent, and she consents only if he fulfills his condition. If it is not fulfilled, even through compulsion, she does not consent to the betrothal. The Bach misunderstood the Ran and therefore compared the first case, in which the claimant demands an oath from the defendant, to betrothal, forcing himself to say that betrothal differs because otherwise the woman would remain chained. The Ran's intent is as I have explained, and therefore a claimant's demand that the defendant swear is not comparable to betrothal. This is clear. See also what I wrote in Nekudot HaKesef, Yoreh Deah, end of siman 236. The distinction applies where the condition was stated at the outset as part of the act. If the act was originally completed without qualification and only afterward the parties added conditions, then the opposing party is exempt because of the other's unavoidable delay only when the condition concerns something on which the substance of the transaction depends. If the principal transaction does not depend upon it, he remains bound. Accordingly, in contemporary betrothal arrangements, where the match is first made without qualification and only afterward the parties stipulate, for example, that the wedding will take place on a certain day, recording this in the engagement conditions without stating that the match was made on that condition, one party remains obligated to carry out the match even if the other missed the date through compulsion, because the essence of the marriage does not depend on the precise date. The Taz makes this distinction there, and it is compelled by Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 137, and the Rama's gloss there. But where the original act itself was expressly made conditional, the opposing party is exempt even if the condition is not essential and the delay resulted from compulsion, as is evident from the Jerusalem Talmud cited above. This is contrary to the implication of the Taz, who does not distinguish these cases. I have also challenged his other arguments at length there. See there and examine carefully.
And he shall swear when the other party demands it, etc. The same is true in the second case: he may later appear, take the oath, and collect. The Sema initially writes this, and his later suggestion that perhaps this principle does not apply to one who swears and collects is incorrect. Rather, it is included in the phrase, “and similarly in every analogous case.” The Bach agrees, and this is the primary ruling.
All this applies where a kinyan was made from him, etc. The Sema writes that in the first case the kinyan must be made before an eminent beit din and must take effect “from now,” as stated below in siman 207, although he need not deposit his legal rights. The Bach disagrees, maintaining that because no definite forfeiture of money is involved, an ordinary kinyan suffices. The Levush follows the Sema and requires a kinyan before an eminent beit din. His language implies that the second case likewise requires such a kinyan, since he states this at the end regarding the entire siman. Beit Yosef, below in siman 207, folio 296a, before the words “and what our master wrote, that it is called an eminent beit din,” likewise writes that the Rambam relied upon the place where he set forth the laws of asmachta, and that the case concerns a kinyan before an eminent beit din together with deposit of one's legal rights. In the latter clause, however, since the other party already possesses the property, the first party loses his right in every event. See what I write in siman 207.
