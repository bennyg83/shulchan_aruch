He was prevented by unavoidable accident. The Shach discusses the Jerusalem Talmud's dispute. Rabbi Yochanan holds that unavoidable accident is treated as nonperformance, while Reish Lakish treats it as though the act had been performed. Rabbi Yochanan's reasoning is that although the Torah exempts a person from liability for unavoidable accident, we do not say that the Torah affirmatively creates performance through unavoidable accident. If a woman agreed to kiddushin only on condition that the man marry her on a particular day and he failed to do so because of unavoidable accident, the condition nevertheless was not fulfilled. Ran states this in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh. Reish Lakish, however, who treats accident as performance, appears difficult from the Mishnah in Gittin: if a man says, "This is your get on condition that you serve my father," or, "on condition that you nurse my son," and the father or child dies, the get is not effective. Why is this not unavoidable accident treated as performance? Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel disagrees and validates the get where the obstacle did not arise from her, but the Bavli explains his reason differently, namely that the husband's intent was for his own benefit and he meant service or nursing only for as long as needed. I later found that the Jerusalem Talmud directly links the dispute of Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish to the dispute between Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel and the first Tanna, implying that Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's reason there is precisely that accident counts as performance. The Bavli can be understood as explaining the Mishnah according to Rabbi Yochanan, while Reish Lakish must explain Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel through the accident principle. My learned son Rabbi Ze'ev Wolf asked from the Mishnah at the end of Perek HaTikabel: where a man instructed, "All of you sign," and one of them died, the get is void. Rabbi Yochanan holds that two sign as witnesses and the rest sign only to satisfy a condition. If one of the additional signers dies, why should unavoidable accident not count as fulfillment? According to the reading preserved by the Shach, however, Rabbi Yochanan is the one who holds that accident counts as nonperformance, while Reish Lakish holds that it counts as performance. This fits their respective positions in Gittin: Reish Lakish treats all signers as witnesses, so the death of one necessarily voids the get; Rabbi Yochanan treats some as only a condition and consistently holds that accident does not count as fulfillment. This supports that textual reading. Tzemach Tzedek discusses Reuven, who obligated himself to marry Shimon's daughter if Shimon would send him a sudar kinyan by Rosh Chodesh Nisan, with a penalty of one hundred zuz if he failed to marry her. Shimon sent a messenger, but unavoidable flooding delayed him and the kinyan did not arrive on time. Tzemach Tzedek rules that Reuven is exempt because the condition on which Reuven's obligation depended was not fulfilled. Unavoidable accident can exempt the person who was supposed to perform, but it cannot create a new affirmative liability upon the other party. He nevertheless suggests that if Reuven has not yet entered another engagement, he should still marry Shimon's daughter or pay the penalty, because Shimon's unavoidable accident should prevent him from losing his right. He distinguishes this from kiddushin by arguing that kiddushin become void once the condition fails and require new kiddushin, whereas an engagement can still continue if the party waives strict compliance. In my view, this distinction is far too subtle and separates things that are legally alike. Even in kiddushin the authorities rule that the party for whose benefit the condition was made can waive it. More fundamentally, if a person's obligation was undertaken only on condition that the other party perform a specified act, and that act did not occur, there is no basis to compel him to perform merely because the other party was prevented by accident. This is true in kiddushin, engagements, and all other matters. Whenever the condition is not fulfilled, the reciprocal obligation does not arise, even if the failure resulted from unavoidable accident. The Shach likewise implies that where the engagement itself depended specifically upon that condition, failure to fulfill it even through accident releases the opposing party. This is clear and straightforward.
