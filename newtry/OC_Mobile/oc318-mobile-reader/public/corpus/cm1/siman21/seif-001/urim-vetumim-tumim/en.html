That he was prevented by circumstances beyond his control, etc. Bach cites Semag as follows: the Geonim ruled this way, and Semag adds that he found a parallel in the Yerushalmi in Kiddushin. The case is a conditional kiddushin document: "If I do not come by a certain day to marry you, the kiddushin shall be nothing." An unavoidable circumstance occurs. R. Yochanan says the unavoidable event is treated as though the condition was not fulfilled, while Reish Lakish says it is treated as though it was fulfilled. The halachah follows R. Yochanan against Reish Lakish. Hagahot Maimoniyot says similarly. Bach asks: according to the text as written, R. Yochanan holds that even though the man was prevented by circumstances beyond his control, he is treated as failing the condition and the kiddushin are void. Since we rule like R. Yochanan, the Yerushalmi would prove exactly the opposite of the Geonim, namely that an unavoidable circumstance does not excuse the failure. Bach therefore emends Semag. Instead of "and so I found in the Yerushalmi," the text should read, "however, I found in the Yerushalmi," meaning that the Yerushalmi appears contrary to the Geonim. Shach asks a powerful question. The Bavli in Nedarim 27 explicitly rules that where a person says, "If I do not arrive by the specified time, my rights shall be void," and an unavoidable circumstance prevents him from coming, his rights are not void. The same rule is brought below in Shulchan Aruch siman 55. Why would Semag need any proof from the Yerushalmi at all? And according to Bach's emendation, why would a contrary Yerushalmi matter where the Bavli expressly rules like the Geonim? At first glance this is a strong objection. There is, however, another difficulty already in Rambam. Rambam records the Nedarim law in Hilchot Mechirah chapter 11. Yet he records this present law separately in Hilchot Sanhedrin chapter 7. Why repeat the same principle twice? This indicates that the two passages address different aspects of the law. According to Bach's reading of Semag, the distinction can be understood through the dispute brought by Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 232, seif 12. If a person had thirty days to appear and was free to come throughout the first twenty-nine days, but chose to wait until the last day, and only on that final day an unavoidable circumstance arose, Ran holds that he is still considered prevented by circumstances beyond his control. Agudah disagrees. He says the person should have come earlier and should not have waited until the last moment. Rambam appears to follow Ran. In Hilchot Mechirah he quotes the Gemara's broad language: if illness or a river prevented him from coming, the condition does not take effect. That can be read as a case where the impediment persisted throughout the relevant period, which even Agudah can accept. But in Hilchot Sanhedrin Rambam writes more precisely: if he brings proof that he was prevented "on that day." This suggests that the impediment existed specifically on the final day, even though earlier he could have come. Rambam teaches that even such a last-day impediment counts as ones, following Ran and rejecting Agudah. Semag then cites the Yerushalmi's dispute between R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish. How should that dispute be understood? If the person was prevented throughout the entire period, why would R. Yochanan hold that the condition fails? The Bavli's proof from the Torah's treatment of a coerced woman, "and to the girl you shall do nothing," makes it obvious that genuine unavoidable compulsion excuses nonperformance. Therefore the Yerushalmi likely concerns a case where the person was free earlier and became prevented only at the end. R. Yochanan holds that this does not excuse him because he should have come earlier and not waited until the deadline, while Reish Lakish treats the final-day impediment as sufficient. On this reading, the Yerushalmi supports Agudah and appears contrary to Rambam and Ran. Perhaps Kesef Mishneh, who cites the Yerushalmi as support for Rambam, had a different textual version: R. Yochanan said the unavoidable event is treated as though he did not violate the condition, meaning the condition is regarded as satisfied despite the last-day impediment. Then the halachah follows R. Yochanan and supports Rambam. But that reading has no firm basis in our Yerushalmi. The accepted text indicates that R. Yochanan voids the kiddushin and Reish Lakish maintains them. Shach himself proves this from the Yerushalmi's continuation. R. Yochanan instructed his daughters to structure their conditions in accordance with Reish Lakish's concern lest another beit din later follow Reish Lakish and create a problem of mamzerut. That implies Reish Lakish is the one who maintains the kiddushin. There is additional evidence from the Yerushalmi's question according to Reish Lakish: how should the condition be drafted? It answers that one should say, "If such-and-such day arrives and I have not married you, there shall be nothing between us." This wording is designed to make the expiration depend solely upon the day passing, regardless of the reason. That makes sense only if Reish Lakish ordinarily says that unavoidable circumstances preserve the kiddushin. Thus Bach's reading of the Yerushalmi text appears correct. Shach, however, answers the underlying problem differently. He writes that kiddushin are unique. A woman becomes betrothed only through her own consent. If she accepted kiddushin specifically on condition that the marriage occur by a defined day, then regardless of why the man fails to come, the consensual condition upon which she agreed has not been fulfilled. By contrast, where a person undertakes a monetary obligation, we can presume that had he foreseen an unavoidable impediment he would not have accepted the condition in that form. This is a good distinction and is supported by Ran in Kiddushin. Yet it creates difficulty with Mahari Weil's ruling, cited by Rama in Yoreh Deah siman 236, seif 6. There, a man and woman accepted a ban upon themselves to marry one another by a fixed time. The date passed because of unavoidable circumstances, and Mahari Weil rules that the ban still binds. That resembles the Yerushalmi's conditional kiddushin. The woman likewise entered the engagement only by her own consent. Why should unavoidable circumstances preserve the ban if they do not preserve the kiddushin? Shach in Nekudot HaKesef attempts to distinguish: the original engagement was made without a condition, and the time condition was added only later. Therefore the underlying engagement remains even if the later deadline fails. But this does not adequately explain Mahari Weil. Mahari Weil says explicitly that the time condition existed at the time the formal oath or ban was undertaken. If that formal undertaking was conditional, why is it not void when the condition fails? The earlier engagement may still exist as a social agreement, but beit din would then enforce that engagement on its own basis. What relevance would the separate prohibition of the oath or ban have? Mahari Weil specifically says the parties require release from the oath, showing that the issue is the conditional oath itself. He also writes that after formal release from the oath they are no longer bound to each other, which again shows that the entire enforceable relationship is rooted in that undertaking. The distinction therefore remains difficult. Shach himself acknowledges in Nekudot HaKesef that his explanation of Mahari Weil is strained. Therefore, I suggest another resolution which preserves the printed text of Semag, as Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh apparently had it. The Bavli in Nedarim proves only that where an unavoidable circumstance is established, it can excuse nonperformance. But our case raises another question: who bears the burden of proving the ones? Suppose one party says, "If I fail to appear by the fixed date, you may swear and I will pay." The deadline passes. He then claims, "I was prevented by circumstances beyond my control," but he brings no witnesses. Do we refrain from extracting money because perhaps his claim is true? Ordinarily, in monetary doubt, we do not remove money from the possessor and the burden of proof lies on the claimant. Perhaps the other party must prove that there was no unavoidable circumstance. This issue is not resolved by Nedarim, where the unavoidable circumstance was known and established. Semag and Rambam can therefore use the Yerushalmi to address precisely this evidentiary question. The dispute between R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish need not concern an ones established by witnesses. If the impediment were proven, the view that it has no effect would face the Bavli's fundamental principle and the Torah's own rule concerning compulsion. Rather, the Yerushalmi can be speaking where the man merely claims loudly that he was prevented, without proof. R. Yochanan says the unproven claim is treated as though he violated the condition. We do not assume an ones that is not established. Reish Lakish is concerned that perhaps his claim is true and therefore maintains the kiddushin at least as a matter of doubt. The halachah follows R. Yochanan. Thus the party who failed the condition must bring proof of the ones. If he cannot prove it, we do not leave the matter in monetary doubt. We treat the condition as failed. This explains Rambam's exact language: "If he brought proof that he was prevented on that day, he is exempt from the kinyan." The words "brought proof" are essential. Without proof, he must fulfill the undertaking. One might otherwise have said that because money is being extracted from him, the claimant bears the burden to prove that there was no ones. Rambam teaches the opposite: the person relying on ones must establish it. Kesef Mishneh's statement that Rambam has support from the Yerushalmi can now be understood precisely. This is entirely different from the Gemara in Nedarim, answering Shach's objection. This approach also implies that Semag and Beit Yosef do not accept Ran's conceptual distinction between kiddushin and monetary conditions. If they did, the Yerushalmi would prove nothing for civil cases. That in turn explains Rama's ruling in Yoreh Deah following Mahari Weil: unavoidable circumstances can excuse even in consensual undertakings between a man and woman, provided the ones is established. The Yerushalmi is relevant not because kiddushin are conceptually unique but because it establishes the burden of proof concerning the alleged ones. Derishah gives another interpretation. He says the Yerushalmi's dispute concerns a person who was prevented throughout the original thirty-day period, but after the thirty days the impediment ended and he still failed to perform. One view says that once the original period was disrupted by ones, the condition is permanently nullified and need not be fulfilled even afterward. The other says that when the impediment ends, the obligation to fulfill the condition revives. Sema relies on this interpretation to derive a practical rule. But that reading itself depends upon the alternate text which treats R. Yochanan as preserving the kiddushin despite nonperformance. According to the correct text explained above, R. Yochanan voids the kiddushin. The proof would therefore run in the opposite direction. Moreover, Derishah's reading inserts an essential fact not stated in the Yerushalmi at all, namely that the person still failed to come after the impediment ended. For that reason I do not regard the Yerushalmi as proof either way on that separate question. Rambam's wording, "he is exempt from the kinyan," may independently suggest that the condition is entirely discharged, but Derishah's proposed proof from the Yerushalmi is not valid. The more convincing interpretation is the evidentiary one stated above.
