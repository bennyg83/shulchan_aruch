Shach, seif katan 3. Note: If a case occurs in which a seller does not deliver the merchandise by the time fixed by the buyer, then if the seller is content to retain only the earnest money given by the buyer, the buyer may certainly say, “I maintain that the halachah follows the Taz,” since the Shach’s arguments are not conclusive. But even if the seller does not agree to limit himself to the earnest money, the buyer is still obligated to accept the merchandise despite the seller’s failure to deliver on time, unless the delay was expressly made a condition that voids the sale. The reason is that in the Shach’s era merchants may not generally have written such time clauses as a matter of routine. When a party specifically inserted one, it could therefore be understood as a genuine condition, based on a clear presumption that he meant the deadline literally. In our time, however, most merchants customarily write such clauses, yet in practice when delivery is late they still accept the goods. The customary purpose of the clause is therefore often only to urge the seller to diligence, not to make time of the essence. Accordingly, unless the contract explicitly states that failure to deliver by that date cancels the sale, the sale remains valid. In such a case even the Shach should agree. It is preferable to minimize dispute among the authorities.
