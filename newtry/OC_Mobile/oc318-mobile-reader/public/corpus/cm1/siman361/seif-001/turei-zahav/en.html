[Seif 3] Ordinary robbery does not produce the owner's despair. This ruling requires analysis. Here the Tur rules generally that ordinary robbery does not imply despair, while in siman 368 the Tur and Rambam distinguish: with a gentile robber the owner ordinarily does not despair, but with a Jewish robber he ordinarily does. It is forced to say that the present case concerns a gentile. Kiddushin 52b also states that one who seized another's money and betrothed a woman with it does not effect betrothal, because we follow the Sages against Rabbi Shimon, who holds that ordinary robbery produces despair. This plainly includes a Jewish robber. My father-in-law answered that Kiddushin concerns Torah-law betrothal, where no distinction is made until despair is known, whereas the distinction in siman 368 is rabbinic. This cannot stand, because Rava ruled practically that no bill of divorce is required, and all authorities rule that one who betroths with stolen property before the owner's despair does not effect betrothal. Maharil likewise ruled that where a student placed another person's money in a servant woman's hand and declared betrothal, there was no betrothal, because the money was not his and no authority follows Rabbi Shimon's rule of presumed despair. Even though the money might be viewed as stolen from the woman herself, since she was authorized to receive it, Maharil rejected that characterization, and major authorities agreed. This refutes the stringency requiring a bill of divorce in such a case. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 114a teaches that if tax collectors took one's donkey and gave another, or bandits took one's garment and gave another, the substitute belongs to him because the owners despaired. One who rescues property from a river, an army, or bandits acquires it if the owners despaired. Rav Ashi distinguishes: with gentile bandits the ordinary presumption is no despair because gentile law may restore property, but with Jewish bandits ordinary despair exists, for the owner says, “Who can compel them?” Elsewhere the Sages and Rabbi Shimon dispute whether ordinary robbery or ordinary theft produces despair. Ulla says they dispute only where despair is not known, while Rava says they dispute even where despair is known. The Gemara challenges Rava from the baraita about tax collectors and bandits and answers that it follows Rabbi Shimon and treats both as forms of robbery. Several difficulties arise. We rule like Rav Ashi that with a Jewish bandit there is presumed despair, as the Rif, Rambam, Rosh, and Tur all hold, yet we also rule like the Sages that ordinary robbery does not produce despair. The dispute cannot concern gentiles alone, since the law of ritual impurity in that sugya applies to Jews. Any proposed distinction must also explain why the Gemara could not answer the challenge to Rava in the same way. Kiddushin explicitly shows that the Sages' rule applies even to a Jewish robber. Maharshal answers that Rav Ashi's distinction concerns a thief, because “bandit” in the Mishnah means thief, whereas a robber never creates presumed despair. He therefore sharply criticizes the Tur, which understands the Rosh to distinguish between Jewish and gentile robbers. But the Rambam also writes that one who rescues from Jewish bandits keeps the property because the owners ordinarily despair, while one who rescues from gentile bandits or tax collectors does not, because gentile authorities may restore property from the robber. The Rambam begins with “bandits” and ends with “robber,” showing that he speaks of robbery. Maharshal's attempt to say that “robber” is mentioned only to teach a greater novelty regarding gentiles is strained. Had that been the intent, the Rambam would have said “even a robber,” and tax collectors are certainly robbers. The Beit Yosef's claim that the Sages' rule concerns gentiles is likewise rejected by the foregoing sources. The correct explanation is that the Tur and the other authorities recognized two different settings. Despair alone must be firm and definite. Where another acquisitive factor joins it, however, even a weaker presumed despair may suffice. Thus despair joined with a change of name can acquire even where the change of name is weak, and despair joined with a change of possession is stronger still. In the dispute concerning hides, there is no change of possession. A slight change of name exists, but it is insufficient to support presumed despair from robbery. Therefore the Sages hold that ordinary robbery does not create despair. The same is true in betrothal, where the property must belong to the husband before he gives it to the woman. Her receipt is not the relevant change of possession that would perfect his ownership. Hence doubtful despair from ordinary robbery cannot make the money his. The Tur's separate stringency that despair alone may sometimes effect doubtful betrothal concerns clear and strong despair, not the weak presumption in ordinary robbery. The Mishnah concerning rescue from bandits, however, necessarily involves a change of possession, because despair alone does not acquire. The rescuer must have obtained the item through appeasing or purchasing it from the bandit, not by forcibly taking it. The Tur's wording, “one who rescues from the robber or buys from him,” equates rescue with purchase. When change of possession joins despair, the despair is strengthened. Thus with a Jewish robber, the owner's thought that no one can compel him creates sufficient presumed despair, and this is precisely Rav Ashi's distinction. With a gentile robber, the owner does not despair because he expects the authorities to restore the property, so even change of possession does not create a presumption unless actual despair is known. The earlier baraita about a garment taken by bandits concerns Jewish bandits, as Rashi explains, and therefore ordinary despair combined with change of possession suffices. This also explains the Gemara's challenge to Rava. Rava holds that even known despair does not help according to the Sages in the disputed case. If explicit despair is ineffective there, a fortiori a change of possession cannot strengthen merely presumed despair. The baraita, which recognizes despair in robbery, would then fit neither the Sages nor Rabbi Shimon unless interpreted as the Gemara does. Accordingly, all the rulings are consistent. The present siman concerns despair joined only with a change of name, without change of possession, and therefore ordinary robbery does not imply despair. Siman 368 concerns despair joined with change of possession, and therefore distinguishes Jewish from gentile bandits. The Rambam follows the same structure. This resolves the matter, with the help of the Blessed One.
