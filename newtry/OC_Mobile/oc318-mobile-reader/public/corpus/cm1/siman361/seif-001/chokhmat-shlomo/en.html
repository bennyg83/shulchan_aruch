Seif 4. "Despair of recovery together with a change of possession effects acquisition." Note: I was uncertain about a case where the robber sells one half of the stolen object to another person without designating a particular physical half. Is this considered a change of possession or not? According to the view that there is retroactive clarification, and with an object that can be physically divided in that manner, there is no doubt that the half ultimately falling to the purchaser is considered to have undergone a change of possession. My question concerns an object that cannot be divided in that way, or even one that can be divided according to our ruling that there is no retroactive clarification. We rule like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov in Nedarim, that each partner entering the jointly owned property is entering his own portion, and the authorities explain that each partner is regarded as having ownership throughout the whole. Likewise, we rule that brothers who divide an inheritance are legally purchasers. It therefore requires analysis whether the entire stolen object is considered to have undergone a change of possession or not. At present I have found no explicit source for this law. I did, however, notice the language of the Rambam in Hilchot Issurei Mizbeach 5:7: "One who steals or robs and offers the animal as a korban, the korban is invalid. If the owners despaired of recovery, the korban is valid, even if it was a sin offering whose flesh is eaten by the Kohanim." The Raavad wonders about the word "even," because this appears to reverse the Gemara in the fifth chapter of Gittin, where the greater novelty is said with respect to an olah. The Kesef Mishneh is forced in his explanation. In my view the Rambam can be understood well. One might have thought that because despair alone does not acquire, the consecration can take effect here only through despair together with a change of possession. This is readily understandable for an olah, which is wholly burned and wherever it is, it is in the treasury of Heaven, and therefore the consecration is a change of possession. With a sin offering, however, one might have thought that because the Kohanim have an entitlement to the flesh, although the portion belonging to Heaven is regarded as being in the treasury of Heaven wherever it is, the Kohanim's portion is not yet in their possession and they have not yet acquired it. Their portion might therefore remain in the robber's possession. Since the korban is then shared between Heaven and the Kohanim, this could resemble a case where only half was sold and there was no complete change of possession. The Rambam therefore teaches the novelty that, as the Gemara says, the Kohanim receive from the table of Heaven. Their portion too is thus regarded as in the possession of Heaven, so the entire animal enters the possession of Heaven and there is a complete change of possession. The Gemara's reverse formulation concerns Rabbi Yehudah, who holds that despair alone effects acquisition. Since on that view no change of possession is required, a sin offering presents no greater novelty than an olah, and the Gemara must explain the novelty in the opposite direction. According to our ruling, however, that despair alone does not acquire and a change of possession is required, the Gemara's difficulty does not arise, for a sin offering can indeed be the greater novelty, teaching that this too is considered a change of possession. From this there would appear to be a proof that even if a defined part is sold to a purchaser, so long as the entire object was not sold there is no complete change of possession. Examine carefully. The practical ruling still requires study.
