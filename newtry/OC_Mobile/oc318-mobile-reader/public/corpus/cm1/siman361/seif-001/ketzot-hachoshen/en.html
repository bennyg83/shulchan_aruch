Despair alone. Tosafot says despair is not ordinary ownerless designation, because if it were, even an object that came into one's hand before despair should be acquired once the owner later despairs. Rashi elsewhere appears to understand despair through the concept of ownerless property. The distinction is that a lost object taken for return before despair becomes held in the owner's legal hand through the finder as custodian. Later despair cannot operate against the owner's own legal possession. If the finder took it intending to steal, he never became the owner's custodian and the object remains like a lost object on the ground, so later despair can acquire. This explains why despair after receipt can fail in lost property without proving that despair is wholly unrelated to ownerless designation. It also helps explain the difference between a theft that comes into one's hand unlawfully and a found object that enters lawfully. The finder who takes for return becomes the owner's custodian; the thief does not. Therefore the mechanics of later despair differ even if despair is conceptually similar to removal of ownership.
He did not acquire. Rabbah compares despair in theft with despair in lost property but notes that a found object is acquired only where the owner despaired before it entered the finder's hand. Tosafot explains that if despair came afterward the finder would still owe value, so one cannot simply derive complete theft acquisition from that case. In my view the essential difference is that a finder who takes an object for return becomes the owner's custodian, and his hand is legally the owner's hand. Later despair therefore cannot acquire the object for him. A thief is not the owner's custodian. Accordingly, despair might acquire for the thief unless the unlawful manner of receipt itself blocks acquisition. This follows Ramban's explanation that one who lifts a lost object intending to steal can later acquire through despair because, from the owner's perspective, it remains like an object on the ground rather than in the owner's custodial hand. Thus the question whether despair alone acquires in theft is genuinely distinct from the law of a finder who received the object for return before despair.
