It is not considered a change of domain. Because a son is the continuation of his father and is not treated as a purchaser.
After the owner's despair, they are exempt. I explained the reason in the preceding seif and in the Tur. See there.
But if their father left property. In that case the father's assets became subject to the victim's claim immediately when the robbery occurred. The Mechaber writes here without qualification "property," because nowadays even the movable property of orphans is subject to creditors and to one who was robbed. The Tur says this explicitly in siman 371 and also in the present siman.
Whether the heirs are adults or minors, etc. Beit din acts even against minor orphans with respect to evidence of robbery, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 110. Baal HaTerumot likewise writes this in gate 14, and Darkei Moshe cites it in siman 28, seif 9. All the more so, if the stolen object itself remains intact, it is removed even from minor heirs, as the Tur writes here.
If the adult heirs say, etc., they are believed. They are believed through a migo: had they wished, they could have said, "We ourselves returned it to you." Even according to the opinion that one who robbed before witnesses must return before witnesses, as the Mechaber writes in the following seif, that applies to the robber himself; his sons are believed. The law specifically says the adult heirs themselves must make the claim. They must also make a definite claim. If they say only, "Perhaps our father returned it to you," or, "Perhaps our father bought it from you," they are not believed on an uncertain claim, and we do not advance for them a claim that is not sufficiently common. So writes Rabbeinu Yeruham, netiv 26, part 3, cited in Darkei Moshe.
But if there are witnesses that he robbed it, etc. The reason is that the migo, "we returned it to you," would be a more brazen claim than saying, "We know that our father settled accounts with you and nothing remains owing."
