(Collected note) It is not, etc. This entire passage is the Tur's language. His statement “even if he consumed it” is not intrinsic to the rule of change of possession, because a genuine change of possession without despair does not acquire. It is mentioned to emphasize the later clause: after despair, even without a change of possession the second person can be exempt in the circumstances described by Tosafos and the Rosh. The principal ruling nevertheless requires study, because Bava Kamma 114a regarding one who rescues property seems to involve an involuntary transfer, and Sukkah 30b would not need to invoke the rule that land cannot be stolen if movable property also failed to undergo change of possession in the hands of the merchants.
Or he gave it. The Tosefta states that one who robbed and exchanged, consecrated, gave as a gift, extended on credit, paid his debt, or sent gifts to his father-in-law remains liable, while the recipient from him is exempt.
Some say, etc. Tosafos in Bava Batra 44a and Nimukei Yosef.
Or he gave it, etc. Bava Kamma 114a discusses a case of despair alone. Rashba asks that there is also change of possession, and answers that the recipient received it mistakenly, believing it was the giver's property, or received it against his will. Tosafos offer a similar distinction. Rabbenu Tam raises a difficulty from the later chapter, but the latter version can be understood as dealing with a voluntary transfer, like the Mishnah there. This also answers Rabbenu Tam's objection. Rashba gives additional resolutions, and Rashi likewise explains the passage.
But if, etc. The Yerushalmi asks, where a thief stole and the item was then stolen from him, to whom must the second thief pay, the first thief, the owner, or both? Its conclusion shows that returning it to the first thief in the owner's presence does not necessarily discharge the second thief, because the owner may otherwise demand it again. The Tur's statement that payment to the first robber does not necessarily exempt the second follows this approach, although one could argue that the Yerushalmi speaks before despair. Our Yerushalmi has a different wording and appears uncertain. Tosafos in Bava Kamma hold that in some such cases there is a change of possession, while Rashba in his novellae and responsa states the Tur's rule more categorically.

(Collected note) If he paid him, etc. Compare the rule in Bava Kamma chapter 2 concerning one who rents, etc., and in the beginning of chapter 3 of Bava Metzia. Mordechai writes in the name of Ri that before despair the second person is liable as one holding stolen property, but after despair his liability can be through Rabbi Nasan's rule rather than the law of theft. Thus if the first robber forgave the second or was paid by him, the second may be exempt.

Even, etc. See Beer HaGolah and the Maggid Mishneh. The Gemara explicitly discusses one who robs and feeds the stolen property to his adult children, and the Tosefta likewise states that adult sons and daughters are liable where it was before despair. This follows Tosafos and Rashba, unlike the Baal HaMaor's narrower reading.
