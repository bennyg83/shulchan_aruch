Seif 7. "He died and left the stolen property to his sons." Note: See my work on Choshen Mishpat, third edition, responsum 51, in my reply to the rabbi of Leshnov, where I discussed at length the law of one who stole property before the owners despaired of recovery and another person came and consumed it. Many novel practical rulings emerge there; see there. In my responsum to the community of Monasterzyska, printed in the supplements to my work Tuv Taam VaDaat, I proved from Bava Batra that only an ordinary heir's share is not considered to be in the possession of a purchaser, whereas the firstborn's double portion, which the Torah calls a gift, is regarded as the heir's possession in the manner of a purchaser and constitutes a change of possession. See there and examine carefully. Also see my work on Even HaEzer, ninth edition, responsum 81, where I wrote a novel ruling relevant here: although one who stole less than a perutah need not return it, if the victim seizes that very item he need not return it to the robber and need not pay him, even to fulfill an obligation before Heaven. He may say, "I took what was mine." The reason is that perhaps it was among the minority of cases in which the victim did not waive it. Moreover, although the robber is exempt by strict law from returning less than a perutah, he may remain obligated before Heaven, and wherever an obligation before Heaven exists, seizure can be effective. Examine carefully.
