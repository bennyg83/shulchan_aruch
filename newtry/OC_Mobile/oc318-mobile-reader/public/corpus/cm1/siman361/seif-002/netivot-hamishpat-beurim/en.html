Ye'ush together with a weak change of name. Tur indicates that this acquires d'oraisa. At first glance this is puzzling. Physical change is learned from the verse "that which he stole," and without the verse even an irreversible change would not acquire. If the verse cannot independently teach a reversible change, from where do we know that reversible change plus ye'ush acquires d'oraisa? Tosafot in Bava Kamma 65 explain that irreversible change derives from one verse and reversible change from "that which he stole." They also explain elsewhere that "that which he stole" supports the rule that a later consumer can be exempt after ye'ush according to Rava. It follows that the same verse can be understood as teaching two connected rules: after ye'ush, the relevant reversible change can acquire, and after ye'ush a later consumer can be exempt because the original owner's relation has already weakened. Thus Rava, who places the later-consumer rule after ye'ush, can also read the reversible-change derivation as operating after ye'ush. Rami bar Chama, who places the consumer rule before ye'ush, can understand the reversible change as acquiring even earlier. This allows Rabbah's statement that "change acquires" to stand according to halachah even though the case requires ye'ush. The principal acquiring force is the change, while ye'ush removes the reason the change would revert when the victim recovers the object. Tosafot explain that a mere change of designation can acquire once the thief's intention is sufficient to establish the new use. Ramban similarly writes that a change of name made by the thief does not acquire before ye'ush because the victim can reverse it upon recovery; after ye'ush, once the item has left the victim's effective control, the new designation becomes complete. This also resolves a difficulty from siman 360. A reversible physical change does not acquire even with ye'ush where the change was produced by someone else and the thief himself never designated the item for the new use. A reversible change of name can acquire when the thief himself fixes the new identity. Therefore if the thief takes a bar of metal and himself makes coins from it, that can acquire after ye'ush through the new name, even though the physical process could theoretically be reversed. It is no weaker than the Gemara's example of myrtle becoming a hoshana. This also helps answer the question in Temurah 5 against Rava's rule that an act performed contrary to Torah should be ineffective. The Mishnah of stolen wood made into vessels can be understood as a case where the thief himself creates the reversible change of designation after ye'ush. Since the acquisition depends on the thief's own prohibited act, the Gemara can ask that the change should not help. A change occurring by itself would not create the same thief-generated change of name.
