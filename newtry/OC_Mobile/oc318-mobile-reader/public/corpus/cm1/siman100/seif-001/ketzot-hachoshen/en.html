Had it been. Mordechai records a responsum of Maharam concerning Reuven, who deposited property with Shimon. Levi, a creditor of Shimon, seized the deposit for Shimon's debt. Maharam ruled that Levi must return it to Reuven. The fact that Shimon may be liable to Reuven for negligence in allowing Levi to seize it does not make the deposit into Shimon's property. One cannot collect a custodian's debt from the depositor's object. Mordechai considers whether, according to Rabbi Shimon, who treats a thing causing monetary liability as money, the result might differ because the deposit causes liability to Shimon. I find even that suggestion difficult. Rabbi Shimon teaches that one who damages consecrated property for which another bears responsibility can owe that responsible person because the object causes him money. We do not find the reverse rule, that a person's creditor may seize sacred or third party property merely because his debtor would have to replace it if lost. Even according to Rabbi Yosei HaGelili, who gives owners some property interest in lower sanctity offerings, the injured party can benefit only because the sacred property itself remains within its permitted use; the consecrated estate does not suffer an ordinary transfer to private ownership. Here, taking Reuven's deposit for Shimon's debt would directly take another person's property. The doctrine of a thing causing money does not permit that. This remains difficult in Mordechai's formulation.
