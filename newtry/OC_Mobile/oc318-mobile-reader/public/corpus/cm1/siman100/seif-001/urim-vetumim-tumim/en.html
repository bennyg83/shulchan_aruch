For if they were movable property, beit din would collect it immediately. The Bach and Taz ask that this implies no collection period is granted for movable property, whereas above in siman 73 a borrower receives the ordinary beit din period even when the creditor already holds a pledge. They answer that a pledge is different because the creditor accepted it with an expectation of delay. That answer is strained. Moreover, the Tur and Rosh themselves write above in siman 73 that the reason a borrower receives thirty days even where the creditor holds collateral is that had there been no pledge and the creditor sued him, beit din would have granted the ordinary collection period. Their explanation assumes the opposite of the Bach and Taz: the pledge does not create the period, it merely leaves intact the period that exists in ordinary collection. The Derishah suggests that movable property in the debtor's own possession should have been sold earlier if it was readily marketable, whereas pledged property was not in his possession and was therefore less available for sale. This too is strained. A better answer follows the Sema's own distinction in seif katan 2 between land and movable property. With land there is little concern that the debtor will hide it or that a later creditor will seize it, while movable property creates both risks. Accordingly, where the movable property is actually delivered to the creditor or beit din for collection, those concerns disappear. It should then receive the same thirty-day period as land. The same is true of collateral already held by the creditor. Because the property is secure and cannot be hidden or diverted to another creditor, the ordinary collection period applies. There is therefore no contradiction.
