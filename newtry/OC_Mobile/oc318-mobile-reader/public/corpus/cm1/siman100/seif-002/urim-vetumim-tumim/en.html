Even if he came. Mordechai records a question to Rabbenu Tam. If a creditor sued in Sivan on a debt expressly due in Tammuz, does the borrower receive an additional beit din collection period from Tammuz until Av? Rabbenu Tam answered that in any event a debtor who fixed a specific payment date receives no additional beit din period, because Rabbenu Tam follows the authority who holds that one who agreed to a fixed time cannot ask the court for further time afterward. Mordechai therefore does not explicitly resolve whether an early demand before maturity affects the later court period. The Shach understands the Rama to infer from Rabbenu Tam's response that according to the authorities who do grant a beit din period even after a fixed contractual date, the borrower in this case also receives that period beginning only when Tammuz arrives. This inference is not compelling. Rabbenu Tam may simply have answered the case according to the legal rule he considered controlling. Since he holds that a fixed maturity date itself eliminates the later court period, why should he discuss the separate question whether the earlier demand in Sivan counts toward it? Even a first demand in Tammuz would not help under his view. The Rama apparently understood that Rabbenu Tam could have answered more directly that the Sivan demand does not count toward a court period that begins only at maturity, a point that would also matter in an ordinary thirty-day loan. For example, if a creditor demanded payment on the second day of a standard thirty-day loan, would that early demand consume part of the later beit din period? Rabbenu Tam did not use that rationale. The Rama therefore inferred that the early demand does not count, and that Rabbenu Tam needed the separate rule that fixing a contractual date eliminates the later court extension. It follows that even though an ordinary unstated loan term is thirty days, if the creditor demands payment on the second day, the borrower still receives the full beit din collection period after the loan itself matures, resulting in as much as sixty days. Mordechai also cites Rashbam that no beit din period is granted for a deposit or similar property that must be returned in kind. Rashbam brings proof from the chapter HaShoel, where the Gemara says that a borrowed object is returned in kind and therefore the borrower does not come to write a document for the lender because there is no loan term. At first glance that passage concerns the ordinary thirty-day term of an unspecified loan, not a beit din collection period. What does it prove about the latter? A court period is not written into the original transaction at all. It is granted only if the debtor later asks the court for time. This might seem to imply that the beit din period and the ordinary thirty-day loan term are actually one concept, namely the standard time Chazal allow a borrower to obtain money. But that cannot be correct in light of the Rama's ruling and the general language of the poskim. Rather, Rashbam compares the two by analogy. Just as an item that must be returned in kind receives no automatic thirty-day loan term from the verse, because the object itself should simply be restored, so too there is no reason for beit din to grant an additional collection period when the very object exists and must be returned. The two rules are distinct but rest on the same logic.
