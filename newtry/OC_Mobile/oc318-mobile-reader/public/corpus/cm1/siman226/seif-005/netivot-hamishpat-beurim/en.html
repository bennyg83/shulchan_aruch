Even though the challenge was later nullified. Although the Rosh, cited by Sema, seif katan 11, writes that if a challenger says "I have witnesses" or "I have a deed," this is called a challenge, one must not misunderstand this to mean that even if it later turns out that he had no witnesses or deed at all, the sale is permanently void. If that were so, any buyer who later saw that the market price fell could always cancel his purchase by arranging for someone to appear and say, "I have witnesses" or "I have a deed." Moreover, the principal reason a challenge is treated as a defect in the transaction is that the buyer is immediately forced to litigate. As long as the challenger produces no witnesses, the buyer need not litigate with him, and there is no defect. Rather, to cancel the sale permanently there must be a substantive challenge, meaning that the challenger actually produces witnesses or a deed. Once that happens, even if it is later established that the witnesses lied or the deed was forged, the fact that the buyer was required to stand in litigation before being able to use the property peacefully is itself a defect. The Tur's statement that even a person who merely says "I have witnesses" is called a challenger applies only to the rule that, if the buyer has not yet paid, he need not release the purchase money from his possession until the challenge is disposed of by beit din.
