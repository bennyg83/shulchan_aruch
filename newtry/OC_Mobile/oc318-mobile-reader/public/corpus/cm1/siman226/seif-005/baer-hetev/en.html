The money. This is Rambam's wording. The Rosh and Tur, however, do not understand the case this way. They explain the Gemara as discussing a case where the purchaser has not yet paid the seller for the field but acquired it by another mode of acquisition. Rambam and the Mechaber understand the Gemara's phrase 'he trod the boundaries' to mean that the purchaser flattened the raised boundary ridges and made them level with the rest of the field, thereby improving the field, and that this act is the essential act of acquisition. The Rosh and Tur interpret it differently: the purchaser walks along the field's boundary to see whether the field is as the seller represented it and to examine what work it requires. According to them, if the purchaser has already paid, he cannot recover the money from the seller even if beit din sees a substantial basis for the adverse claim against the field, because the seller may say, 'Show me the judgment by which the property was taken from you, and I will pay you,' as the Rosh writes in the responsum cited by the Tur. It is surprising that Rama did not mention any of this here. Sema. Shach writes that Maharshal likewise rules this way in Bava Kamma, chapter 1, siman 19, and proves it from the Gemara. See there. See also Taz for his explanation of Rambam's words.
Entirely. The Rosh and Tur write this regarding a case where the purchaser has not yet paid. They explain that the adverse claim has not yet been established, but someone merely came and said, 'I have witnesses and a deed showing that the field is mine.' They further write that if the claim has been substantiated, meaning that the claimant has shown us his deed even though it has not yet been formally authenticated, then even if the purchaser already trod the boundaries, the money is not taken from the purchaser. Rama, however, places his ruling upon the Mechaber's case, where the money has already been paid but the purchaser has not yet used the property at all. It therefore appears to me that in such a case Rama holds that even if the adverse claim has been substantiated as described, the purchaser cannot retract and recover the money from the seller until the documents are fully authenticated. At that point, perhaps even Rambam agrees that the purchaser may retract and recover his money, since he has not yet used the property, even though the property has not yet actually been taken from him in court. Sema. See Taz for his explanation of Rama's words. See there.
He retracted. It appears that the matter does not depend upon the purchaser's unilateral retraction, but upon authorization from beit din. If the adverse claim is nullified before beit din grants him permission to retract, the purchaser can no longer withdraw. An exception is where the adverse claim was based on a debt instrument against the seller that is not presently available to be torn up. In that case, Sema writes that writing a receipt is insufficient. Sema.
