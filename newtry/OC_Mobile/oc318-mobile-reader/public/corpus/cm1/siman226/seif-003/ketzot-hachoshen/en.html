His own responsibility. In Perek Almanah Nizonet the Gemara asks whether a widow who sold property for her maintenance may later return and seize that property for her ketubah. Rav Yosef teaches that where a widow sells, the heirs bear the warranty, just as beit din sales carry warranty from the estate. The question is whether she may nevertheless recover the property because of that warranty, or whether the buyer can say, "You may not have accepted warranty for claims of others, but surely you accepted responsibility against your own claim." The Gemara proves that only where she left other property can she later collect the ketubah, implying that if she sold everything she cannot seize back from the buyer. This raises a difficulty from the rule in siman 104. If beit din sells land to satisfy an earlier creditor, a later creditor cannot seize it because the purchaser stands in the place of the earlier creditor's lien. Rashba says there is no distinction whether beit din transferred the land directly to the creditor or sold it to another on his behalf. A purchaser from the widow for maintenance should similarly stand in the place of the maintenance lien. Why then could she ever return and seize the property for her ketubah? According to the authorities who hold that an earlier oral loan does not prevail against a later written loan, the matter is simple. Maintenance resembles an oral obligation because a wife and daughters do not collect future maintenance from encumbered property. Her ketubah is a written obligation and can therefore precede it. But the accepted ruling in siman 104 gives priority to an earlier oral debt over a later written one in the relevant setting. We must therefore rely on a different distinction. Maintenance is rabbinic, while the principal ketubah obligation is biblical according to Tosafot and Rosh. An ordinary creditor therefore precedes future maintenance, as ruled in siman 97. So too, even though the buyer receives the maintenance lien, the ketubah can be superior and the widow can seize. This also explains why the Gemara asks specifically about property sold for maintenance rather than asking whether one who sold for part of her ketubah can seize again for the remainder. Shitah Mekubetzet says the Gemara could have asked that as well. According to the analysis here, it could not. A purchaser for part of the ketubah stands with the same ketubah lien, and where the claims have equal priority, what was already collected remains collected. But maintenance is a weaker rabbinic claim and can be displaced by the ketubah. Beit Yosef elsewhere suggests a different reason for preferring ordinary creditors to maintenance, namely that future maintenance is treated as a later debt. That would make the present sugya more difficult, because once she actually sold property for maintenance, the purchaser has already collected, and a later creditor does not generally undo a completed collection when both claims belong to the same person. The distinction that maintenance is rabbinic while ketubah is biblical therefore gives the cleaner explanation. Tosafot asks why this does not contradict Rami bar Chama, who had suggested elsewhere that even responsibility against oneself may not be implicitly accepted. According to what I have written, Rami bar Chama can hold the ketubah itself rabbinic. The two liens would then be equal, and the purchaser's earlier maintenance collection would stand without needing to assume an implicit personal warranty. This resolves Tosafot's question.
