(But the beit din evaluates, and so forth.) The Tur cites these words in the name of the Rosh, but there he sets out three distinctions. If the challenge consists only of words, where the challenger says that he will bring witnesses, then only after the purchaser has taken possession may he not retract, while before taking possession he may retract. If the challenger offers a plausible basis, such as saying, “I have a deed, but it has not yet been authenticated,” we wait thirty days for him to authenticate it; if he is a person of means, we wait longer, according to the judge's assessment. If there is no actual challenge before us, but merely a rumor that a challenge exists, then even before taking possession the purchaser may not retract. The Rama's wording here implies that where there is a plausible basis, if the purchaser has taken possession he may not retract. It is also difficult why the Rama omitted the rule that we wait thirty days, as the Tur writes. There is no difficulty. The Rama comments upon the Rambam, whose ruling the Mechaber copied, and who speaks of a case where the purchaser has already paid the money, as the Sema writes in seif katan 8. Therefore only two distinctions apply here. If there is an actual challenge, then after taking possession he may not retract. There is no place here to wait thirty days for the challenger to authenticate his deed, since the purchaser has already paid the seller. A plausible basis is effective only to permit the purchaser to retract before taking possession, in which case the seller returns his money. Without a plausible basis, he may not retract even before taking possession. This appears to me entirely clear. I saw one who disputes the Rama on the basis of the Tur's words cited above, but there is no substance to this objection, for the Tur's words are correct in their own context, as I have explained.
