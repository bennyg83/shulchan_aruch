That it shall belong to Shimon. See Sema, seif katan 20, who notes another practical difference. One should not say that even when a creditor collects from the buyer, the creditor need not pay the buyer for the improvement because he can say, “If you remain silent, good; otherwise I will return the deed to the original owner.” Returning the deed is effectively a resale back to the borrower. In siman 119, concerning intermediate or inferior land, the right depends on who owns the land. There the purchaser can say, “If you remain silent...” because the first purchaser transferred to the second every substantive right he had in the body of the field. Even if the field is reconveyed, the relevant property right travels with the land. Here, however, the creditor’s right to say, “My land produced the improvement,” depends not on the abstract ownership of the soil but on who is the litigant at the time of improvement. When the buyer held the land and improved it, the creditor can say the improvement arose on land already subject to his lien. Later resale to the borrower does not change the historical fact that the improvement occurred while the buyer held it. The borrower himself cannot be met with the argument “my land produced the improvement” in the same way, even if the land remains legally in the buyer’s title, whereas the buyer as litigant can be. Because this right does not inhere in the body of the field itself, the rule “what the first sold to the second was every right he had” does not apply, and the buyer cannot rely on “if you remain silent.” The same is true of removing the creditor by payment of money. If the law did not recognize the buyer as a proper litigant, merely handing the borrower money that still belongs to the buyer would not permit the borrower to remove the creditor unless the money was actually gifted to the borrower.
