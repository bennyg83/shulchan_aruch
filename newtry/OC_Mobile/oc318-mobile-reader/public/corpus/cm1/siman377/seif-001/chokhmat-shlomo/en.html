Seif 1, in the gloss. "A boundary path of which the public took possession with permission may not be ruined." Note: I have already written a lengthy responsum concerning a path of which the public took possession in my work on monetary law, third edition, responsum 43. It is clear to me that in our times the law of the kingdom governs this matter, and in many circumstances the old rule is no longer applicable; see my responsum there at length. It appears to me further that this rule applies only where use of the path is convenient for the public. Since the use is beneficial and convenient, one can say that the public acquired it by an act of possession or received it as a gift, and we advance for the public any plausible claim. But where the use is inconvenient, such as where houses stand on both sides and the open strip lies in the middle, while behind the houses there is only a narrow open place that can be used only with difficulty, use under hardship is not legally significant use. Such use does not establish a public right of possession. Since the rule itself is a novelty, it should not be extended beyond its stated scope. I also believe that the rule applies only where the majority of the residents of the place have used the path. If only a minority used it, it does not have the status of a path possessed by the public, and we should not expand the novelty further. There is also a conceptual reason. The Rashbam understands "a boundary path of which the public took possession" to mean that the public performed an act of possession and improved the path. In the earlier responsum I wrote that this is necessary when they now come to establish possession before us, whereas if they have already been using it we may presume that they originally performed a valid act of possession. At present it appears difficult: why should we presume that? In every doubt involving land we normally leave the land with its prior owner. Why should we assume that the public made a valid acquisition? It appears that the public is different, for the public is considered in possession as against an individual, and therefore we do not apply the ordinary presumption that land remains with its owner. But, as I wrote in that responsum, the principle that the public is treated as in possession against an individual applies only to the majority of the town. Two or three people are not treated as the public. In such a case the ordinary law returns, and absent proof they cannot dispossess the original owner. Even if some authorities disagree with the Rashbam, the land still remains in the possession of its owner in a case of doubt. This appears correct in practical law. This was the substance of my responsum to the community of Bozanov, although there I stated the ruling without fully explaining the reasoning. I explain it here. As to the window mentioned in the question, the Rama in siman 154 states that everything follows local custom. In our times, with stone construction, even without wooden frames the opening has the legal status of a window and is not merely a hole, and possession can be established through it. This is clear and correct.
