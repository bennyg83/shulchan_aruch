A public road passes through it, etc. See Baer HaGolah, letter 3, who notes that the Rambam concludes there: "What is the minimum width of a public road? Sixteen amot," and he wonders why the Mechaber omitted this. This implies that he understood the Rambam's concluding clause as explaining the opening clause, and from this one might infer that the Rama's rule concerning a boundary strip in which the public has established possession likewise applies only where they made an actual public road, meaning one sixteen amot wide. However, Responsa Penei Yehoshua, part 1, Choshen Mishpat, siman 4, rejects this in the case before him. He writes that one cannot say that "a boundary strip in which the public has established possession" means specifically a full public road sixteen amot wide, despite the apparent implication of the Rambam at the end of chapter 13 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon. The statement in the first chapter of Bava Batra concerns alleyways open through to another city, which the residents wished to close and which the residents of the other city could prevent them from closing because of Rav Yehudah's rule. An ordinary alley is certainly not sixteen amot wide. Moreover, had such a distinction existed, some posek would surely have recorded it, and there is further clear proof from the chapter Lo Yachpor. The Rambam's statement about sixteen amot instead concerns an ordinary public road sold or granted as such, as the Mechaber already wrote above in siman 217, seif 4, following the Mishnah as explained by Rashbam and Tur. Penei Yehoshua further discusses a community that bought land and a bathhouse from Reuven. Earlier, Shimon had bought adjacent land from Reuven and built a fence several amot away from the bathhouse, leaving a vacant strip between the fence and the bathhouse. The community also dug a well behind that vacant area and people crossed the open strip to draw water. Shimon later demolished the fence and excavated into the open area toward the bathhouse, claiming that his boundary actually extended to the bathhouse wall and that he had initially left the strip open voluntarily, producing both witnesses and a deed. Penei Yehoshua ruled that Shimon's claim was ineffective for two reasons. First, the rule stated in many places is that a boundary strip in which the public established possession may not be damaged. Although Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Batra write that this means they took possession with permission, this case too was with permission. Even if Shimon could prove that he acted under a mistake and that his waiver was mistaken, Tosafot cannot mean only an intentional, mistake-free grant, since their proof is from the chapter HaManiach, where the waiver itself was mistaken. Nimukei Yosef, in the chapter HaMokher Peirot in the name of Ramah, likewise writes that if the public took possession with the owner's knowledge, even through a mistake as in the Mishnah's case where he took the road and gave another at the side, it may not be damaged. Second, in this case the matter is even clearer because Shimon himself built the fence, thereby making a complete waiver, like the incident of Rav Anan who came and assisted in building the wall and thereby waived immediately. He cannot merely assert that his waiver was mistaken where there is no objective basis for such a mistake, for then every three-year chazakah could be challenged by saying that the owner mistakenly failed to protest because he did not know the land was his. This case is stronger than mere assistance because Shimon himself built the wall. It resembles the Rosh's ruling that one who admitted before witnesses is not believed to say he erred, even with a migo, because a person does not admit until he has carefully considered the matter. Certainly here, when he now produces his proof only after the fact, he is not believed. Although one could distinguish the case in Sanhedrin because there he had declared before beit din that he had no proof, a major concrete act such as fencing his own courtyard while leaving space for another is far stronger than mere words before beit din. Penei Yehoshua adds that one might initially argue for Shimon because Rashbam explains public possession as meaning that the public leveled and prepared the strip for passage, implying that passage alone does not create chazakah. One might also distinguish the open alleyways of Bava Batra because an alley is made for passage, so walking is its normal mode of use and acquires it, like a vineyard path. Yet this does not help Shimon here, because the community used the area as one uses a courtyard, not merely for walking but for all forms of use, and a thing is acquired through its ordinary mode of use. Furthermore, since all the poskim state simply that a public road may not be destroyed because the public established possession, without requiring a formal act of chazakah, one should not innovate such a requirement. Rashbam's words can also be read not as requiring a formal acquisition, but as explaining the expression that the public "took possession" by pressing and preparing the boundary strip. His novelty would then be that even before they actually walked there, once they performed an act on the strip it may not be damaged. Since Rashbam supplies no proof for a stricter requirement, the community cannot be dispossessed on that basis, especially since they dug a well in the land, which certainly constitutes acquisition of the entire area. In sum, the community unquestionably prevails. See there, and also part 2, siman 94, on this matter, and what I wrote below in siman 417, seif 2, seif katan 2.
