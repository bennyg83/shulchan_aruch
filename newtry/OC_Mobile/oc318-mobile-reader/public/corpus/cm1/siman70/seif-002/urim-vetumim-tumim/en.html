Shach wonders how Rama can cite such an opinion when the Gemara in Shevuot says that a matter a person was not expected to remember may simply escape his attention. It is difficult, says Shach, to distinguish a loan before witnesses from one without witnesses merely through a solitary reading of Rambam. But the view is not solitary. Raavad, as cited by Baal HaTerumot, and authorities cited by Gidulei Terumah explain the Gemara differently. When Rava says, "A matter that was not imposed upon him to remember is not in his mind," he does not mean the debtor is believed to say payment occurred contrary to witnesses. He means only that the debtor is not established as a confirmed liar merely because he inaccurately named the witnesses before whom he says he paid. If he later says, "I paid after that," he can still be believed on a new payment claim because his earlier mistake about the witnesses did not permanently disqualify him. Ramban gives a similar explanation. The case may involve other witnesses who actually testify to payment. We do not reject them merely because the debtor previously identified the wrong witnesses. He may have confused one set of witnesses with another because the identifying detail was not something he focused on. Under this reading, where there are no other payment witnesses, the debtor is not believed to overcome the original lender merely by saying, "I paid before Ploni and Ploni," when those named witnesses deny it. Rashba in responsum 961 cites these major authorities and says he does not feel able to decide against them. Thus Rama correctly presents the issue as a genuine dispute. Rambam's wording also supports a distinction between a definite undertaking and an informal recollection. If the debtor says decisively, "I paid you before Ploni and Ploni," one can understand him as accepting that evidentiary test: he rests his defense on what those witnesses will say. If they deny payment, he loses. But if he says, "Did I not pay you near a certain pillar?" or, "Was it not before Ploni and Ploni?" his words can be mere attempts to jog the creditor's memory, not a formal acceptance of those witnesses as decisive. In such a tentative formulation, the witnesses' denial does not establish him as a liar because people commonly confuse incidental details. This explains Rambam better than saying simply that the debtor remembers one context more strongly than another. Accordingly, Mechaber's concise formulation should be read with this dispute in mind. Where the debtor affirmatively bases his defense on named witnesses and they contradict him, several major authorities require payment. At minimum the matter is a safek din, and a possessor may say kim li like those authorities.
