And they say, "It never happened." He thought that he had paid before witnesses and was mistaken, because a matter a person is not expected to keep in mind is not necessarily remembered. Sema. The Shach objects: according to that reason, why should it matter whether he designated the witnesses? He therefore explains that the principle applies to the witnesses themselves. When they were not designated, they may not remember the event. When they were designated, they are expected to remember. The Shach concludes that neither the Mechaber's formulation nor Rama's gloss is fully primary, but both explanations are true: the matter may not have been kept in mind by either the borrower or the witnesses. Therefore, if the borrower says he did not designate the witnesses and they later come and contradict him, he is exempt even if he continues to insist that he clearly remembers paying before them. Although one can no longer say that the borrower himself forgot, it is still possible that the witnesses did not remember because they were not designated. Conversely, if he says that he did designate them and they come and contradict him, so the witnesses themselves are expected to remember, he may still later say, "I was mistaken," because the borrower himself may have failed to keep the matter in mind. But if he says he designated them and, after they come and contradict him, continues to insist that he still remembers clearly, then neither explanation applies: the witnesses were designated and he himself maintains certainty. He must therefore pay.
