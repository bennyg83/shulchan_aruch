And words. This is because unexpressed intent is merely words in the heart. But if, when receiving the money, the lender explicitly said that he was receiving it as a deposit, and the money was then lost through unavoidable circumstances, it is not payment. This is unlike the ruling in siman 120, where even if the lender expressly says that he will not accept the money as payment and the borrower throws it before him as payment, the borrower is discharged. The Sema explains that here the lender can say that so long as the witnesses are not present it is as though the due date has not arrived. The Taz challenges this because we rule that compelled payment is valid even before the due date, with a distinction only regarding a change in currency as stated in siman 74, seif 2. He therefore explains that the present ruling excludes only a case where the lender says nothing about the loan. If unexpressed intent is legally significant, it is as though the lender explicitly said he accepts the money as a deposit. Even if the borrower says he gave it as payment, so long as we do not see that he intended to force payment upon the lender, we presume that he too agreed with the lender's terms at the time of delivery. The Taz brings proof from the Gemara's rule that formerly a seller would hide for twelve months, and so forth. The Chacham Tzvi in his glosses rejects that proof but agrees with the practical ruling and writes that the Taz's reasoning is correct.
