And the words this one said. If the creditor expressly says that he does not accept the money as repayment but only as a deposit, and the money is then lost through unavoidable accident, the debt remains unpaid. Although repayment made against the creditor's will is generally valid, this case differs because until the designated witnesses are present the debt is treated as not yet due. The Sema, Bach, and Shach all explain the rule this way. There is an apparent difficulty. Siman 74 rules that where there is no concern of currency change or another prejudice, a borrower may generally repay even before the due date against the creditor's will. Why should this case be different merely because the agreed date has not yet arrived? The answer is that ordinary fixing of a term is primarily for the borrower's benefit. Since the creditor has no legitimate current prejudice, the borrower may waive his own benefit and pay early. Here, by contrast, the postponement is for the creditor's benefit, to prevent the borrower from evading proof of payment. A term created for the creditor's protection cannot be unilaterally waived by the borrower even after the original concern has practically disappeared. Bach writes the same principle in siman 74 where the term was fixed because of a risk of currency change benefiting the lender. Even if the risk later disappears, the borrower cannot force early payment because the term was originally established for the lender's benefit. The present case is analogous. This creates some tension with the Shach's disagreement with Bach in siman 74, because here the Shach himself relies upon the principle that the debt is still within its term. Nevertheless, the proper conceptual basis is that the term was created for the lender's protection, not merely that it is technically before maturity.
