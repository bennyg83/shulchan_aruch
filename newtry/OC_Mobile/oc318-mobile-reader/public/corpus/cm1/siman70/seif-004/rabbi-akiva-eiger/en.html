[Sema, letter 11] For he could say that he paid him before witnesses. Note: Knesset HaGedolah disputes the Sema on this point. According to the Ran’s reasoning, that there is an “we are witnesses” presumption, which is cited by the Sema in seif katan 13, here too the migo would stand against witnesses. See there. In my humble opinion, however, one who examines the Ran’s language will see that where the debtor cannot clarify the condition, there is no such presumption at all. At the time of payment he can rely on the fact that, should the lender later cast suspicion upon him, he can deny the entire matter by saying that it never occurred. This is clear. Although according to Knesset HaGedolah it would appear that if the lender admits that part was repaid privately, and the debtor has a migo that he could have said, “I never made the condition,” he is believed in either event. Even according to the Ran’s reason, that there is a presumption equivalent to witnesses, that reason is absent here, because once the lender admits that part was repaid, the presumption has been weakened. Mishneh LaMelech writes likewise according to the Ran. See there. See also the Bach in this siman, who explains the Ran’s reasoning that the “we are witnesses” presumption is like an ordinary chazakah, making this a migo against a chazakah, and we hold that a migo is not accepted against a chazakah, siman 78. His words are astonishing, for on the contrary, siman 78 treats a migo against a chazakah as an unresolved question, which results in stringency for the claimant and leniency for the defendant. See Shach, siman 118, seif katan 7. According to this, the Shach’s words are well explained, and the Knesset HaGedolah’s objection to the Sema, with which we began, is also removed. Examine this carefully.
