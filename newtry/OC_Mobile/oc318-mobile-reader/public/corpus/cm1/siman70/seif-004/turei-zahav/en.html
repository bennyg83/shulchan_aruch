(Seif 5) “He did not receive it as a deposit.” Rashi writes that although the creditor says that at the moment of receipt he intended merely to hold the money, unexpressed intentions have no legal effect. Rashi does not mean that an express statement by the creditor, “I accept this only as a deposit,” would necessarily make it a deposit and leave the debtor liable for accidental loss. The debtor’s designation controls, for when he gives the money as payment it is payment even against the creditor’s will, as ruled in siman 120. Rather, Rashi addresses a case in which nothing was said about the loan. If the creditor then expressly says that he accepts only as a deposit, the matter is no longer merely an unexpressed intention and it becomes a deposit. Were unexpressed intention legally effective, it would be as though he had expressly limited acceptance to a deposit. Although the debtor says that he gave the money as payment, when we do not see him insisting on forced payment we assume that he agrees to the creditor’s expressed limitation. The Sema writes that forced payment is ineffective here because the stipulated witnesses are absent, making the debt analogous to one not yet due. This is very difficult. The condition itself is no longer relevant, for Tosafot explain that Rav Nahman’s statement was jocular: the creditor’s concern was only to secure the debt against a false plea of payment, and once actual payment is made he suffers no loss. Nor is payment against the creditor’s will different before and after maturity, except regarding currency depreciation as discussed in siman 74. Once the creditor admits receipt, it is as though payment occurred before the stipulated witnesses. If he chooses to trust the debtor and accept private payment, he must accept it even against his will, just as he would if the named witnesses stood there. The enactment of Hillel concerning houses in walled cities also shows that a creditor cannot defeat repayment merely by hiding or by imposing a condition requiring payment before particular persons. The glossator answers that Hillel’s enactment concerned an ordinary sale, not a failed condition; if a genuine condition is not fulfilled, the transaction is void from the outset and the purchaser’s occupancy may itself involve interest. Nevertheless, the glossator agrees that the Taz’s practical ruling is correct for the independent reason given above.
