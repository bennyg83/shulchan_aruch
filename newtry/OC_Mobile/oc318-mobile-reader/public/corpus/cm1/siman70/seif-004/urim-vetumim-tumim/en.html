And they testified that he repaid before them, he is exempt. Shach argues at length for Rif and the many authorities who hold that even if other witnesses testify to repayment, the debtor is not thereby exempt where the lender had specified particular witnesses. In truth, this depends on the preceding dispute. If one holds that the debtor is not even believed to say, "I repaid before witnesses who later disappeared," then the Talmudic case where the lender said, "Do not repay me except before so-and-so and so-and-so," and the debtor paid before other witnesses must involve those other witnesses actually being present. Otherwise the debtor would already lose for lack of proof. On that reading, the Gemara teaches that even actual other witnesses do not help because the lender disqualified them for this debt. That supports Rif and Raavad. But according to Rambam's reading adopted by Mechaber, the debtor is believed to say the named repayment witnesses are now unavailable. The Gemara's case can therefore involve the debtor's own claim about other witnesses who are gone, rather than actual witnesses standing before beit din. Without the special condition he would have been believed. Once the lender named specific witnesses, however, the debtor cannot substitute a vague claim about others. If actual valid witnesses do appear and testify to payment, the debt can therefore be discharged. Thus Mechaber's ruling is coherent with Rambam's textual reading. According to Rif, however, Shach and Raavad are correct that the stricter result is more natural. The two laws are interconnected and should be treated consistently.
Nevertheless, the lender, and so forth. Shach objects to Rama's rule and asks where he obtained it. Mordechai states the law according to Rif, who holds that if the lender specified particular witnesses, other witnesses cannot establish repayment. Within that system, the lender may say the payment proven by other witnesses was for another debt. But Mechaber follows Rambam, who does not disqualify all other witnesses. Why then should the lender be able to say, "That payment was for a different obligation"? Rama can be understood in a narrower case. The witnesses merely testify that money was paid, but do not identify the debt for which it was paid. Ordinarily, a lender's claim of "that was payment on another debt" is relatively weak, as Rosh explains above in siman 58. A person does not usually ignore the debt currently being demanded and apply payment to some separate obligation. Here, however, the weakness runs the other way. The borrower had agreed, or was required, to pay this particular debt only before designated witnesses. Why would he deliberately pay it before other people and thereby expose himself to serious proof problems if the designated witnesses later died or disappeared? That conduct itself supports the inference that the witnessed payment related to another debt. Therefore the lender's "another debt" claim is materially stronger here. And since many authorities hold that the other witnesses are completely ineffective in any event, Rama can certainly allow the lender to make this allocation where the witnesses do not expressly identify the payment. If, however, the witnesses specifically testify that the money was paid on this very debt, the issue returns to the underlying dispute about whether other witnesses are valid at all.
Rather, he should pay him before beit din. Shach says witnesses alone are insufficient because they may die, leaving the debtor unable to prove payment. Nor can the debtor simply take a written receipt from the witnesses, because that would be testimony from writing. Taz says similarly. I do not understand this. If the lender is presently demanding payment, the debtor can refuse to pay until the witnesses write a proper receipt at the lender's instruction. Such a receipt is unquestionably valid. The real reason to advise payment before beit din is different. According to Rif and those who agree with him, even actual other witnesses may not help where the lender specified particular witnesses. If the lender later seizes property, he can say kim li like those authorities. Likewise, a dayan who ruled according to Rif would not necessarily have his judgment reversed. Why should the borrower voluntarily enter that dispute and leave his money exposed to a possible second collection? Payment before beit din eliminates the uncertainty. There is still another possible route: obtain a handwritten receipt from the lender himself. Perhaps the concern is that according to R. Sherira Gaon, discussed above, a handwritten instrument can require particularly strong authentication. The lender might later deny the signature, and the borrower may be unable to produce adequate proof. One could immediately authenticate the receipt in beit din, but once the parties are already going to beit din for that purpose, it is simpler and safer to make the payment there. Thus the rule is best understood as practical protection against a real halachic dispute, not as an absolute inability to document payment through witnesses.
