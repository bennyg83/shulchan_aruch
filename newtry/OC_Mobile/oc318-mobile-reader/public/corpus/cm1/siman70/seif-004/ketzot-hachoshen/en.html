He is not believed. The Sema writes that from here to the end of the siman the case is where the condition, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," was made before witnesses. Otherwise the borrower would be believed through a migo that no such condition was made. The Shach says Maggid Mishneh and Beit Yosef support this. Knesset HaGedolah disagrees and argues that even if the condition itself was not witnessed, once the borrower admits the creditor said it he cannot claim private repayment through a migo of denying the condition, because that is a migo against the strong presumption that after such a warning a borrower would not repay privately. Tosafot in Ketubot, however, appears to support the Sema. The Gemara seeks a case where Rabbi Yehoshua admits a person is believed through migo, such as where the claimant says, "You owe me a maneh," and the defendant says, "I repaid you." The Gemara does not use a case where the creditor had said, "Do not repay me except before so and so." Tosafot asks why, and answers that the Mishnah did not wish to lengthen its wording. This implies that if the condition itself lacked witnesses, the borrower would remain believed through the migo that the condition never occurred. One can nevertheless reconcile Tosafot with Knesset HaGedolah in a limited way. If the condition names specific witnesses, and the borrower claims, "I paid before those witnesses and they later went overseas," that is not a migo against overwhelming evidence. People may indeed repay before the designated witnesses without knowing they will later leave. In such a case, where no one can prove the condition, the borrower can be believed through a migo that there was no condition. But if the condition was simply, "Do not repay except before witnesses," and the borrower admits the warning yet claims he paid privately, that conduct is so contrary to ordinary behavior that the migo is weaker and may be treated as against witnesses in effect. This explains why Tosafot specifically formulate their question with designated witnesses. Thus the Sema's broad statement can be narrowed: where the claim of repayment is one that ordinary people might actually make despite the condition, a migo can help if the condition was unwitnessed; where he claims a private repayment directly contrary to the admitted warning, Knesset HaGedolah has stronger grounds to reject the migo. See siman 139 for a comparable migo concerning a rental allegedly made before named witnesses who later departed.
I repaid you before others. In Shevuot Rava explains that when a creditor says, "Do not repay me except before Reuven and Shimon," the purpose is to prevent the borrower from later evading him. Rashi explains that if the borrower produces other witnesses, the creditor can say those witnesses are false. Tosafot asks how the creditor can simply disqualify valid witnesses. Rabbeinu Yitzchak therefore explains that the borrower claims, "I paid before witnesses, but they went overseas." The borrower cannot defeat the special condition through unavailable witnesses. Rashi's explanation can nevertheless be understood by treating the stipulation as a clause of special credibility. The creditor effectively says, "You shall not be believed to say payment occurred until you repay before Reuven and Shimon." This resembles granting the creditor credibility equal to two witnesses until the designated payment occurs. Once the borrower actually pays before Reuven and Shimon, however, that special credibility ends immediately. Tosafot's precise language supports this: the creditor is believed "until I repay before Reuven and Shimon," not until Reuven and Shimon later come and testify. Thus if the borrower says, "I paid before Reuven and Shimon and they went overseas," he should be believed once the act itself occurred, according to this reading. Rosh, however, phrases the rule as continuing until Reuven and Shimon testify. Rabbeinu Yerucham similarly rules that even if other witnesses testify that payment occurred before Reuven and Shimon, that does not help. These authorities understand the condition to require the testimony of Reuven and Shimon themselves, not merely the actual payment in their presence. Tosafot's wording points to the more lenient interpretation.
I will not repay you. Taz asks why the borrower must repay before beit din. Why are ordinary witnesses insufficient, especially since other witnesses can sometimes prove the designated payment? He answers that the borrower needs written proof, and testimony in writing would otherwise raise the rule of oral testimony rather than written testimony. The Shach gives a similar answer. This is difficult. Siman 81 makes clear that in a waiver or release there is no need to say, "Write," because it is obviously beneficial to the released party that the matter be recorded. Rama says the same in siman 39 concerning matters clearly to the litigant's benefit. Ran explains that an admission concerning land can be written without express instruction because the obligor is certainly content with the writing. The same should apply to a release from a special repayment condition. Therefore ordinary witnesses could prepare written proof, and the question returns. The correct explanation is that a condition, "Do not repay me except before Reuven and Shimon," makes the debt effectively not yet due until those witnesses are present. The Sema writes that the creditor can refuse payment until they arrive. If the creditor can delay receipt, all the more so the borrower can say, "I will not pay until the agreed payment time arrives." The only way to compel present payment is therefore through beit din, because the original condition did not exclude beit din. Beit din stands in place of the designated witnesses for purposes of judicial payment. The Tur in siman 71, citing Mahari of Trani, explains that although the condition can disqualify ordinary witnesses, it does not disqualify beit din unless expressly stated. Beit Yosef here likewise says repayment before beit din works for this reason. Thus the need for beit din is not about written proof. It is because until the specified witnesses are available the contractual due date has not arrived, and beit din provides an alternative mode not barred by the stipulation.
