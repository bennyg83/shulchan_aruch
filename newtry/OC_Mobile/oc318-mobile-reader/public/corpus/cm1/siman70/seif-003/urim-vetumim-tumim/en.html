Sema cites Ran that even if the borrower does not expressly agree, once the lender says, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," the borrower must comply. We are witnesses that once the lender openly says he does not trust private repayment, the borrower has no reason to create a dispute by paying without proof. Shach says the borrower can claim kim li like Ramban and Rosh, who hold that if the lender imposes this condition only after the loan, silence is not enough. Unless the borrower expressly agrees, he may later say, "I ignored your warning." Shach is correct that the burden of proof normally rests on the claimant where major authorities disagree. However, his wording seems to imply that even at the time of the loan the borrower can defeat the condition by expressly protesting. I do not understand how that case exists. If the lender says before giving the money, "I lend only on condition that you repay before witnesses," and the borrower protests, yet the lender nevertheless hands over the money without restating anything, the lender may indeed have accepted the protest. But if the borrower first protests and the lender then gives the money while again stating the condition, the borrower's acceptance of the money is itself acceptance of the loan terms. Silence at the moment of receiving the funds is equivalent to agreement. Thus a true dispute is more natural when the condition is imposed only later. I would add that even after the original loan, once the debt becomes due, the lender can sometimes say: "Either pay me now, or from this point onward do not repay except before witnesses." The borrower cannot necessarily refuse both options. At maturity the lender is entitled to immediate payment. If he grants additional time only on condition of witnessed repayment, that is functionally like a new extension of credit on stated terms. There is support from Rosh's discussion of ketubah. According to Rif, R. Yochanan's rule that one who pleads payment against a beit din condition is not believed applies in a place where ketubot are not written. Rosh asks what practical need there is for the rule, since a woman can warn her husband at divorce, "Do not pay me except before witnesses." Why would that warning help if the husband could simply protest and privately pay later? The answer must be that at the moment the obligation becomes presently collectible she can demand either immediate payment or future witnessed payment. His protest cannot force her to accept an unsecured private-payment arrangement. This is similar to the end of a loan term. One might say Rosh speaks specifically of a warning made before beit din. But not every ketubah is collected through a formal judicial proceeding, and Rosh's reasoning is broader than a final judgment case. The distinction is therefore substantive, not merely based on location. There is also a related question regarding a loan originally made without witnesses. If the lender later imposes a witnessed-payment condition at maturity, perhaps the borrower can say: "You trusted me completely from the outset, even to deny the loan itself. You cannot now unilaterally change the evidentiary terms." That argument has more force than in a loan originally made before witnesses, where the lender never gave such complete trust. The issue therefore may depend on the original structure of the loan. Shach further says that if the lender makes the warning before beit din, everyone agrees it works even against the borrower's protest, citing Rosh and Tur. That too needs qualification. Rosh's case concerns a person already adjudicated liable by beit din. The reason is that after forcing the creditor to litigate, the debtor cannot undermine the judgment through unproved private payment. But according to Shach's own ruling elsewhere, a final judgment may already block a simple payment claim without any special warning. A mere warning uttered in beit din, without an actual adjudication, is not necessarily the same. Thus the strongest practical rule is: At the time of the original loan, an expressly stated witnessed-payment condition is binding when the borrower accepts the money. At maturity, the lender can likely condition any extension on witnessed repayment. A later unilateral warning during the running loan, without the borrower's agreement and without a new extension or judgment, remains disputed between Ran and the stricter authorities on one side and Ramban and Rosh on the other. The possessor can invoke the relevant major opinion where necessary.
And I repaid you before so-and-so and so-and-so, and so forth. Bach writes that this works only if the debtor names the witnesses specifically. If he merely says, "I repaid you before witnesses," he is not believed. Bach explains that when the debtor identifies particular witnesses, he is afraid to lie. If they merely went overseas, they may return and contradict him. Even if they died, other witnesses may eventually testify that they were with those people continuously and no repayment occurred. But if he names no one, there is no comparable fear. Shach agrees with Bach. I am surprised. Rosh and Ran ask why a debtor who says, "I repaid you privately, without witnesses," should not be believed through a migo that he could have said, "I repaid you before so-and-so and so-and-so, but they went overseas or died." According to Bach's reasoning, that migo is weak. If the debtor merely says, "I repaid before witnesses," without identifying them, Bach says he is not believed. But if he identifies them by name, he becomes afraid of eventual contradiction. Then the supposed alternative claim is not truly available to him. And if a vague claim of repayment before unnamed witnesses is not believed, then certainly an admission of private repayment should not gain credibility through that route. It therefore appears that Bach's distinction can be maintained only within the stricter approach of Ramah and Rashba, but Rosh and Ran disagree. Shach himself, in the preceding seif katan, cites Rosh, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh. He should not then state Bach's rule here as though undisputed. For practical halachah, the possessor can certainly say kim li like Rosh, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh. Perhaps Shach did not actually rely on Bach's theory of fear. He may instead have intended Beit Yosef's reason: if the debtor truly repaid before witnesses, why does he not know who they were? A vague claim is therefore factually weaker. But if so, he should not have cited Rashba's explicit fear-based rationale as though it were the same thing.
He is believed. See Shach, who discusses at length whether the reading of Rif and those who agree with him should be followed, under which the debtor is not believed. The matter remains balanced enough that the possessor may perhaps say kim li. There is one case where all should agree. Suppose the borrower never expressly agreed to the lender's condition, and we impose the witnessed-payment requirement only because of the view of Ri Migash and Ran that once the lender openly insists on witnesses, we presume the borrower would not expose himself to the risk of private repayment. If the borrower later says, "I did repay before witnesses, namely so-and-so and so-and-so, but they are now gone," he should be believed. The rationale requiring witnesses was never a formal contractual condition accepted by him. It was only an evidentiary presumption that a reasonable borrower would comply with the lender's demand. He now says he did exactly that. Rambam's question applies with force: what more could he have done? He cannot imprison the witnesses to ensure their future availability. Shach's answer that the borrower is bound by the condition does not fit this case, because there was no actual condition accepted by the borrower. Sema writes that this entire seif concerns a condition made before witnesses, because otherwise the borrower has a migo that no such condition was ever made. He refers to Derishah and apparently to Maggid Mishneh, but I do not find this in Maggid Mishneh. If anything, Maggid Mishneh points the other way. Maggid Mishneh asks why the borrower should not be believed to say, "I paid you privately," through a migo that he could have said, "I paid before named witnesses who are now gone." He answers that once the borrower accepted a condition requiring witnessed repayment, he must satisfy that condition and an ordinary migo does not replace it. If the condition itself was unwitnessed and the borrower now admits that it was made, the same logic applies. He still accepted it. And if we understand Maggid Mishneh, as Shach does, to mean that once the lender openly insisted on witnesses we treat private repayment as inherently implausible, then an unwitnessed condition creates the same presumption. Thus the borrower's migo of denying the condition does not necessarily help. It is a migo against the very evidentiary structure created by his admitted conduct. Maggid Mishneh's objection to Raavad is narrower. Raavad explains the case of "I repaid before so-and-so and so-and-so and they are gone" as one where the lender never conditioned witnessed repayment. Maggid Mishneh asks: if there was no such condition, then of course the debtor's migo of denying the condition should work. Why would any Talmudic opinion reject him? That is a valid question. But both Raavad and Maggid Mishneh agree that where the debtor admits a binding witnessed-payment condition, a claim of private repayment is not rescued merely by a migo. Accordingly, under Mechaber's adopted reading, the clean distinction is: If the debtor says he repaid before the required witnesses and they are unavailable, he can be believed. If he says he repaid without witnesses despite the condition, he is not believed merely because he could have denied the condition.
