Whether he said it to him at the time of the loan. See Shach, seif katan 9, who writes that even at the time of the loan, if the borrower protests, he need not repay in the presence of witnesses. At first glance this is difficult. If the borrower protests and the lender nevertheless gives him the money, the lender has accepted that arrangement, and according to everyone the borrower should not be required to repay before witnesses. Tumim likewise asks this. There is no difficulty. The Rosh writes that where the warning was not given at the time of the loan, because the lender originally trusted the borrower, the borrower did not now concern himself to respond. This implies that even if the money has already been delivered before witnesses and the lender then imposes the condition that repayment may be made only before witnesses, while the witnesses are still present, this is still considered the time of the loan. Although the lender cannot recover the money once the borrower has acquired it, if the borrower remains silent, his silence is an admission that he accepted the money on that condition, as the Rosh writes. But if he protests, there is no acceptance. Tumim appears to understand that if the due date arrives and the lender says, "Pay me immediately, or do not repay me except before witnesses," this too is considered like the time of the loan. In my view this is incorrect, because the Rosh's reasoning in Bava Metzia 17 still applies: since the lender trusted him initially, the borrower did not feel compelled to respond now and did not accept the warning. This is no different from the Rosh's discussion there, where he asks what the Sages gained by their enactment, since a woman could warn her husband at the time of divorce, "Do not repay me except before witnesses." One might ask what such a warning accomplishes if he can protest, and infer the above rule. But in truth there is no proof, because the Rosh wrote that according to Rambam's view, under which a protest is ineffective. As to Maadanei Yom Tov's question on the Rosh, that if so, why does the Gemara ask regarding a widow what the Sages gained by their enactment, since she could warn the heirs, the answer is simple. With a widow there is concern that the husband set aside bundles of money for payment when he saw his end approaching. A warning by the widow therefore does not help, because the heirs can say that their father had already designated money for her payment. A warning at the time of betrothal or marriage is also not applicable, as Tumim himself writes. Divorce is different. We do not ordinarily suspect that payment was set aside, and according to many poskim we do not suspect it even if the husband died suddenly. Thus the Rosh properly gives the remedy of warning at the time of divorce that payment be made only before witnesses.
He is believed. See Shach, seif katan 14, who concludes that the party in possession may say that he follows Rif, who holds that the borrower is not believed and must fulfill the condition, since it is as though he expressly stipulated that the borrower would not be believed without witnesses. According to this, if the borrower protested when the warning was given after the time of the loan, his obligation does not arise from a binding condition. The only basis for requiring witnesses is Ran's reasoning that there is a chazakah that one would not repay without witnesses, because why would a person place himself into such a dispute by paying privately? Therefore, if the borrower claims that he did repay before witnesses but those witnesses later died, everyone agrees that he is believed. Tumim likewise writes this, and it is straightforward.
