[A.A. From here to the end of the S.M., to the witnesses of D.D., he was faithful in his body, and he did not speak with him
Whether he said, etc. The Sema cites the Ran and Maggid Mishneh as understanding the Rambam to mean that even if the debtor expressly protests, “I do not wish to repay only before witnesses,” he must nevertheless repay before witnesses. In my view, nothing in the Rambam compels this. His language, that the debtor must repay before witnesses “because of the condition,” implies mutual acceptance, as with other conditions. The Rosh and Tur expressly rule that whether the stipulation was made at the time of the loan or afterward, an express protest by the debtor leaves him free to repay without witnesses. Rabbenu Yerucham cites Tosafot and several decisors likewise. Although we do not follow them in requiring express acceptance after the loan, because silence is sufficient, their ruling regarding express protest remains persuasive. The Ramban also states it expressly. The Mishnah and Rambam speak of a silent debtor. Moreover, the Ran explains that the stipulation is not based upon a new obligation, but upon the factual presumption that after being warned to pay only before witnesses, the debtor would not pay privately. That presumption does not apply where he expressly rejected the warning from the outset and may say that he relied on his protest. If the creditor warned him before beit din to pay only before witnesses, even the Rosh, Tur, and Rabbenu Yerucham agree that an express protest does not help, since the debtor had already forced the creditor to bring him before beit din. Yet even this may require study, for how can one person unilaterally compel another to accept a condition?
"And if he said he was not in favor and forgot about it. He said, “If you don’t forget, you are not faithful.”
He is not believed. He is not believed through a migo that he could have said, “I fulfilled your condition and paid before the named witnesses, but they went overseas or died.” This is not a good migo. It is more convenient for him to say, “I paid you privately and forgot your warning that I must pay before witnesses,” than to say that he paid before named witnesses who then died, for people say that one who wishes to lie places his witnesses far away. This is the Rosh’s explanation. The Maggid Mishneh answers that there is no migo because the debtor admits that he did not fulfill the condition. Since he obligates himself to fulfill it and acknowledges that he did not, and payment has not been established, we require him to carry out his condition. This answer requires study. The Beit Yosef offers another explanation: once the creditor said, “Do not pay me except before witnesses,” we are themselves witnesses that the debtor would not have paid privately, and where there is such an evidentiary presumption no migo applies. Perhaps this was also the Maggid Mishneh’s intent. See what I wrote below in siman 133, seif 2, seif katan 2.
The creditor collects from him without an oath, etc. This refers only to an oath before collection. After the debtor pays, he may impose a hesset oath upon the creditor, as is clear from siman 71, seif 5. Certainly the same applies here.
Before so-and-so and so-and-so, etc. This implies that the debtor is believed only when he identifies particular witnesses and says that he paid before them. If he merely says that he paid before witnesses who later went overseas or died, he is not believed, because failure to identify them indicates falsehood. This follows the Ramah and Tur, and the Bach writes that the language of the Gemara implies it. The Rashba also writes this. If, however, there are circumstances indicating that he genuinely does not know or has forgotten who the witnesses were, he appears exempt according to the version that rules him believed. The Ran disagrees. See there.
He is believed, because he says that he fulfilled the condition. The Rif and Ri Migash disagree even here, and Responsa Ramban, siman 97, and the Raavad agree with them. Although the Maggid Mishneh challenges the Raavad’s interpretation that the version “he is believed” speaks where there were no witnesses to the stipulation, he raises no objection to the substantive ruling according to the version “he is not believed.” Hagahot Ashri likewise states that this is the practical halachah: the debtor must pay now, and if the named witnesses later come and testify that he paid, the creditor must return the money. This appears to me the primary reading of the Gemara. The Rosh and Ran say that Rav Asi concedes to Shmuel when the debtor says, “I paid before so-and-so and so-and-so, but they went overseas.” That is difficult. If Rav Asi and Shmuel agree in that case, why did Rav Pappa in Rava’s name need to state a separate ruling about it, particularly when the phrase “the halachah is” appears to apply there as well? Further, if Shmuel disagreed only when the debtor says that he paid privately and relied upon a migo, the Gemara should have said expressly that because he could have claimed payment before absent witnesses, he may also claim private payment. Instead, Shmuel says directly that he may claim payment before named absent witnesses. The baraita cited against Shmuel states broadly that one told to pay before witnesses “must pay or bring proof that he paid,” which indicates that no unsupported claim suffices. The dispute of the tannaim likewise suggests that the first tanna requires actual proof, whereas Rabbi Yehudah ben Beteira allows the claim of payment before absent witnesses. Rav Pappa’s two formulations also cannot reasonably contradict one another, first saying that one warned to pay before witnesses must do so, and immediately afterward saying that a claim of payment before witnesses who left the country is believed. The better reading is therefore “he is not believed.” Rav Asi’s statement means that in every form the debtor must establish payment through witnesses. Shmuel allows the particular claim that payment was made before named witnesses who are now unavailable. The Mishnah’s unqualified ruling that the debtor is liable refutes Shmuel, and the attempted tannaitic resolution is rejected. Rav Pappa therefore rules in accordance with Rav Asi and the first tanna. The Rambam’s objection, asking what the debtor was supposed to do if the witnesses died or departed and whether he must imprison them forever, is not decisive. One cannot reject a tannaitic and Amoraic rule merely by such reasoning. The debtor accepted the condition and can protect himself by obtaining a written receipt, as the Raavad explains. Thus the law appears clear in principle, although because many authorities disagree and the debtor is in possession of the money, practical application requires study.
