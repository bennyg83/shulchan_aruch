Whether he said it at the time of the loan. See Tumim. If at the time of the loan the lender stipulated, "Do not repay me except in the presence of witnesses," and the borrower remained silent, his silence constitutes acceptance according to all opinions, as the Rosh writes. If the borrower protested and said, "I do not accept this condition," and the lender nevertheless gave him the money, the lender accepted the borrower's refusal, and the borrower need not repay specifically before witnesses. When the condition is imposed after the loan, however, there is a dispute. Ramban and Rosh hold that even if the borrower remains silent he need not repay before witnesses unless he expressly accepts the condition. No kinyan is required, only acceptance. Ran writes in the name of Ri Migash and Rambam that even if the borrower protests, he is not believed to claim that he repaid without witnesses, because we are witnesses to the practical reality that once he saw the lender insisting upon witnessed repayment, he would not repay otherwise. This is therefore a doubt in law, and the burden of proof rests upon the one seeking to extract money. See Tumim. I concluded that if the loan was for a fixed term and when the term expired the lender said, "Either pay me immediately or do not pay except before witnesses," all agree that this is equivalent to a condition made at the time of the loan. The borrower cannot reject it and must repay before witnesses. Likewise, if the lender sued him and beit din entered judgment, and in the presence of beit din the lender said, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," then even according to the view that one may ordinarily claim payment of a beit din judgment, in this case he certainly is not believed. See Tumim.
And if he says that he repaid without witnesses, etc. Why does he not have a migo that he could have said, "I repaid you before witnesses and they died"? The Rosh answers that such a claim looks false, because one who wishes to lie normally distances his supposed witnesses beyond verification. Ran and Maggid Mishneh give another reason: there is a presumption that he did not repay in defiance of the lender's explicit insistence on witnesses, so this would be a migo against a presumption. See Tumim. The difference between the two explanations is where the lender imposed the condition that the borrower not repay without witnesses, the borrower accepted it, and now admits that he paid without witnesses. According to the Rosh's reason, the borrower is believed through the migo, because that migo is otherwise sound. According to Ran and Maggid Mishneh, and likewise Raavad, he is not believed, because the same presumption applies. Therefore the Sema's categorical ruling in seif katan 11 that he is believed by this migo requires study.
Without an oath. This means before payment. Afterward the borrower may sue in beit din, "You owe me one hundred because I paid you twice," and the lender then owes a hesset oath like any ordinary defendant. So writes the Shach.
In the presence of so and so and so and so, etc. The Shach writes that the borrower must identify the witnesses by name, and it is not enough to say generally, "I paid before two witnesses and they died." See Tumim, where I note that the Rosh, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh disagree.
He is believed. The Shach discusses at length the view of the Rif and those who follow him, who read the Gemara as ruling that the borrower is not believed. A defendant in possession may therefore claim that the law follows those authorities. This requires study. Nevertheless, if the lender stipulated, "Do not repay me except before witnesses," and the borrower expressly protested, even those authorities who otherwise hold that the borrower is still required to repay before witnesses agree that he is believed to say, "I repaid before witnesses and they died." See Tumim.
