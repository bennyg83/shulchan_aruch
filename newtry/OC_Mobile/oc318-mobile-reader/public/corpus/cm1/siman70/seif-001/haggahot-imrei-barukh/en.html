(Shach, seif katan 16) If so, why engage in pointless mutual payments? Rather, certainly, etc. Note: According to what is explained above in siman 33:12 in the Rama, and in the Shach there, seif katan 9, one may say the following. Even according to those who hold that after the lender is repaid the borrower may sue him on the basis of the same witnesses, treating the first payment in their presence as a new loan, this applies only where the borrower did not produce those witnesses before repayment. When the lender denies payment, the borrower pays him again before witnesses or beit din, and afterward sues him on the basis of the witnesses who saw the first payment, which can then be treated as a loan. This explains the Gemara's phrase, "if he is clever." But if the witnesses already testified before the second payment and their testimony was rejected because of the condition requiring payment before specified witnesses, it is as though the borrower expressly disqualified them. Their testimony cannot later be accepted after repayment, because we say that they are merely seeking to uphold their first statement. In such a case the argument of pointless mutual payments does not apply. The Maggid Mishneh's point can also be understood as follows. Why assume that every condition, "do not repay me except before so-and-so and so-and-so," disqualifies other witnesses? As elsewhere the holder of a document has the weaker hand, and the same should apply to ambiguous conditions in a document or oral stipulation. Perhaps the intent was only that the borrower not be able to claim that he paid before other witnesses who later left or died, as Rabbeinu Tam and the Rambam understand the Gemara's expression concerning rendering them absent. For this reason the Ran preferred Rabbeinu Tam and the Rambam's view over the Rosh and his associates. According to the Rif, however, if we say that the lender ordinarily intended by this condition to disqualify all other witnesses, why should we not also say that he ordinarily intended to disqualify them concerning the very act of handing over the money? Without such a disqualification, if the witnesses falsely testify that payment was received when in truth it was not, the lender's only way to protect himself would be to make a false claim that he did receive the money but applied it to another debt. He himself knows that he received nothing and that there was no other debt. See Milchamot Hashem, which had to devise a reason why we do not say that the condition also included disqualification of witnesses who testify to the delivery of the money itself.
