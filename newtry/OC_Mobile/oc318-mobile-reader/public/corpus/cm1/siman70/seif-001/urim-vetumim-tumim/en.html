And even to a Torah scholar, and so forth. The Gemara Bava Metzia 75 says, "All the more so the master, who is preoccupied with his learning." This seems to imply greater concern specifically with a Torah scholar, whereas Mechaber says one should use witnesses even with a Torah scholar. Knesset HaGedolah, citing Maharshal, asks another question: what do witnesses solve? The borrower can still later say "I paid." One could answer that the lender should lend before witnesses and stipulate, "Do not repay me except before witnesses." But the poskim did not say so, and it is strained to assume such an unstated condition. The true concern is not deliberate denial. We ordinarily do not suspect a borrower of brazenly saying, "You never lent me anything," because a person does not easily act with such brazenness before his creditor. That principle is why one who denies everything can sometimes avoid a Torah oath. The concern is forgetfulness. The borrower may later say, "I do not know whether you ever lent me." This is especially plausible for a Torah scholar absorbed in his studies, which is exactly the Gemara's expression. If the loan was made without witnesses and the borrower says, "I do not know whether you lent me," he can be exempt in human court. If witnesses saw the loan, however, he cannot use that uncertainty. At most he can say, "I do not know whether I repaid," which ordinarily obligates payment. We do not fear that he will respond with a deliberate false denial against known witnesses. Thus lending before witnesses directly addresses the real concern. This also explains the Gemara's specific case where the borrower asked that ten zuz be sent through a messenger. If the creditor uses a trusted messenger and declares the messenger credible or structures the agency properly, the messenger can itself create evidentiary force. If the borrower later says he does not know whether the money arrived, the known delivery can still establish the debt. The phrase "all the more so the master who is preoccupied with learning" then addresses not the prohibition of placing a stumbling block before someone likely to lie, but the social harm of suspicion and dispute when a scholar may genuinely forget. For ordinary people, the principal concern can be stronger: if they later say they do not know, they may not voluntarily satisfy the debt beyond strict law. A Torah scholar will ordinarily fulfill a moral obligation even where beit din cannot compel him. Thus there is no contradiction between the Gemara and Mechaber. Witnesses are advisable for everyone, and the precise concern varies with the borrower's circumstances.
