And he is also believed to say, "I paid." Shach notes that Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 47, explicitly says the debtor is not believed to say "I paid" regarding this type of defective document. It is indeed surprising that Mechaber does not mention Baal HaTerumot. The explanation lies in the reason each authority gives for the ordinary rule that a debtor may say "I paid" against his own handwriting. Ramban explains that because handwriting does not collect from encumbered property, the debtor is less careful to recover the paper after payment. Rosh gives a different reason: a person can simply forget that he once issued a private handwritten obligation. Baal HaTerumot compares the present defective document to an antedated note and says that just as the debtor cannot say "I paid" against that note, he cannot say it here. Gidulei Terumah already asks that the analogy is weak. An antedated formal note may still appear valid because its defect is not always obvious. From the true later date it was also capable of being written as an ordinary document, and its purchaser-facing character remains partially recognizable. The debtor therefore remains careful with it. Our present document, by contrast, was never legally capable of being written as a valid instrument, for example because the seller had already transferred the property to his minor son before executing the sale paper. The defect is objective and discoverable. According to Ramban's reason, this is decisive. Since such a paper cannot collect from purchasers, the borrower or obligor has less reason to preserve it after payment, and the ordinary payment defense should remain available. According to Rosh's reason, however, the result can differ. One does not simply forget a dramatic event such as deliberately arranging an earlier transfer to a minor child or creating a document under coercive circumstances. The very history of the defective instrument is memorable. Therefore Rosh's rationale supports Baal HaTerumot's stricter result. Baal HaTerumot elsewhere appears to adopt Rosh's explanation of handwritten obligations. Thus his position is internally consistent. Mechaber, however, follows Ramban's explanation above in siman 69, as Shach himself notes. He therefore need not follow Baal HaTerumot's analogy here and can allow the payment defense. Tur also presents no contradiction. Tur follows Rosh's general rationale but rules that even an antedated note can allow a payment claim once Chazal removed its ordinary document status. All the more so here, where the paper was never validly writable. There is another layer concerning documents created under coercion. The Gemara says a statement that "our words were a moda'ah" can sometimes be believed because the document was "capable of being written." Ran gives two explanations. One is that the sale document could be written to rescue the coerced party from immediate danger. Under that reading, the paper was never written for the buyer's substantive benefit and can remain mere paper against him. The other is that it could be written so the buyer would at least have evidence to recover the purchase money. Baal HaTerumot likely emphasizes the second explanation. If the document was legitimately written to protect the buyer's money, it has some real documentary status, much like an antedated note, and the debtor should not casually leave it outstanding after payment. Mechaber elsewhere accepts that the witnesses can say a moda'ah existed even where their signatures are independently established. That is easier if "capable of being written" refers to a separate written moda'ah rather than substantive validity of the forced sale document itself. On that approach, the sale paper remains fundamentally defective. This further supports Mechaber's lenient payment rule. Thus Mechaber's omission of Baal HaTerumot is deliberate and follows his broader theory of why handwritten or defective documents permit a payment defense.
And the same applies to every document that was not legally capable of being written. One may ask from the rule below in siman 373 concerning a person who sells stolen land. If the true owner later takes the land from the purchaser, the purchaser can recover from the seller through the sale document, even against encumbered property. Yet at the time of sale the seller had no right to convey the land, so why is this a document that could validly be written? The Gemara itself answers that the seller wants to maintain credibility with the purchaser. He hopes later to acquire the land from the true owner and thereby validate the transaction. The document therefore serves a legitimate present purpose and is considered capable of being written. This also resolves the apparent contradiction in Tosafot Bava Metzia 14. Tosafot say that when the true owner takes the stolen field, the purchaser's claim for his money is a debt evidenced by a formal document. Yet in the case of the orchard, Tosafot describe the money claim as merely oral. The difference is that in the stolen-field case the original sale document was legitimately writable for the credibility reason the Gemara gives. Still, there is a deeper issue. The Gemara says a sale before witnesses ordinarily creates enough publicity to collect from encumbered property. It asks why an oral loan should not do the same and answers that people publicize sales but borrow discreetly. Now suppose a seller first transferred the field to his minor son and later sold it again. The witnesses to the second sale may not know of the earlier transfer. Their public testimony that a sale occurred still creates publicity. Even if the resulting sale document is legally invalid, why should the purchaser's money claim become no more than an ordinary oral loan? The public sale occurred. Tosafot therefore understand the Gemara's statement "this document was not capable of being written" as doing more than merely invalidating the paper. It reduces the money claim to the status of an ordinary oral obligation, including the ordinary payment defense. Purchasers can therefore say perhaps the seller already repaid the buyer. Shitah Mekubetzet likewise explains that the central question is whether payment is pleaded for purchasers once the sale paper has no valid documentary standing. Baal HaTerumot follows a different view of the rule that a sale before witnesses collects from purchasers. He limits it to a sale in which property warranty was expressly stated. Without that, mere public sale is not enough. Within his system, once the written instrument is invalid, there is no purchaser-facing lien regardless of any payment defense. He can therefore maintain that the original obligor himself still cannot say "I paid." But our halachah does not follow Baal HaTerumot's restriction regarding a witnessed sale. A public sale can create purchaser-facing effect more broadly. Accordingly, Tosafot's logic becomes necessary: if the document was not legally capable of being written, the obligation is reduced to oral-loan status and the debtor can say "I paid." This supports Mechaber's ruling and resolves the issue.
