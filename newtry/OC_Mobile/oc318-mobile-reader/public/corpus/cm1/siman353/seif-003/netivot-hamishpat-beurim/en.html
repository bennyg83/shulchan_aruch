Tumim notes that the stronger question is from the case of an heir, where takkanat hashuk does not apply. Pnei Yehoshua similarly asks from the beginning of HaGozel U'Ma'akhil, where Rami bar Chama says heirs are exempt because the heir's domain is like a buyer's domain. That appears to show that ye'ush plus change of possession can exempt entirely. In my view whether the second holder must return value depends on two different ways to understand ye'ush. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 69 suggest that ye'ush can operate as a limited transfer to the thief. Because the thief remains obligated to return value, his acquisition can be viewed as retaining a monetary remainder in favor of the victim. When the thief later transfers the object, the buyer receives it subject to that retained value obligation. This resembles a reserved interest in the acquisition. But Tosafot also suggest that ye'ush is like hefker to the world. Everyone could theoretically acquire, except that while the property is needed for the thief to fulfill his restitution duty, others are barred from taking it. If the thief then willingly transfers it, the buyer can acquire from the victim's hefker rather than through the thief's title. On that model the buyer owes no value to the original owner. This distinction can explain the dispute whether an heir's domain counts like a buyer's domain. If ye'ush is a limited transfer to the thief, inheritance is not a true change of possession because the son comes only through the father's estate. If ye'ush is world-hefker, then after the thief dies the restitution duty no longer blocks acquisition in the same manner, and the heir can acquire from hefker like anyone else. Accordingly, Rami bar Chama, who treats the heir like a purchaser, likely understands ye'ush as hefker and therefore exempts even from value. Rava, who does not treat the heir as a purchaser, can understand ye'ush as a limited transfer to the thief and therefore require the buyer to return value. Rava can derive this from the Mishnah itself. The Mishnah teaches specifically that when the robber fed the stolen object to others they are exempt. Tosafot ask why it did not simply say that even if the stolen object itself was given to them, ye'ush and change of possession exempts them. According to Rava, the answer is that while the body would be acquired, the recipients would still owe value. Only after consumption are they fully exempt in the sense taught there. This also resolves the ox-and-cloak proof. According to the view that an heir's domain counts like a purchaser's, the acquirer can indeed be fully exempt from value under the hefker model. The Gemara's reasoning therefore fits. There is still a difficulty from priestly gifts in Bava Kamma 115, where the Gemara says that had ye'ush occurred the purchaser of the innards would be protected. But priestly gifts are different: once a valid acquisition has occurred, even through the wrongdoer, the buyer can be exempt from value because no individual claimant has a direct monetary claim against him. The same model helps answer Tosafot's questions elsewhere concerning consecration after ye'ush. Where ye'ush creates a hefker-like state, hekdesh can acquire as a new possessor even if the thief himself has not yet fully acquired. The practical rule is that Rambam's requirement to return value reflects one strand of the sugya, not an unavoidable consequence of every ye'ush plus change of possession case.
