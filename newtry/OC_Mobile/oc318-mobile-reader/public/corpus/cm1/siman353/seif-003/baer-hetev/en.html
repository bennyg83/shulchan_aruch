Possession. The Sema explains: for example, he sold it or gave it to another person, or to one of his sons during his lifetime. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 70.
They divide it. The Shach writes that this is the primary ruling. The Rama likewise rules in siman 356, seif 3, and cites the view of many great authorities who hold this way; see there at length.
Notorious. This follows the Rambam's wording. He holds that the market regulation was not enacted for a notorious thief, as the Tur and Mechaber cite in his name in siman 356, seif 2. Therefore the text specifies a notorious thief. By contrast, for one who is not notorious, the market regulation applies, and the robbed owner must reimburse the purchaser what he paid. Here, since the purchaser acquired it after the owner's despair, he need not return it at all, for there is no purpose in merely exchanging payments: the purchaser would pay its value and the owner would then have to reimburse him under the market regulation. In the Derishah to siman 356 I wrote that even according to the Rambam, repayment from the purchaser to the robbed owner can apply after despair where the thief normally sells stolen goods below their value. The purchaser must then return to the robbed owner the excess value, up to the item's true worth. Accordingly, this law need not be limited specifically to a notorious thief. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes: The Darkei Moshe, Bach, and Sema likewise wrote this in siman 356, seif 3, and all challenged the Mechaber, who wrote that according to the Rambam the purchaser returns nothing where the thief is not notorious. I say that their challenge is improper, for the law follows the Mechaber. The reading in the Rambam, 'If he was not a notorious thief, he gives neither the object nor money at all,' is correct, as the Maggid Mishneh states that this is the verified text. There is proof from Bava Batra 44a, and all the authorities rule so, as I wrote in siman 37, seif 17. The Rambam himself, at the beginning of chapter 16 of Hilchot Edut, rules that if a robber sold a garment to Levi, the robbed owner may testify about it because the garment will never return to him, since the purchaser acquired it through despair and change of possession. If the purchaser nevertheless had to return the excess value, the robbed owner would be directly interested in the testimony. It is forced to say that the case concerns one known to have bought it only for its present value. Rather, he returns nothing. That case concerns a robber who is not notorious, and the law of a thief and a robber is the same, as stated at the beginning of siman 368. This is clear in my view of the Rambam.
And some say. The Shach writes that the words of the Rama and Sema here, in siman 356, and elsewhere require study. According to the Mechaber, and according to what I proved, the Rambam holds that only in the case of a notorious thief must the purchaser return the money. If so, no one disputes him. What the Tur writes, that Rabbeinu Yitzchak disagrees, follows the Tur's understanding that according to the Rambam even when the thief is not notorious the purchaser must return the money. Tosafot to Bava Kamma 67b clearly implies that Rabbeinu Yitzchak holds that where the item came into his hands unlawfully, despair together with change of possession is ineffective and he must return the money. If so, with a notorious thief as well it came into his hands unlawfully. This requires study. See there for what he writes further.
