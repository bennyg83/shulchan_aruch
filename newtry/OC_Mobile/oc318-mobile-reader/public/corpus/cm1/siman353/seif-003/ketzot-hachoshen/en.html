Some say that he need not pay him the value. Rashba cites Raavad that even though despair plus change of possession transfers the body of the object, the purchaser must still return its value to the original owner because the object came through an unlawful chain of possession. Rashba questions whether such an acquisition is sufficiently complete for the requirement that a lulav be "yours." At first this is difficult. Even if the purchaser owes the object's value, why should the object itself not be fully his? A thief who acquires through change of act or change of name still owes money for the original theft, yet the acquired object counts as "yours." The distinction is this. A thief who acquires through change owes money because of a separate personal debt that arose at the original theft. The proprietary acquisition itself is complete. A purchaser who acquires through despair plus change of possession did not himself commit the theft and had no prior debt. If he nevertheless must return the value, that obligation must be understood as a reservation built into his very acquisition. He receives the body of the object, but the original owner retains a monetary claim attached to that transfer. The acquisition is therefore not completely unqualified. This is why Raavad compares it to collateral or an express apotiki. The object is in the purchaser's ownership, but the unresolved monetary right means the ownership is not fully free. Some authorities hold that such encumbered ownership cannot satisfy the requirement of "yours." If the object is later lost in the purchaser's hands, he may not have to pay its value, because he never incurred an independent debt as a thief. The original owner's right was a reservation in the acquisition itself. Once the object is gone, there may be nothing further to collect from the purchaser. This explains the difference between despair plus change of possession and the thief's own acquisition through change. In the latter, the object is fully acquired and the monetary debt is independent. In the former, if value must be returned, the monetary reservation qualifies the acquisition itself.
A difficulty still arises from despair of a lost object. Tosafot says that even according to the opinion that despair can acquire from a thief, a finder who lifted the lost object before the owner despaired and only afterward the owner despaired may acquire the body but still owe its value. In a found object there was no debt at the moment of lifting. A finder is at most an unpaid or paid custodian, and custodians ordinarily incur monetary liability only through a later breach. If the owner later despairs without any negligence, why should the finder owe value? This seems to show that even acquisition through despair itself can be qualified: the finder may acquire the body while remaining obligated monetarily because the object first came into his possession before despair. That would undermine the claim that any such monetary obligation means the object is not truly "yours." However, according to what I wrote in siman 361, the reason later despair does not ordinarily acquire a lost object that entered the finder's hand before despair is that the finder's custodial hand is legally the owner's hand. The object is treated as though still resting in the owner's domain. Therefore, even according to one who says despair acquires from a thief, the finder need not acquire the body at all in that case. Tosafot's formulation can then be read within another conceptual approach, but it does not force us to define every despair acquisition as partial. Thus one can still maintain that where despair itself validly acquires for the thief, the thief's ownership is complete and his continuing monetary debt is only the prior theft debt.
