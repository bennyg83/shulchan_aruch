A change of possession, etc. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 70.
Others disagree. They require the owner's despair to precede the change of possession. This is the view of the Rosh, Tur, Rashba, Rashi, Mordechai, Aguda, Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri, Baal HaMaor, and Ramban, and it is the principal ruling. The Rama and Maharshal also rule so. A further proof follows from Abaye's rule that despair not yet known to the owner is not despair. The Gemara therefore asks how a thief who took from one person and gave to another could transfer title, since the owner had not seen the theft and despaired; it answers by limiting the case to an armed robber. This shows that the owner's despair must be known to have occurred before the transfer. There is no sound basis to distinguish a sale from a gift, since the recipient of a gift is treated like a purchaser for this purpose. The Rif should also be understood this way. Accordingly, the rule that ordinary theft is presumed to cause despair applies only when it is known that the owner became aware of the theft. If it is not known that he knew, there is no despair. The Aguda and Hagahot Ashri expressly state that where the owner did not yet know and the thief gave the item away, the recipient must return it to the original owner; if the owner already knew, the recipient acquires it.
If he purchased from a notorious thief, etc. The Sema, Darkei Moshe, and Bach understand the Rambam to require even one who bought from a non-notorious thief to return to the owner the excess of the article's value over the price paid. They therefore criticize the Mechaber, who writes that according to the Rambam such a purchaser returns neither article nor money. Their criticism is incorrect. The verified reading of the Rambam is precisely that if the seller was not a notorious thief, the purchaser gives neither the article nor money. This follows from Bava Batra and from the Rambam's own law of testimony. Where a robber sold land, the victim cannot testify concerning it because he remains interested in recovering it. But where he sold a garment after despair and change of possession, the victim may testify because the garment will never return to him. If the purchaser still owed the victim the excess value, the victim would plainly remain interested in the testimony. The passage must therefore concern a non-notorious thief, and the purchaser owes nothing at all. This establishes the Rambam's view clearly.
Some say he need not, etc. The Rama, Sema, and other authorities require study. Once the Mechaber and the correct text of the Rambam are understood to require return of money only when one bought from a notorious thief, there is in fact no clear dissent. The Tur's statement that Rabbeinu Yitzchak disagrees rests on his mistaken understanding that the Rambam requires payment even where the thief was not notorious. Tosafot imply that where the object came into the purchaser's possession unlawfully, despair and change of possession do not free him from returning the value. A purchaser from a notorious thief likewise receives it unlawfully. Rashi's discussion of one who recognizes his utensils also proves no disagreement, because that Mishna concerns an ordinary, non-notorious thief. The Rambam's phrase 'he returns the money' means the excess of the item's value over what he paid. Otherwise there would be no distinction between returning the money and returning the object. The Maggid Mishneh's initial explanation is therefore correct: the Raavad objected only to the corrupt text before him, not to the verified version. The Rambam's reason is sound. Since the purchaser bought from a notorious thief, the object came to him unlawfully and the change of possession is incomplete. He therefore returns the excess value, much as the Baal HaMaor explains that one who receives a replacement animal from tax collectors through their mistake must return the excess because the transfer was not complete. Although we do not follow the Baal HaMaor in that particular case, because there the transfer is treated as complete, his reasoning applies here. The Rambam and Mechaber are therefore correct and no authority has been shown to disagree.
