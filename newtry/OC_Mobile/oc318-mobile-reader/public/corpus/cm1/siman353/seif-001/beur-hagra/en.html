The Rif derives from Rav Pappa that a reversible change does not acquire. Tosafos in Sukkah, however, write that it acquires rabbinically, because Rabbi Elazar does not dispute Abaye's principle but only explains the Mishnah as dealing with a Torah-level change. Rabbi Yochanan likewise states that acquisition is due to the enactment for penitents. The Tur carefully distinguishes: at the beginning of the siman he writes that even a reversible change gives the robber the improvement, while the stolen object itself is returned and the owner pays him for the improvement by which he made it into a utensil. Tosafos instead hold that he acquires the object entirely because of the enactment for penitents, and similarly regarding the improvement of shearing and offspring. Rabbenu Tam and Ri disagree over the basis: according to Rabbenu Tam a reversible change itself acquires rabbinically, while according to Ri the acquisition is only because of the enactment. A practical difference is a case where that enactment does not apply. Yet according to both, he acquires fully and pays the owner the value of the original object. The Tur's rulings in simanim 353 and 362 appear at first inconsistent, but on examination are reconcilable. Rabbenu Tam regards shearing and birth as an irreversible change, whether the animal was already pregnant when stolen or became pregnant in the robber's possession; Ri treats it as reversible and therefore needs the enactment for penitents. The Rosh ultimately rules like Rabbenu Tam. Accordingly, the Tur's statement that what was born or shorn is a complete change is consistent with his later statement that in a merely reversible change the body returns to the owner while the robber receives the improvement.
