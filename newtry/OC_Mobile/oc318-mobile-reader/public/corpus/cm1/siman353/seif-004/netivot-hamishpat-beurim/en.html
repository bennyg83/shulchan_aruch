From a notorious thief. See Shach, seif katan 5, who notes that Rambam requires return of value specifically when the item was bought from a notorious thief. Shitah Mekubetzet at the beginning of HaGozel U'Ma'akhil indeed says so. Rambam appears to derive this from Bava Kamma 114 concerning tax collectors and robbers who take one person's donkey and give him another. The Mishnah says the replacement belongs to him, while a baraita says he returns it to the first owners. Tosafot ask both from ye'ush plus change of possession and from the contradiction between the Mishnah and baraita. Rambam resolves it by distinguishing a notorious thief. Because purchasing from such a thief itself begins in prohibition, takkanat hashuk is weaker. The body can belong to the buyer after ye'ush and change of possession, as the Mishnah says, but he must return the excess value, as the baraita says. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Raavad that he need return only the amount by which the object's value exceeds what he paid. One should not, however, rely on Rambam to combine two leniencies, saying both that change of possession followed by ye'ush acquires and that wherever acquisition occurs no value need be returned. When the buyer received the stolen item before ye'ush and knew it was stolen, even Rambam should agree that later ye'ush cannot help him acquire the body. By knowingly receiving stolen property he became obligated to preserve and return it to the true owner. Ramban explains that ye'ush cannot operate once the object is held by someone who has become the owner's custodian, because it is legally as though it remains in the owner's domain. Therefore, if it first came to him as a deposit before ye'ush, even a later sale after ye'ush may not help. The earlier custodial relation prevented the ye'ush from taking effect. This is the intent of Yam Shel Shlomo, who says that in such a case change of possession followed by ye'ush is only like ye'ush alone. One might object according to Tosafot's model that ye'ush is like a gift to the thief: if the thief later receives that gift-interest, why should a prior purchaser not acquire through it? The answer is that the thief already possessed, at the time of sale, the future right that if ye'ush later occurs the object will become his in the relevant sense. A seller can sometimes transfer all rights that will later accrue to him when that right already exists conceptually at the time of sale. This differs from a purchaser who knowingly became the true owner's custodian before ye'ush, because in that case the owner's retained possession through the custodian prevents ye'ush from arising at all.
