The owners despaired. Tur treats despair alone as an uncertain acquisition for stringency. Beit Yosef says he found no parallel ruling, but the Talmud explicitly recognizes that after despair a thief can sometimes consecrate through the combination of despair and change of name. The larger difficulty is from kiddushin. Even if despair alone does not acquire, giving the stolen object to the woman appears to add change of possession, which normally completes acquisition. Rambam accordingly rules that if the owners despaired, kiddushin with the stolen property can be valid, because the woman acquires through despair plus change of possession. Rosh and Tur nevertheless treat the kiddushin only as doubtful. The reason is that kiddushin money must come from the husband as his property. Under the Rosh's theory, despair followed by change of possession works because the purchaser receives the object permissibly. The thief himself does not acquire through that change of possession, since the object originally came to him unlawfully. Therefore, when the woman receives the object, she may acquire it, but the husband never owned the kiddushin money. One cannot simply say, "When she acquires the object he acquires her," because the husband's own acquisition does not arise through the change of possession. This differs from despair plus change of name. There the thief himself acquires by the change affecting the object, so his acquisition and his consecration can occur together. Thus Rosh and Tur can maintain that kiddushin is valid only if despair alone already gave the thief enough ownership. Because that point is doubtful, the kiddushin is doubtful as well.
Rambam follows a different theory. He holds that even change of possession followed by despair can acquire. This shows that change of possession itself has enough acquisitive force to combine with later despair even though the purchaser initially received the object before despair. Accordingly, in kiddushin Rambam can say that when the woman acquires through the combination of despair and change of possession, the husband too acquires sufficiently at that moment for the kiddushin and his acquisition to coincide. Rosh, however, holds that change of possession before despair does not acquire. That confirms that the later purchaser's right depends on receiving the object only after the owner's despair, when it comes to the purchaser permissibly. The thief himself never gains from that change of possession. Therefore he cannot supply his own money for kiddushin through that route. This also resolves Tosafot's question why consecration after despair is explained by change of name rather than simply by change of possession into consecrated ownership. Under the Rosh's approach, change of possession benefits the recipient, not the thief who is trying to consecrate. The thief therefore needs a change, such as change of name, that actually acquires the object for him. Then his acquisition and consecration can arise together. Under Rambam, another difficulty arises because he ordinarily requires the purchaser after despair plus change of possession to return the object's value unless marketplace protection applies. Consecrated property does not receive the marketplace enactment. If consecration would immediately incur an obligation to restore the value, there is no net benefit to consecrated property. The principle that speech to consecrated property is like delivery applies only where there is a real gain to the consecrated estate. Therefore simple change of possession into consecration does not suffice. Change of name, which can create complete acquisition, is needed.
