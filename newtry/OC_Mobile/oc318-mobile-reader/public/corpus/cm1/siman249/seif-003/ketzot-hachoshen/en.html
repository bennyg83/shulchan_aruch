One who gives a gift to a slave. A gift given to a slave merely "on condition that your master have no right in it" does not prevent the master from acquiring. But Perishah writes that if the donor attaches the condition to the body of the gift, such as, "on condition that you use it for this particular purpose," the master does not acquire it. Taz rejected this and claimed no authority extended the rule from a married woman to a slave, except where others give money specifically for the slave's emancipation. Rambam states explicitly otherwise: if one gives a gift to a woman on condition that her husband have no right in it, or to a slave on condition that the master have no right in it, the husband or master acquires. But if the donor conditions the body of the gift itself that it be used for a particular purpose, neither the husband nor the master acquires. Shach likewise agrees with Perishah. Thus the distinction applies to a slave as well.
The master acquires the very property as well. Lechem Rav rules that the principle, "Whatever a slave acquires, his master acquires," applies even to property the slave had acquired before he was sold to this master. Several Talmudic passages support this, since they do not distinguish prior property from property acquired during enslavement. The Jerusalem Talmud concerning one who is half slave and half free appears particularly strong. A found object discovered on the day allotted to the master belongs to the master, while one found on the slave's own day belongs to the slave. The Jerusalem Talmud asks whether on the following master's day the master can then take what the slave acquired on his free day, implying that once he again has full slave status, the master's proprietary rule reaches property previously acquired while free. The Babylonian Talmud appears different. It says that damages paid for an ox goring him on the master's day belong to the master and on his own day belong to him, with no indication that the master takes the latter amount the next day. The distinction follows from the two Talmuds' different conception of a half slave, half free person. According to the Babylonian Talmud, his personal prohibition status is never divided by days. He remains half slave and half free at all times. The alternating days divide only labor and monetary yield, like partners in an indivisible courtyard who use the property on alternating days. When he works an entire day for the master, part of that day's labor is effectively exchange for the corresponding use of the master's half on the slave's own day. Therefore property acquired on his own day genuinely remains his and does not pass to the master the next day. The Jerusalem Talmud, however, treats the division as reaching even personal status. It says that kiddushin made on the master's day is ineffective, while on his own day it can be effective. Under that approach, on his own day he is fully free and on the master's day fully enslaved. Once he becomes a full slave again, the ordinary rule that the master acquires what the slave owns can reach property he acquired while free. The Babylonian Talmud refuses this alternating status because bodily sanctity, once freedom has taken effect, does not simply disappear and return. Therefore the person remains permanently half free and half enslaved, and only labor is divided. The Jerusalem Talmud must hold a different theory of temporary bodily status. A related Jerusalem Talmud passage says that Rabbi Yochanan holds kiddushin of a half slave, half free person ineffective, and likewise his role in divorce ineffective, while Shmuel is concerned for the divorce. This should not be read as requiring a case where he married a woman of similarly divided status. The Bavli's own discussion indicates that if a person is not within the institution of kiddushin at all, he also lacks the ordinary capacity to generate the relevant heirs and marital effects. The better interpretation is that Rabbi Yochanan views him as lacking effective kiddushin even on his own day, whereas Shmuel follows Rabbi Yehudah's approach that his partial free status gives him sufficient connection to the Jewish community for some legal consequences. The Jerusalem Talmud's comparison to Rabbi Yehudah's liability for kidnapping one who is half slave and half free is based on the principle that even "part of his brother" can be enough. This entire discussion illustrates why the Jerusalem Talmud can let the master's ownership reach property acquired on the alternate day, while the Bavli's permanent mixed status cannot.
