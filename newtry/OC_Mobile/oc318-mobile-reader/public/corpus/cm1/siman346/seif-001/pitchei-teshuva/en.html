Themselves, and not an agent. See Baer Heitev and Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 168, concerning three partners who owned a vinegar factory in another city. All three engaged in purchasing for the business and commonly sent their sons there to supervise. The sons returned home for the festival while a partnership bundle of money remained with one of them. After the festival the fathers chose to send only one son back to the factory, and the son holding the bundle handed it to the traveling son. He placed the partnership money together with his own funds in a pouch sewn into his stockings, and all the money was lost on the road. This method was somewhat negligent because money is normally kept in a sewn pocket in the garment over the chest, not in stockings from which it can fall when descending from a wagon. The partners wanted the entire loss placed on the father of the negligent son. The questioner asked whether that father could be exempt because the negligence occurred while the owners were working for the custodian, as in siman 176, seif 8, perhaps treating the sons as their fathers' agents, and whether sons supported by their fathers might even have the fathers' legal hand. Chatam Sofer answers that the fathers themselves were not negligent; the negligent person was the son, who was only their agent. Even if the father were a paid custodian without any exemption, if he personally transferred the deposit to his adult son and the son was negligent, the father would ordinarily be exempt because a person deposits with his wife and children in mind, while the negligent son himself must pay. The son is not a partner or owner, and the sons are not considered borrowed to one another. Each is only his own father's agent. If the son cannot pay, there is a dispute under siman 291, seif 24, whether the father becomes liable, but in this particular case even Rosh would agree that the father is presently exempt, because the fathers themselves intentionally entrusted the money to their sons, who then transferred it among themselves, and Shach rules that where it is known the first custodian does not personally guard the item he is exempt. Had the son not been negligent but the money simply been lost in a manner for which an unpaid custodian is exempt, the father, as a paid custodian, might have been liable. But because the son himself was negligent and liable, the father is exempt until the son acquires assets and can reimburse. Chatam Sofer adds that even if one of the fathers himself had lost the money through negligence, he would likely be exempt here. The partners themselves continued working in the partnership business in their own city. Moreover, even if they had delegated the entire operation to their sons, this would not be the Gemara's unresolved case of saying to an agent, "Go and be borrowed together with my cow." Here the partners first undertook reciprocal obligations to perform the partnership work, and only afterward mutually agreed that their sons would perform their duties in their stead. Procuring a substitute after one has personally undertaken service is no less than performing another act of service for the borrower. The Gemara's doubt concerns one who never personally entered the service relationship and instead merely lent his cow while another person performed service. Here the fathers explicitly accepted one another's sons in their places, so the exemption applies. If, however, they had merely told their sons, "Be borrowed for us together with our cow," without the fathers themselves undertaking service, that would remain the ordinary unresolved agency question, and money could not be extracted from the possessor. The questioner's suggestion that a supported adult son's hand is legally the father's is incorrect; Rif's comparison to a slave rests on the slave's body being legally his master's. Chatam Sofer therefore concludes that, for the present, the partnership bears the loss equally, although when the negligent son later acquires his own money he must reimburse the others for their shares.
