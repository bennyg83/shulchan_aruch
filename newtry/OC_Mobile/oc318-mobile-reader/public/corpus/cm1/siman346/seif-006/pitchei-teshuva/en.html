The owners themselves, and not an agent. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 168, discusses three partners who owned a vinegar factory in another city. All three handled the purchases needed for the business and commonly sent their sons to supervise it. Before a festival the sons returned home. A partnership bundle of money was held by one son, and after the festival they decided to send only one of the sons back to the factory. The first son handed him the partnership money, and the traveler placed it together with his own money in a pocket sewn into his stockings. On the road all the money was lost. This was considered close to negligence, because money is ordinarily kept in a pocket sewn into a garment over the chest, not in stockings from which it may fall when one descends from a wagon. The partners therefore wished the loss to fall solely upon the father of the negligent son. The question was whether the father should be exempt because the negligence occurred while the other owners were employed in the partnership work, as in siman 176 seif 8, or whether the sons were merely their fathers' agents, so that the dispute of Rif and Ramah applies as to whether an owner's agent is equivalent to the owner for the law of an owner being employed with the borrower. Perhaps, alternatively, sons who are adults but supported at their fathers' table have the legal status of their fathers' hands, so all would agree that this is a case of the owners being employed and there would be exemption even for negligence, with the loss borne equally by the partnership. Chatam Sofer answers that if one of the fathers himself had been negligent, one would indeed need to analyze whether the presence of the other partners' sons at the work counts as the owners themselves being employed. Here, however, the fathers were not negligent. The negligent person was the son acting as agent. Even if the father were a paid custodian without the owners being employed with him, if he personally handed the deposit to his adult son and the son was negligent, the father is generally exempt because one who deposits does so with the understanding that the custodian's wife and children may guard it, while the son himself is liable. The son is not a partner, is not an owner, and the sons are not considered borrowed to one another, since each acts only as his own father's agent. The negligent son must therefore pay, and the father is exempt. If the son lacks means, the authorities dispute whether the father must pay, as in siman 291 seif 24. Rosh holds that he does, but in this case even Rosh would agree that the father is exempt until the son acquires his own assets, because Shach there writes that where it is known that the custodian does not personally guard the deposit, the father is exempt. All the more so here, where the fathers themselves entrusted the business to their sons and the sons transferred the money among themselves. Had the son merely lost the money without negligence, he would have been exempt as an unpaid custodian while the father, who is a paid custodian, might have been liable; this is the intent of Shach there and of the cited responsa. But once the son was negligent and is personally liable, the father is exempt. Chatam Sofer adds that even if one of the fathers himself had lost the money through negligence, he would still be exempt here. Even if one holds that where an owner tells an agent, "Be employed with my cow," the agent is not equivalent to the owner, these fathers themselves were also engaged in partnership business in their own locations. Moreover, even if they had entrusted the entire operation to their sons, the fathers would still be exempt. This is not comparable to the Gemara's question of one who tells his agent to be employed with his cow. There the owner did not initially obligate himself to be employed with the borrower, but lent his cow and sent another person together with it. Here the fathers mutually agreed that whatever work they themselves were obligated to perform could be performed by their sons in their stead. That arrangement is not merely sending an agent to be employed with the cow. If, however, they had expressly told their sons, "Be employed for us with our cow," it would remain a doubtful matter like any other agency, and money could not be extracted from the party in possession. The suggestion that an adult son supported by his father is legally his father's hand, like a slave whose hand is his master's hand, is incorrect, because Rif's reason regarding a slave is that the slave is legally comparable to his master's body. In any event, Chatam Sofer concludes that the partnership as a whole must presently bear the loss, though if the negligent son later acquires assets he must reimburse the others for their shares. He recommends compromise if possible; otherwise the law is as stated. See there.
And there are those who disagree. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 177, discusses one who rented a horse from Shimon and also hired Shimon's father to accompany him and show him the road. When Shimon rented him the horse, he explicitly reduced the rental price because his father would accompany the renter and supervise the horse. On the return journey the horse became somewhat lame. The renter wished to leave it on the road, but the owner's father told him to ride it home because this would not harm the horse. When they arrived, the horse became very ill, and the owner claimed that the renter had been negligent by riding it after it became lame. Shevut Yaakov first notes that riding such a horse would ordinarily be clear negligence, as explained in siman 309 seif 3. Nor could one exempt the renter merely because the owner's father was with him, if the father were only the owner's agent, since there is a dispute whether an agent is equivalent to the owner and doubtful monetary law does not permit extraction. In this case, however, the father was not sent as the son's agent. He was independently hired to accompany the renter, and the owner expressly relied upon his father's supervision when reducing the rent. Since the father instructed the renter to continue riding and stated that this would not harm the horse, the renter was not negligent and is exempt. In volume 3, siman 177, Shevut Yaakov discusses one who gave another a debt document to collect from the borrower and bring him the money, instructing him that if travel was dangerous he should hire another Jew who lived there to accompany him. The agent did so, and the money was later lost on the road. He analyzes whether the agent is liable as a paid custodian or exempt because he followed the owner's instructions and had another person with him. See there for the distinctions.
