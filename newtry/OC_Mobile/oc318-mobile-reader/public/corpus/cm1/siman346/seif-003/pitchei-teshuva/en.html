And one of them was borrowed to him, etc., this is a doubt. The Tur writes that where one borrows from two partners and one partner is working for him, the unresolved question is whether the exemption applies to that partner's share. This implies that with respect to the other partner's half there is no doubt and the borrower must certainly pay. Perishah states this explicitly. Shulchan Aruch, however, follows Rambam's wording and concludes generally that if the animal dies he does not pay, which could sound as though he is exempt from the entire animal. Nimukei Yosef already questions this and suggests that Rambam's concluding phrase may refer only to the immediately preceding case of two partners who borrowed, not to this case. If so, when one borrows from two partners the exemption applies only to the half of the partner who was working for him. Mishneh LaMelech likewise writes that Rambam's wording is inconclusive. Shitah Mekubetzet states plainly that the doubt concerns only the share of the partner who was working, while the other partner's half must certainly be paid. Ketzot HaChoshen adds that the exempt partner takes nothing from the half that the borrower must pay to the other partner. Although partners normally share collections from a damaged or doubtful debt, here the first partner's loss results from a personal Torah exemption tied specifically to his own service, and that exemption cannot reduce the other partner's right. The nonserving partner therefore receives his full half, and the serving partner receives nothing from it. The same applies to the reverse unresolved case of partners who borrow: the doubt relates only to the share of the partner whose circumstances create the exemption. See Nimukei Yosef, the Tur, Ketzot HaChoshen, and Divrei Mishpat.
