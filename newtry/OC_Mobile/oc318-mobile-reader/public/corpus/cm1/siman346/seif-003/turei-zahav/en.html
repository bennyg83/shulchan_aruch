(Seif 17: Because he is like a purchaser) To explain this rule, the Gemara discusses a woman who rented a cow from another and then married. According to Rabbi Yose, the cow returns to its original owner, so the question is whether the husband is a borrower, liable for unavoidable accidents, or a renter, exempt from them. The answer is that he is neither renter nor borrower but a purchaser. Tosafot add that he is nevertheless an unpaid custodian and liable for negligence, and infer that one who purchases an animal for thirty days, although the transaction is phrased as a sale rather than a loan or rental, is an unpaid custodian. The Rosh's understanding appears to be that a husband is exempt from negligence even when his wife rented the animal from another. His status in his wife's property is that of a purchaser, but because this animal is not hers, the title “purchaser” does not attach to it; nor does he ever enter an ordinary custodial category. If the animal belonged to his wife, he would in any event be exempt because the owner is continuously with him in service. Tosafot's statement that he is an unpaid custodian therefore does not establish practical liability for a husband, who is exempt in all cases. It establishes only that the legal category of unpaid custodian can accompany a purchaser, with practical effect in the separate case of a thirty-day purchaser. This is also the Mechaber's meaning: “because he is like a purchaser” means that if the cow belonged to his wife he would possess it as purchaser; since it belongs to another, he has no direct legal relationship to it and is not even an unpaid custodian. The Rama's gloss about a thirty-day purchaser addresses the separate case, where custody with the owner does not apply because the animal belongs to someone else. The Sema's claim that even the wife's rented cow requires exemption based on custody with the owner is unnecessary and difficult to understand.
