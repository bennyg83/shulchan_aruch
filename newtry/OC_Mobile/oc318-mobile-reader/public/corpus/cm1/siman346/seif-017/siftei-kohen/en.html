But he is not exempt because of guarding with the owner, etc. The Sema finds this difficult, arguing that the Tur implies that the husband is exempt here also because the owner was with him. In truth, the Rama means precisely that guarding with the owner alone would not exempt him unless he is treated as a purchaser. The practical difference is a robber who deposits the article and similar cases. The Sema's distinction between a cow borrowed for life and one borrowed for a fixed term is also unsupported. Once the husband is a purchaser, he is not the lender's custodian but his wife's custodian, and according to the Rosh and Tur he would be exempt for negligence because this is guarding with the owner. There is no reason to distinguish a lifelong loan from a thirty-day loan. Yet the Rosh and Tur themselves are difficult. The Gemara states that when a woman borrowed a cow and then married, according to Rabbi Yose, whose ruling we follow, the cow returns to the original owner and the husband is regarded as one who borrowed from that original owner; guarding with the owner therefore does not apply. Even if the husband is ultimately classed as a purchaser, Rabbeinu Yitzchak's view that he is at least an unpaid custodian should render him liable for negligence to the original owner. The better reading is that Rabbeinu Yitzchak indeed holds the husband liable for negligence. Tosafot, the Nimukei Yosef, the Aguda, and Hagahot Maimoniyot all state this expressly. The Rambam, Ramban, Raavad, Rashba, and Ran, however, exempt the husband even from negligence because he is a full purchaser and bears no custodial obligation at all. The Rama's wording is therefore clear: the exemption is not based on guarding with the owner but on his status as purchaser. His later ruling that a person who purchases an animal for thirty days is an unpaid custodian can be reconciled because a husband has a complete and potentially permanent proprietary right in his wife's property, whereas a thirty-day purchaser has only a temporary right.
