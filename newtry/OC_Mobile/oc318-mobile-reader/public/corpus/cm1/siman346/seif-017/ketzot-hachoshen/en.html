But he is not exempt by reason of custody with the owner present. Sema distinguishes a woman who effectively has the animal for the animal's entire lifetime from an ordinary fixed-term borrower. In the former, her husband's later status resembles a purchaser, and he can be exempt from negligence because of purchaser status rather than because the owner was with him. My son, Rabbi Yosef Dov Ber, correctly objected that if the borrowing lasts for the animal's entire life and the owner has no remaining practical right to reclaim it during its lifetime, the arrangement may lack ordinary borrower liability altogether because not all benefit belongs to the borrower in the usual way and the lender has no retained present use. Sema's parallel case of an arrangement lasting for the woman's lifetime is likewise not obviously stronger than an ordinary purchase for thirty days. Since the property is ultimately to return to the original owner, a temporary purchaser can remain liable for negligence. The distinction therefore requires study.
