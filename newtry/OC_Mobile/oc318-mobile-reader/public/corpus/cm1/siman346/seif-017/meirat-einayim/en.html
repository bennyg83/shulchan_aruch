A woman who borrowed and afterward married, etc. This is Rambam's wording at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot She'eilah. The Maggid Mishneh writes there that this was an unresolved question in the Gemara that was resolved. One must say that even though the Gemara's question is phrased about a woman who rented an item and then married, Rambam holds that the same applies if she borrowed it. Nimukei Yosef presents the question this way as well, and so does the Beit Yosef. See the Derishah.
But he is not exempt by virtue of guarding while the owner is with him. Darkei Moshe also writes this, but it is surprising. The Tur's wording appears to prove that even in such a case the husband is exempt from negligence because of the rule of guarding while the owner is with him. If his exemption were based only on the fact that a husband has the status of a purchaser regarding his wife's property, he would nevertheless be liable for negligence like an unpaid bailee, comparable to one who purchases property for thirty days, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch write afterward. If the reason were only guarding while the owner is with him, however, there would still be a difficulty where the husband has no direct dealings with the woman but only with the original owner; this is why the purchaser status is also needed. Tosafos and the Tur explicitly place the law of a thirty-day purchaser, who is liable like an unpaid bailee, next to the rule that a husband is a purchaser in his wife's property, not only regarding property that is actually hers but even regarding a cow she rented and then married. They nevertheless conclude that the husband is exempt because the owner is with him. See there. In the Perishah I reconciled the Tur and Mechaber according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh by distinguishing between a case where she rented or borrowed the cow for the entire lifetime of the cow or for her own lifetime, similar to one who says "lend it to me for as long as it remains good" in siman 341, and a case where she rented or borrowed for a fixed period such as thirty days. In the first situation the husband is exempt even if he was negligent and it was stolen, by virtue of his purchaser status, not because of guarding while the owner is with him, because the cow itself is not actually hers. The Mechaber and Rama here, and the Tur in seif 19, address this case. But if she rented or borrowed for a fixed term such as thirty days, then although the husband has the status of a purchaser, he is liable for negligence, no better than an actual purchaser for a limited term, as Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Tur write in seif 21. According to that distinction there is no difficulty. Examine carefully.
For the object is not hers but merely borrowed in her possession, etc. I already explained that Rambam understood the Gemara's reference to renting as not exclusive, and the same applies to borrowing. Therefore the reason must be that the object is not hers, not that she would otherwise receive the value of the dead animal from the borrower and keep it without paying her own owner, which would constitute conducting business with another person's cow. Examine carefully.
The woman is liable to pay, etc. See below, siman 424, seif 10. If she has nichsei melog, nichsei tzon barzel, or an addition to her Torah-law ketubah, she must sell her interest for its present value in order to pay. The present case therefore speaks where she has no such property, relying on what was written there in siman 424. Darkei Moshe likewise implies this. The Gemara in chapter HaChovel, Bava Kamma 89a, asks regarding damages why she should not sell nichsei melog and pay, and answers that she has none. Since the questioner considered it obvious that where she has nichsei melog she must sell them to pay, the same principle applies to other monetary liabilities. Otherwise the Gemara could not have asked its question so simply. Examine carefully, and see the Derishah.
