Even if he was negligent. See Sema and Shach, who ask from seif 18, where one who takes an object for thirty days is treated as an unpaid custodian. The deeper question is why the thirty-day taker should be an unpaid custodian at all if he never expressly accepted guarding. Ordinarily a custodian must receive the object with an understanding equivalent to "guard it for me," as explained in siman 291. Why should this differ from partners, whom Shach in siman 176, seif katan 15, does not automatically treat even as unpaid custodians because sometimes one watches and sometimes the other, with overall responsibility remaining shared? The answer must be that in a thirty-day purchase arrangement there is a clear circumstance showing that the object was delivered on the understanding that the recipient would guard it. He takes it to his own house for his use for thirty days. The seller clearly does not intend to accompany him home to guard the object. Therefore the meshichah made for acquisition is also understood as meshichah for guarding. Ramban in Milchamot at the end of HaChovel writes that where the circumstances clearly show delivery for guarding, explicit words "guard it for me" are unnecessary. Partners in the body of an animal are different. If both bought from a third party, the original meshichah was only to acquire from the seller. It was not done so that each partner should become custodian for the other's share. Even if one partner sold half his animal to the other, unless they also agreed at the time of sale on alternating periods of use and guarding, neither can force such a schedule. The other might demand gud o agud, insist on renting to outsiders where that is appropriate, or propose that they work and guard together. Therefore, when one partner later takes the animal for his own work, the circumstances do not necessarily show that he accepts custody of the other's share. He may intend merely to guard his own half while using it pending a later division of times. Once they actually establish alternating periods, the partner using it can become a paid custodian of the other's share because his compensation is the reciprocal use or guarding he will receive in the other's period. This explains the husband-and-wife case. Suppose a wife brought an animal into the marriage without informing her husband that it was borrowed. When the husband later takes it for his ordinary marital use, he does not thereby become its custodian unless he expressly accepts custody. He can compel her to perform marital duties without assuming a new custodial obligation. If she wants the borrowed animal guarded at the higher level required of a borrower, she herself must remain responsible for that guarding. Indeed, where handing the animal to the husband would reduce the level of guarding, she would not be permitted to do so without remaining involved, just as siman 291 restricts transfer to a lower level custodian. At the outset of the marriage, therefore, the legal arrangement can be understood as giving him the marital use-right while she remains the one responsible for guarding. Her later absence from the work does not by itself show that he accepted custody. This also explains the second clause, that the husband is treated as a purchaser. Rosh in Ketubot 79 rules that if she brings him a cow in which she owns only the fruits, those fruits must be sold and land purchased with the proceeds, while he eats the fruits of that land. One might therefore think that if she simply gives him permission to use the cow he becomes a full borrower. Nevertheless, he is exempt because she has merely lent him a use-right in something that is, for the marriage, treated substantially as his own economic property. The money with which replacement land would be purchased would itself also come under his marital fruit-rights. By the enactment of Usha he is treated as a purchaser even regarding the body for the relevant marital laws. Thus the Gemara's statement that a husband is a purchaser through the enactment of Usha means that, for these purposes, the property is treated almost entirely as his. If, however, he waived his fruit-rights and retained only a body-interest, while she lent him the fruits, the case resembles two partners where one owns the body and the other owns the fruits. If the body-owner borrows the fruit-right from the fruit-owner, he is a borrower at least with respect to the value of that fruit-right.
He is not exempt because of shemirah b'baalim. At first glance this is difficult. The case must be one where the wife did not tell him before the marriage that the object was borrowed. If she had informed him, Mechaber later says he can be liable for accidents. If he did not know, why should the fact that the object was not actually hers matter? He accepted the arrangement believing it belonged to her, and on that assumption the owner was with him and he should be exempt. This resembles siman 291, seif 4, where a custodian can say, "I accepted only the guarding of a silver dinar." The answer is that Mechaber's later liability where she informed him must mean she informed him at the time of marriage. Her disclosure then functions as a condition: she is unwilling to enter the marriage and bring him this use-right unless he accepts the accident liability attached to the borrowed object. She grants the marital benefit only together with that obligation. If, however, she brought it into the marriage without disclosure, the marital acquisition was created unconditionally and she herself retained the underlying borrower responsibility. Even if she later tells him, before he begins using the animal, that it is borrowed, he now uses what has already become his marital use-right. According to the conclusion that a husband is treated as purchaser, he is using his own property-right. Accordingly, the statement that shemirah b'baalim does not exempt means that once he knows the object itself belongs to someone else, her presence is not the owner's presence. His exemption, where applicable, rests instead on his purchaser status, not on shemirah b'baalim.
And similarly a robber. At first glance this is difficult. Ordinary robbery is presumed to lead to the owner's despair in many settings, and siman 361, seif 6, says that if a second person consumes stolen property after despair, the second can be exempt under the verse "that which he stole." If so, all the more where the object is merely lost in the second person's possession, the original victim's claim may remain only against the robber, while vis-a-vis the robber the second person's relationship could be shemirah b'baalim. We must therefore establish the case where it is known that the owner did not despair. Still, if the second person knew the object was stolen and used it, he himself would be an unauthorized borrower or robber, because before despair the object remains in the original owner's domain. If he did not use it, mere meshichah does not necessarily create borrower liability. If he was not told that the object was stolen, he can say, "I never accepted ordinary accident liability. I believed the person who lent it was the owner, so I understood the arrangement as one with the owner present." It therefore appears that the case is where the actual owner of the object entered the second person's service, but the borrower did not know that this person was the true owner. Above, seif 4, such a case can still create a normal borrowing relationship without the borrower's conscious reliance on shemirah b'baalim. Even so, another difficulty arises. Suppose the object was worth one dinar when stolen and four dinars when it later died in the borrower's hands. The robber's principal obligation to the original owner is measured at the earlier theft value in the relevant case. But the borrower becomes liable at the time of the accident, when the object is worth four. If the borrower pays four to the robber, one might say the robber acquires that later benefit because a robber can acquire the changed or broken remains, and later appreciation after the decisive change belongs to him. The borrower's accident payment likewise arises only at the later time. This is not the problem of one person doing business with another's cow, because by the borrowing stage the robber can have a proprietary interest under robbery law. Nevertheless, in my view shemirah b'baalim still does not apply. At the time the borrowing began, the robber was not yet the true owner of the object in the relevant legal sense. Ownership for shemirah b'baalim must exist at the beginning of the borrowing. It is not enough that the robber later acquires the object through change or another event. Accordingly, even if the robber later purchases the object from the true owner for money after he had already lent it out, he is not treated retroactively as "the owner with him" at the beginning of the borrowing.
