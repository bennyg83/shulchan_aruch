Seif 1. "One who sees another person drowning in the sea, etc." It appears to me, at first analysis, that there is a novel distinction here. The obligation to rescue with one's own body applies only when the rescue is not degrading to the rescuer. If the only available rescue would involve significant humiliation to the rescuer, and he cannot hire others to save the victim, then perhaps he is not obligated to perform the rescue personally when the humiliation or burden is excessive, such as where he is elderly or in a comparable circumstance. The reason is that the duty to save another's body is derived from the mitzvah of returning a lost object, as the Gemara and Sema write. In the law of lost property we rule from "you may hide yourself" that there are circumstances in which one may refrain, such as an elder for whom the act is inconsistent with his dignity. The same exemption could therefore apply to restoring another's body. One might object that even if the positive mitzvah of returning a lost object does not obligate him, saving a life is different because of the verse, "Do not stand idly by the blood of your fellow." But if that verse created an independent obligation even when the rescue is beneath his dignity, how did the Gemara prove that the verse is needed to obligate him to spend money on rescue by asking that a personal rescue is already derived from "you shall return it"? Perhaps "Do not stand idly by" is needed precisely to obligate personal rescue even where the ordinary dignity exemption would apply. The Gemara's reasoning indicates that the dignity exemption also limits the prohibition of standing idly by. There appears to be a clear proof from Sanhedrin 75a. The Gemara asks, according to the view that the woman in the case was unmarried, why the Sages went to such lengths and allowed the lovesick man to die rather than permit the demanded contact. Rav Pappa answers that it was because of the family's disgrace. But could a man be allowed to die merely to prevent family disgrace? The family members themselves are ordinarily obligated to save him with their bodies and money. It must be that where rescue itself entails serious disgrace, they can be exempt, based on the principle that sometimes one may turn away, and there is no violation of either the positive duty of restoration or the prohibition against standing idly by. This appears correct and clear to me. Nevertheless, the matter requires great study from Rashi to Bava Kamma 100a, on the words "visiting the sick and burial." Rashi writes that burial includes even an elder for whom such conduct is inconsistent with his dignity, and nevertheless the Gemara teaches that he is obligated. This indicates that even burial can override the ordinary dignity exemption, and all the more so saving a living person where danger to life is involved. The matter therefore requires considerable study. Examine carefully.
Seif 1. "And he is able to save him himself, etc." Note: A rabbi asked me about his sister-in-law, may her name be erased, who killed her husband with poison, whether one should save her from danger. I replied that this is addressed explicitly in Niddah 61a, where the Gemara says that although the Sages called a report evil speech, one must nevertheless take practical precautions. Rashi explains there in a manner indicating that one may be prohibited from rescuing the suspected killers. According to Rashi there is certainly no obligation to save such a person. Even according to Tosafot there in the name of the Sheiltot, their discussion concerns only a case where it remained doubtful whether the people had actually killed. Where it is known with certainty that the person murdered, they could agree. Moreover, even if one viewed the matter as a dispute, I do not believe one applies the ordinary rule of resolving a doubt involving life toward leniency for the murderer, because she has herself intentionally taken a life. There is a proof from the Torah's law of the blood avenger: if the blood avenger encounters the murderer and kills him, the murderer has no bloodguilt protection. If even a blood avenger who kills such a murderer can be exempt, all the more where the murderer killed intentionally there is no basis to invoke an unresolved life question in order to create a duty to save her. It therefore appears that one is certainly not obligated to rescue her. May Hashem comfort the bereaved, remove disgrace from His people Israel, and merit us to see the consolations of Zion and Jerusalem. Amen.
