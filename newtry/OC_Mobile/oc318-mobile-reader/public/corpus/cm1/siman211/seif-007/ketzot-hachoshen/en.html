A deposit in another person's hands. Yam Shel Shlomo writes that a person can consecrate mortgaged land because land always remains in the owner's legal domain, but cannot consecrate rented, borrowed, or pledged movables while they are in another's possession, because they are not presently in his domain. In my view the opposite is proven by Tosafot in Perek HaSefinah. Tosafot discusses an artisan's utensil taken for examination and says that if the user were merely a borrower, the owner could consecrate it even while it was borrowed, just as a lessor can consecrate rented property. Nimukei Yosef agrees. Tosafot in Bava Kamma provides another strong proof from double payment. A thief stealing from a custodian pays double to the owner. The Torah's principal case of theft is itself from the custodian's house. If borrowed or rented movables were considered outside the owner's domain so that he could not consecrate them, the owner should likewise have no ownership position supporting double payment. The laws of a borrowed animal likewise assume that the owner retains his proprietary interest while the borrower uses it. If the borrower later pays and becomes entitled to the double payment in certain circumstances, the Gemara treats the prior owner's ownership as substantial enough to divide or retain rights depending on the case. There is also practical proof from the law of lending or renting an animal to a gentile over Shabbat. If the gentile wrongfully keeps it beyond the agreed return time, the Jewish owner can declare it ownerless to avoid the Shabbat ownership problem. Although one can argue that the loan term ended and the animal then returned fully to his legal domain, the earlier sources are sufficient. Therefore borrowed and rented movables that are not denied remain legally in the owner's domain. He can consecrate them and, in principle, sell them subject to the borrower's or renter's temporary right. See also siman 291.
If the custodian denied the deposit. Once the custodian denies it, he becomes like a robber and the property is outside the owner's domain. If the robber later wants to return the object, one might think it again becomes like an ordinary deposit and can be consecrated. Baal HaMaor appears to allow this. Ramban in Milchamot disagrees. He writes that once the object came to be held under the legal status of robbery, even the robber's later willingness to return it does not automatically restore it to the owner's domain for consecration. The object remains in the robber's possession until actual restoration. This dispute helps explain the question in the first chapter of Bava Metzia where two people hold a garment and one consecrates it without first seizing the other half. Tosafot reads the problem as whether the opponent's silence after the consecration counts as admission, thereby making the disputed half like a deposit. According to Ramban, the question can be read differently. Even if the opponent's silence amounts to an admission, the object may remain legally outside the consecrator's domain because it had already been held adversely. The Gemara asks whether the special force of consecration, where speech to Heaven is like delivery to a person, can overcome that defect. The Gemara ultimately brings the bathhouse case and the rule of Rabbi Yochanan that neither a robber nor the robbed owner can consecrate stolen property. This indicates that property held under robbery remains outside the owner's consecration power until it is truly restored. On Ramban's approach, later willingness to return is not enough by itself.
He cannot transfer it. Bach writes that ownerless designation differs from gift or consecration and can apply even to property outside one's possession or not yet in existence, because ownerless designation is rooted in the law of vows. I do not agree. Just as one cannot transfer or consecrate property outside his domain, one cannot declare it ownerless. The Gemara in Perek Merubah expressly links Rabbi Dosa's ability to declare gleanings ownerless after the poor have taken them with the view that a person can consecrate property outside his possession. The Gemara says the modest people and Rabbi Dosa said the same principle. If ownerless designation worked independently of possession, that comparison would fail. Bach's proof from the laws of a recipient saying, "I do not want the gift," is misplaced. The sources he cites concern property the recipient had already acquired. Once the gift was in his hands, his declaration could function as ownerless designation. They do not show that one can declare ownerless property not yet acquired or property held adversely by another. Nor does the verse comparing abandonment to the abandonment of the Sabbatical year prove that the mechanism is a vow. It proves only that once property is validly declared ownerless, anyone may acquire it. There is no basis to infer that the owner can abandon what is not in his legal possession. Therefore the ordinary rules of legal possession apply to ownerless designation just as they do to gift and consecration.
But a loan, since it was given to be spent. Baal HaMaor holds that an ordinary loan can be consecrated. In his view, only property wrongfully withheld by a robber is outside the owner's domain. A debtor who is not refusing repayment holds a debt that remains sufficiently in the creditor's domain. Ramban strongly disagrees. He proves that a loan is outside the creditor's possession from the fact that an oral loan cannot ordinarily be transferred except through a three party arrangement, from the law that betrothal with one's own outstanding loan is ineffective, and from sale law. In his view, a loan is like future property for consecration because the actual money was given to be spent and no present monetary object remains in the creditor's possession. The proof from kiddushin depends on how that law is understood. According to Tosafot, betrothal with one's own loan fails because the woman receives no new benefit, whereas an assigned third party loan can provide new benefit. On that theory the kiddushin case alone does not prove the debt is outside the creditor's domain. Ran, however, explains that one's own loan is not itself "money" or "money's worth" presently available. It is only an intangible monetary claim. A third party debt can still create a new benefit to the woman, but the original lender's own debt is not presently a tangible monetary object. On Ran's explanation Ramban's proof is strong. Since the debt is only an intangible claim until collected, the creditor cannot consecrate it as present property. See also siman 101.
