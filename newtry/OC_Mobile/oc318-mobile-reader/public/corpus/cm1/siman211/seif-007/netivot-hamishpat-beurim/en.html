Shach, seif katan 5, cites a dispute whether an owner can consecrate the excess value of collateral held by another above the secured debt, and suggests that perhaps everyone agrees it can be sold. He does not explain the distinction between consecration and sale. The difference is straightforward. The view that even the excess cannot be consecrated does not mean the owner can later retract because no sanctity took effect at all. Shach himself, in Yoreh Deah siman 221, follows Rashba and Ran that leased or mortgaged property cannot be consecrated so as to uproot the tenant’s or creditor’s present right, even where the property is worth more than the debt. Yet the consecration can bind the owner and take effect after the lease or mortgage ends. The Yerushalmi and Rivash cited there show this. Where the tenant prepaid, he continues to live there without paying hekdesh; where he did not prepay, rent may go to hekdesh. The consecration does not destroy the tenant’s possessory right, but it binds the owner. The same applies to a mortgage with deduction. If the excess were consecrated immediately as a co-owned sacred share, the creditor’s continued residence could become prohibited because hekdesh would be a partner. Therefore the consecration cannot operate against the creditor’s current right. A sale is different. Even if the purchaser becomes co-owner, the tenant or creditor may continue to live there until the end of his term. The sale therefore does not uproot the existing servitude and can take effect now. Indeed, I would go further: the owner can even lease the property to a second tenant during the first tenant’s term, to begin afterward, as Shach writes in siman 312, and the first landlord cannot retract before the first lease ends. If Rabbeinu Tam validates a transfer of “this field that I will inherit” because inheritance arrives automatically, all the more so a mere waiting period, which passes automatically, is not a true lack of present capacity. Even authorities who disagree concerning future inheritance can agree regarding a term that will certainly expire. Ketubot 59 treats a ten-year mortgage as problematic because redemption is still required, not because of the mere lapse of time. Mishneh LaMelech also writes that mere passage of time is not a davar shelo ba la'olam. Nevertheless, even where only time is lacking, the object must be specifically identified, like “this field.” Tur in siman 312 indicates that an unspecified house that is currently rented to another cannot be effectively leased for the future; because the particular subject was not identified, the future acquisition is too indefinite. One cannot prove the contrary from the law of a father who writes property to his son “from today and after death,” where the son cannot sell the fruits during the father’s life. That arrangement depends not merely on passage of time but on the father’s death and on the father’s retained fruit rights. If the son sells only the future fruits and dies before the father, the buyer cannot later acquire the fruits because the body passed to the son’s heirs and the buyer never acquired the body from which the fruit rights flow. This is different from a lease that ends solely by the passage of time. The general principle is that a future right delayed only by a definite term can be acquired now with “from now,” provided the subject is specifically identified. Where the parties use ordinary present acquisition language and merely state that the current owner will continue using the property for thirty days, the transaction is also treated as “from now” unless they expressly defer the acquisition itself. A kinyan made now would otherwise be purposeless if both parties could simply retract throughout the period. This is consistent with the discussions in Nedarim concerning acquisition after thirty days.
