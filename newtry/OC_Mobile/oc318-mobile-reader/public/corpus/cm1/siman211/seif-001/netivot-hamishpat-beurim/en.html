Something not in his possession. Shach, seif katan 1, points to a responsum of Ra'anach, siman 66. It states that even where a person personally obligates himself to acquire a known item that is not in his possession and is not readily available in the market, the obligation to purchase that item does not take effect. A personal obligation regarding future property helps when one obligates himself to sell it if and when he later acquires it, not when he purports to bind himself to procure an unavailable particular item. If he undertakes a penalty for failing to use efforts to sell it, the penalty can itself be asmachta. Even where the penalty is framed in an effective anti-asmachta form, if the particular item can be obtained only at an excessive price, the obligor is considered under ones and is exempt. I had reached this conclusion independently above in siman 60, and was pleased to find it in Ra'anach. Ra'anach further writes that if one undertook a penalty for failing to use efforts to sell and then died, the heirs are exempt. Shach also points to a responsum of Rama concerning one who accepted arbitrators with a kinyan and the arbitrators later wrote that one heir should inherit more than his Torah share. Even according to Rabbeinu Tam, who validates a transfer such as “this field that I will inherit,” if the arbitrators use the language of inheritance their ruling is ineffective because it stipulates against Torah inheritance law. Regarding abandonment of property not presently in one’s possession, Bach, responsum 124, writes that one can abandon it because hefker is akin to a vow. Ketzot challenges him from Bava Kamma 69 and the tzni'im, where one cannot consecrate property that is not in one’s possession. In my view there is no difficulty. Bach certainly agrees that once the property is already outside one’s possession, the present hefker does not take effect. His discussion concerns advance renunciation of chametz before it becomes halachically outside one’s control. After chametz has become prohibited, the Gemara expressly says it can no longer be nullified. Bach means only that before the Torah places future chametz in one’s legal domain for purposes of the prohibition, one may state that he does not wish to acquire that Torah-created status when it later comes into being. This resembles saying in advance, “I do not wish to acquire the gift another person will later give me.” One cannot ordinarily disclaim a future inheritance that will come automatically, or abandon fruit not yet grown on one’s own tree, but one can refuse a future benefit that another will later attempt to confer. So too with chametz: the Torah itself creates a special legal ownership status, and one may state beforehand that he does not wish the Torah to place it in his domain.
This field. In my view, even according to the opinion validating a sale of “this field that I will inherit from my father,” that is true only where he is the sole heir. If there are several brothers, then even if this particular field ultimately falls to his share, the buyer does not acquire it. This is obvious where it came to him through an equalization payment, because that is exactly like “this field that I will later purchase.” But even if it fell to him by lot, since we rule that brothers who divide are legally purchasers from one another, it resembles a later acquisition. Siman 154, seif 17, supports this: a condition tied to a particular brother receiving a parcel is ineffective because brothers who divide are treated as purchasers. One should not object from Bava Batra 159 concerning a firstborn who sold his double share, according to Rabbeinu Tam’s interpretation. That case can concern a sale of the firstborn share to one of the brothers. No later external division is needed, and although the seller cannot choose which physical parcel will satisfy it, the defined firstborn share is still more specific than an unspecified future field. Pnei Yehoshua asks against Rabbeinu Tam from kiddushin after a husband’s death. A woman might seem analogous to a specific field, yet we rule like the Sages that one cannot betroth her now to take effect after her husband dies. In my view the cases differ. Nedarim 30 says that one may consecrate an object to take effect after he himself redeems it, but not after someone else redeems it. A woman after divorce resembles property redeemed by others, because the husband cannot unilaterally make her available to himself again. Ran explains that kiddushin is judged from the husband’s side: the woman does not “place herself” into his domain; she merely renders herself available and the husband acquires. After divorce she may not consent to become available again. Thus even if the husband can presently divorce her, future remarriage depends on a new voluntary act of hers, unlike inheritance of a specific field, which comes automatically. As to retraction before the father dies where the seller said “from now”: according to Rashba and Ramban, even R. Meir’s transfer of a davar shelo ba la'olam with “from now” cannot be retracted, and all the more so this case, which even the Sages can recognize. Even according to those who allow retraction from an ordinary davar shelo ba la'olam despite “from now,” I would say that “this field that I will inherit” is different because it is treated as sufficiently present and the kinyan takes effect now. Rabbeinu Tam’s proof itself points this way. He derives the rule from a son who sold property he expected from his father and died before his father, where the grandson later claims through the grandfather. If the original sale had not taken effect until the grandfather’s death, it could not operate after the seller-son had already died. Thus according to Rabbeinu Tam the acquisition must take effect immediately.
