Seif 7. And if he denied it. Movables deposited with another who denies holding them cannot be sold by the depositor, because they are not in his effective control. Even if the custodian admits possession but refuses to return them, they may likewise be considered under the custodian's control. If he refuses to return them unless he receives some payment, or offers to pay money in their place, we do not ordinarily presume complete repudiation. At the time of the depositor's sale, the sale may therefore take effect to the extent of the seller's practical power over those movables or their substitutes. Regarding the rule that one later exerted himself and acquired the item, if the second acquisition was at a higher price than the depositor's sale, the presumption that he acted to uphold the first transaction may not apply. The same may be true where a new method became available to recover the movables elsewhere. The core of the rule depends on an undertaking, as stated in Shulchan Aruch. Siman 374 rules that where the purchaser knew the property was not the seller's, the rule does not apply. The same should hold where he knew the property was not in the seller's control. When a person sells grain or similar goods held by non-Jews, he knows at least that collection is uncertain. Merchants who sell a contractual right they hold against a non-Jew usually intend something resembling maamad sheloshtan, transferring the purchaser into their own position. The original seller then has no further substantive interest, even though he may still need to present the new purchaser to the non-Jew so that the latter recognizes him. That is not necessarily a missing act in the subject matter itself. If the parties dispute whether the seller also guaranteed performance if the non-Jew failed to deliver, the purchaser must prove that such a guarantee was expressly stated or clearly implied by their language. As to the money paid by the purchaser of such a contractual right, general implied warranty may include responsibility until the seller effects the customary maamad sheloshtan or written substitution placing the new purchaser in his position. Before that substitution the transaction is not fully completed, since the non-Jew will not deliver to the second purchaser. Even if this is not technically property outside the seller's control, because fewer than three acts remain before delivery, the sale is still not fixed until the transfer mechanism is completed. If before that time the non-Jew refuses delivery, the sale may therefore fail because the seller can no longer complete the substitution. Even if the first purchaser had acquired the merchandise through situmta, there is an implied warranty that the second purchaser bought only on condition that he would be placed in the first purchaser's position according to commercial practice. This is stronger than a mere disclosure of intent and follows from the parties' conduct itself. Perhaps a refusal by the non-Jew caused only by a later rise in price does not retroactively prove that the property was already considered denied property at the time of sale. Still, since the second purchaser acquired only on condition of the substitution, once the non-Jew's refusal prevents that substitution the transaction cannot be completed.
