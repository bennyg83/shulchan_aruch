If the river was not accustomed to rise. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Chavot Yair 156 concerning Reuven, who rented a horse to Levi for eight days so Levi could ride three days to a nobleman. Levi was detained there through an accusation, and later for an additional month. Reuven demanded rent for the entire period. Chavot Yair ruled that where the horse was expressly rented for eight days and Levi derived no benefit from it during the enforced delay, Levi pays only for the eight days; his failure to return it sooner was at most indirect causation. If, however, the horse was hired without a fixed number of days, or for a stated daily rate, he must pay for every day. Ketzot HaChoshen agrees with the basic distinction drawn from the Rosh but holds that even then one deducts as for an idle worker. Netivot HaMishpat and Shevut Yaakov disagree with Chavot Yair's exemption where Levi could have sent the horse back with a trustworthy agent and retained it for his own convenience, in which case he must pay the daily hire, with an appropriate deduction for inactivity. Shevut Yaakov further writes that where the owner is already in possession, possession may determine the loss when the underlying rule is based on who is extracting money. See all these sources.
And if the river was accustomed to rise, the loss is the renter's. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 56, addresses an apparent contradiction with the rule that there is no compensation for an animal's idleness. He first suggests that here the renter pays only for the additional day on which he actually rode the animal, not for later days when it stood idle. He later explains more broadly that where the renter originally took the animal under a rental relationship, the rental continues until it is returned, and therefore payment is due under the rental contract, not as damages for idleness.
