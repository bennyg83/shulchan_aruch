The river rose and he had to delay. Chavot Yair discusses a rented horse taken for a journey expected to last several days. The renter was detained by a government official for much longer and argued that the delay was unavoidable. He distinguished a fixed eight-day rental from a rental at a stated daily rate without a fixed term. In my view the governing rule follows Rosh's responsum and the Shulchan Aruch. If both parties were unaware of the later obstruction and the rental was for a fixed trip or fixed term, the resulting loss can fall upon the owner according to the ordinary law of an unforeseen impediment. If, however, the horse was rented simply at a fixed daily rate without a total term, the renter pays the daily rate for the entire period he retained the horse. Even then the owner must deduct the amount attributable to the animal's enforced idleness, like the law of a worker who is paid as an idle laborer. Chavot Yair suggested the owner might prefer continued riding because an animal benefits from remaining accustomed to labor. The sources state expressly that where hired drivers or animals are idle because the work disappeared, the payment is reduced by the value of their idleness. Rosh likewise applies this to an animal rented by the day. Nor do I agree that a prior conversation about the expected duration becomes irrelevant merely because the final taking was "without specification." If the owner asked how long the renter would remain and the renter answered no more than two days, and the rental was then concluded without restating the term, the earlier specification remains part of the agreement. This is like one who negotiates for half a garment and then makes the kinyan on the garment without repeating the quantity. The transaction follows the terms already fixed. A rule concerning an unstated personal condition in a sale is different. Here the duration was expressly discussed between the parties.
