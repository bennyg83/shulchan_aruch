If the owner merely sent an agent to hire wagoners in the customary manner, and the known custom among Jewish wagoners is to stipulate for full pay only in the event of an early sale, then even if the agent's written contract uses broad language such as 'in whatever manner it may occur,' the owner may still be exempt in an unforeseen accident, because the underlying custom defining the agreement is limited to sale. As to returning the barrels or containers, that obligation appears to remain upon the wagoners, although they need not make a special journey of many parasangs solely to retrieve them. One may deduct only the amount for which drivers could reasonably be hired there to bring them back, especially where empty wagons regularly travel that route and can be hired cheaply. The owner cannot be made to bear this return obligation merely because the principal journey was interrupted by accident. The rule in siman 310 that where neither party knew of the possible event the loss falls upon the animal's owner is based on the burden of proof, and the same principle applies to commercial agreements to deliver produce to a place by a fixed date when an unforeseen accident occurs. The Sema's explanation that a party should have stipulated applies where both knew of the relevant possibility, not where neither knew. In such a case the governing principle is that the claimant bears the burden of proof. Even if the animal owner has already received the rental money, the underlying question is whether the rental obligation took effect under the circumstances; once that is decided, possession of the money follows that determination. I recently had before me a sale of grain purchased from a nobleman who had undertaken to deliver it to a certain place. The purchaser sold his entire right to a second buyer, both saying that the sale took effect immediately and absolutely, while neither anticipated that war would interrupt delivery. The nobleman later unloaded the grain midway. At first sight, since the second buyer acquired the first buyer's rights immediately and the first buyer held the money, one might say that the accident does not undo the sale. Yet the nobleman's duty to deliver was itself merely an obligation to perform, not a presently owned tangible object. A right evidenced in writing may in some circumstances be assigned by writing and delivery, but without an effective assignment the second sale remains dependent upon performance of the delivery condition. An accident can exempt the person who would otherwise be liable, but it cannot ordinarily be used to impose a new liability on the other party. Therefore the general rule returns: where neither party knew, the loss falls upon the one seeking to impose rental or acquisition liability. The first buyer's possession of the second buyer's money does not change that analysis because the question is whether the sale remained effective. Nor was the nobleman entitled to withhold part of the agreed delivery, since his own contract was conditioned on delivery to the designated place. During the subsequent transport, heavy rain wet the grain. That loss does not appear chargeable to the first buyer where the grain was transported in the customary manner with straw and sacks. Even if the rain occurred during transport necessitated by the earlier accident, once the grain ultimately arrived at the agreed destination and the sale became effective retroactively, the intervening accident does not itself impose liability. The sample shown at the time of sale was dry, but ordinary moisture from customary transport does not necessarily void the sale, absent an express stipulation. The parties had contracted for grain of the nobleman's sample, with knowledge of normal transportation practices. The second buyer had also participated in preliminary price discussions. The first buyer later obtained the grain at a lower price than anticipated and resold it at a higher stated price, without representing what he himself had paid. The second buyer admitted before a witness that he did not rely on the first buyer's representation of his cost. Therefore there is no basis to treat the resale as an agency purchase or as dependent upon the first buyer's own acquisition price, nor as a sale 'on trust' that would affect the law of overcharge. Ketzot HaChoshen's discussion of disclosure of intent in hiring a horse requires distinction: where a transaction necessarily needs a defined boundary and merchants normally state it, prior discussions may help clarify that boundary; but an earlier discussion that was not incorporated into the later transaction does not automatically become a condition. Likewise, payment above the ordinary wage does not always prove an implied condition when another reason exists for the extra payment. Similar distinctions may defend the ruling of Chavot Yair. Questions of whether attached structures are treated as land or movable property can likewise affect possession and the burden of proof. In the actual matter there were several additional uncertainties, including whether the nobleman had delivered grain matching the sample and the loss caused by rain, and we therefore arranged a compromise by kinyan sudar. In another case, one leased the local right to sell kosher meat in a village for a year at a high price because the village lay on a busy commercial road. Midyear the lessee moved to the city, while others moved into the village and he subleased to them for the remaining four months at a lower rate. He claimed that those four months were disproportionately valuable because they included the Tishrei festivals and many fairs, and therefore he had paid only half the annual rent unless he retained the right against the new residents through year's end. The original lessor sought to retract from permitting the new arrangement. We compromised because this resembles siman 310: where both sides knew that circumstances might change, the party who should have stipulated bears the consequence. Had the new residents been very wealthy and one party sought continuation while the other refused, possession and the burden of proof would govern. Since neither side stipulated clearly what would happen if one wished to terminate, the original lease may not have extended to such disputed circumstances. Whether the pricing structure itself establishes a compelling implied condition is uncertain, particularly for movables, so compromise was appropriate.
