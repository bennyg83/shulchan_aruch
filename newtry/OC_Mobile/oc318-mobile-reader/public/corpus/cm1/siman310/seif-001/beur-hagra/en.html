And it is still, etc. The Rosh. Rashi likewise writes there 79b, s.v. "it became ill or, etc." This is also reflected in the Gemara's distinction there between an animal hired for carrying a load and one hired for riding.
Although, etc. For the halachah follows Shmuel in monetary law.
Because ultimately it will not, etc. According to Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation cited in Tosafos there, s.v. "im." According to Rashi, who explains that it does not return, this means for two or three days until he finds another. Ramban.
He pays. This follows Rabbeinu Chananel's approach, cited above, because the renter can say, "your fortune caused it." Rashi, however, explains, "and your fortune also caused it," and he explains the Mishnah similarly, so that both suffer the loss. See what I write below. Maggid Mishneh brings proof for Rambam from the Tosefta, cited by the Rif and Rosh: one who hires a worker who is seized for royal labor may not say, "I am here before you"; rather, the employer pays him according to the work he performed.
When does this apply, etc. The Gemara there. Rambam explains that this qualification applies to all the preceding cases. Ra'avad writes that it applies only where the animal became mentally unfit. Maggid Mishneh writes that it applies also where it became ill, as the Rif and Rosh write, and this is our reading in the Gemara and the Tosefta. But where it was seized for royal service there is certainly no such distinction; therefore the Gemara and Tosefta do not mention it in that case. The Tosefta states the case of royal seizure regarding a worker, as above. Maggid Mishneh.
And if he did not provide, etc. This follows Rambam's view that even if the animal died the same rule applies, as stated below. There, however, if its value is sufficient to purchase or hire another animal, he must purchase or hire one. Here, by contrast, he did not obligate the animal itself to be sold or hired out in such a case. If he wants the full rental fee, he must provide another donkey.
If the animal died, etc.; and if its value is insufficient, etc. This also refers to the first clause, an unspecified donkey. According to Rambam there is no distinction between "a donkey" and "this donkey" except regarding whether it was hired for a load. His reading of the Gemara is: "what are the circumstances? If, etc., he must provide another donkey. Rather, where he said 'this donkey,' if its value is, etc." He reads "rather" in place of "if." He explains the first question as follows: if he said an unspecified donkey, why should the law be stated only for riding? It should apply even for carrying, since the Mishnah says he must provide another animal. This is inferred from the clause where the animal became ill and he may say, "here is yours before you," which is then established as "this donkey." The Gemara then asks a separate question, "if its value is sufficient, etc.," which applies even to an unspecified donkey.
And if there is not, etc. According to Shmuel, as above, and specifically where the proceeds are insufficient even to rent another, as stated there, "it was necessary, etc., if there is, etc." The Rif and Rosh further prove this from the case of the orchard there 109b, where the Gemara says that the principal is not consumed. They answer that there it is possible to sell. This is Rambam's approach. Rashba, Rosh, and other poskim, however, hold that where an unspecified donkey died or broke down, all the lessor's property became obligated to the renter, and he must provide another donkey from his own funds. This is the meaning of the Gemara's question and answer according to our reading, and this is also the Rif's view. Ra'avad explains that this is only where they made a formal kinyan, but they disagree and write that pulling the donkey itself obligates the lessor's assets. According to them there is no distinction between riding and carrying even with "this donkey"; the mention of riding is not exclusive. Ra'avad's version of the Gemara did not contain the word "to ride." This is the meaning of the gloss, "others say, etc." It does not fully spell out their position, for according to them, with an unspecified donkey, the rule of selling or renting the animal's remains does not apply as stated above. Maggid Mishneh writes that according to their approach, if the animal became ill or mentally unfit when hired for riding, its law is like one that died or broke down, and the distinctions are then between "a donkey" and "this donkey." If it was hired for carrying, however, he need not provide another animal, though the lessor loses his rental fee. See there. The Tur has another approach. See there.
