If the loss was not ordinarily expected to increase, the loss is borne by the owner of the animal. One hired wagon drivers to transport liquor to a designated place, with an agreement that even if it was sold before reaching that place they would be paid as though they had transported it the entire way. Several parasangs before the destination, war broke out and it became dangerous to proceed. The liquor owner's agent, who traveled with the wagoners, ordered them to unload before the war zone. Since this was an event neither side could reasonably have anticipated at the outset, when there had been no report of war in that country, it appears that the loss falls upon the hired workers. The language concerning an early sale does not establish that the owner assumed the risk of such an unavoidable event. The purpose of the sale clause was apparently to prevent the owner from hastily selling along the road to the wagoners' detriment, thereby protecting their expected fee. It does not follow that they were guaranteed payment where an unforeseen accident prevented performance. If, however, the written agreement states that they are to be paid even if the liquor is unloaded midway 'in whatever manner it may occur,' the language may encompass the accident. If it merely says 'if it is unloaded' without such broad language, the matter remains doubtful and requires further study.
