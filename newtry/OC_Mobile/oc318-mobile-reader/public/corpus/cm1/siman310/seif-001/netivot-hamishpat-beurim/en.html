On the outward journey. Sema explains, following Rabbeinu Chananel, that the case is impressment by the authorities who do not search private houses. Even if the donkey will never be returned, the renter can still owe full rental because the coercion affected only the renter's use, not the body of the animal in the ordinary sense. The key distinction is whether the accident occurs to the owner's property itself or only to the renter's intended use. If someone forcibly prevents the renter along the road from using the animal for his work but does not take the body of the animal, the renter can be told, "your fortune caused it." But if the animal itself is seized, the accident has fallen upon the owner's property, and the owner cannot shift the loss to the renter. For this reason, Sema's explanation that a permanent impressment means merely that the animal never returns is difficult. If the animal is permanently taken away, that is like captivity, and the owner must provide another animal. Likewise, the reason is not that where authorities search houses the case resembles death because "the Angel of Death makes no distinction." Rather, the decisive point is that where the coercion reaches the body of the animal, the owner's fortune is implicated. The same principle applies to a rented house requisitioned by soldiers. If the tenant can still live there together with them, the house remains usable and he pays the full rent. If the entire house is taken so that he cannot live there, he pays only for the period he actually used it.

See Sema, seif katan 5, who seeks to align Rambam with Tur and implies a distinction whereby with a generic donkey the carcass is not specifically bound to the renter, while with "this donkey" it is. In my view Rambam can be explained more naturally. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 79 ask why a dead donkey differs from a particular house that fell. They answer that a house that fell is not ordinarily sold, whereas the carcass of a donkey is normally sold in order to acquire another donkey. Accordingly, where the donkey merely becomes ill and is expected to recover, it is not ordinarily sold. One waits for recovery. Therefore the owner is not required to sell it in order to buy or hire another. The case resembles a particular house that fell. The remaining question concerns a generic donkey. Why are the owner's other assets not bound to provide another donkey, like a generic house that fell? Rambam appears to follow the view of Ramah in the laws of rental houses: if the owner has no other house, he is not compelled to purchase one, because he purported to rent something not presently in his possession. Likewise here, if he has no other donkey, he is not forced to buy one. But if he does have another donkey, he must provide it. Rambam's wording is exact: "He is obligated to provide another animal, and if he did not provide it he returns the rent." The obligation exists where he has another suitable animal. If he has none, he returns the unused rent. With "this donkey," by contrast, he has no duty to provide another animal at all. This is obvious from the more severe death case and therefore Rambam did not need to restate it separately.
