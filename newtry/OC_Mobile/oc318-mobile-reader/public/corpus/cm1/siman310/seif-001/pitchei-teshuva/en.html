If it was not accustomed. See Baer Heitev and Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 156. Reuven owned a horse that he rented out. Levi needed to ride three days to a nobleman and hired the horse for eight days. While there, the nobleman brought a false accusation against Levi and detained him. On the tenth day Reuven sent an agent to retrieve the horse, but Levi said that he expected to finish his business the next day and return home, so the agent returned without it. The accusation then intensified and Levi was detained another month. Reuven demanded rent for the entire period; Levi argued unavoidable accident. Chavot Yair ruled that Levi pays only the agreed eight days. He had rented the horse expressly for eight days and derived no benefit from it while detained. His failure to return it immediately, or after Reuven sent for it, is at most indirect causation and does not create rental liability. Reuven's seizure of Levi's property without witnesses does not help, because Levi is substantively exempt and there is no valid denial claim. Reuven must return what he seized, and Levi pays only eight days' rent. This applies where the term was fixed at eight days. If he hired the horse without a fixed term at a known daily rate, or agreed on half a gold coin per day without specifying the number of days, then even though the trip was known ordinarily to take only three days, he pays the daily rate from beginning to end. Chavot Yair says no idle time deduction is made because the owner may prefer the horse to remain accustomed to riding. Even if the owner asked beforehand how long Levi would remain with the nobleman and Levi answered one or two days at most, but afterward took the horse without formally restating the term, Chavot Yair treats the rental as open ended, comparing it to conditions in sale law. Ketzot HaChoshen cites him and disputes several details. As to the principal law of detention by false accusation, Ketzot agrees from the Rosh, the source of this seif, that where neither party knew of the possibility, the loss falls on the owner. If the horse was hired at a daily rate without a fixed total term, however, the renter pays the daily rent for every day, as Shulchan Aruch states here, but deducts the amount corresponding to an idle worker. Chavot Yair's analogy to preferring that the animal remain accustomed to riding is contrary to the explicit rule in Bava Metzia that when workers go and find no work, payment is reduced as for idle workers, and the same applies to rented animals. Rosh, responsa rule 92, and Sema say so explicitly. Ketzot further rejects Chavot Yair's view that an initial statement of one or two days is irrelevant if the final rental was made without repeating it. A condition omitted from a later sale may be waived, but here the parties had already fixed the rental term, and the subsequent completion of the rental is presumed to follow that initial agreement. Netivot HaMishpat also rejects Chavot Yair's exemption beyond eight days. The case of a river rising differs because the accident affected the donkey itself so that return was impossible. Here the horse could have been returned by messenger, and the rule against entrusting another's deposit to a third party does not bar such return in this context. Indeed Levi refused to send it because he expected daily to finish his business and wanted the horse available for his own return. Thus he retained it for his own benefit and must pay. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 115, similarly discusses one who rented a horse for a two day journey and paid for ten days in case of delay, but then fell dangerously ill and remained there about twenty days. He too rules against Chavot Yair and requires payment for every day, because the horse could have been sent back through a trustworthy traveler and the renter kept it for his own benefit. Still, only idle animal rent is due for the extra period. Shevut Yaakov also disputes Chavot Yair's statement that seizure by the owner is ineffective. Even if the case is compared to an unexpected river rise, the underlying rule in Bava Metzia is that when neither party should have anticipated an accident, the burden rests on the one seeking to extract. Thus if the owner is already in possession, the renter bears the loss. The rule that the owner reimburses feed expenses does not prove otherwise, because there too the renter is holding back rent and also physically holds the animal. See there.
