One who rents an animal and it becomes ill, etc. The Mechaber follows Rambam's very concise wording in chapter 5 of Hilchot Sechirut, based upon the Mishnah and Gemara in chapter HaOmanin and according to Rambam's interpretation. The Ir Shushan mixed the Tur's wording with that of Shulchan Aruch and did not notice that they disagree in several laws and even in the interpretation of the Gemara. It is surprising that Rama did not record the Tur's view and clarify the Mechaber's brief and obscure language, as is his usual practice. I will therefore explain each point, with God's help, as necessary.
The Tur writes, based on the Gemara: if one rents an animal and it becomes blind, meaning its eyes are impaired, or its legs become worm-infested, or it suffers another illness of the same type, such that it remains fit for the work but causes the renter extra effort to keep it traveling properly, then even if he rented "an unspecified donkey," the owner can say, "Here is yours before you." In such a case we say that the renter's own fortune caused the difficulty. End of the Tur's words. The Mechaber's phrase, "or it was taken, etc.," means that a different event occurred: one of the king's officers took this animal from the renter to ride it in royal service, as such officers customarily did. The Mechaber writes, "even though it will not ultimately return." The Gemara and Tur do not use that wording; rather they say, "even in royal service from which it does not return." Rashi and the Tur explain that it does not return by itself. The owner must follow it until the royal officer encounters another animal, at which point that other animal is taken and the first is returned. The Tur's explanation of the phrase "if it was taken along the route he was traveling" follows Rashi: the renter was himself supposed to travel on the same route as the royal officer. Therefore, although he must walk after the animal until another is found, the owner need not provide another animal. But if the renter needed to go east and the royal officer went west, it is like the animal died. Rambam and the Mechaber, however, write that it will not ultimately return, and therefore apparently do not accept that interpretation, for according to Rashi it eventually returns, though not by itself. It therefore appears that Rambam interprets "royal service from which it does not return" literally, or at least as meaning that it will not return while the renter still needs it for his work, which also counts as not returning because a later return is of no benefit to him. This also appears from Tosafos at the end of Bava Metzia 78b. Nevertheless, the owner need not provide another animal when the royal servant took it "along its route." They interpret this phrase as Rabbeinu Chananel does, meaning that the officer happened upon him while he was traveling with the animal. We then say the renter's fortune caused it, for had he remained at home and used the animal there, no one would have searched for him. This differs from a case where the animal was not taken on the route but the officers searched for it at his home. There the Gemara says that the owner must provide another animal, because it resembles the case of death: just as the Angel of Death would have taken it wherever it was, so too the officers would have searched for the animal and taken it even had it remained with the owner. See the Perishah for further discussion. It follows that the Mechaber's phrase "if it was taken along its route" refers to the clause concerning royal service. The following words, "the owner can say, etc.," apply to all three preceding clauses.
And what he writes, "When does this apply? When he rented it to carry, etc." This refers to the cases where the animal became ill or mentally impaired, not to the case of royal seizure.
And what he writes, "that it is possible to cast it down without concern." Meaning, there is no significant concern if the animal falls under its load, because this load will not be ruined by the fall. The only result is extra labor and delay in reloading it. But where the animal was rented for riding, there is concern that it may fall on a bridge and into the water or into a pit. Likewise, if it was rented to carry glassware or similar goods, the load may be damaged if it falls from the animal. Therefore the owner must provide another animal.
He returns the rental payment, etc. The Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam holds that whenever the animal did not die, even if it was rented for riding and the owner said, "this donkey," the donkey is not subjected to the renter such that he may rent or purchase another from its value. See there. The Tur disagrees. According to the Tur, if it was rented for riding or for carrying glassware and its legs became worm-infested or it became mentally impaired, the law is the same as if the animal died. Regarding death, he writes afterward that even if the owner wishes to return the rent, the renter may say, "I do not want my money back; I want an animal." If he rented an unspecified animal and rental prices increased, the owner must add as much as necessary from his own property to provide another animal. If he said, "this donkey," and its value is sufficient to purchase or rent another, this animal is sold and another is purchased or rented. See there. See also what I write below in resolving the major difficulties raised against Rambam by the Rosh and Maggid Mishneh, from which I proved that Rambam too agrees with the Tur on this point.
He must provide another animal. The Tur writes: Ra'avad explains that this is specifically where the owner made a kinyan, for otherwise how did he obligate himself? At most there is a mi shepara, and once the animal died there is no one upon whom to impose mi shepara. My master the Rosh writes that by the renter's meshichah of the donkey, all the owner's assets became obligated to provide a donkey until the destination. End of his words. Rambam and the Mechaber conclude, "and if he did not provide one, the renter may sell, etc.," which implies that they disagree with both views. According to Ra'avad and the Rosh, either the kinyan or the meshichah would subject all the owner's assets to provide another animal. According to Rambam, apparently only this particular animal is subjected, even where he rented an unspecified animal. Yet he agrees with the Rosh that no formal kinyan is required, because Rif, Rashba, Rabbeinu Yonatan, and the Tur follow the Rosh. See there.
He rents another animal with its proceeds. This is stated as an even greater novelty. It goes without saying that if its value is enough to buy another animal, even an inferior one, he purchases it, because the principal value of the first animal is not consumed. Even if its value suffices only to rent another animal, he nevertheless sells the carcass and rents another, because we rule like Shmuel that we do not concern ourselves with consumption of the principal.
