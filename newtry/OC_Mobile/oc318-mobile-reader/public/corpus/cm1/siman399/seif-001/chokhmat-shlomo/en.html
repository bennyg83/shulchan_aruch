Seif 1. "A pregnant cow that caused damage, etc." Note: See Shach, seif katan 1, who cites Tosafot that in a case of a fine we do not apply the rule "since he cannot swear, he must pay." The reason is that no Torah oath is imposed in connection with a fine, because if the defendant were to admit the fine he would be exempt. The Shach later in this siman also rules like the Raavad. It appears to me that there is a proof for this from Bava Kamma 74b. The Mishna teaches that payment cannot be imposed through one witness. The Gemara asks, "Is that not obvious?" and answers that the next clause, exemption by the defendant's own admission, is stated in a manner parallel to one witness, teaching that one who admits a fine and later witnesses come is liable, contrary to Rav Huna. The Gemara then discusses Rav Huna's rule itself. This is difficult: why challenge Rav Huna from a baraita and labor to reconcile it if his view has already been rejected by the Mishna, which appears to show that one who admits a fine and later witnesses come is liable? I suggest the following. Why does the Gemara ask that the case of one witness is obvious? Perhaps the novelty is that even an oath is not required, although ordinarily one witness imposes an oath and an oath can lead to payment. But this itself needs explanation: why should he not swear like any other defendant? Tosafot answer that no oath applies to a fine because an admission would exempt him. That answer works only if we already know that admission to a fine exempts. If we did not know that rule, why should a single witness not impose an oath? The Gemara's question can therefore be read as a dilemma. If the Mishna merely teaches that one witness cannot directly impose the fourfold or fivefold payment, that is obvious. If it teaches exemption from an oath, why is he exempt? One cannot answer that admission to a fine exempts him, because then that principle is already contained in the first clause and the later clause "by his own admission" becomes unnecessary. Moreover, the Mishna should have said "he does not swear," rather than "he does not pay." One might alternatively have explained that the Mishna concerns a thief who does not contradict the witness but says that he does not know whether the animal he slaughtered or sold was the stolen one. We might have thought that a single witness is believed in such a circumstance, just as after seclusion in the sotah process, once the preliminary warning was established by two witnesses, one witness can suffice regarding the ensuing conduct because there is a basis for suspicion. Similarly, once theft itself was established by two witnesses, perhaps one witness could suffice for slaughter or sale. There is also a view in Tosafot that when the Torah gives credibility to one witness and the defendant does not swear against him, the witness may be treated as two. The Mishna could therefore teach that even so the one witness does not impose payment. Yet if the thief says "I do not know," why should he not be obligated to swear and then, being unable to swear, pay? The answer that "since he cannot swear, he must pay" does not apply to fines rests precisely on the rule that there is no oath obligation on a fine because admission would exempt him. That would again prove the admission rule and render the later clause unnecessary. The Gemara therefore must explain that the later clause teaches the further rule that if he admitted and witnesses subsequently came, he is liable. This reasoning, however, depends on our ruling like Rabbi Abba that one who cannot swear must pay. Rav Huna himself could hold unlike Rabbi Abba and reject that rule in a claim of "I do not know." He could then explain the Mishna as a case where the thief says he does not know. The novelty would be that despite the basis for suspicion, like sotah, one witness is still not sufficient. No oath would be imposed because, according to those authorities cited by the Shach in siman 75, a Torah oath is not imposed on a claim of uncertainty. On that interpretation the Mishna does not prove that admission to a fine exempts, and if the thief made a definite denial perhaps he would indeed have to swear. The clause of exemption by his own admission would therefore still be necessary. Thus, according to our law like Rabbi Abba, the Gemara's initial question is sound and leads to the conclusion that admission followed by witnesses creates liability. But according to Rav Huna himself the Mishna can be interpreted otherwise, so it did not yet refute him. The Gemara therefore needed to challenge him separately from the baraita. This provides a clear proof from the Gemara for Tosafot's position that, in the final analysis, ordinary oath rules and the rule "since he cannot swear, he must pay" do not apply to a fine. Examine this carefully.
