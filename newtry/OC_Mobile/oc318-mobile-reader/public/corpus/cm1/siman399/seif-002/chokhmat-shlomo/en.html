Seif 2. "A pregnant cow that gored, etc." Note: The source of this law is the opening Mishna of the chapter Shor Shenagach Et HaParah, ending with the rule, "This is a great principle in monetary law: the burden of proof rests on the claimant." See my explanation in my work on Yoreh Deah, fifth edition, Hilchot Milah, siman 266. On examining the matter I noticed something new. The Rambam and the Mechaber reverse the order of the Mishna. The Mishna first discusses an ox that gored a cow and then says, "Similarly, a cow that gored an ox." They begin with the pregnant cow that gored and only afterward discuss an ox that gored a cow. This reversal must have a reason. We rule in the case of a doubtful heir and a definite heir that a doubt cannot displace a certainty. Tosafot ask the same principle in the case of one litigant claiming "all of it is mine" and the other "half is mine." Accordingly, where a pregnant cow gored and it is uncertain whether the fetus also participated in the goring, one might argue that the cow certainly caused damage and only the fetus is doubtful, so the doubtful factor should not displace the certain one. This, however, may depend on the dispute of Rabbi Akiva and Rabbi Yishmael in Bava Kamma 36 and elsewhere whether the damaging ox itself becomes assigned to the victim or whether the victim is merely a creditor. Tosafot in the opening sugya imply that according to the view that the owner can satisfy the claim with money, the victim has no proprietary interest in the ox and we focus on the tortfeasor himself, who remains in possession. According to Rabbi Yishmael, who regards the victim as a creditor, one could say that since we are judging the liable person and some liability is certain, a doubt cannot displace the certainty. According to Rabbi Akiva, who holds that the ox itself is assigned, we focus on the specific property, and the cow and fetus are two separate bodies. The principle that a doubt cannot displace a certainty therefore does not apply. In the reverse case, where the injured cow was pregnant, we focus on the victim's loss. Since at least part of the loss was certainly caused, one might reason that the doubt should not displace the certainty and should include the fetus as well. According to this, the original Mishna can be understood as following Rabbi Yishmael, who treats the victim as a creditor, and therefore proceeds from lesser to greater novelty: not only where the ox damaged the cow, where the doubt concerns the extent of the victim's loss, but even where the pregnant cow itself caused the damage and there is a definite damaging body, we still do not say that a doubt cannot displace a certainty. We, however, rule like Rabbi Akiva that the ox itself is assigned to the victim. The greater novelty therefore runs in the opposite direction. In a case where the pregnant cow gored, there is no basis at all to apply the certainty principle because the cow and fetus are two bodies. But even where an ox gored a pregnant cow, where one could more plausibly say that the victim certainly lost something and the doubt should not displace that certainty, we nevertheless do not apply the principle. This explains why the Rambam and Mechaber reverse the order. In the cited work on Yoreh Deah I offered another resolution along the approach developed there. Examine carefully.
