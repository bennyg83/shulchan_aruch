If it appears to the beit din, etc. See Responsa of Rabbi Moshe Rothenburg, Choshen Mishpat, siman 19, who analyzes what precisely must "appear to the beit din." Does this mean that the debtor has no other land remaining, even if he still has movable property, since concealment or loss is possible; or that he is utterly impoverished and has nothing at all; or perhaps neither circumstance prevents the sale, since although he owns nothing now he may acquire property by the time collection becomes due? He concludes that the intent is a third possibility: where the beit din knows that the sale is being made in order to evade creditors, or that the debtor intends to leave for a distant place, and the like. This, he writes, is the simple and clear meaning of the Rama here. He further discusses a case of two partners who owned a house together. The house burned down, leaving only the land. One partner demanded, "Either buy my share or sell me yours," because he no longer wished to remain in partnership. The other partner was subject to a creditor's lien and to his wife's ketubah, and those lienholders sought to prevent the transaction. The local custom was that when such a claim was made the beit din issued a formal adjudication vesting the property in one partner. The question was whether such an adjudication could be issued when it would extinguish the liens, and even if the adjudication was proper, whether the lienholders could attach the money or require that land be purchased with it so that their liens would remain, since the debtor would otherwise be left with no land and was a poor craftsman. He rules at length that if the law requires the adjudication, the lienholders cannot prevent the sale or attach the proceeds, just as in the law of one who sells property to a non Jew in siman 111, seif 13. Although one might ask how beit din can actively extinguish their liens by issuing the adjudication, nevertheless, since the partner can compel the owner himself by the claim "buy or sell," he certainly can assert the same claim against the lienholders. Their lien cannot be stronger than the owner's own right. He may say to them: if you wish your liens to remain, buy my share, or allow me to buy the whole property. He need not suffer a loss merely because the debtor is poor or because their liens will thereby fall away. Just as a wealthy partner may assert "buy or sell" against a poor partner even when he knows the poor partner cannot buy, so too here. He further analyzes the Rama in siman 171, seif 6, that one may not assert "buy or sell" for less than market value; seif 5, concerning division where the house would lose more than one fifth of its value; seif 14, that the claim is not made regarding mortgaged property; and the Shach there, seif katan 11. His conclusion is that in the case at hand no regard is given to the wife or the creditor, and the customary adjudication is issued. He adds that if, when the loan was made or when the marriage took place, this land was already held in partnership, the liens certainly cannot defeat the partner's claim, because from the outset the lien attached subject to the preexisting right of "buy or sell." Likewise, if the land was purchased jointly only after the marriage and loan, the liens never acquired more than the debtor's limited joint interest, which from the moment of acquisition was subject to the partners' reciprocal right to compel a buyout. The preceding analysis is therefore needed chiefly where the debtor originally owned the land alone at the time of the loan or marriage and later entered the partnership, or where the house originally had a legally divisible size. Even there, however, he concludes that the partner does not lose a right granted him by Torah law merely because others hold liens against his partner. See there.
