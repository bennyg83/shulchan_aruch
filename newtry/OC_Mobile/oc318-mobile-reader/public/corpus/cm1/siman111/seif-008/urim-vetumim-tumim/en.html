Even if they are inferior land. It is straightforward that if the debtor sold land while movables remain in his possession, the creditor cannot collect from encumbered property, because the purchaser can say, "I left you a place from which to collect." However, in Bava Kamma 109, concerning one who robbed his father, swore falsely to him, and then the father died, the Mishnah teaches that he pays the principal to his father's sons or brothers. If he lacks sufficient property, or does not wish to pay directly, he borrows money and creditors come and collect from what he pays. Tosafot write that where the stolen object no longer exists, one can readily say that he pays the heirs with land and his creditors therefore collect it from them, even though he did not designate it as an apotiki and he owns other property. Where the stolen object still exists, however, how can the creditors collect it? Tosafot answer that he made it an apotiki, and then ask that if one made his ox an apotiki and sold it, the creditor cannot collect it. They are forced to distinguish between a sale and a gift, or to say that in this setting a gift is weaker. See there. The Maharsha asks regarding Tosafot's initial assumption: how could they say that where the stolen object no longer exists, he gives the heirs land and the creditor collects it even without an apotiki and even if the robber owns other property? We do not collect from encumbered property where unencumbered property remains. The Maharsha answers that Tosafot's phrase "even if he has other property" means other movables, not other land. See there. This would imply that where the debtor retains movables, a purchaser of land cannot say, "I left you a place from which to collect." This requires study, because we find no authority who joins the Maharsha in such a ruling. In truth, the Maharsha's question can be answered simply. The rule that one does not collect from encumbered property while unencumbered property remains is an enactment for tikkun olam, not the essential law. In Perek HaNizakin the Gemara asks whether this enactment applies to a gift, since had the recipient not exerted himself and pleased the giver, the giver would not have given him the gift. Accordingly, Tosafot could plainly hold that property transferred by the robber solely in order to fulfill the mitzvah of returning what he stole is not protected by this enactment. It is no stronger than a deathbed gift of the entire estate, which is not considered encumbered property for this purpose. See Gittin 50b, Tosafot s.v. shekhiv mera, and the Rashba's novellae, who explicitly says that tikkun olam does not apply to a deathbed gift. The present transfer is certainly no stronger. The case where the stolen object remains extant and was made an apotiki is different. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 11b, s.v. kegon, hold that under the strict law a creditor does not collect even from heirs, and even where a slave was made an apotiki, collection from heirs occurs only because the Sages did not distinguish between heirs and purchasers. Thus the property is not truly subject to the lien. The creditor collects from the debtor himself only because beit din compels him to fulfill the mitzvah of paying his debt, not because the object is encumbered in a manner that survives to the heirs. If under the essential law the creditor cannot collect from heirs, he likewise cannot collect from a deathbed recipient, who is no weaker than an heir. Nor should he collect the object paid to the victim's brothers and sons, who have no personal mitzvah to pay the robber's creditor. Tosafot's question is therefore sound, even though their ultimate answer is strained. In the earlier case, by contrast, collection is available under the essential law and only the tikkun olam enactment would obstruct it. That enactment plainly does not apply to this peculiar restitution transfer, so there is no difficulty. According to the Maharsha, however, there remains a question. Why did Tosafot enter into difficulties by establishing the extant stolen object as an apotiki? They could have answered simply that the robber encumbered his movables to the creditor together with land, in which case the creditor can collect them even from encumbered property without doubt. The Maharsha apparently holds that this would create the problem that if the robber retains other property, the recipient could say, "I left you a place from which to collect." He answers that if the debtor retains only movables this argument would not help because, once the movables were encumbered together with land, their law returns to that of land. Yet even then Tosafot could have said that he made the stolen object an apotiki and also encumbered that apotiki together with land. Then everything would be resolved, because the recipient could not say, "I left you a place," since the object was an apotiki. At first glance Tosafot still requires explanation. Therefore, it appears that Tosafot's difficulty is according to Abaye, who holds in Pesachim 31 that a creditor collects retroactively. Before actual collection the creditor may consecrate the property, because it is revealed retroactively that it was his. If the property is chametz, the creditor violates the prohibition of possessing chametz. See Tosafot in Gittin 41, who explain that according to Abaye the property is considered to stand in the creditor's domain, and the debtor has no power to consecrate it or effect other dispositions. If so, the Mishnah in which the robber borrows and pays the heirs, and then creditors come and collect what he paid, is difficult according to Abaye. Since the creditor collects retroactively, it is revealed that the property stood in the creditor's domain from the outset and was never truly the robber's. The robber therefore did not fulfill his obligation of restitution and payment, because he paid with property that was not his at all. This is a powerful difficulty according to Abaye. Tosafot therefore establish the Mishnah as a case where the stolen object remains extant and the robber made it an apotiki. The Gemara there makes clear that Abaye's rule of retroactive collection applies to land, which does not require further collection in the same sense, for if the debtor sells it the creditor can remove it from the purchaser. It does not apply to movables, which still require collection, because the debtor can sell them and thereby defeat the lien. The Maharsha writes this explicitly in Pesachim on the passage beginning "if you say that he collects retroactively." See there. Accordingly, even though the stolen object was made an apotiki, it still requires collection, because the debtor can sell it or otherwise dispose of it. Retroactive collection therefore does not apply, and the robber validly fulfills restitution according to Abaye. It is no longer a case of paying with property that was never his. Tosafot could not instead say that the movables were encumbered together with land, because that makes their law equivalent to land for all purposes, as is well known from the authorities. The original difficulty according to Abaye would then return: the creditor would collect retroactively. Tosafot's words are therefore clear, and there is no need for the Maharsha's strained explanation. This is precise.
He may not collect from encumbered property. Consider a case where Reuven has two claims against Shimon, one a loan evidenced by a promissory note and one an oral loan. Reuven comes to collect from Shimon in circumstances where the written loan has priority over the oral loan. Reuven nevertheless collected Shimon's unencumbered property for the oral loan, and now seeks to use the written loan to collect from purchasers of Shimon's property. The purchaser argues: "I left you unencumbered property from which to collect. If you chose to apply it to your oral loan, who told you to do so? Your written loan had priority." At first glance there is room to doubt the law from the following passage. Ketubot 96b discusses a widow who sells property of the orphans. Rabbi Yehudah holds that she writes whether she sold for maintenance or for payment of her ketubah. Rabbi Yose holds that she may write simply that she sold, because that strengthens her position. The Gemara initially assumes that Rabbi Yose holds the property to stand in the widow's presumptive possession. If the orphans claimed that they had paid her maintenance, they would therefore not be believed. This is why she may leave the purpose of the sale unspecified, thereby improving her position. Rashi explains that if all of her husband's remaining property is eventually exhausted, she can then collect her ketubah from purchasers who bought fields from her husband. If she had already designated the prior sale as being for her ketubah, then her remaining claim would be maintenance, which cannot be collected from purchasers. The Gemara rejects the initial premise and says that everyone agrees the property stands in the orphans' presumptive possession. Rabbi Yose's reason is instead the principle taught by Abaye the Elder: if a dying person says, "Give two hundred zuz to so and so, my creditor," the recipient may choose to take it as a gift or as payment of his debt. If he takes it as a gift, his position is stronger. Rashi explains that a gift cannot be collected from purchasers, whereas for his debt he can collect from purchasers. Similarly, a widow cannot collect maintenance from purchasers, but can collect her ketubah from them. See there. Tosafot, s.v. veRabbi Yose, ask that the purchasers should be able to say, "We left you a place from which to collect." They answer first that the case is comparable to one where the unencumbered property was later destroyed, in which case the creditor may collect from encumbered property. We cannot say that the widow caused her own loss by using the property, because she sold it for the necessary purpose of maintenance. Alternatively, the case involves purchasers who bought only after she made the earlier sale. Rashi, both in the initial assumption and in the final explanation involving Abaye the Elder, writes that she collects the ketubah from purchasers who bought from the husband, not from purchasers from the orphans. The second answer of Tosafot therefore does not fit Rashi, unless one adopts the strained assumption that the husband left property available for sale by the heirs while her maintenance and ketubah liens remained outstanding. According to Rashi, one must use Tosafot's first answer. Yet according to all opinions the ketubah has priority over maintenance, whether according to those who disagree with Rav Hai and hold that a later written loan precedes an earlier oral loan, or according to the Rashba, who follows Rav Huna that maintenance is specially treated leniently. See there and what I wrote above in siman 104. Nevertheless, according to Rashi the purchaser cannot say, "I left you a place from which to collect." This seems to support the same result in our case: the purchaser cannot reject the written loan merely because the creditor used the unencumbered property for his later oral loan. It is treated like unencumbered property that was destroyed. The Ritva likewise adopts Rashi's language that she collects the ketubah from purchasers who bought during the husband's lifetime. However, Rashi's explanation raises several difficulties. If Abaye the Elder's case concerns purchasers who bought during the decedent's lifetime, what strengthening of the creditor's position is gained by taking the two hundred as a gift and then collecting his debt from encumbered property? The purchasers can say: "I left you unencumbered property, namely the property you received as a gift. Collect from that." Here Tosafot's answer that the unencumbered property was destroyed does not apply. According to Tosafot's second answer, involving heirs who sold after the earlier transfer, one can explain that after the recipient takes the gift the heirs sell the remaining unencumbered property. His debt then collects from that later sold property because its sale is later than the gift. If instead he had taken the earlier property as payment of his debt and the heirs later sold the remainder, he would lose the gift, because a deathbed gift does not collect from encumbered property unless the testamentary document itself has sufficient publicity, and it is only like an oral loan. See siman 41 and the gloss to seif 3. But Rashi does not establish the case as involving heirs who sold afterward, so the difficulty remains. There is another difficulty. According to Rashi, the unresolved question above on 94b, whether a creditor may collect from encumbered property when the unencumbered property was destroyed, should have been resolved from this baraita. Rashi's explanation requires Tosafot's first answer, that the case is like property destroyed, which would prove that collection from encumbered property is permitted. Likewise, the Gemara on 97 asks whether a widow who sold property for maintenance may later return and collect that same property for her ketubah. That too should be resolved from this baraita. If she can collect it back, what improvement does she gain from leaving the earlier sale undesignated? The purchaser could tell her to collect from the property she herself sold for maintenance, and there is no destruction because that property remains fully available for collection. There is also a difficulty in Rashi's explanation of the initial assumption. Rashi says the strengthening of her position is that she can later collect from purchasers, yet the Gemara only afterward gives Rabbi Yose's reason as the rule of Abaye the Elder. The Maharsha also asks why Rashi does not explain Rabbi Yehudah's reason for requiring her to state that the sale was for maintenance: otherwise, when she later seeks to collect the ketubah from purchasers, they could claim that perhaps the property she already sold had been sold for the ketubah rather than maintenance. It therefore appears that Abaye the Elder's case can be explained as follows. The dying person owns property that he acquired only after he borrowed from this creditor. If the creditor takes that property toward his debt, and his loan lacked a clause encumbering subsequently acquired property, he cannot later collect the gift from encumbered property. But if he takes this particular field as a gift, he can still collect his debt from property that had already become encumbered to the debt. The purchasers cannot say, "Collect from your gift," because subsequently acquired property was never encumbered to his loan. According to the view that an after acquired property clause is effective even without being expressly written, the Ritva establishes Abaye's case as a partial deathbed gift where no automatic after acquired lien applies. Thus in every version the recipient's stronger position is intelligible. The same structure may explain the widow who sells. The field she sells for maintenance can be one that the husband purchased or inherited after the marriage, where her ketubah did not include an after acquired property clause. As to that field, her ketubah is only like an oral loan, as explained at length above in siman 104, and her maintenance claim is likewise oral. Neither has priority over the other in that field. If either claim first collects, its collection stands. Her position is therefore strengthened by selling this after acquired field for maintenance, while retaining her ketubah claim against purchasers of property that was actually encumbered to the ketubah. They cannot say that she left them a place from which to collect, because the field used for maintenance had no written ketubah lien at all. This also answers the question whether she could later collect for her ketubah from the very field she sold for maintenance. She could not, because that field was after acquired property not covered by her ketubah lien. This is straightforward. Now, Tosafot write that the widow's action is not considered a self inflicted loss because she acted for the purpose of her maintenance. One must understand this. If under the strict priority rules her ketubah precedes maintenance, as stated above in siman 104, why is leaving the earlier claim and collecting the later one not considered a loss she caused with her own hands? It must be that because the property stands in the widow's presumptive possession for maintenance, it is as though she has already collected it for that purpose. It therefore cannot be called a loss caused by her own action; the property is already regarded as hers for maintenance. Tosafot may have intended this when they said that she acted for the purpose of maintenance. On that basis the Gemara's initial assumption is also clear. At first it did not establish the matter as a case of a field subsequently acquired by the husband and a ketubah lacking an after acquired property clause, because that is uncommon, particularly according to the view that an after acquired property clause is a scribal presumption. To say that it was explicitly excluded is likewise uncommon. The Gemara therefore had to use Tosafot's first answer, that her act was not deemed a self inflicted loss. That reasoning implies that the property stands in the widow's presumptive possession. If it stood in the orphans' possession, choosing to use the property for the later maintenance claim rather than the earlier ketubah would indeed be a loss she caused herself. This supports the initial assumption. In the conclusion, however, the Gemara says that according to all opinions the property stands in the orphans' presumptive possession. Rabbi Yose's strengthening of the widow's position is like Abaye the Elder's case. As explained, the advantage there can arise from after acquired property. The same can therefore be true of the widow: the particular field sold for maintenance was after acquired property not subject to the ketubah lien, and the purchasers cannot say she left them a place. The Gemara cites Abaye's case only as a general model. Whatever precise mechanism strengthens the creditor there can similarly strengthen the widow here, and the subjects remain parallel. Thus the difficulty based on "I left you a place" is resolved without assuming that the unencumbered property was destroyed. This after acquired property analysis also resolves Tosafot's separate question how the widow may deliberately leave the sale undesignated and thereby appear to deceive purchasers, since in truth she had already collected her ketubah. Tosafot answer that as a matter of law maintenance itself should also have been collectible from purchasers. Yet even if her collection from the first purchaser is substantively proper, there seems to be a serious wrong to another purchaser who will later be subjected to recourse. The purchaser from whom she collects the ketubah may have a warranty claim against the purchaser of the field she sold for maintenance. Had the first field truly been sold for the ketubah, that purchaser would possess all the strength the widow herself had under the ketubah, as the Ran writes at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui. Another earlier creditor could not remove it. How could the Sages advise a course that shifts the loss unjustly? One could answer with difficulty that when she originally sells a field worth one hundred for her ketubah, she certainly leaves another one hundred for maintenance. The strategy matters only if the later one hundred is exhausted by maintenance, since maintenance has no fixed limit. She can then treat the first hundred as also having been for maintenance and collect the ketubah from purchasers. The purchaser from whom the ketubah is collected can proceed not against the first purchaser, but against the second field purchaser, because that second field was genuinely sold only for maintenance and his right is weaker. But this depends on an exact pattern of retained property and is strained. According to what was stated above, however, the matter is straightforward. The field sold for maintenance was acquired by the husband only afterward and was not covered by the ketubah's after acquired lien. Either the purchaser from whom she later collects the ketubah likewise lacks an after acquired clause, in which case he has no recourse at all against that field because it was subsequently acquired and sold; or he does have such a clause. Rashi holds that where one creditor has an after acquired clause and another does not, the creditor whose lien includes subsequently acquired property can prevail even if later. There is therefore no injustice. He has a valid legal right to collect. I also found that the Ritva in Bava Metzia, in the discussion concerning increased value, writes that if a creditor first collected the increased value from unencumbered property, he cannot afterward collect the principal from encumbered property. The purchasers can say, "I left you a place from which to collect your principal. Who instructed you to collect the increase instead and leave the principal?" This explicitly supports what I have written. We therefore return to the law. If a creditor holding a written loan with priority chose to use the unencumbered property to collect a later or inferior loan, he cannot afterward collect the earlier written loan from purchasers. They may say, "I left you a place from which to collect." All the more so because the purchaser is in possession, and the burden of proof rests upon the party seeking to extract from him.
