He may not collect from encumbered property. Consider a case where Reuven has two debts against Shimon, one evidenced by a note and one an oral loan, in circumstances where the debt evidenced by the note has priority over the oral loan. Reuven collected his oral loan from Shimon's unencumbered property, and then seeks to collect the debt evidenced by the note from Shimon's purchasers. The purchaser argues: I left you unencumbered property from which to collect the secured debt. If you chose instead to use that property for your oral loan, who instructed you to do so, since the debt evidenced by the note had priority? Tumim is uncertain about this, based on Ketubot 54, concerning a widow who sells property for support. R. Yehudah says that she may write the deed of sale without specifying its purpose, because this strengthens her position. Rashi explains that if her husband's estate is later exhausted she can then collect her ketubah from purchasers who bought property from her husband. Tosafot ask that those purchasers should be able to say that she left herself a place from which to collect. They answer that it is like a case where the unencumbered property was destroyed, in which one may collect from encumbered property, and that it is not considered that she caused her own loss, because she sold the property for necessary support. Although everyone agrees that the ketubah precedes support, the purchaser still cannot say that she left herself a place from which to collect. Tumim therefore considers that in our case as well the purchaser may perhaps be unable to make that argument, because the property is treated as though it was destroyed. Ultimately, however, he cites the Ritva, who rules that the purchaser can say, I left you a place from which to collect, and Tumim labors to reconcile the sugya in Ketubot. In my view the matter is straightforward in accordance with the Ritva. In Ketubot, because the widow herself is compelled to sell property for her support without authorization of beit din, she can no longer collect her ketubah from the very purchasers who bought from her for support, because of the principle that a person is deemed to have accepted responsibility for her own acts. Therefore, relative to her, the property is truly like unencumbered property that was destroyed, and she may collect from other purchasers. But if beit din sold land to provide for her support, so that the principle of personal responsibility does not apply, she could certainly collect her ketubah from those who bought from beit din for her support. Consequently, she could not then collect from purchasers who had bought from her husband earlier, because they could say that she left herself a place from which to collect. The same applies to another creditor who has no authority to sell on his own and may act only through beit din. If beit din sells property for payment of the oral loan, the creditor could later collect the secured debt from those purchasers, and consequently he cannot collect from purchasers whose purchases preceded that sale, because they can certainly say that he left himself a place from which to collect. Likewise, if the creditor himself took the property in satisfaction of the oral loan, he cannot collect the secured debt from earlier purchasers. His own collection cannot place him in a better position than if the property had been sold to another. Moreover, support is different because the widow is compelled to sell without beit din, and Chazal gave her this authority for that very reason. She is also compelled to collect support before her ketubah so as not to lose her support, and therefore her act is not considered a deliberate dissipation of available assets. By contrast, where a creditor chooses to satisfy an oral loan first, that is considered that he caused the loss with his own hands. Tumim further questions Tosafot in Ketubot. Tosafot ask how the widow can say that she sold for support when in truth she sold for her ketubah, since that would involve falsehood. They answer that because by law she may also collect support from purchasers, and because she can say that she sold for support, purchasers should have protected themselves by leaving enough property for her ketubah. Tumim asks that this still causes injustice, because when she later collects her ketubah from purchasers who bought during her husband's lifetime, those purchasers will then recover from the purchasers who bought the field she sold for support. If in truth that field was sold for the ketubah, whose lien preceded the earlier purchasers, those earlier purchasers should not be able to recover from it. This question is surprising. Tosafot's answer that purchasers should have been wary because she could say she sold for support necessarily applies only according to Tosafot's second answer, which concerns purchasers who bought after her sale. According to their first answer, where the purchasers bought during the husband's lifetime and the property is treated as destroyed, that explanation cannot apply, because those purchasers did in fact leave enough property for her ketubah. Thus Tosafot's statement applies only to their second answer. As to Tosafot's principal question that the purchasers should be able to say that she left herself a place from which to collect, there is another difficulty. That defense works only where, at the time of collection, there remains property available for collection. Rashi explains that the concern is that the heirs' property may later be exhausted. Likewise, there is reason to fear that the particular field she retained may be destroyed or decline in value, at which point she would be able to collect from purchasers who bought during her husband's lifetime. If she had written that she sold for her ketubah, however, she would then be unable to collect from them, because she would be bound by the representation made in the deed.
