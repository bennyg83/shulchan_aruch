Even if they are inferior land. By Torah law a creditor collects from inferior land. The Sages required intermediate land only for the benefit of lenders and borrowers. They did not extend that enactment against purchasers, just as they did not extend it against heirs. Therefore, whenever the debtor has any property from which collection can be made, the creditor may not seize encumbered property from purchasers. The same applies even if the debtor has movable property. Although movable property is not encumbered to the creditor, collection from the debtor himself may be made even from the cloak on his shoulders, and therefore the creditor may not proceed against purchasers. See Tumim.
He shall not seize, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that if Reuven has two claims against Shimon, one by note and one oral, and Reuven first collects Shimon's free property for the oral debt and then seeks to collect from purchasers for the documented debt, the purchasers may argue, 'We left you property from which to collect. Who told you to use the free property for your oral loan?' If the earlier collection was from movable property, from after acquired property, or from other assets in which the documented creditor had no priority, so that an oral creditor who collected first would retain what he collected, Reuven may still proceed against the purchasers. He can say, 'I lawfully collected that property for the oral debt, because what an oral creditor collected remains collected.' If, however, the documented creditor had priority in the free property but chose to use it for the oral loan, he may not later seize from purchasers for the written debt, because they can say that they left him a place from which to collect. See Tumim.
Therefore, etc. The word 'therefore' refers to the concluding rule that among successive recipients of gifts, the one whose deed is earlier has priority. The later recipient's property is treated, relative to the earlier recipient, like free property. One does not collect from encumbered property where free property remains.
Into nine shares. Here everyone agrees that division follows the proportional amounts. Although above in siman 104 we rule that creditors do not divide proportionately to the amount of their debts, there the issue depends upon liens, and the lien securing a debt of one hundred attaches at the same moment as the lien securing two hundred. Here the matter depends upon estimating the donor's intent. Since he intended to give one beneficiary more than another, we divide according to those proportions.
Whoever is earlier in the deed has priority. If the last recipient holds inferior property while the earlier recipient holds intermediate property, the creditor collects from the later recipient, because his property is free relative to the earlier recipient. Riaz writes in his glosses to Rif, chapter HaNizakin, that if one gave intermediate land to his son and retained inferior land, the creditor nevertheless collects from the intermediate land. Knesset HaGedolah cites this. It appears that Riaz follows the authorities in Even HaEzer siman 100 who hold that a gift to one's son is not treated as an ordinary gift but as inheritance. According to those who disagree there, the same disagreement applies here.
