The son's creditor collects from it. The responsum of the Rosh and the Tur explicitly infer this because the son wrote to the creditor that property subsequently acquired would be encumbered. The Sema and Bach wondered why a clause encumbering subsequently acquired property is necessary. The Bach sought to explain that there were two creditors, one who lent to the son during the father's lifetime and another who lent after the father's death. Yet one who examines the responsum of the Rosh will see that at its conclusion he writes: "Therefore the son's creditor collects the land for his debt, whether the debt was incurred after his father's death or during his father's lifetime." It is clear that there were not two creditors. No second creditor is mentioned at all. At first I thought that there was indeed only one creditor, but he held two debts against the son, one lent during the father's lifetime and one after the father's death. The responsum also makes clear that, besides the particular land at issue, the father left his son other land and movables. The Perishah already asks why the creditor should not collect from those assets. One must say that he had waived his right to collect from those particular lands. The debt lent after the father's death could nevertheless be collected from those lands and movables. If the earlier loan, made during the father's lifetime, did not include a clause encumbering subsequently acquired property, then, as we clarified above, where a creditor lacks such a clause and the debtor subsequently acquires property and afterward borrows again, that property becomes encumbered to the later debt even while it remains in the debtor's hands. The creditor could therefore not collect the earlier debt from the other property from which he had waived collection, while regarding the later debt the debtor could tell him, "I left for you the property subject to that debt. Go to the field concerning which the condition was made, for it is encumbered to you for the later debt and not for the first." The creditor would thus lose part of his claim. If, however, the first debt included an after acquired property clause, he collects that earlier debt from the disputed field and then collects the later debt from the other land and movables inherited by the son, so that all of his debt is recovered. Nevertheless, this explanation did not satisfy me. First, it does not fit well with the question in the responsum. Second, the Tur did not mention that other land had been left or that there were two loans. Why then did he copy the requirement that the son wrote an after acquired property clause and thereby leave room for error? There must be a substantive reason. The principal difficulty is the one raised by Maharam Alshakar in a responsum concerning the next responsum in that collection. The Rosh was asked what should be done in a case where Reuven had a single daughter, but she and her husband owed money to another person. If Reuven's estate passed to them by inheritance, their creditor would collect it, and Reuven's efforts would be for nothing. Reuven therefore asked the Rosh for advice, stating that if no solution existed he would transfer the inheritance away from his daughter and give it to others. The Rosh advised him to convey all of his property to his daughter as a gift subject to a reservation that no lien or creditor should take effect upon it. He explains at length, based on the authorities, that this is not comparable to the gift of Beit Choron. See there. Maharam Alshakar asks: according to the Rosh, she is the sole daughter. If the condition is voided, her creditor can collect from her by way of inheritance. This requires study. Maharam Alshakar sought to distinguish between language of "on condition that" and language of "except." With "on condition," there is a condition. If creditors come to collect, the condition is violated, the gift is void, the property returns to the father's estate, and the heir then inherits it. With "except," however, the giver makes an actual reservation. The father retained for himself the right that no debt should attach, and that reservation itself is never uprooted. See his lengthy discussion. Despite all of his elaboration, I do not understand the distinction clearly. What does it help that the father reserved this right? At his death, does the reserved right disappear into the air, or is it extinguished entirely, as in a reservation "for me and not for my heirs," or do his heirs inherit that reserved right like any other property? If his daughter is his sole heir, she inherits the reservation itself, and her creditor should still collect. Moreover, the Rosh himself concludes that the creditors cannot collect because doing so would annul the condition, the gift would then be void, and the land would return to its original owner. According to Maharam Alshakar's understanding, there is no annulment of a condition and no annulment of the gift at all. There is only a complete reservation in the property that prevents any creditor's lien from taking effect. Maharam Alshakar himself also asks why, if there is such a major legal distinction between "on condition" and "except," Rabbenu Yaakov in the Tur remained silent and did not state it. He presses to maintain his distinction, but omitting such a major law remains difficult.
Therefore, it appears that the Rosh holds as follows. So long as the condition has not been violated, the gift and its condition remain fully effective. The creditor's argument is that he should be permitted to collect and thereby violate the condition, with the result that the gift becomes void and the property then falls to the son by inheritance. This works where the land itself is encumbered to the creditor. The creditor comes and forcibly collects the field, saying, "My lien rests upon it." If his collection thereby annuls the gift, that is precisely what he seeks. This is the case where the debtor wrote an after acquired property clause, so that the subsequently acquired field itself became encumbered. If there was no after acquired property clause, however, the creditor has no lien upon this field. His right is only the principle stated by the Gemara, "even from the cloak upon his shoulders," meaning that repayment of a debt is a mitzvah and beit din compels the debtor to pay. If he has no property available for payment under the terms of the gift, he is not presently within that coercive obligation. The son can then say: "I am not obligated to annul my father's condition and gift. My father's intent was that the gift and its condition remain effective, and I will honor that intent." The Rosh holds that beit din cannot compel the debtor to violate the condition merely in order to create property from which the creditor may be paid. Where the creditor has an actual lien upon the field, by contrast, he collects it without the debtor's consent. This is why an after acquired property clause is necessary. See Responsa Ramban siman 106, which likewise discusses one who gives a gift to his daughter on condition that her husband have no authority over it. Where she is the heir, the husband acquires rights in the gift after the father's death. The Ramban's proof is from one who purchases a field from his father and then the father dies, where the field is treated as an ancestral field. According to the Rambam in chapter 4 of Hilkhot Arakhin, that proof is not conclusive, because we rule like Rabbi Yehudah and Rabbi Shimon, who hold that the Torah specially included the case since it was destined to become an ancestral field. In any event, the Ramban does not say that the husband automatically takes control because the condition of the gift becomes void and the property therefore becomes inheritance. This implies that one cannot compel the woman to violate the condition. The same applies here. Without an after acquired property clause there is nothing compelling the debtor to annul the gift, and therefore the Rosh required such a clause. The creditor then comes and enforces his lien in any event. There is therefore no contradiction between the two responsa of the Rosh. In the second responsum, the daughter's creditor had no after acquired property clause. The Rosh could therefore advise that an appropriate condition in the gift would suffice, because the creditor had no direct lien on the subsequently acquired property. This is well resolved. There is another possible explanation. It is a general principle that a person does not utter a condition or wording entirely in vain. Even apparently superfluous language is interpreted as having legal effect, all the more so an entire condition that would otherwise be meaningless. If a father gives property on condition that no creditor's lien take effect upon it, but the property will nevertheless become subject to the creditor by inheritance immediately upon his death, what did the condition accomplish? It would be empty words. We should therefore understand the condition as though he expressly stipulated that if a creditor attempts to collect, the property should instead pass as a gift to his other relatives or family members. This is the evident intent and is treated as though explicitly stated. That reasoning, however, applies where the gift is to take effect only at death, or from now and after death, or one hour before death. In such a case, if the condition accomplished nothing upon death it would be entirely pointless. But where he gave an absolute gift effective immediately during his lifetime and imposed the condition, the condition does have a meaningful function during his life. We can say that he intended only that strangers not take control of the property during his lifetime, but did not contemplate or intend the post death result. After his death, therefore, the Rosh's first responsum would apply: violation of the condition voids the gift, the property becomes inheritance, and the creditor collects. In the second responsum, by contrast, the Rosh's advice concerned a gift structured to take effect from now and after death, or one hour before death. A person does not impose a meaningless condition. The condition must therefore mean that if the creditor would otherwise collect, the daughter is not to inherit the property at all, and the Rosh correctly ruled that the creditor has no power to collect. This distinction is logical. Yet if so, the original question remains why the Tur did not state this important distinction. The first explanation, however, is expressly reflected in the requirement that the debtor wrote an after acquired property clause. In any event, where the giver gives the property to take effect after death and there is no after acquired property clause, whether the creditor can collect remains a matter requiring study, because the responsum of the Rosh appears to indicate the opposite.
