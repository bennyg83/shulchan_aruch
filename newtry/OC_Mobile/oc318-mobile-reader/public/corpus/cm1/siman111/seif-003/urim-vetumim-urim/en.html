The condition is valid. A person can expressly condition a gift however he wishes. This is unlike the gift of Beit Horon, where the rule that a gift one cannot consecrate is not a gift arose because there was no express valid condition. Here, if a creditor tries to collect, the condition is not fulfilled, the gift is void, and the field returns to the donor. The creditor has no claim on another person's field. Therefore, if the donor himself also owes the creditor and the gift predates the debt document, the creditor can collect whichever way one analyzes it.
And the seller stipulated with him, etc. One reason is that if the condition voids the sale, the seller must return the purchase price. Tur gives another reason: what interest did the seller have in making such a condition unless the purchaser induced him to do so? It therefore looks like an evasive transfer. A practical difference arises if the land increased in value. Under the first reason, the creditor has no claim to value beyond the original purchase price. Another difference arises where there are later creditors. If the sale is void and the purchaser recovers only money from the seller, money has no priority among creditors. Under the second reason the sale remains valid and the creditor collects the entire land with his priority. The unqualified language of Tur and the Shulchan Aruch implies the second rationale.
