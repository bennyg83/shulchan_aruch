If they gave, etc. See Sanhedrin 71a, Pesachim 88b, Nazir 24b, and Kiddushin 23b concerning property given to a woman or slave on condition that the spouse or master acquire no rights in it. Rabbenu Tam explains that the Gemara’s course follows Rav Sheshet, as do all these passages; Nedarim 88b likewise attributes the position to Rabbi Meir. Rav Hai Gaon and Ramban rule like Shmuel, understanding the dispute as whether a woman’s or slave’s hand is legally the husband’s or master’s hand. Rabbenu Tam and Raavad rule like Rav and Rabbi Meir because the issue in Nedarim is one of prohibition. Rif, Rambam, and the rulings cited in Even HaEzer 85:11 and Yoreh Deah 222 and 267 follow the Rabbis and Rabbi Elazar. Here, however, all agree that the condition is effective. A slave’s hand is his master’s and a slave cannot acquire independently, and similarly with a wife; but the recipient of a gift is not the creditor’s hand, for a creditor has no ownership in the debtor’s assets, only a lien. We follow Rava in Pesachim 31a that the creditor acquires only from the time of collection, since if the debtor had money he could redeem the property. Although Nedarim 48a states that any gift that cannot be consecrated is not a gift, that applies only to a sham transaction, as the Gemara and Ran explain. Here the donor gives the property for every purpose except satisfaction of a debt or ketubah. If the creditor were to seize it, the gift would be annulled, like a conditional bill of divorce whose condition is violated. The creditor would then be taking another’s money for his debt; therefore, in either event, he cannot collect and the property remains with the recipient. See citation 46.
But if, etc. Tur rules this way, citing his brother Rabbi Yehuda’s responsum, brought by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 99. A sale cannot be annulled unless the purchase money is returned, so the seller certainly wishes the condition to be annulled. Moreover, even if the sale is annulled, the money becomes a loan and is recoverable at least by rabbinic law. Since a sale is made to obtain the price, we presume the purchaser appeased the seller, and the seller cannot simply undo it. Rosh, however, writes in a responsum in the name of Maharam, cited by Tur and Shulchan Aruch at the end of siman 99, that the buyer fails to acquire only where the transaction was intentionally contrived.
