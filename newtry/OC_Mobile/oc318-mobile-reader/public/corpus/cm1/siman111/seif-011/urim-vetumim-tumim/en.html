Yet one should examine the Gemara in Bava Batra 44b, where Shmuel says: If Reuven sold a field to Shimon without responsibility, he may not testify for him concerning it, because he thereby preserves it before his creditor. The Gemara asks: If Reuven has other land, the creditor should return against his own property. Rashbam explains that one does not collect from encumbered property where free property is available. And if he has no other land, what difference does it make to him? This is difficult, for perhaps he has free property in a distant place. If the creditor cannot collect locally from encumbered property, he must travel far away to collect his debt. But if Reuven testifies so that the field remains with the purchaser, the creditor will seize locally, for why should he trouble himself to travel, and even the purchaser cannot defer him to free property in a distant place. Reuven's distant free property would then remain secure from collection. It appears that the Rashba, Responsa no. 1005, gives the reason why the creditor need not travel to a distant place: by strict law he could collect from purchasers, and it is only because of tikkun ha'olam that one does not collect from encumbered property where free property exists. It is therefore improper to require him to trouble himself to distant places, since that would create a closing of the door before borrowers. See there. This is satisfactory if the basic law permits collection from purchasers because the lien is d'oraisa, and the Sages merely removed that right because of tikkun ha'olam. In a case involving significant burden and distant travel, they may be understood to have left the Torah law in place. But according to the opinion that a lien is not d'oraisa, by strict law there is no collection from purchasers at all. The Sages instituted collection from purchasers because of closing the door, and they themselves said that where free property exists, because of tikkun ha'olam one does not collect from encumbered property. The matter then returns to Torah law, under which purchasers are not liable at all. Accordingly, even where the free property is distant and collection would be burdensome, so long as free property remains available in any fashion, what basis is there to take money from the purchaser, whose property is not subject to a Torah lien? Shall we remove money from the purchaser contrary to Torah law merely because collection is inconvenient for the creditor? The Rashba's reasoning does not apply, because there is no strict-law right of the creditor that should prevail over the purchaser. The creditor has no inherent claim to the purchaser's property, and wherever he can collect elsewhere there is no basis to seize from the purchaser gratuitously contrary to law. Accordingly, the Gemara's question there is directed specifically to Shmuel, and Shmuel holds at the end of Bava Batra that a lien is not d'oraisa. Therefore the rule stated here does not apply according to him. Rather, one never seizes from a purchaser where there is free property, regardless of where it is located, and the Gemara properly asks from either side of the dilemma. This also explains why the Gemara raises this question upon Shmuel and not earlier upon the baraita that teaches that one who sold a field may not testify concerning it. It could have asked there as well whether he has other property. According to what we have written, however, the baraita may hold that a lien is d'oraisa, and the case may be where he has free property in a distant place. The Gemara therefore asks specifically against Shmuel, who holds that a lien is not d'oraisa. This also resolves what Tosafot write, s.v. "if," that the Gemara could have answered that the debtor made the field an apotiki. For there is a difficulty from the earlier answer concerning one who sold a cow or cloak, that he may testify where he purchased and immediately sold it, after which the Gemara asks that perhaps the creditor stipulated a lien on after-acquired property. How does the Gemara know that Shmuel's concern is specifically that the seller not be deemed wicked for failing to pay his debt? Perhaps he does not say this at all, and the only concern is that he may have made this field an apotiki for his creditor. If so, with a cow or cloak that he bought and immediately sold, it was impossible to designate it as an apotiki, because he sold it immediately. And as for after-acquired property, how could one make an apotiki by telling the creditor that he shall have no payment except from whatever property the debtor may later acquire? Perhaps he will acquire nothing and the creditor will be left empty-handed. Where there is no apotiki, there is no concern of interested testimony, as the Gemara asks, so how could the Gemara raise its objection? According to the foregoing it is clear. The Gemara sought to establish the baraita according to the halachah, under which we rule that a lien is d'oraisa. Therefore there is no difficulty, for the witness remains interested lest he have free property in a distant place. Examine this carefully.
