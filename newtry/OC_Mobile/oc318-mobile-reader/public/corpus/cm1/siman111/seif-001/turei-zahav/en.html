(In the Shulchan Aruch, seif 4, another son). This means that he comes with a claim from whichever side one considers it, either through the gift or through inheritance, since at least one of these rights necessarily exists. The Tur, in the name of the Rosh, explains at length that the father retained a right that if the gift were voided the property would revert to him, and the son inherits that right. This answers a possible claim by the son's purchasers that the father had already removed himself from the property when he made the gift, while the gift was accepted only after his death, so that they acquired as though from ownerless property. It would resemble the property of a gentile, which becomes ownerless once the gentile receives payment, while the Jew does not acquire until the deed reaches him, as explained in siman 194. The Rosh therefore teaches that a residual right remained with the decedent and passed to his heir. It appears that the case is one where the son sold the property and a creditor now seeks to seize it from the purchaser. The Tur therefore invokes an after-acquired-property clause. It is unnecessary to explain, as Sema does in seif katan 5, that the clause refers to the borrower later acquiring the property.
