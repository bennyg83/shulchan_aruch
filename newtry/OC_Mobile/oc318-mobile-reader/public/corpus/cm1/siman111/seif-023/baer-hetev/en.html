They divide it. The Bach writes that although a lien may also attach to property the debtor will later acquire, this case concerns one who did not write an after-acquired property clause. Therefore the lien did not attach to movables that were not then in his possession. His words are astonishing, because even if he wrote such a clause for both creditors, where he borrowed from one and then another and only afterward acquired the property, they divide it, even if he wrote an after-acquired property clause to both, as stated in siman 107, seif 6. This is explicit in the Gemara and all the poskim. Shach.
To the first. The Sema writes that the case must be where the debtor did not write to the second creditor that movables were secured together with real property, because otherwise they would be pledged to the second rather than to the first, like real property acquired between two successive loans, unless he wrote to the first creditor both a clause securing movables together with real property and an after-acquired property clause, then acquired movables or lent money to others, and only afterward borrowed from the second creditor and wrote the same two clauses to him. In that case too the property is pledged to the first creditor rather than the second. His words are astonishing, because it is obvious that the clause securing movables together with real property cannot benefit the first creditor unless the first creditor also has an after-acquired property clause, as the Sema himself wrote in siman 104, seif katan 15, and siman 113, seif katan 14. Rather, when the Tur writes that the relevant property was acquired before the later loan, the case plainly is that both the first and second creditors were given both an after-acquired property clause and a clause securing movables together with real property. Perhaps the Sema was reluctant to explain it this way because then, in the opening case where the debtor lent money after borrowing from both creditors, the same would seem to apply, as the Bach writes in the passage cited nearby, and therefore the Bach too was forced into a strained explanation here. I have already written, however, that this is incorrect. Rather, the opening case applies even where both creditors were given both clauses, as explained above. This is clear. Shach.
