The Shach writes in seif katan 23 that certainly, if the creditors have a claim against land, they do not need an agav clause, because the later debtor is encumbered to the first creditor by virtue of Rabbi Natan's rule. However, Responsa Rashba no. 914 appears explicitly to disagree. The Shach nevertheless concludes that the Baal HaTerumot is primary, and argues that the Rashba's statement that we rule a creditor acquires only from the time of collection is, in his view, irrelevant, because Rabbi Natan's lien is not governed by the principle that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection. This is evident from the Gemara in Pesachim, for otherwise why would orphans who collected land on their father's debt be subject to collection by their father's creditor? See there, where the Shach discusses this at length. The Shach did not examine the Rashba carefully. The Rashba agrees that where land was collected on the debt, Rabbi Natan's rule applies. He does not dispute this at all, and as the Shach himself notes, the Gemara proves it. The Rashba's disagreement concerns only a case where movable property was collected. Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply directly to movables in that setting, as is clear from the Gemara, because they are treated as though they were still in the hands of the first borrower. There is therefore no rule of priority. The first creditor's claim is instead that his own document encumbered those movables through agav. To this the Rashba responds that because a debt instrument is not itself tangible money, it cannot be encumbered through agav. It is in that context that he writes that we rule a creditor acquires only from the time of collection and that the debt itself is not tangible property. See what I wrote above at the end of siman 85. Accordingly, all of the Shach's proofs fall away. Nevertheless, Tosafot in Ketubot 100b, s.v. "these matters," appear to support the contrary view. Tosafot ask how debt instruments belonging to orphans may be sold without public announcement, since they are movables of the orphans, and answer that the instruments had been designated for payment of the ketubah. If designation alone gives the claimant a sufficient right in them, then certainly a lien created through land and agav, which is stronger than mere designation, should do so. Indeed, I am surprised at the Rashba. Why should a creditor whose document contains an agav clause be unable to proceed against the debtor of his own borrower, not on the basis of the second promissory note as such, but on the basis that the money which my borrower lent you was itself money encumbered to me? Return that money to me, or its equivalent. Money can be encumbered through agav. If the borrower had sold encumbered movables to the second party, the first creditor could pursue them. The same should be true of the money. If the second party says that he transferred the money to others, the creditor should pursue those recipients until he reaches property from which payment can be made. Whether the debtor gave away or sold movables or money should be governed by one rule. According to the version of the Rashba's responsum before us, it appears that the second debt already existed before the first creditor lent money to the borrower. If so, the first creditor cannot say that the money of the later loan was encumbered to him, because the borrower had already lent it out before becoming indebted to him. In a case where the second loan was made afterward, even the Rashba could agree. This is also the view of the Maharit in his responsa, Choshen Mishpat; see there. However, the correct textual reading of the Rashba's responsum, as is evident from the context and as the Knesset HaGedolah writes, is that even if the second loan was later than the first loan, the difficulty remains. Perhaps the Rashba holds that when the first lender extended his loan and received an agav clause, his intention was not that the lien should attach to ordinary currency. In the Rashba's time there was no market-protection enactment, as explained above in siman 5. If every coin remained subject to seizure by the creditor, who would take even a single coin from the borrower, knowing that the next day the creditor might demand that very money as encumbered property? Commerce among Jews would become impossible. It must therefore have been understood from the outset that the lien did not attach to coins and money ordinarily spent in commerce. If so, even in this case, where market protection itself is irrelevant because the borrower has no practical concern whether he pays one creditor or another, the lien never attached to such money in the first place. Examine this carefully.
