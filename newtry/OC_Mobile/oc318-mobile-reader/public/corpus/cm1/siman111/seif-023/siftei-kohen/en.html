["After he borrowed from both of them, they divide, etc. The Bach writes that although a lien also takes effect upon property the debtor will later acquire, this case concerns one in which he did not write an after acquired property clause, and therefore the lien did not take effect on movables that were not then in his possession. His words are astonishing, for even if he did write such a clause to both creditors, where he borrowed from one, then from another, and only afterward acquired the property, they divide, even if he wrote an after acquired property clause to both, as stated above in siman 104, seif 6. This is explicit in the Gemara and all the authorities."]
["They divide. This applies even when they collect land. Although Rabbi Natan's lien applies to land and priority is ordinarily recognized regarding it, when the debtor lent money to his own creditors, those creditors became encumbered at that time to both of his lenders by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien."]
["And he wrote agav to the first, etc. The Sema, seif katan 41, writes that the case must be one where he wrote an agav clause to the first lender but not to the second. Otherwise the property would be encumbered to the second and not to the first, like land acquired by the borrower between two loans taken successively, unless he wrote to the first lender both an agav clause and an after acquired property clause, then acquired movables or lent to others, and afterward borrowed again from the second lender, to whom he likewise wrote an agav clause and an after acquired property clause. In that case as well, the property is encumbered to the first and not the second. These words are astonishing. It is obvious that agav cannot benefit the first lender unless an after acquired property clause was also written to him, as the Sema itself writes above in siman 104, seif katan 15, and siman 113, seif katan 14. Rather, it is clear that the Tur's words, 'or before he borrowed from the latter,' concern a case where an after acquired property clause was written to both the first and the second, and an agav clause was likewise written to both. Perhaps the Sema was reluctant to explain it this way because then the first clause, where the debtor lent only after borrowing from both, should likewise have been explained as a case where such clauses were written, as the Bach whom I cited immediately above understood. For that reason the Bach was also pressed here and wrote that it is difficult, because when the agav clause was written to the first lender, the document was not yet in his possession, so agav could not be effective. He therefore explains that the debts owed to the borrower arose before he borrowed from the latter, and that after those debts arose, but before borrowing from the latter, he wrote agav to the first, who thereby acquired them. I have already written immediately above that this is not correct. Rather, in the first clause as well, the law applies even if he wrote both agav and an after acquired property clause to both creditors. This appears clear to me. Examine it carefully."]
["To the first, agav, etc. This is where the final debtor has movables. But if the creditors come to collect land from the debtor of their debtor, the first lender of the original borrower precedes the second lender even if no agav clause was written to the first. Since only land is available, the first has priority and we extract it by virtue of Rabbi Natan's lien. This is evident from Baal HaTerumot at the end of gate 43. However, Responsa Rashba, siman 904, cited by the Beit Yosef above in siman 104, seif 4, appears explicitly to disagree. Nevertheless, the ruling of Baal HaTerumot seems primary. The reason stated by the Rashba, that we rule like Rava in Kol Sha'ah that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward, does not appear correct to me. The Gemara there in Kol Sha'ah, Pesachim 31a, says that with respect to Rabbi Natan's lien the principle that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection does not apply. Rather, it is as though the second debtor had borrowed directly from the first creditor. Otherwise, why would a creditor be able to collect land from heirs who had themselves collected land on debts owed to their father? See there carefully. The same is evident from the Ran in Shnei Dayanei Gezerot, from the Ramban, and from the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 85, seif 6, in accordance with Baal HaTerumot at the end of gate 43. It is not reasonable to distinguish by saying that a purchaser is weaker than a lender. Moreover, it is explicit there that the creditor collects the entire parcel from the purchaser. If that distinction were correct, the purchaser should at least acquire half of the land by virtue of being a creditor through the warranty on the purchase money, since warranty is an assumed condition even in the purchase of a debt document, as above in siman 66, seif 34. Examine this carefully."]
["Agav, etc. It appears that if the borrower's creditors encumbered movables to the borrower together with land, those movables acquire the status of land, and the borrower's first creditor has priority as with land, even if the first creditor was not given an agav clause. Although the debt document itself did not become like land, once the movables were made legally equivalent to land the first creditor has priority through Rabbi Natan's lien. This is also implied by the wording of siman 85, seif 6; see what I wrote there in seif katan 23. It is not reasonable to distinguish by saying that a purchaser is weaker than a lender. Moreover, it is implied there that the creditor collects all of the movables that had been encumbered together with land. If this were not so, the purchaser should at least acquire half of those movables, as I wrote immediately above. Examine this carefully."]
