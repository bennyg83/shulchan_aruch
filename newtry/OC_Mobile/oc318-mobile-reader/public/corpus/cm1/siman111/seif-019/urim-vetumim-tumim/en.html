The Taz asks that above, siman 42, seif 9, where one transfers an obligation to give something after Pesach without specifying which Pesach, he must give it after the first Pesach. The Taz answers that there the reason is expressly that otherwise there would be no end or fixed limit and the document would become void. Here, however, the recipient must return the property for as long as the donor lives, and if the donor dies before it is returned, the condition can no longer be fulfilled. This does not accord with what is implied below in siman 241, seif 7 in the Rama, where the condition applies even to the recipient's children and they may fulfill it. The Derishah answers that here the property must be returned only so long as the donor is alive. That would be a sound answer if the condition had said "to me," implying to me and not to my heirs. But the authorities copied the condition without that limitation, and if so there would appear to be no end to the obligation, just as above in siman 42. In truth, however, there is no difficulty. First, there the document states that the obligor undertook to give him fifteen gold coins after Pesach. If we do not interpret this as the first Pesach, there is no end to the obligation and the entire document becomes meaningless, and the document surely was not written for nothing. Here, by contrast, the principal content of the document is the gift itself. The condition merely requires eventual return. The document therefore does not become void. Its essence, the gift, remains effective, and the more the condition is weakened, the stronger the gift becomes. What concern is there if the condition is ineffective? We find similarly that if one gives a gift to his only son subject to a condition that no creditor's claim shall take effect upon it, the invalid condition does not undo the gift itself. The Rosh's concern there is to avoid nullifying the document, but here the document remains effective even if the condition has little practical force. Indeed, even the condition here has effect, because the recipient may not diminish the land, damage it, or deliberately cause its loss, since he is required to return it as he received it. If he damages it, he violates the condition. Whenever a condition has some field of operation, even partially, it cannot be called entirely void. This may explain their position. In my view, however, there is not even the beginning of a difficulty. There, the language itself is uncertain. He said "after Pesach," and it is unknown which Pesach he intended. In such a case, in order to uphold the document, the holder of the document has the stronger hand. Here there is no ambiguity of language at all. He said simply, "on condition that you return it to me," and because he fixed no time, the plain meaning is that it may be returned whenever the recipient wishes. What difference does it make if that interpretation weakens the condition? Since the language itself is not doubtful, there is no basis to undo the gift. The Rosh said his rule only where the language itself is uncertain. Here there is no uncertainty in the language. An unqualified statement is as though he explicitly said, "Return it to me whenever you wish." The distinction is clear.
