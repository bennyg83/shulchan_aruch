If, etc. The exemption was stated only where the property was entirely devastated, not in a case of ordinary coercive loss, as the continuation explains, because such aggressors are commonly subject to reversal. This is taught in the Jerusalem Talmud, Nedarim 3:4 and Sheviit, final chapter, halakha 1. Although Bava Metzia 15a discusses tax collectors who seized the field, and Bava Kamma 116b says that if the loss resulted from the robber the seller is liable, the Jerusalem Talmud explains that this is a special penalty imposed upon a robber.
(Supplement) Unless, etc., because, etc. The Jerusalem Talmud, Sheviit, final chapter, halakha 1, states that one who mortgages a field and sells it does not effect a permanent sale. Rabbi Yose derives this from the distinction that an ox can readily be concealed, whereas land cannot. This explains why one who designates an ox as an apotiki and sells it effects a sale. Even if the field is sold to a violent person, Rabbi Yudan, father of Rabbi Matanya, states that violent persons are apt to fall. Thus the creditor continues to rely upon the field, notwithstanding its sale to a violent possessor. See citation 90. See citation 160.

(Supplement) Some say that the same applies if, etc. Rashba writes this in his novellae to Bava Kamma 8b, interpreting the Gemara’s case of orphans as minor orphans. Even when unencumbered property remains in their possession, the creditor may collect from purchasers. He rejects Rashi and Tosafot’s explanation that the document is returned to its owner so that the owner can transfer it to the orphans, because merely returning a deed does not convey the underlying property. One must instead say that the creditor withdraws from them in satisfaction of their father’s unpaid debt. We rule that orphans who collected land for their father’s debt are treated accordingly, as stated in Ketubot and Pesachim. The opposing view follows Rashi’s explanation.
