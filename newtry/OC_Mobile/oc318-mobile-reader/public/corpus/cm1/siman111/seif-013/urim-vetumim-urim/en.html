But if a violent person seized them, etc. The reason is that this differs from property destroyed by natural disaster. There the property is gone entirely. Here the property still exists, but a violent person presently sits upon it, and violent possessors may eventually fall from power. See Tumim. I wrote that this ruling is disputed and requires study in practical law, because the opposing view appears more correct. I also wrote that even according to this opinion, it applies only where the violent seizure occurred after the loan. If the property was already in the violent person's hands when the loan was made, all agree that the creditor may collect from encumbered property. See there.
If the borrower converted. See Tumim. According to Rashba, whose view the Mechaber cites regarding a violent robber, Rashba also writes that if the debtor sold the property to a violent non-Jew, the creditor cannot collect from encumbered property. One must explain that there the law of the non-Jewish court still permits the Jewish owner to recover the property, but the non-Jew is violent and refuses to comply. The situation therefore is not considered permanent destruction, because violent possessors may fall. Here, however, the debtor converted and under their law and legal system the creditor has no means of collection. All agree that this is equivalent to the property having been destroyed, and the creditor may therefore collect from encumbered property. See there.
Minor heirs. Since beit din does not presently proceed against minor heirs, this is treated as though the property were unavailable, and the creditor may collect from encumbered property. See Tumim. I clarified that most authorities disagree and that the halachah does not follow Rashba on this point.
Although he cannot seize them. This means that even if the debtor expressly encumbered movable property together with land, but the creditor cannot recover it from purchasers because of the market protection enactment, the purchaser still cannot delay payment by pointing to those movables. The creditor may say, 'I will pay you from my own property.' The same applies where the debtor sold property to a non-Jew.
