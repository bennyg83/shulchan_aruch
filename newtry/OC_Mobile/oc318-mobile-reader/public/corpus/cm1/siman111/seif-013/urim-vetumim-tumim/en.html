But if a violent person robbed them, etc. The Shach, seif katan 9, was already perplexed by this ruling, and the matter requires study. Apart from the fact that it is difficult to accept that when free property is geographically distant the creditor need not travel after it, yet when it is held by violent wrongdoers he must wait, the Ritva in Ketubot, in the discussion of free property that was ruined, writes explicitly that if extortionists, meaning violent men as explained in the Rashba's own responsum, took the property, the creditor collects from purchasers. He likewise writes that in a time of emergency such property is treated as though it was destroyed, and he testified that this was how he ruled before his teachers. In truth, it appears that the Rashba follows his own view that a person's property is like his guarantor, and we rule that one does not collect from a guarantor first. The Rashba himself writes similarly in his novellae to Gittin, in HaNizakin, regarding the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property where free property exists, explaining that it falls under the rule that one does not first collect from a guarantor. See there, where he writes that this case is actually more stringent because, in the end, the creditor's lien rests upon all of the debtor's property. The Rashba follows his own position, cited by the Beit Yosef below in siman 129, letter 11, that even if the borrower is violent, the guarantor is nevertheless not compelled to pay until the borrower is first sued in beit din. If so, the same applies here, since a person's property serves as guarantor. But according to the ruling of the Mechaber, and the Rama's determination below in siman 129, seif 10, following the Rambam, that one may collect from the guarantor when the borrower is violent, the same is true regarding property. When the free property is in the hands of a violent person, one may collect from purchasers, who are like guarantors. This follows all the more strongly, because, as stated, there is greater reason to exempt an ordinary guarantor than encumbered property, as the Rashba himself writes. It also appears that even the Rashba said his ruling only where, at the time of the loan, the free property was not yet in the hands of violent men. The lien therefore took effect upon that property, and only afterward was it seized by violent men, so the creditor's misfortune caused the loss. But if at the time of the loan the property was already in the hands of violent men, the creditor never extended the loan in reliance upon it, and from the outset his intention was directed toward the property held by purchasers. In such a case one cannot say that his field became impaired by subsequent events. This also resolves why the case should not be worse than free property located far away. In the distant-property case the situation already existed when the loan was made, and the creditor therefore relied upon encumbered property. Here, by contrast, the deterioration occurred afterward, and the creditor's own fortune caused it. One should not extend the Rashba's ruling further than its proof allows. His proof is only from the fact that the Gemara asks about property that became ruined and does not ask about property taken by robbers. Every such doubt in the Gemara concerns property that became ruined after the loan, not beforehand. What basis is there to infer more than the Rashba's own evidence establishes?
However, if the borrower converted, etc. In Responsa Rashba no. 1029 he writes that if the borrower sold the property to a violent gentile, the creditor still cannot seize encumbered property. The Gidulei Terumah writes that the same applies to the case mentioned here, based on the Rosh's responsum, where the borrower converted, for what difference is there whether the borrower himself converted or sold his property to a powerful gentile? In my view the cases are not comparable. The Rashba's case is one in which, even according to the laws and customs of the gentiles, the Jewish creditor has a valid priority right to collect from the property purchased by the gentile. The only obstacle is that the gentile is powerful and violent, and who can litigate against one stronger than himself? In that circumstance the Rashba treats the obstacle merely as a lion crouching over the property, for ultimately violent men fall, and therefore the creditor cannot yet seize encumbered property. But if under gentile law this Jewish creditor has no legal right of priority to seize property purchased by a gentile, there can be no greater destruction than that. He will never be able to collect from that property. What relevance is there to saying that violent men ultimately fall? Will they convert and submit themselves to Jewish law? In such a case everyone agrees that he may collect from encumbered property. This is what the Rosh means when he writes that the borrower converted, so that the creditor can no longer collect under their law. He states explicitly in the responsum that under their legal system the creditor has no right to collect. All authorities agree to this without dispute. The Shach's difficulty in seif katan 9 against the Rashba, that even if the gentile is violent, how does the expectation that violent men will fall help, since the gentile will not return the land gratuitously, is therefore no difficulty. The case is one in which their law itself permits the Jew to seize the property, and only the gentile's present power prevents enforcement.
Minor orphans, etc. I found the source of this ruling of the Rashba in Bava Kamma 8b. The Gemara says that the purchaser may say: If you remain silent, well and good, but if not, I will return the deed to its owner. The Gemara asks that, if so, the same should be said regarding damages, and answers that the case is one of orphans who are not presently liable to make payment. The Rashba explains in his novellae to Bava Kamma that "I will return the deed to its owner" means either that the sale will be completely rescinded, or that the purchaser will give the land back to the orphans in payment of his debt to their father. He was troubled by why adult orphans should not be liable to pay, since orphans who collected land in satisfaction of their father's debt are liable. He therefore explains that the Gemara is speaking of minor orphans. The purchaser would return the deed to them as payment, but since they are not presently liable and beit din does not administer the property of minor orphans for collection, the claimants then return against the purchasers, because this is equivalent to the free property having been destroyed. The Rashba writes: "It appears to me that from here we learn that if the borrower died and left free property in the hands of minor orphans, who are not presently subject to collection, the creditor may now collect from purchasers. The purchaser cannot say, 'I left you a place from which to collect,' because the case is like one in which the free property was destroyed." Thus this Gemara is the entire source of his ruling. Yet the other authorities do not agree with the Rashba's interpretation that the orphans there are minors. Tosafot, the Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, Rosh, Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch below in siman 119 do not explain the passage that way. See there. If so, the foundation falls together with what was built upon it, and there is no proof for this ruling. On what basis should such a new rule be created? Rabbenu Yerucham therefore correctly writes that many authorities disagree with the Rashba, and this ruling cannot stand once its proof has fallen away. Tosafot in Bava Batra 157b, s.v. "he collects," likewise labor over the statement that the principal is collected from encumbered property and the improvement from free property. If free property exists, they ask, how may the creditor seize encumbered property? The purchaser should be able to say, "I left you a place from which to collect." Tosafot do not answer that the free property is in the hands of minor orphans, so that the creditor first seizes from purchasers and, when the orphans grow up, the purchaser may collect his reimbursement from the orphans' free property. Rather, this indicates that Tosafot do not accept the Rashba's rule. One should not ask against the Rashba how collection from purchasers is possible while free property remains in the hands of minor orphans. Could the purchasers not say: If you remain silent, well and good, but if not, we will return the deed to the minor orphans and you will then have to wait, so that it should be treated as though the deed had already been returned? According to the Rashba's interpretation of the Gemara, the argument there does not mean that the deed is literally returned to the orphans in every case. Rather, it means that the purchaser transfers the property to them in payment of what he owes them. The baraita is therefore established where the purchaser still owes money to the orphans' father, as the Rashba states explicitly, speaking of minor orphans and purchasers who collected on their father's debt. Accordingly, not only where purchasers owe the orphans nothing can they not tell the seizing creditor, "If you remain silent," but even where they actually owe the orphans they still cannot do so. Their debt itself is subject to the creditor's lien under Rabbi Natan's rule, because they owe the orphans' father while the orphans' father owes the present creditor. They have no authority to pay the minor orphans contrary to Rabbi Natan's rule, as explained above in siman 86. At the Gemara's initial stage it could properly speak of returning the property to the orphans, because so long as the original debtor had other assets, Rabbi Natan's lien did not apply. But according to the conclusion that property held by minor orphans is treated as destroyed, Rabbi Natan's rule again applies, and the purchaser cannot transfer the property to the minors. This is consistent. The Rashba, who holds in a responsum, as cited by the Beit Yosef above in siman 110, that one does not collect from a guarantor while the debtor's property is in the hands of minor orphans, nevertheless rules here that one may collect from purchasers even though a person's property is like his guarantor. He must hold that the purchaser's position is weaker than that of a personal guarantor, because the creditor has a direct lien upon the property, as he writes in HaNizakin. According to the Mechaber's ruling above in siman 110, however, the logical relationship would have to be reversed. Examine this carefully.
