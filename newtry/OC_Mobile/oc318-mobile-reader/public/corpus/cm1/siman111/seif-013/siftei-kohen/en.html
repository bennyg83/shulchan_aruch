A violent robber seized it, etc. This ruling is taken from the Ran in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui and from the Maggid Mishneh, who cite a responsum of the Rashba. I examined the Rashba's responsum, siman 1039, and found that its conclusion is not compelling. This ruling therefore requires study. In particular, the Rashba writes that if the borrower sold the property to a violent gentile from whom it cannot be recovered, the creditor has no claim against the earlier purchaser. That purchaser may say, 'You were left property from which to collect,' because the property was legally available for collection even though it is now in the hands of a violent person, and men of force are liable eventually to fall. This is difficult. Since the borrower sold the property, the gentile certainly will not return it unless the borrower returns his money. How can the earlier purchaser say that property was left for collection? Can the fact that the gentile will not submit to Jewish law, which would require collection first from later purchasers, create the defense that available property was left? One must therefore explain the case as involving a small amount of money where the land is worth more, and the borrower stipulated with the gentile that the land would be returned upon repayment, but the violent gentile refuses to honor the condition. Examine this carefully.
