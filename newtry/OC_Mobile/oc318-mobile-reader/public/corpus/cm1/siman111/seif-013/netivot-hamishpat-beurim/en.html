A violent robber. Tumim rules that the stated law applies only where the property later fell into the hands of a violent robber. If, however, the land was already in the robber's hands at the time of the loan, and the loan was extended principally in reliance on the property that remained in the debtor's hands, the creditor may collect from the purchaser who bought that property. It appears to me, however, that if the debtor himself is violent and the creditor cannot collect directly from him, the creditor still cannot turn to purchasers. The purchasers may say that they left him a place from which to collect, provided the debtor retained a field comparable to the one they purchased. Had the purchaser not bought this field and it had remained with the seller, the creditor would still have been unable to collect it because of the debtor's violence. The purchaser therefore caused the creditor no loss by buying it. This is obvious, and it is also demonstrated in several places that one does not collect from encumbered property when unencumbered property exists, even where the debtor himself is violent. A guarantor is different. If the debtor is violent, the guarantor must pay. This is also unlike the case of one who converted his religion cited in the Rama. There, as appears from the responsum of the Rosh, the property in the debtor's hands consisted of assets acquired under the laws of the non-Jews, under which the creditor could not collect those assets, whereas had this particular field remained with the debtor the creditor could have collected it. In that situation it is indeed comparable to the available property having been destroyed. A guarantor likewise undertakes precisely on the understanding that if the debtor does not pay, he will pay. This does not apply to purchasers.

And there are those who disagree. See Shach seif katan 14, who is surprised because the Gemara clearly states that a creditor may collect from property inherited by heirs even according to the view that after-acquired property is not subject to a lien, because of the mitzvah upon heirs to pay their father's debt. See Tumim, who answers that if after-acquired property is not subject to a lien it resembles movable property, as explained by the poskim. With movable property, even though it is not subject to a lien because the creditor did not rely on it, if the debtor expressly wrote in the document that both land and movable property were bound, then since we see that the creditor did rely on those assets, he may collect from the heirs by law because of the mitzvah to pay the father's debt, and beit din compels them. See Tumim siman 107. The same applies to after-acquired property. Even if it is not technically bound, where the debtor expressly wrote an after-acquired property clause, the heirs are nevertheless under a mitzvah and may be compelled to pay from it because the creditor relied on it. Without such an express clause, even that mitzvah obligation does not apply to that property. Regarding a deathbed gift, Tumim rules that the creditor does not collect from the recipient. The Gemara says that he collects from heirs only because of the mitzvah to pay their father's debt, and that mitzvah does not apply to the recipient of a deathbed gift.
