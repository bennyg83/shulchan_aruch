The Shach explains that if the second creditor's debtor holds only land, the first creditor need not have a lien on movables together with land in order to collect through Rabbi Natan's rule. Likewise, if the second debtor himself subjected movables together with land, the first creditor can sometimes reach those movables even without such a clause in his own document, because the second creditor's right is treated as already held and passes through Rabbi Natan. This follows from Pesachim. Land already subject to the intermediate creditor is treated as though in his hand, because if the debtor sells or mortgages it, the creditor can still recover it. That strong right becomes subject to the first creditor through Rabbi Natan. Ordinary movables are different because the debtor can sell them free of the lien. In our times, after the market overt enactment, even a clause subjecting movables together with land does not ordinarily let a creditor follow sold movables. Therefore such movables are no longer treated as though already in the intermediate creditor's hand. The Shach's special rule based on the second creditor's movable lien consequently has little application today. By contrast, if the first creditor himself has a clause subjecting debt instruments together with land, his right in the intermediate debt instrument is direct. Debt instruments can be subjected incidentally with land. He can therefore reach the later collection even if the second creditor lacked a comparable movable lien. If the final debtor pays land, the first creditor certainly can recover it through Rabbi Natan, just as heirs who collect land on their father's debt remain subject to the father's creditor. If the final debtor has cash, he ordinarily must pay cash. The later creditor cannot manipulate the form of payment merely to create a stronger Rabbi Natan position where no loss would otherwise occur. If he has no cash and pays land, the first creditor can then collect that land.
Sema's words imply that if the debt instruments were already in the borrower's hands before each loan was made, a clause subjecting them together with land would not require an after acquired property clause. This appears inconsistent with Sema's own ruling in siman 85, where even an earlier sale of a debt instrument can require an after acquired clause. The point therefore remains difficult.
