Movable property together with land. See Shach seif katan 18 through the words, "they should have explained." There is a textual error there, and it should read: "All the more so, if the purchaser already collected land on the debt instrument, then the seller's creditor..." The word "or" is erroneous and should read "then." See Ketzot HaChoshen, who writes that the principal basis of Tosafot in Pesachim 31 for distinguishing land from movable property is that movable property is not ordinarily subject to liens, since if the debtor sells it a creditor cannot collect it from the purchaser. Accordingly, nowadays, when we have the enactment protecting ordinary commerce, under which even where movable property was expressly pledged together with land the creditor does not collect it from a purchaser, the lien of R. Natan should likewise not apply. In my view, however, according to what I wrote in siman 88, even where heirs collected land for their father's debt arising from an oral loan, their father's creditor may collect that land from them. This is so even though, had they sold it, the creditor could not have collected it from the purchaser because the original debt was oral. Nevertheless, the lien of R. Natan applies because by strict law land is subject to a lien, since liens are d'oraisa. All the more so regarding movable property expressly pledged together with land, where the inability to collect after a sale results only from the enactment protecting commerce and not from the law of the Gemara, the lien of R. Natan certainly applies.
Together with land. See Sema seif katan 34. There is a textual error there, and it should read: "For this matter we require that Yehudah pledged..." The word rendered "concerning" should read "we require." The Sema writes that the case concerns debt instruments already in his possession, and therefore no after-acquired property clause is needed. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this from siman 85 seif katan 22, where the Sema writes that even where the earlier creditor sold a debt instrument, an after-acquired property clause is required. There is no difficulty. The Sema's reasoning there is that through acquisition together with land one transfers only movable property that is presently his, not movable property that is not his but is merely subject to his lien. Accordingly, when the purchaser has already collected movable property from the debtor through the debt instrument, this is as though the debtor delivered or sold and gave those movables to him. Since movable property that was merely subject to the seller's lien was not itself transferred together with land, the earlier creditor cannot seize the movables after the purchaser has already collected them. Here, however, the case is where the purchaser has not yet collected on the debt instrument and the earlier creditor seeks to seize the debt instrument itself from the purchaser. He certainly can do so, because the instrument itself belonged to the seller, and movable property actually owned by him can unquestionably be pledged. Since the instrument itself was pledged together with land, the creditor may seize it from the purchaser. By contrast, once the purchaser has already collected movable property through that instrument, those movables cannot be removed from him, because movables that were merely subject to the debt were not included in the pledge together with land. Nor can the creditor sue the purchaser on the ground that he returned to the debtor the instrument that had been pledged to the creditor, thereby enabling the collection. Even according to the opinion in siman 107 seif 4 that heirs who sell land may be considered to have damaged the creditor's lien, that case is different because no other source of collection remains, since they are heirs and can pay only from that land. Their sale therefore damages the lien. Here, when the purchaser surrendered the instrument in order to collect it, the creditor could still have collected elsewhere. Certainly, a purchaser who resells a field before a creditor comes to collect is not personally liable to that creditor. Even a later creditor who collected land and then sold it is not personally liable to an earlier creditor, as explained in siman 104 seif 1. Only where the later creditor sells land encumbered to the earlier creditor to a violent person is he considered to have damaged the earlier lien, for the reason given by Sema in siman 107 seif katan 15: at the time of sale he sold what was not fully his, because it remained encumbered to the earlier creditor. By contrast, an ordinary purchaser from the debtor who resells land is not considered to have damaged the creditor's lien unless the field was an apoteiki or it had already become established that the debtor had no other property. So too here, a purchaser of a debt instrument who collects on it before it becomes established that the debtor has no other property is not liable as one who damaged the lien.
