If he acquired property and bequeathed it, the creditor collects, and some disagree. The Shach writes in seif katan 14 that where he acquired property and then bequeathed it, the creditor collects, and that this is demonstrated by the Gemara in Bava Batra 157b; he therefore regards this as the primary ruling and expresses surprise that the Mechaber cites the dissenting opinion. Yet one should rather be surprised at the Shach for dismissing that opinion so completely without noting that our great teacher the Ramban holds precisely like the dissenters. The Rivash, responsum 392, cites the Ramban as ruling that even from land inherited by the debtor's orphans a creditor does not collect where the debtor did not write a clause encumbering after-acquired property. At first glance, Tosafot also seem to follow the Ramban and hold that property acquired and then bequeathed is not encumbered. Tosafot ask why the matter cannot be resolved from Rabbi Natan's rule concerning orphans who collected land in satisfaction of their father's debt. This is difficult, for perhaps that case is collection from the debtor himself. It is strained to say that Tosafot's question is only upon the proof from the case of one who is prudent and can seize the property, because that case too concerns collection from the debtor himself; this is not the implication of Maharsha and his textual reading. Moreover, Rabbi Yochanan certainly knew that collection from the debtor himself is effective, and he merely understood the phrase "can seize it" to imply an enforceable lien, for otherwise what is gained by seizure if the debtor can sell the property? Also, why abandon the Mishnah and resolve the issue from Rabbi Natan? Rather, their question is evidently directed to the Gemara's difficulty from the Mishnah concerning a house that fell, which the Gemara establishes according to Rabbi Meir, while Rav Nachman does not hold like Rabbi Meir. This implies that Tosafot agree with the Ramban. Yet independently one must understand Tosafot's formulation, for what was their initial assumption? Surely they knew the Gemara's answer that the collection is by virtue of Rabbi Natan. Why do they write "furthermore" as though offering a second answer? Rather, Tosafot hold as I wrote above in siman 86 in the name of Hagahot HaRosh and Tosafot Yeshanim in Ketubot: if Reuven lent to Shimon in Nisan and Shimon lent to Levi in Iyar, Levi is not encumbered to Reuven under Rabbi Natan, because Shimon's loan to Levi came into existence only after Reuven's loan. I concluded there, however, from the difficulty concerning antedated or postdated documents, that where the first creditor has an explicit clause covering after-acquired property, Hagahot HaRosh agrees that Rabbi Natan applies. Tosafot's question is therefore that Rav Nachman states without qualification that orphans who collected land in payment of their father's debt are liable, even if the debt they collected arose only after their father's own debt. In that case Rabbi Natan would not apply unless the father's creditor had a clause covering after-acquired property. The passage must therefore concern a debt with such a clause, which would prove that after-acquired property becomes encumbered. Tosafot first answer that the Gemara chose a stronger proof; their second answer invokes Rabbi Natan and rejects Hagahot HaRosh, holding that Rabbi Natan applies in all such cases. The two answers therefore differ over a major rule, and Tosafot's language is exact. See what I wrote at the end of simanim 85 and 86. The Tur likewise did not seek to marshal force against the Ramban and decide contrary to him. In siman 60 he writes that if the debtor wrote a clause covering after-acquired property, the creditor collects from heirs and purchasers, which is a proposition accepted by all opinions. In siman 112 he reproduces the Baal HaTerumot's phrase, "even according to the opinion that acquired and bequeathed property becomes encumbered." He therefore did not decide against the Ramban. To the contrary, his phrasing almost treats the Ramban's view as primary, since "even according to the opinion that it becomes encumbered" implies that the principal view is that it does not. The matter is therefore one of balanced doubt. The Shach's proof from the Gemara is as follows. The Gemara asks whether, according to the Sages who hold that a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence, an explicit after-acquired-property clause nevertheless takes effect when the debtor later acquires property and then bequeaths or sells it. It seeks to resolve this from the Mishnah concerning a house that fell upon a father and son. The Mishnah implies that only if the son died first can the son's creditors not collect, but if the father died first the son's creditors take the property, even though it was after-acquired property. The Gemara rejects this proof by saying that there is a mitzvah upon orphans to pay their father's debt. It then asks that Rav and Shmuel hold that an oral loan is not collected from heirs, and answers that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. Thus, were it not for Rav and Shmuel's rule, the Gemara would have said that heirs must pay even where the after-acquired property itself was not legally encumbered. The Shach means that, since we do not rule like Rav and Shmuel and an oral loan is collected from heirs, the same should apply where no after-acquired-property clause was written.
In truth, however, this proves nothing. The Gemara itself says, "You speak of collection from the debtor himself? Even the cloak upon my shoulders is available." The Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, beginning of part 4, explains that just as movable property is not subject to a lien and yet may be collected from the debtor himself, so too after-acquired property that was not encumbered may be collected from the debtor himself. Thus the Gemara understands that if an after-acquired-property clause is ineffective, the later property is not legally encumbered at all, even while free in the debtor's possession. It is compared directly to movables. We do not say that once the property comes into existence the lien attaches automatically. See at length what I wrote above in siman 104. If so, why does the Gemara challenge Rav Nachman's answer from Rav and Shmuel's rule? Even those who disagree with Rav and Shmuel and hold that an oral loan is collected from heirs do so because they maintain that a lien is d'oraisa. Here, however, the after-acquired property is not subject to any lien and is equivalent to movables. The answer must be that Rav Nachman, who says that the reason is the mitzvah upon orphans to repay their father's debt, follows his own view that a lien is not d'oraisa, as Tosafot write in Gittin 50. According to him, an oral loan is collected from heirs because of closing the door before borrowers, and the same rabbinic policy can operate here despite the absence of a Torah lien. Why, then, does Rav Nachman phrase the matter as a mitzvah to repay? Rav Pappa at the end of Bava Batra gives the reason as closing the door, not as the mitzvah. The explanation is that these are one concept. Were there no mitzvah, the Sages would not confiscate the orphans' money merely to prevent closing the door; and because of the mitzvah alone they would not compel payment. Since there is a mitzvah, they enacted coercive collection because of closing the door. This is demonstrated by Rav Pappa's rule that collection is not made from minor orphans because payment of a creditor is a mitzvah and minors are not subject to mitzvot. Yet Rav Pappa also says that collection from heirs was instituted because of closing the door. Necessarily, as stated, the two reasons operate together. Ramban writes explicitly at the end of Bava Batra that because Rav Pappa makes payment depend upon a mitzvah, this proves that he holds the lien is not d'oraisa, and therefore invokes closing the door. Thus the two concepts are linked. Accordingly, Rav Nachman's answer, "There is a mitzvah upon the orphans," necessarily assumes that the later property is not legally encumbered, and collection is only by virtue of the mitzvah together with closing the door. First, all of this applies only according to the opinion that a lien is not d'oraisa and heirs pay by rabbinic enactment. According to the halachah, however, we rule that a lien is d'oraisa. We therefore do not need a separate rabbinic institution based on closing the door and the mitzvah in order to collect from heirs; the strict law itself places a Torah lien on the inherited property. Ramban writes this explicitly at the end of Bava Batra. If, then, no after-acquired-property clause was written and the later property never became encumbered, what basis is there to collect from the heirs? Rav Nachman's statement is no proof, because he holds that a lien is not d'oraisa and collection was instituted because of closing the door, whereas we do not rule like him on that premise. Second, even according to Rav Nachman's framework, closing the door applies where an after-acquired-property clause was in fact written. Although the Gemara's initial assumption was that such a clause may not create a valid lien, the creditor nevertheless relied upon it, since it was written. But where no such clause was written, the creditor could have had it included and failed to do so. His reliance was plainly not upon future property. What closing of the door is there? He caused his own loss by failing to write the clause and did not rely upon the later acquisitions. This also explains why, if the after-acquired property is not encumbered and yet Rav Nachman allows collection from heirs because of the mitzvah, the same rule is not applied to the orphans' movables. In truth, where the debtor wrote "movables and land," even without an agav clause, most authorities hold that collection may be made from the heirs, as I wrote above in siman 107. Although the movables were not legally encumbered because no agav was created, the creditor relied upon the express writing. By contrast, where nothing was written, there was no reliance. The same distinction applies to an after-acquired-property clause. There is therefore a major difference between a clause that was written but, under the Gemara's initial assumption, ineffective, and a clause never written at all, even though under our conclusion such clauses can be effective. This is the core of Ramban's reasoning. Since we rule that a lien is d'oraisa, collection from heirs is limited to what actually became encumbered. Property acquired later without an express clause never became encumbered at all. Ramban's view also has support from the Gemara's treatment of the Mishnah of the fallen house. When the Gemara asks against Shmuel from the implication that the son's creditors collect if the father died first, although Shmuel holds that an after-acquired-property clause is ineffective, it responds that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir. Why does it not instead answer that the Mishnah follows the opinion in Bekhorot that a lien is d'oraisa, according to which an oral loan is collected from heirs? To the contrary, according to that opinion the difficulty is greater, because where the later property itself never became encumbered there is no closing-the-door enactment to supply what is missing. Likewise, in Bekhorot the Gemara asks against the ruling that a woman does not collect her ketubah from property that is merely "prospective": Shmuel says that a creditor does collect improvement. Why not answer that the case is one in which there was no after-acquired-property clause, so that even property that came to the debtor during his lifetime and later passed to heirs would not be collectible? The Mishnah could follow the opinion that a lien is d'oraisa. Moreover, if our ruling that an oral loan is collectible from heirs by itself established collection of after-acquired property, the Gemara would not need the special leniency of ketubah at all, and the entire discussion would collapse. Necessarily, inherited after-acquired property is never collected where no clause was written. The Mishnah there must concern a written after-acquired-property clause. Ramban's position is therefore strongly supported. There is, however, an apparent difficulty against Ramban from his own words, cited by the Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, part 4, ruling 15. Ramban brings proof that a creditor does not take property that is merely prospective from a Tosefta and Yerushalmi which interpret "his vineyard" as excluding prospective property: if one dug a pit, caused damage, died, and only afterward an inheritance fell to his heirs, the sons do not pay from that inheritance. But why specifically speak of an inheritance that came only after his death? A tort claimant ordinarily has no written after-acquired-property clause. According to Ramban, even if the inheritance fell to the tortfeasor during his lifetime and he then died, the claimant should not collect it from the heirs. The Mishneh LaMelech already raised this question and left it unanswered.
What appears in resolution is one of two approaches. First, according to the opinion that a creditor collects from heirs, the basis is that, just as with an oral loan the Torah lien attached at the time of the loan to all the borrower's existing assets because a person's property is his guarantor, so too all property that the debtor would later acquire was encompassed in the original lien. This can be true only if a lien can attach to something not yet in existence, namely according to Rabbi Meir. According to the Sages, who hold that a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence, he likewise cannot create a lien upon it. The reason we nevertheless rule that an express after-acquired-property clause is effective is, as Rashbam writes, because of closing the door, not because of strict Torah law. Thus, for an oral loan and the Torah law alone, where closing the door through an express clause is not relevant, property acquired after the loan is not encumbered because the lien could not attach to it at the time of the loan. Accordingly, the baraita and Yerushalmi may follow Rabbi Meir and those who agree with him that a person can transfer something not yet in existence. Since a lien is d'oraisa, even later-acquired property becomes encumbered and is collectible from heirs, unless excluded by the verse concerning prospective property. The Gemara frequently establishes Mishnayot and baraitot according to Rabbi Meir. According to the Sages, however, one cannot transfer or encumber something not yet in existence. Where the loan document contains no after-acquired-property clause, there is no closing-the-door concern, because the creditor chose not to obtain such a clause and thereby caused his own loss. He therefore cannot collect the later property from the heirs. One should not ask what the Sages do with the verse "his vineyard" that excludes prospective property, because the same question exists according to those who hold that a lien is not d'oraisa and that an heir is not liable for his father's damages even where assets were left. They must apply the verse elsewhere, and the Sages can do the same. This also explains the Mishnah in Bekhorot that daughters who receive support do not take from improvement, which the Gemara attributes to the leniencies of ketubah because support conditions have the law of a ketubah. These obligations are not ordinary written debts and contain no express after-acquired-property clause. How, then, could the daughters claim improvement from heirs in the first place? One might have answered that the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir, as many passages there do. Alternatively, because the obligation of support arises only after death, the heirs themselves are treated as the debtors, making collection effectively from the debtor himself. The Rosh, in the name of Rabbenu Yonah, uses a similar concept regarding improvement created by heirs. Or, since the obligation is a condition imposed by beit din, and the Gemara in HaNizakin says that such obligations are deemed written as against free property though not against purchasers, the Sages may have instituted that as against free property they be treated as written in every legally effective manner, as though an after-acquired-property clause had been included. Thus the daughters would have collected from the heirs but for the special leniency of ketubah concerning improvement. We have therefore clarified that Ramban has responses to the arguments against him and that his view is highly plausible. The matter remains a genuine doubt. Nevertheless, who can come after the determination of the Mechaber and Rama below at the end of siman 112, who rule that collection may be made from heirs? The Bach, however, holds that even the first opinion agrees that one does not collect from a deathbed gift. It is true that the Baal HaTerumot writes that according to the first opinion the creditor collects even from a deathbed gift. Still, the Bach may properly distinguish this case, and he has support from the Gemara. The Gemara says, "You speak of collection from the debtor himself? Even the cloak upon my shoulders," when discussing the question whether acquired property that was sold or bequeathed becomes encumbered. Rav Nachman answers the proof from the fallen-house Mishnah by invoking the mitzvah upon orphans to pay their father's debt, and the Gemara then asks that Rav and Shmuel hold that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. Why does it need to bring a difficulty from elsewhere? Shmuel himself is the one who is uncertain whether inherited after-acquired property is encumbered. If he held that the mitzvah to repay itself suffices, what doubt could he have? It is strained to answer that the mitzvah applies only to sons inheriting a father because of honoring one's father, whereas another heir has no such mitzvah. First, no such distinction is found. Second, the Mishnah itself says that the house fell upon "his father or another person from whom he inherits," showing that other heirs are included, and still Rav Nachman invokes the mitzvah. In any event, the entire force of the opinion permitting collection from heirs is Rav Nachman's statement concerning the mitzvah. A deathbed gift does not involve a mitzvah of heirs to pay their father's debt from inherited property, and the creditor also lacks the advantage of collection from the debtor himself. As I wrote above in seif katan 4, the Gemara itself supplies support that without an after-acquired-property clause one does not collect from a deathbed gift. The Bach's ruling is therefore sound and may be relied upon to avoid extracting property from one in possession. One may still argue that Rav Nachman's answer based on the mitzvah follows the view that a lien is not d'oraisa, and only under that view is there a distinction between automatic inheritance and a deathbed gift. According to the view that a lien is d'oraisa, perhaps no such distinction exists. The matter requires study.
