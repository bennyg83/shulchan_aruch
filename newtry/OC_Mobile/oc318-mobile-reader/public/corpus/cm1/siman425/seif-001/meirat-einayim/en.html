All those liable to beit din's death penalties nowadays are not within our power to flog, etc. The Tur likewise writes this in the name of Rav Natronai Gaon. Although he speaks of those liable to death and then says we cannot flog or beat them, in Perishah I explained that the meaning is: not only can we not administer the four death penalties themselves as commanded by the Torah, but we also lack authority as a matter of ordinary law to substitute another punishment. We may act only as an emergency measure to prevent breaches of law. See there.
For the purpose of safeguarding the matter, etc. Meaning, we do not impose such punishment merely to exact justice for one who was already murdered or injured. But if there is concern that others will do the same in the future, authorities may impose measures to prevent recurrence. See Perishah.
And they warned him, etc. Meaning, a formal warning and explicit acceptance of the warning are unnecessary. It is enough to say to him: the person you are pursuing is a Jew, and the Torah says, "Whoever sheds man's blood, by man shall his blood be shed." After the fact, even if they did not say this to him, the pursued person may still be rescued at the pursuer's life. Proof comes from a minor pursuer, who lacks the capacity to comprehend such a warning, yet the Gemara says he too may be killed in order to save the victim. See Perishah.
All Israel are commanded to save him. This is learned from the verse in Parashat Ki Teitzei concerning the betrothed girl: "But to the girl you shall do nothing... she cried in the field and there was no one to save her." This implies that if someone is available to save her from the person seeking to violate her, he is commanded to rescue her by whatever means are possible. From her case we learn the law of a pursuer who seeks to kill another, because immediately after that verse Scripture says, "For just as a man rises against his fellow," etc. Chazal explain that this passage both teaches and is itself taught: the law of a murderer is learned from the betrothed girl, for just as she may be saved at the life of the one pursuing her, so too a person pursued by one seeking to kill him may be saved at the pursuer's life. Conversely, aspects of the girl's law are learned from murder. This is not the place to elaborate.
Even though he has not yet killed. This does not mean that all the more so, if he already killed, we now turn and kill the murderer, for nowadays we do not administer beit din's death penalties. Rather, the Mechaber adopts Rambam's wording. Rambam had just stated that an ordinary murderer is not executed until judgment is completed after the killing. He then explains that a pursuer is different: he may be killed even before he has killed anyone and without any prior adjudication.
One who comes through a tunnel, etc. This law is learned from Parashat Mishpatim: "If the thief is found in a tunnel and is struck and dies, there is no blood for him. If the sun shines upon him, there is blood for him." The reason he has "no blood" is that every thief knows a person will not simply stand by silently while his property is stolen. The thief therefore enters with the understanding that if the owner resists, he will kill him. He is consequently treated as a pursuer, and the owner may save himself at the thief's life. The plain meaning of "if the sun shines upon him" would be that if the thief is found breaking in during daytime, we assume he did not come prepared to kill, because he knows many people are available to help the owner; if the owner confronts him, he will flee. But if that were the entire point, Scripture could simply have said, "if the sun shines." Why add "upon him"? Chazal therefore expound that it teaches a case where it is as clear as sunlight that the thief did not come to kill the householder, such as where he is known as a faithful friend, or where a father tunnels into his son's house. In the latter case, even without specific proof, we presume that a father does not intend to kill his son but only to steal. Such a father or faithful friend retains "blood," meaning it is forbidden to kill him when he comes through the tunnel. By contrast, when a son tunnels into his father's house, we do not presume that he came without willingness to kill. So it is in the Gemara. See the Tur for further disputes and opinions.
One who engages in counterfeiting. It is written there that this includes, for example, those who threaten arson or who clip coins. The wording "the governments object to it" is deliberate. If the authorities do not object, he does not have the law of a pursuer. Nimukei Maharam of Rothenburg states this explicitly.
