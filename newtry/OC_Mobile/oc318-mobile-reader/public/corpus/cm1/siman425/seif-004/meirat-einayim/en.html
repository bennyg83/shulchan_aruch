So that he will not kill me. Meaning, before you succeed in stopping him he may kill me when he sees that you are trying to kill him because of me.
We do not listen to her. This implies specifically where we can say that she objects to the violation but fears for her life lest he kill her. We do not listen to her, because the Torah is concerned that such degradation not be inflicted. But if she says, "Leave him alone and do not kill him," in a manner indicating that she does not care about this degradation, then we do listen to her. This also appears from the wording of the baraita and Rambam, which specifically say, "lest he kill me," as the Mechaber writes. The Tur, however, writes that even in such a case we do not listen to her. Beit Yosef explains that in the Tur's view, even then it is possible she cares about the degradation but merely does not want the pursuer to be killed because of her.
Zealots may strike him. The Tur writes: one who is zealous for God's honor may kill him. This is learned from the episode of Pinchas and Zimri and is a halacha transmitted to Moshe at Sinai.
We do not instruct him to do so. The Tur concludes that even if the offender did not withdraw, and he killed the zealot, he is not executed for doing so. This is a statement in the Gemara. It is surprising that Rama omitted it. Perhaps it is because the Tur then continues, "if another person killed the zealot, he is executed," regarding which Beit Yosef writes that it is so obvious it need not have been recorded. Since Rama omitted that second law because of its obviousness, he may have omitted the first along with it because the Tur wrote them in one clause. More likely, Rama omitted it because the Mechaber here records only forms of killing that relate to safeguarding public order. In this case, the offender can withdraw, and if he instead kills the zealot there is no issue of preventive public discipline. The Tur included it only incidentally as an additional novelty.
