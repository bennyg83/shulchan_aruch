An Israelite apikores, and they are, etc. The Mechaber followed the text of Rambam as printed with Kesef Mishneh at the end of chapter 4 of Hilchot Rotze'ach U'Shemirat HaNefesh. But that printing does not continue exactly as the Mechaber writes, "and the apikorsim are those who deny the Torah," because that would be imprecise after already defining an apikores earlier. Rather, the printed text there says, "the apikorsim are those who worship idolatry," etc., through "one who violates shatnez defiantly is an apikores, and those who deny the Torah," etc. Yet this version too is difficult, because it is unclear to what "and those who deny the Torah" refers. The more precise text is therefore the large-format printing of Rambam, which reads, "the minim among Israel are idolaters," etc., through "he is an apikores, and these are those who deny," etc. I found the same in a very old printing of Rambam, and this is also the Tur's wording.
To remove them. The Ir Shushan writes that the reason it is a mitzvah initially to remove them is by analogy to an idolatrous city. This does not appear correct. Only where an entire city, or its majority, becomes an idolatrous city is the prescribed punishment by sword. Individual idolaters are subject to stoning, except that nowadays we do not administer the four Torah death penalties. Therefore, when the source says "remove them," it does not mean the exact Torah punishment. Rather, it is as though it said: if we have power to remove them, it is a mitzvah to remove them publicly so that those who remain will hear and fear; if not, one should at least bring about their downfall indirectly, as the source concludes.
He first removes it. This is also Rambam's wording. It would seem proper to delete the conjunction before "removes it," so that the meaning is: if a ladder was already in the pit before the person came, he removes it and says, "I am occupied," etc. According to the text before us, the word "first" must mean that he acts in advance and removes it.
Where there is no war between them and us. If there is actual war, the maxim concerning killing the best among the non-Jews applies, meaning one may bring about their death. Tosafos write likewise. See Sefer Shevet Yehudah, which records a response to sectarians on this matter, explaining that the praiseworthy quality meant there is that they impose death upon wrongdoers and do not search for merit on behalf of evildoers. See Yoreh Deah siman 158 concerning these laws.
In a place where the fields belong to Jews. This does not mean specifically Eretz Yisrael, but any place where Jews own fields. This was likewise stated above in siman 34, seif 13. Although the Mechaber writes in siman 409 that it is permitted to raise small livestock in Syria and in deserts, which might imply permission even where Jews living there own fields, otherwise the same would be true even in Eretz Yisrael, that law concerns the decree against raising small livestock lest they come to graze in Jewish fields. Chazal imposed that broad decree only in Eretz Yisrael because of the special concern for loss to the land. It is nevertheless certainly prohibited to pasture them directly in Jewish fields even outside Eretz Yisrael. With small livestock the Sages were stricter concerning shepherds, because even if one does not personally watch them enter Jewish fields, they readily jump and wander there on their own and he should prevent them. Although above in siman 34, seif 13, Tur and Mechaber write that shepherds of large livestock are also disqualified, they explain there that this applies specifically where the animals are their own, because we suspect they will allow them to graze in Jewish fields for their own benefit. Here the case includes even one who shepherds other people's animals. Alternatively, with respect to the rule that one need not save such persons, the Sages said this only about shepherds of small livestock. See what I wrote further in siman 34.
For example, one who eats neveilot out of appetite. Meaning, when kosher meat is unavailable he eats neveilah in order to satisfy his appetite. One who acts defiantly is where kosher meat is available and he nevertheless chooses neveilah in order to rebel against the command of his Creator, may He be blessed.
It is a mitzvah to save him. Beit Yosef distinguishes between an apostate who eats neveilot out of appetite and a shepherd of small livestock. Although the shepherd too acts for appetite and personal benefit by allowing the animals to graze in Jewish fields, the shepherd transgresses every day and throughout the day, unlike one who eats neveilot out of appetite. In Perishah I added another distinction: one who eats neveilot out of appetite sins only against God, whereas the shepherd also steals and sins against his fellow Jew.
