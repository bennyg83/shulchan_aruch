One who hires false witnesses. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 145, concerning one who sued another to return ten gold coins that he had paid him to give false testimony, after which the other did not testify and now denies the entire matter. Shevut Yaakov asks whether the claimant may require an oath. At first glance, he writes, even if the other admits receiving the money he should not be obligated to return it, because the payer should be penalized for hiring false witnesses, similar to the case in Rosh Hashana 22b. Wherever the Sages penalize the transgressor, the money is left with the opposing party, and we do not say that it should be distributed to the poor, as in the case of a document containing interest. The apparent reason is based on Bava Kamma 38b. In truth, however, this is not so. In the case in Rosh Hashana, where the Sages told the witness that the two hundred zuz were given to him as a gift, the essential reason was to make a protective enactment for the matter. Here there is no such need. If this person has behaved in this way, it does not follow that all Jews are accustomed to hiring false witnesses. He also did not commit the act publicly and is not established as one who acts this way. See what I wrote above, siman 2, seif katan 1. Since there is no need for a protective enactment, we have no authority to impose a penalty merely because he committed a transgression. We may not compare one rabbinic penalty to another unless we find that the Sages expressly imposed a penalty in a comparable case, such as a document containing interest and the like. Indeed, we find in Bava Kamma and in Choshen Mishpat siman 32 that one who hires false witnesses is exempt under human law. Therefore, in this case the witness must return the money, and if he denies everything he must take a hesset oath. This is the substance of his ruling; see there. Ateret Tzvi and Ma'amar Kaddishin cite the words of Shevut Yaakov briefly and write that in their opinion he should be penalized and the money given to the poor, for property declared ownerless by beit din is ownerless. The Shach writes something similar at the end of Yoreh Deah siman 171. In my view, this is not clear. Although the Shach there writes in the name of Responsa Mas'at Binyamin, siman 34, that it is proper to penalize the borrower by requiring him to give the profit to the poor of the city, the responsum itself explicitly states that this is only when a protective enactment is needed because the circumstances of the time require it. The Shach's intent must therefore be the same, although he abbreviated in citing it. Accordingly, in our case, where Shevut Yaakov repeatedly emphasizes that there is no need for such a protective enactment, we have no authority to penalize him. Moreover, the case of interest can be distinguished from this case. Birkei Yosef, entry 3, also writes: If there is a permanent judge in the city and he deems it necessary to punish such conduct, he may do as we have heard that beit din may administer lashes and punish beyond the letter of Torah law, provided that the circumstances require it, such as where the one who hired the witness has previously done so twice, or where the people of the region engage in such conduct and there is concern that if they refrain from punishing it the practice will spread. If either of these two conditions is absent, beit din cannot impose any monetary obligation on the offender. Certainly, one who is not a beit din cannot do so, and the public does not have the status of a permanent judge in this matter. See Mahari ibn Migash, manuscript responsum, siman 161. End of quote.
