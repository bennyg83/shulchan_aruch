Seif 2. In the gloss: “Likewise, if he did not hire them,” etc. Note: This is the opinion of Tosafot in the chapter HaKones. The Shach questions them from Kiddushin, where the Gemara wonders whether the first Tanna could mean that even under the laws of Heaven he is exempt. It appears to me that Tosafot can be defended by distinguishing the reason why agency does not operate for a transgression. If one holds that two verses that teach the same exceptional rule do not establish a general rule, and one therefore derives outside those verses that there is no agent for a transgression by ordinary reasoning, then wherever the basic principle “the words of the Master and the words of the disciple, whose words should be obeyed?” applies, the principal is exempt even under the laws of Heaven. The actor chose to disobey Hashem and bears the responsibility. But according to the opinion that the exceptional verses can teach broadly and the ordinary exemption requires a specific derivation excluding agency for sin, one can distinguish human liability from heavenly liability. The Torah may remove formal legal agency and thereby exempt the sender in human court while still leaving moral responsibility under the laws of Heaven. Accordingly, the Gemara’s alternate answer, that the verse “he himself” is expounded and the sender remains liable only under the laws of Heaven, is addressing the view according to which the scriptural derivation is needed. The Gemara need not offer the parallel answer according to the opinion that two verses do not teach a general rule, because according to that framework the sender may be exempt even under the laws of Heaven. This also explains the language in Bava Kamma concerning one who hires false witnesses. The Gemara says that one might have thought he is exempt even under the laws of Heaven because the witnesses should have obeyed Hashem rather than the hirer, and teaches otherwise. The wording itself implies that where the ordinary principle of “whose words should be obeyed?” fully applies without an additional factor such as payment, it is reasonable to exempt even under the laws of Heaven. This is consistent with Tosafot and not with the Shach’s broad objection. The Shach also asks from the primordial serpent, where the Gemara indicates that the instigator is exempt. Mishneh LaMelech and Pnei Yehoshua distinguish a case in which one person receives the benefit while another would bear the liability. They suggest that in such a case even heavenly liability does not transfer. I am not persuaded that Rava’s statement, “We never find in the entire Torah that one person benefits and another is liable,” necessarily includes the laws of Heaven. The Torah sources he invokes concern liability enforceable by beit din. It is possible that under the laws of Heaven the instigator is still responsible. Rava’s statement can have a practical consequence even according to our halachah in the laws of meilah. Nimukei Yosef writes that one who sends fire through a minor is ordinarily exempt, but if the child is so young that he has no independent legal hand, the adult can be liable because the child is like an instrument or courtyard. Accordingly, if an adult tells a very young child to eat consecrated food, one might have thought the adult liable as though he directly caused his courtyard to consume it. Rava teaches that where the child personally derives the benefit, we do not impose the meilah liability on the sender. Tosafot explain elsewhere that an adult agent can acquire consecrated property upon lifting it, but a very young child lacks such acquisition. Rava’s rule is therefore needed. Thus there is no proof from the serpent that heavenly liability is always absent.
