But he transgresses "distance yourself from falsehood." So writes Nimukei Yosef. I do not understand why the person does not also violate the Torah prohibition, "you shall not place a stumbling block before the blind," since he caused the witnesses to testify falsely in beit din and thereby violate a Torah prohibition. Even if Rama follows the view in Yoreh Deah siman 151 that lifnei iver applies only where the sinner could not otherwise obtain the prohibited means, here the witnesses testified falsely only because they were paid. Who else would hire them and give them money to violate Hashem's command? Perhaps the answer is that the baraita's statement that he is liable before Heaven means liable to compensate the injured party, as Shach explains: he does not satisfy his Heavenly obligation until he pays because his fellow has a justified grievance against him for the damage caused. If the underlying claim was actually true, the injured party has no monetary grievance. The hirer still acted improperly toward him through the false mode of proof and violated "distance yourself from falsehood," and he must appease him for that wrongdoing, but this does not create a monetary obligation to compensate him. The separate prohibition of lifnei iver concerns the relation between the hirer and the false witnesses whom he caused to sin. Payment to the injured litigant does not remedy that transgression. Even if he gave away all his wealth, he would still have caused the witnesses to testify falsely. Thus Nimukei Yosef speaks specifically of the wrong relevant to the injured litigant. If the matter was substantively true, there is no compensable loss to him; only the offense of pursuing the case through false testimony remains. The lifnei iver violation is a separate matter between the hirer and the witnesses and therefore is not part of the rule of liability before Heaven toward his fellow.
