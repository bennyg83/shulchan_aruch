Likewise, if he did not hire them, etc. Shach, seif katan 3, asks from Kiddushin 43a. One who tells his agent, "Go kill this person," leaves the agent liable and the sender exempt. Shammai the Elder says the sender is liable. The Gemara interprets "liable" as liable by the laws of Heaven and asks whether the first Tanna exempts even by the laws of Heaven, concluding that the difference may be between a greater and lesser Heavenly punishment. There is no difficulty. "Greater judgment" means that the sender is treated before Heaven as though he performed the act himself and receives the punishment appropriate to the act. Thus David was held responsible for Uriah's death as though he shed his blood. "Lesser judgment" means that the sender is punished for causing the sin but not with the same measure as the actual perpetrator. Here, however, "liable before Heaven" means obligated to compensate his fellow. A false witness who causes wrongful monetary extraction has a duty to pay. A person who hires the false witness is treated, for this monetary Heavenly obligation, like the witness himself. That is the "greater judgment" relevant here. The authorities therefore ask how mere instruction can create such full liability when the halachah generally does not treat the sender as the actor in a transgression. Nimukei Yosef distinguishes between merely telling and actually hiring: payment creates a stronger form of causation, and everyone can agree that the hirer must compensate by the laws of Heaven. This is also the sense of Chavot Yair, though its discussion is somewhat confused. Rashba's novellae to Bava Kamma suggest another explanation. Rashba infers from the Mishnah's precise wording, "one who hires false witnesses," that mere verbal inducement does not create the same liability. He rejects the thought that "hires" was chosen merely to teach that despite payment there is still no liability in human court, because that would be obvious: the witness's false testimony is itself only indirect causation for many purposes. Why is that obvious? In Kiddushin one view holds that the sender in a transgression can be liable even in human court where agency applies. The answer is that false testimony itself does not constitute a direct physical act. Even according to R. Meir's law of dina d'garmei, Tosafot and Ritzba treat much of this liability as a rabbinic extension. We therefore do not expand agency further and impose human liability on the one who merely caused a witness to cause damage. Even if he paid the witness, there remains uncertainty: the witnesses may retract when threatened, fail under interrogation, or refuse to testify. The hirer's conduct is therefore at most causation of causation for human damages. Accordingly, the Mishnah teaches a substantial novelty: even though the result is only indirect causation, hiring false witnesses creates liability before Heaven. Mere words do not. This can coexist with the general rule that where the agent directly commits definite damage, even a verbal sender may incur a Heavenly punishment. Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef, however, derive the distinction from the case of sending fire through a competent person, indicating that even where direct damage results the sender does not have the full compensatory Heavenly liability unless he hired the actor. Ritva appears to disagree and holds that even verbal persuasion can create liability. He can understand compensating the victim as a lesser Heavenly judgment rather than the "greater judgment" reserved for the moral punishment of a murderer. Shach brings support from the serpent, which could have argued, "the words of the Master and the words of the student, whose words should be obeyed?" That is not persuasive. The serpent's punishment was enormous. The Gemara's rule that the sender avoids the greater punishment fits precisely. Moreover, Tosafot distinguish human wrongs: Torah repeatedly commands us not to harm our fellow and even requires us to help an enemy's animal and return lost property. A person who intentionally causes harm to his fellow violates that interpersonal obligation. The serpent had not yet been commanded within such a framework. One might object that Midrash Rabbah in Parashat Noach says a Noahide can be liable through an agent. But perhaps that command was given only after Adam's time. More fundamentally, Adam's sin involved eating and enjoyment. In matters of forbidden fats and sexual relations, everyone agrees there is no agency for the personal enjoyment, because one person cannot enjoy while another becomes the actor. The serpent was not commanded regarding eating from the Tree of Knowledge; Adam and Chavah were. Agency cannot make the serpent the direct violator of a command that did not apply to it. Another practical distinction follows. If a person has a salaried servant who receives regular support and tells him to testify falsely, that is not necessarily "hiring false witnesses" merely because the servant is generally paid. The payment must be specifically for the false testimony. Proof comes from David and Uriah. Joab was the commander of the army and received royal support and favor, yet Scripture treats David as one who instructed, not one who hired, because no special payment was fixed for placing Uriah in danger. Likewise, the Torah's rule of liability for misappropriation through one's servant or agent cannot mean every servant is automatically a paid agent for each specific act. Chavot Yair seems inclined to treat every agent as hired because he receives salary, but that is strained. I would go further. Perhaps the Mishnah's phrase "one who hires false witnesses" does not describe formal agency at all. He does not say, "Go and testify on my behalf." Rather, he says, "If you testify, I will give you such-and-such payment." This resembles the formulation "whoever feeds her will not lose," which does not create formal agency. That would explain why the Mishnah must speak specifically of hiring. Where there is no agency command, the payment itself is the causal act generating the Heavenly duty. Midrash Rabbah on Parashat Noach supports this distinction. It separately derives liability for "one who hires others to kill his fellow" and then derives killing through an agent. The commentators ask why both are needed. The answer may be that hiring means promising payment if the act is done, without appointing the person as an agent, while sending means direct agency: "Go kill this person." These are distinct forms of causation and therefore require separate treatment. One final question: where a Jew hires non-Jews to testify falsely in a secular court against a violent litigant, could he be liable in human court because the non-Jewish witnesses are not themselves subject to the same Jewish-law disqualification and the sender may be treated as acting through an agent? This is doubtful. Rama below in siman 182 holds that where the agent is not personally subject to the prohibition, the sender can sometimes be treated as principal even in a transgression. But non-Jews may themselves be prohibited from false testimony as part of the Noahide command of laws, since justice cannot exist if witnesses may lie. In addition, we rule that there is no agency for a non-Jew. Shach discusses this at length below in siman 182. Therefore ordinary human-court liability cannot simply be imposed through agency in that case. Rashba's formulation also explains Sema's wording. Sema adds that the false witnesses themselves still retain freedom of choice: despite the hirer's payment, they may refuse when they stand before the judges or when the court examines them. Thus the hirer has not created an inevitable direct loss. For human liability, that remains too remote. For Heavenly liability, however, the deliberate payment that induced the false testimony is enough. This is also why the Mishnah does not simply teach, "one who hires another to damage his fellow." Its choice of false witnesses is deliberate. The damage is produced through judicial testimony, itself an indirect mechanism, and nevertheless the special act of hiring creates a Heavenly duty to compensate. The novelty is precisely that even in this layered causation, payment to procure the false testimony is treated more severely than mere verbal encouragement. According to Tosafot and Nimukei Yosef, the proof from one who sends a fire through a competent person establishes that mere direction does not create the same compensatory obligation. The competent actor remains responsible for his own deed. Only where the sender went further and hired the actor do Chazal impose the fuller Heavenly responsibility toward the victim. Ritva, who obligates even through verbal persuasion, must read "liable before Heaven" more broadly. He can say that any deliberate instigation of damage creates a Heavenly obligation, while the Mishnah chose hiring because it is the clearest and most aggravated case. The distinction concerning a salaried servant follows from the same principle. Regular wages purchase general service, not this particular prohibited act. The servant's existing employment therefore does not convert every command into a specially hired transgression. Only a payment or benefit specifically attached to the wrongful testimony falls squarely within "one who hires false witnesses." This is evident from Joab. His dependence on the king and his regular position did not make David a hirer for Uriah's death. Scripture's treatment of David is through sending and command, not through payment for that act. Likewise, when Scripture or Chazal distinguish an act done through one's servant from one done through one's agent, they do not assume that the servant's salary converts every mission into a separately hired act. The proposed reading of the Mishnah, that the hirer says only, "If you testify, I will pay you," also explains why the rule can stand outside formal agency. The witnesses choose independently whether to act, but the promised payment induces the act. This is a classic case in which hiring strengthens causation without making the witnesses mere instruments. That is why the Midrash can need one derivation for hiring and another for sending. A hired actor can remain juridically independent, while an agent acts under an explicit command. The Torah can assign different forms of responsibility to the two. Finally, even if a non-Jewish false witness is not personally subject to the identical Jewish prohibition of false testimony, it does not follow that the Jewish hirer becomes directly liable through agency. A Noahide is commanded in dinim, and a system of justice necessarily excludes false testimony. Moreover, the general rule is that a non-Jew does not serve as a halachic agent for a Jew. These considerations prevent converting the case into ordinary direct agency liability.
