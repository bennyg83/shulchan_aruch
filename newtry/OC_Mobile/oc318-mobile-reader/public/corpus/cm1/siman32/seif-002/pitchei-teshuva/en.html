One who hires false witnesses. See Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 145, concerning one who demanded that another return ten gold coins paid to him as compensation for giving false testimony, after which the witness did not testify and now denies everything. Shevut Yaakov initially considers whether even if the witness admits receiving the money he should not have to return it, as a penalty upon the one who hired false witnesses, similar to the Gemara in Rosh Hashanah 22b. In places where the Sages penalize the transgressor, the money is sometimes left with the other party rather than divided among the poor. He rejects this. In the Rosh Hashanah case, the Sages' grant to the witness was a special measure to reinforce the matter. Here there is no such public need: the fact that this person acted improperly does not establish that Jews generally hire false witnesses, the act was not public, and he is not established as a repeat offender. Without a public need, we have no authority to invent a fine merely by analogy, especially where the Gemara and Choshen Mishpat siman 32 state that one who hires false witnesses is exempt from liability in human court. Therefore the witness must return the money, and if he denies receipt he must take a rabbinic oath. Some later works cite Shevut Yaakov briefly and argue that the court should fine the hirer by giving the money to the poor under the principle that property declared ownerless by beit din is ownerless, comparing the Shach in Yoreh Deah end of siman 171. This is not clear, because the underlying responsum cited by the Shach explicitly limits such a fine to a time when a public corrective measure is needed. Birkei Yosef likewise cites Mahari ibn Migash that a permanent local beit din may punish beyond the strict law only where circumstances require it, such as when the offender has repeated the act or the practice is spreading in the region. If neither condition exists, a court may not impose such a penalty, all the more so one who is not a court; see there.
