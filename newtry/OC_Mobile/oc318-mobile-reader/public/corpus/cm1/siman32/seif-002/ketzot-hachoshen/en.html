Maharshal rules that where one claimant has a dispute with three people, one becomes the litigant and the other two testify for him, and later those two admit they themselves had a financial share in the claim, they must return the money, even if they still maintain that substantively the claimant was entitled to it. They testified falsely by presenting the whole maneh as belonging solely to the named litigant, and in any event they were interested witnesses. Since Torah law would not have allowed recovery on their testimony, the money was extracted improperly. Maharshal says their later admission is like one hundred witnesses and destroys any migo. In my view money should not be extracted from them on this basis. The case closely resembles Bava Batra 32, where a possessor produces a deed, admits that it is forged, but says he once had a valid deed that was lost. Rabbah accepts the migo that he could have maintained the present deed was valid, while Rav Yosef rejects it. Rashbam explains Rav Yosef because the party himself admitted the present deed was worthless, and a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Ramban already notes that this reasoning is difficult: the whole point of migo is that he could have avoided making the harmful admission. Tosafot instead explains that the party was forced initially to lie or that he is changing his claim. Even those rationales do not eliminate Rabbah's competing view. We rule like Rabbah with land and Rav Yosef with money, but Rashbam explains this as unresolved law, leaving possession where it stands. Tosafot explains the money ruling because a migo cannot extract. Accordingly, once these interested witnesses already possess the money, their migo is one of retention, not extraction, and under either approach money should not be removed from them merely because they later admitted the defect. Tosafot in Ketubot concerning a father who says, "This is my son and he is a kohen," can be read in support of Maharshal's idea that a person cannot rely on a migo when according to his own present statement he is a relative and therefore disqualified. Yet that case differs because the very subject of his testimony is the relationship making him disqualified. Here the principal testimony was that the debtor owed money, and only later did they reveal that they had an interest. That resembles the forged deed case much more closely. The Rosh, in responsa, rules that if the witnesses revealed their partnership before testifying, their later testimony is worthless. But if they already testified, they cannot afterward invalidate themselves by saying they had a share, so long as the named claimant denies their share. If the claimant admits their interest, the testimony is void. This appears to address a case before the money has yet been finally extracted, so the witnesses would need a migo to extract. Once they already hold the money, however, the better rule is not to remove it from them. Thus Maharshal's sweeping requirement of repayment is not established.
