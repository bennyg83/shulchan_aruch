And he is liable under the laws of Heaven. He does not discharge his obligation before Heaven until he pays the injured party for the damage. See Responsa Maharam Alshich, siman 73.
Likewise, if he did not hire them. Tosafot at the beginning of chapter HaKones, Bava Kamma 56a, writes this, inferring it from the Mishnah's specific wording, "one who hires false witnesses." Tosafot also infers it from the rule that one who sends a fire through a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is exempt under human law but liable under the laws of Heaven, whereas if he sends it through a competent adult, the competent adult is liable. This implies that the sender is exempt even under the laws of Heaven. Their words are difficult, however, because Kiddushin 43a states that although there is no agency for a transgression, the sender is still liable under the laws of Heaven. The Gemara there plainly asks whether the first Tanna would hold that the sender is exempt even under the laws of Heaven, and answers that the dispute concerns the difference between the greater and lesser heavenly tribunal, which Rashi explains as greater and lesser punishment. Tosafot's position can perhaps be reconciled with difficulty. Nevertheless, it appears to me that the primary rule is that one who causes damage to his fellow is always liable under the laws of Heaven, even if he did not hire the wrongdoer. The Mishnah's statement that when a competent adult carries the fire, the adult is liable, without saying that the sender is liable under the laws of Heaven, is because once the competent adult pays the victim in full, there is no remaining heavenly liability upon the sender. Ritva in chapter HaIsh Mekadesh writes explicitly: When he sends the fire through a competent adult, the adult is liable and the sender is completely exempt, even under the laws of Heaven, because the victim has already been compensated by the competent adult. This differs from one who hires false witnesses, who is exempt under human law but liable under the laws of Heaven because the victim has not been compensated for his loss. The same principle applies to all the cases at the beginning of chapter HaKones. The Mishnah speaks of one who "hires" witnesses because that is the ordinary case. If he does not pay them, they generally will not obey him, since two people ordinarily will not jointly commit a sin without receiving some benefit. One might apparently bring a proof for Tosafot from Sanhedrin 29a, where the Gemara asks how we know that no defense is advanced on behalf of an inciter, and derives it from the primordial serpent. Rabbi Simlai said that the serpent could have advanced many defenses, but did not, and God did not advance them for him because he had not advanced them himself. What could he have said? "The words of the Master and the words of the student, whose words should one heed?" This is also the rationale for the principle that there is no agency for a transgression. If the sender nevertheless remained liable under the laws of Heaven, that defense should not have helped the serpent before God, since that very proceeding was a heavenly judgment. Although Midrash Rabbah says that the serpent was judged by a court of seventy one, it was still heavenly judgment. This would suggest that there is no agency for a transgression even under heavenly law. The proof can be rejected with some difficulty. See also below, siman 418.
The witnesses must pay. This means that the false witnesses themselves must pay, but not the person who extracted the money through their testimony, because he does not admit wrongdoing and the witnesses are not believed against him. Once they originally testified, they cannot retract and give contradictory testimony. With respect to themselves, however, they are believed to obligate themselves. So explains the Sema. Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Raah discusses this case, contrary to Beit Yosef, and Darkei Moshe likewise writes so. Ramban also states this explicitly in his laws of dina d'garmi, which I cite below in siman 38. Where one person has a claim against three others, and one of the three becomes a litigant while the other two testify, if all three later admit the truth they must return the money, even if they say they had a valid underlying right to take that amount from him. Maharshal so rules in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 4, siman 18.
