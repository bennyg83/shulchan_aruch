The hirer. Meaning, he admits that he hired them. If there are witnesses to the hiring, then either the litigant or the witnesses themselves are liable to pay. The case is where he hired them to testify for someone else, because if he hired them for his own benefit and admits that the testimony was false, he is liable even under human law, Gemara:
And he is liable under the laws of Heaven. He does not discharge his moral obligation until he compensates his fellow for the entire loss, Shach. Under human law he is exempt, because the witnesses should have obeyed the Master's command rather than the student's instruction:
And likewise if he did not hire them. If he hired non Jewish witnesses to give false testimony in a non Jewish court, in a situation where resort to that court was permitted, it appears that he is exempt under human law, Urim VeTumim:
And he does not admit. Meaning, the person who collected the money does not admit the wrongdoing. He is liable to pay, while the person who hired the false witnesses is exempt:
And he extracted. Meaning, the collector admits it, or the opposing litigant admits, and therefore he must return the money. In that circumstance the one who hired the witnesses is exempt:
They are liable to pay. The Maharshal writes in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter 4, siman 18, regarding the rule that three people who claim one hundred from a person may not arrange matters so that one acts as litigant and two as witnesses, because of the verse, "Distance yourself from falsehood." If afterward they admit before the litigant, they must return the one hundred that they extracted. There is no migo, because it is a migo against witnesses. Their admission that they extracted money from him unlawfully is equivalent to one hundred witnesses, and their presumption that they spoke truthfully has also been undermined.
