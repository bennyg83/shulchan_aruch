The charity administrator said to the custodian. Responsa Shivat Tzion, siman 99, discusses a synagogue administrator who collected donations for Pesach flour (kimcha d'pischa) for the local poor. The money consisted of government notes, which he wrapped with the donation list and placed in his pocket, where they were lost. The questioner proposed three grounds for exemption: first, that this was not negligence because he placed them where he kept his own money; second, that even if negligent he should be exempt lest people refuse to serve, analogous to certain guardians of orphans; third, that charitable funds to be distributed among the poor have no specific claimant and therefore create no custodial liability even for negligence. Shivat Tzion rejects all three. It was certainly negligence not to place the notes in the leather pouch customarily used for that purpose before putting them in his pocket. There is no concern that people will refuse to serve, because synagogue administrators are chosen by the community and the office is regarded as an honor, like a guardian appointed by beit din. As to the third argument, if the local practice were simply to distribute money to the poor, there would indeed be room for the exemption. Here, however, the custom was to distribute flour. The communal leaders had already contracted with a miller for a fixed quantity and were personally obligated to pay him. If the synagogue administrator's collection was missing, they would have to supply the deficit from their own funds. Accordingly, the synagogue administrators were custodians appointed by the communal leaders over money effectively belonging to those leaders. Rama states that when the charity administrator tells the custodian, "Guard it for me," the latter is liable. Although Shach says that this rule requires an explicit instruction, because otherwise both administrators may be exempt, that is where exemption of the custodian causes no loss to the administrator. Here, exempting the synagogue administrator would directly cause the communal leaders to pay the miller from their own pockets. In such a circumstance the ordinary arrangement itself is equivalent to an express instruction. Indeed, since each year the communal leaders undertook liability in reliance on the money held by the synagogue administrators, the administrators plainly became their custodians. Thus the collected money is not treated merely as poor funds but as money of the communal leaders, and the administrator is liable at least for negligence if he failed to guard it in the customary manner. This reasoning implies that had the communal leaders not undertaken personal liability, the synagogue administrator would have been exempt absent an explicit "guard it for me," contrary to Netivot HaMishpat cited above.
