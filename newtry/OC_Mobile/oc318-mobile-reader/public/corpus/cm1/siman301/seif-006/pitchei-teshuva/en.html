And not to distribute to the poor. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 199, explains that the rule "to guard, but not to distribute to the poor" exempts the custodian from payment in human law because there is no specific person who can sue him. To fulfill his obligation before Heaven, however, he remains liable, and all the more so since a verbal pledge to charity itself creates an obligation. He discusses a synagogue sexton who collected charity daily in a box and each Friday delivered it to the charity administrator, who counted and distributed the funds. It was discovered that the sexton had regularly stolen through the slot. He was removed and punished, but was not initially fined because the hope was that he would eventually return the stolen money. The sexton later asked whether he was legally required to make restitution and how much. Chavot Yair ruled that he must repay according to a reasonable estimate, and if he wishes to emerge completely clean he should pay enough that he is certain he has not underpaid, like one who says, "I vowed but do not know how much." He should return the money to the same fund so that it reaches the poor of that city. Chavot Yair also recounts a study society whose members contributed weekly to a box, two thirds for communal completion meals and one third for the town's poor. One administrator who had to travel gave the box, containing about fifty Reichsthaler, to his fellow administrator, who negligently guarded it and it was stolen. The first administrator was not negligent, because the second also regularly served in that role and the members had not insisted which of them hold the box. The negligent administrator had to pay the estimated two-thirds belonging to the members, because that money had identifiable claimants as partners or through authorization. The one-third allocated to the poor was exempt in human law, though to fulfill the heavenly obligation he had to pay the poor who normally received from that fund. The wording of the responsum implies that in the first sexton's case as well, restitution was required only to fulfill the heavenly obligation, not enforceable in beit din. This also shows that Chavot Yair treats the exemption as applying not only to negligence but even to direct damage. Tumim above, siman 66, seif katan 69, and Netivot HaMishpat at the end of seif katan 2 here likewise address the point. See Tosafot, Bava Kamma 93a, s.v. ve-raminu.
