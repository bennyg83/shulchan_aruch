If they made a kinyan. Bava Metzia similarly says with hekdesh that liability can be accepted through a kinyan. Ketzot HaChoshen asks why a kinyan is needed for hekdesh, since speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person. If he expressly says, "If it is lost I obligate myself to pay such-and-such," then indeed speech can create the obligation to hekdesh. The Gemara, however, did not wish to establish the case that way. It speaks of someone who merely accepts upon himself the legal status of a custodian. Since hekdesh is ordinarily outside the Torah's custodial category, verbal acceptance alone does not create the normal custodian framework unless supported by a kinyan, except where the wording can be treated as an express monetary undertaking such as "I regard myself as though I received it," as Shach explains.
To become liable for responsibility. R. Aharon Sasson was uncertain whether an unpaid custodian who stipulates to have the law of a paid custodian must swear that the loss was unavoidable, or whether he needs only the ordinary oath against negligence. In his responsum he actually resolves that the stronger oath is required, based on Mordechai. Ketzot HaChoshen rejects the proof by saying perhaps the oath there came only through gilgul. But the Gemara's inference is stronger: the baraita says not merely that he "claims theft," but that he swears, implying a principal oath fitting the higher liability he accepted. Thus when a custodian accepts the legal responsibility of a paid custodian through kinyan, he accepts the corresponding principal oath needed to establish the exempting accident. Conversely, if a paid custodian stipulates to have only the law of an unpaid custodian, then if the object is lost in a manner from which he is exempt, he can still forfeit his fee, because the payment was for successfully performing the agreed guarding.
