To become liable for their responsibility. Mahara Sasson was uncertain about an unpaid custodian who stipulated to have the liability of a paid custodian. If he claims an unavoidable accident, perhaps he intended to assume only monetary liability for proven theft and loss, not the paid custodian's special oath on an uncertain claim. In my view the wording controls. If he said merely, "I accept liability for theft and loss," he assumed only payment where theft or loss is established. If witnesses prove that he was not negligent but it remains uncertain whether the item was stolen or lost through unavoidable accident, he need not take a special oath merely because of that monetary undertaking. But if he said, "I accept upon myself the law of a paid custodian," he entered the full legal status of a paid custodian. That includes the rule that he must swear to an exempting accident or pay. The benefit of the owner's trust supplies the consideration for that enlarged undertaking, just as a formal kinyan could. The Mordechai concerning communal shekalim supports this distinction. When custodians formally undertook paid-custodian liability, they also became subject to the corresponding oath structure. Even if the original principal custodial oath has been satisfied, remaining matters can be included through gilgul where appropriate. Thus the uncertainty of Mahara Sasson applies chiefly where only payment for theft and loss was verbally accepted. Where the custodian expressly said, "I am like a paid custodian," he accepted the entire paid-custodian regime, oath or payment included.
