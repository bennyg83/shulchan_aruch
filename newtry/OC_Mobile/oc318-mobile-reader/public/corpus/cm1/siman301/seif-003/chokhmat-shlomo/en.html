Seif 6. One with whom money of the poor was deposited, etc. Note: I was asked about charity money collected in a household charity box. The householder removed the money and sent it through an agent to the charity treasurer, but the agent betrayed his mission and did not deliver it. Is the householder liable? The correct answer is that the original placement of money in the charity box is merely a voluntary designation comparable to saying 'this amount is for charity,' for which one is not personally responsible if it is subsequently lost. Still, more must be said. Regarding money generally, Choshen Mishpat 292 treats people today like money changers and therefore as paid custodians, because they can use the cash and later replace it. Yoreh De'ah 259 likewise states that one holding charity money may borrow from it and repay it. Thus he can have the status of a paid custodian. Our present siman also states that the laws of custodians can apply to funds of the poor, except that liability may fail because there is no particular claimant. Therefore, if this money had been designated for the poor of that city and their shares were fixed, one could have imposed liability for loss. Here, however, the beneficiaries were not the local poor in a fixed allocation, so there is no claimant. Furthermore, the loss did not occur in the householder's own hands but in the agent's. According to the Rama here, a custodian who transfers property to another custodian can be treated even more severely than ordinary negligence, almost as one who directly causes damage, which might suggest liability. According to the Shach, however, the central rationales are absent here. The donor cannot say, 'I do not want my deposit in another person's hands,' because the money was never entrusted to the householder for his own benefit, nor can the poor say, 'I trusted your oath but not his,' since they never personally appointed him. Accordingly, this resembles a case where the owner regularly entrusts property to the second custodian, and the claim lies directly against the agent. The householder who sent it is exempt. This is clear in my view, and this is what I ruled.
