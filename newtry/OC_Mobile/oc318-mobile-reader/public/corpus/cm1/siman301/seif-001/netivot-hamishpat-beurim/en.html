Some impose liability for negligence. See Shach in siman 66, and Tumim, who correctly rejects the proof from charity funds. Charity is different because Scripture disclosed that the funds are not privately owned by any specific claimant but belong to the class of poor persons. Therefore even one who directly damages such funds can sometimes be exempt, like gifts due to kohanim, because they are money with no specific claimant. Nimukei Yosef proves this in the case of a purse for redeeming captives given to thieves: his question why the custodian should not be exempt under "to guard and not to distribute" shows that even direct delivery to the thieves can be exempt for this reason. The proof from shemirah b'baalim is also not decisive. The Torah expressly exempts even negligence when the owner is with the custodian. The verse "if its owner is with him, he shall not pay" is a decree concerning payment. It does not imply that every other statutory exclusion from the custodial chapter removes ordinary tort liability. With slaves and land, Scripture can be understood as excluding only the special custodial oath and the extraordinary liabilities of custodians. If a person expressly accepted to guard and then negligently failed to do what he undertook, that negligence can be treated as direct damage, as Shitah Mekubetzet writes. By contrast, theft liability of a paid custodian is a special Torah liability even where he used ordinary care. That special liability is excluded for slaves and land. If a person never performed the acquisition necessary to become custodian according to Rambam, he is exempt even for ordinary negligence. Rambam says only that one may place a ban upon someone who took property and refuses to return it. This shows that negligence becomes damage only after an actual acceptance of custody. An outsider who never became custodian resembles one who merely breaches a fence before another's animal and can be exempt. But if he places the item in a public domain or other clearly ownerless location, making it an intentional loss, that is direct damage and he is liable even without custodial status. As to a beginning in negligence followed by accident with slaves or land, there is room to doubt. Rambam treats negligence as payment liability not removed by the statutory exclusion. Since ordinary movable property is liable when the later accident can plausibly be traced to the initial negligence, and Scripture did not expressly exclude land from payment liability of that type, it appears land and slaves are likewise liable under Rambam where the accident follows from the initial negligence. True, the initial negligence is not an ordinary tort for an outsider. But once the person accepted custody, failure to perform that accepted guarding is itself treated like damaging, and the same logic extends to an accident flowing from that breach.
A custodian who transferred to another custodian. Shach says Rosh speaks where the first custodian transferred the object completely to the second. This is very difficult, because Rosh's responsum says explicitly that the first is exempt only when he transferred to someone the owner regularly uses. If he gave it absolutely to another person, he can be liable even where that person is a usual depositary. Tumim's alternative, that Rosh's case was known to involve someone who would throw the property away, is also difficult. In my view Rosh and Maharik do not disagree. Rosh speaks where it is uncertain whether the property is still in the second custodian's hands and the second denies possession. The first custodian is liable because so long as the second has not sworn, we do not assume the item was actually lost. We suspect it may still be in his hands. The first custodian then caused the owner's property to become inaccessible through his own act of transfer. This is treated as direct damage, not merely as the rule that one unable to swear must pay. Indeed, even a person who never accepted custody, but takes another's item from his house and gives it to a third person who later says it was lost, can be liable to the owner. This explains why the first custodian's heirs do not automatically escape merely because he died before being required to swear. The liability is not founded only on "unable to swear, pays." Siman 176 likewise rules that a partner may not deposit partnership property with another person, and if he changes the agreed method and loss follows, he can be liable even though ordinary partnership can involve shemirah b'baalim. The reason is that transferring the body of the property into a situation where it can no longer be accounted for is a damaging act. Kesef Mishneh likewise says that handing the item to another can be treated as damage. Maharik, however, speaks where it is established by witnesses or by the oath of a regular depositary that a true accident occurred while the property was with the second custodian. Then the first person's transfer is not considered negligence merely because the owner would have preferred him to guard personally. We do not presume an intelligent second custodian to be a thief. If he guarded properly and the item was lost through accident, the first did not cause that accident. Maharik's discussion of charity is even easier, because charity funds can be exempt from this entire damage theory as money without a specific claimant. Thus there is no true dispute. Where the second person's actual loss is unproved and he may still be holding the property, the first can be liable as a damager. Where a true accident in the second person's custody is established, the first can be exempt if the transfer itself was not negligent.
