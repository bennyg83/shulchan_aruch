Ordinarily, theft entails despair, even when committed by a gentile. Rama followed the Tur, who understood 'bandit' as an open robber and therefore distinguished between a gentile and a Jew, while treating ordinary theft as presumed despair even when committed by a gentile. The Tur's reading is difficult. The Rosh merely copied the Gemara, which compels the alternative view cited by the Tur: regarding a thief, there is a distinction between a gentile and a Jew, because one does not ordinarily despair after theft by a gentile; regarding an open robber, even a Jew does not create a presumption of despair. In the Gemara, Rambam, Semag, and the other authorities, 'bandit' means a thief, while 'tax collector' denotes an open robber. The dispute of Rabbi Shimon and the Sages likewise concerns Jewish offenders: the Sages hold that ordinary theft implies despair but ordinary robbery does not, whereas Rabbi Shimon reverses the rule. The sugyot concerning ritual impurity, the statements of Rabbi, and the cases of a Jewish bandit all prove this. Baal HaMaor explains that presumed despair after ordinary theft applies to a Jewish thief, but not to a gentile thief, because gentile courts may recover the property; the sugya's 'tax collector' is an open robber and 'bandit' is a thief. Rashba, Tosafot, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Rif, Mordechai, and Maharashal support this distinction. The passages in Sukkah and Kiddushin also establish that ordinary robbery, even by a Jew, does not create presumed despair. It is untenable to say that rabbinically there is presumed despair, since the issue is the owner's actual state of mind and the Sages do not transform what people ordinarily intend. The Tur himself elsewhere writes that one must actually hear that the victim despaired, and that ordinary robbery does not imply despair. Accordingly, the correct rule is: ordinary theft by a Jew implies despair; theft by a gentile does not; ordinary robbery, whether by a Jew or gentile, does not imply despair unless it is actually expressed. I have elaborated because the Tur wrote imprecisely in passing, and later authorities followed him. See also Responsa Rabbi Moshe Alshich, siman 112.
