From Jewish bandits, it belongs to him, etc., until "the non-Jewish authorities return, etc." Meaning, non-Jewish judges may compel return under their own system based on circumstantial assessment even without formal witnesses. The Gemara in the final chapter of Bava Kamma says that non-Jewish judges act forcefully, whereas Jewish judges say, in effect, "Who says matters happened as you claim? Bring witnesses that he stole from you." Rashi gives two explanations. According to the first, non-Jewish courts use force and beat the defendant; according to the second, Jewish judges require formal proof. The Mechaber follows the latter approach and therefore writes that non-Jewish judges may compel the robber even without witnesses. One must explain why the Tur, Mechaber, and Gemara distinguish between a non-Jewish bandit and a Jewish bandit rather than between non-Jewish and Jewish judges, since according to this reason everything depends upon the court system that will recover the property. The answer is that a Jewish robber or bandit is ordinarily sued by the Jewish victim in a Jewish beit din, whereas a non-Jewish bandit must be pursued even by a Jewish victim in the non-Jewish court. This is straightforward. See the Perishah.
But one who rescues property from non-Jewish bandits or a non-Jewish customs collector must return it, etc. Rambam writes this at the beginning of chapter 6 of Hilchot Gezeilah and also in chapter 5, law 9. The Maggid Mishneh explains that Rambam views an ordinary non-Jewish customs collector, even one operating under royal authority, as analogous to a customs collector who has no fixed rate, because ordinarily he takes more than the law permits. Rambam understood Rav Ashi's interpretation in the final chapter of Bava Kamma accordingly. Other commentators do not interpret it this way. The Tur apparently follows those other commentators and therefore does not mention a non-Jewish customs collector in this law.
And what Rambam and the Mechaber write, "he must return it, for ordinarily the owners did not despair, etc." This requires study. In siman 369, seif 5, the Mechaber writes, following Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot Gezeilah: if customs collectors took a person's garment or donkey and returned a different one, he may keep what they gave him because the exchange is like a sale, and ordinarily the owners despaired, provided he does not know with certainty that the replacement is stolen. That case explicitly concerns non-Jewish customs collectors. We therefore see that Rambam holds that the owners are ordinarily presumed to despair even when the customs collector is a non-Jew. Here too, when one rescues a garment, donkey, or similar property from bandits or customs collectors, why does Rambam say that the owners ordinarily have not despaired? The Tur presents no difficulty. Although he rules in siman 369, seif 7, following the Mishnah, that if a customs collector gives one person a cloak in place of his own he may keep it because the owners despaired, he does not distinguish there between Jewish and non-Jewish customs collectors. Yet he also holds here that owners do not despair from non-Jewish bandits because the courts act forcefully. One can distinguish customs collectors from ordinary bandits: a customs collector initially has lawful authority to collect the tax, and even if he takes more than permitted he can easily excuse himself by denying that he overcharged or by offering some explanation. Owners therefore despair more readily. An ordinary bandit, by contrast, simply took the object by force in the victim's presence. Rambam and the Mechaber, however, equate non-Jewish customs collectors with non-Jewish bandits and say concerning both that owners do not despair. This remains difficult. One cannot answer that specifically where the customs collector took one garment and returned another, the recipient may keep it because the owner despaired combined with the fact that the recipient did not know with certainty that this particular replacement was stolen, whereas in the rescue case the property is known to have been stolen, for example where the robbery occurred publicly. Rambam and the Mechaber do not say that the owner did not know "that there had been a robbery"; they say that the recipient does not know with certainty that "this item is stolen." That phrase explains why the person who received the substitute from the customs collector may keep it: the original owner despaired, and the recipient received it permissibly because he did not know at the time that this particular item was stolen. Kesef Mishneh explains it similarly. I offered another explanation in the Derishah and in the Sema to siman 369, because Rambam includes the lack of knowledge for his own reason, whereas the Tur, who follows a different position, omits it. It appears that the contradiction can be resolved by paying attention to two details in Rambam's wording there: first, he writes, "because this is like a sale," before writing that the owners are presumed to have despaired, implying that the despair depends upon the fact that it is like a sale. Second, he says that the owners despaired "from it," specifically from that object. Rambam's view is therefore that an ordinary victim of a non-Jewish bandit or customs collector does not despair, because non-Jewish courts act forcefully and he may hope to recover. This is why one who rescues the property must return it. But when the customs collector takes one garment and gives it to another person in exchange for that person's garment, the transaction resembles a sale. Once the original owner sees that his particular object has already left the customs collector's possession and passed to another, he despairs specifically of recovering that object and instead intends to sue the customs collector for its monetary value. This is why Rambam says that the owners despaired "from it": they despaired from the object, not from recovering compensation. Rambam also follows his own view that despair together with a change of domain transfers ownership even when the despair occurs after the change of domain. According to the Tur, who follows Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh that despair must precede the change of domain, the contradiction does not arise for the reasons explained above.
Ordinary theft entails despair, etc. The reason is that with theft the victim does not know who stole the property from him and therefore has no specific person to pursue for recovery. He consequently despairs even where the thief is a non-Jew. This differs from robbery by force in the victim's presence.
