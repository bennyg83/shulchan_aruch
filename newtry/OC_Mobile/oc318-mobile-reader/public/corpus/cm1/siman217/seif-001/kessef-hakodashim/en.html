Seif 4. One who sells his fellow a right of passage. The holder of a document has the weaker hand. If a deed reserves only a right of foot passage, it does not include transporting a load by animal, perhaps not even by one animal, and possibly a width of one cubit suffices because that is approximately a person's bodily width. If the deed grants passage throughout the courtyard, the right may extend across its width, but still not necessarily with a loaded animal. If it explicitly mentions a load, that likely refers to transport by animal, because otherwise there was no need to specify a load, though perhaps only one animal is included. This is especially so where the grantor reserved the right for himself, since a person who reserves rights generally does so generously. Since a load may project wider than the bearer, the wording may perhaps include the ordinary width required for carrying a load, though still not necessarily an animal. If the deed says passage with an animal, it may still not include a wagon. If it grants foot passage and a cow for milking, since one ordinarily walks beside the cow while milking arrangements are made, enough width must be allowed for a person and the cow at that time. A right of passage for a loaded wagon requires more than the wagon's width, because a person must be able to walk beside it and the load may project beyond it; the width should follow ordinary practical usage. If a deed grants a right of passage in an entry hall between gates but does not say 'from gate to gate,' the grantor may perhaps say that he granted passage only within the hall, and outside it the grantee may use whichever one gate the grantor chooses for entry and exit, because the holder of the deed has the weaker hand. Even if one opening is nearer the grantee, the grantor might permit only the farther one. If a deed states that an entry hall between two houses, one sold to the purchaser, is to be used equally by both parties, and then adds that the purchaser may also place a cupboard beside his wall, the purchaser cannot argue that only he may place one and the seller may not. Equal use remains the governing rule. The additional clause was necessary because without it one might have thought that no one could place a permanent object that reduces the other's use. Once the purchaser is expressly allowed to do so, the seller who retained his rights is certainly no worse, especially because a person who reserves rights does so generously. The word 'also' likewise indicates that the right to place the cupboard follows the same equal-use arrangement. In any event, doubt leaves the original owner in possession.
