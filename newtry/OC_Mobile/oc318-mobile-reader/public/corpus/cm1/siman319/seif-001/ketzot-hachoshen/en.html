Or he deceived him. The Gemara tells of a man who obtained storage from a woman only after betrothing her, then divorced her, and she expelled his wine. This worked because her initial refusal and later consent after betrothal created a complete disclosure that she consented only because of the relationship. Nimukei Yosef says that if she had rented to him unconditionally before the relationship, later hostility would not permit eviction. Rama rules that if a landlord says he rents only because he loves the tenant, later hatred permits eviction. I find this difficult. A statement of motive is not a formal condition, and Maharik shows that language of love and peace does not automatically create one. The exceptional storage case rested on prior refusal and therefore on clear disclosure of intent. One might compare Rosh's rule that an express statement accompanying a sale of land because the seller intends to relocate can matter without full condition law. But Rosh's reason is that people do not ordinarily sell livelihood land. House rental is ordinary commercial activity and resembles sale of movables, where motive alone does not suffice. Therefore, during a fixed term, mere words that the rental was because of friendship should not easily permit eviction. Tur's formulation, where the owner first refused and was then deceived, is stronger and clearer.

Some say he must notify him. Beit Yosef understands Rambam to require no notice as a matter of strict law, only as a measure of piety. Sema and Bach assume some notice is legally required. Shitah Mekubetzet, however, expressly says the woman removed the wine immediately without any notice because the deceiver acted wrongfully. Raavad and Ritva are cited similarly. The wording indicates no notice at all was required. This appears to be Rambam's view, with notice only a matter of piety.
