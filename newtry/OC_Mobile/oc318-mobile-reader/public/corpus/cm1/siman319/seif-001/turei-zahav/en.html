(Seif 1: Without his knowledge) The Sema describes this as an addition of the Rambam, but it appears to me to be an explicit Talmudic rule. Bava Kamma teaches that one whose courtyard has been filled with another's wine jars may break them to enter and leave. Although the Gemara initially limits this to one breaking them to bring out his rights, that limitation is needed only according to the view that a person may not execute judgment for himself. Since we rule that he may, the baraita stands in its simple sense. The Rambam, however, speaks where the owner needs the place occupied by the produce, paralleling the courtyard filled with jars. If he does not need the place, this is a case where one benefits and the other loses nothing. Still, if the owner expressly informs the depositor that he does not agree to provide free storage, the law is with him. Thus, when he needs the place, no notice is required. Nor is notice required in the second case, where the other person deceived him, because the deceiver acted improperly. This is supported by the incident in HaShoel where a man deceived a woman into storing his wine, then divorced her, and she removed the wine to the marketplace and paid the removal expense from the wine itself. Rav Asi justified her conduct by “his recompense shall return upon his head.” No prior notice is mentioned; indeed, had she notified him, the verse would have been unnecessary, since no person can be compelled to provide storage against his will after notice. The incident must therefore involve no notice and no need for the space, and the exemption rested on his misconduct. When the owner does not need the space and the depositor did not act deceitfully, the goods may not be removed until notice is given.
