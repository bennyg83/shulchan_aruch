Into another person's house without his knowledge, etc. This is Rambam's language. He equates bringing goods into the owner's house without permission with deceiving the owner. Rabbeinu Yeruham writes that some disagree and hold that this is true only of a courtyard that is not ordinarily rented, because a person may enforce his own rights. But in a courtyard ordinarily rented out, the rule differs. In the Derishah I wrote that the Rosh and Tur imply that even in a courtyard not ordinarily rented, although the owner may enforce his right and remove the goods from his house, he may not sell part of the merchandise in order to hire workers with the proceeds to remove it, nor may he cast it into an ownerless place where it will be lost. Only where the owner of the goods deceived the householder and acted improperly do we say that as he acted improperly, so may one deal with him in an exceptional manner. See there.
And some say that he must notify him, etc. From Rama's presentation of this as "some say," it appears that he understood these authorities to dispute the view of the Mechaber, which is Rambam's. According to the first view, notifying the owner of the goods is merely an act of piety; if the householder chooses not to act piously, he is exempt even if he does not notify him and the goods are lost. The second view holds that he must notify him, and only after notification is he exempt for subsequent loss; if he does not notify him, he remains liable even for accidents. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe explicitly explain it this way. In the Derishah, however, I proved that there is no dispute. Rambam teaches that as an act of piety the householder should not throw the goods from his house into an ownerless marketplace. Rather, he should notify beit din, and beit din should hire workers to place the goods in a guarded location. He need not notify the owner of the goods as an act of piety, because if he did, the owner would certainly come into his house to remove them, and the householder does not want him entering his house at all. If the householder does not wish to act with this piety and instead wants to take vengeance by removing the goods and placing them in the street, he nevertheless must notify the owner of the goods to come and collect them from the street, because at that point the owner need not enter his house, only the public place where the goods lie. If he fails to notify him, the householder is liable even for an accidental loss according to Rambam. This, in my view, is also the position of the "some say," namely the Rosh and Tur, who write that he must notify him, meaning that he must inform him to come and collect his goods from the street. Thus there is no dispute at all, and this is clear in my eyes. Rama's wording can perhaps be reconciled with difficulty by saying that he inferred from Rambam and the Mechaber's phrase, "they rent a place with part of their value because of the mitzvah of returning lost property," that the notification to beit din occurs only after the goods have already been removed, so that beit din then rents a place to store them. If the notification occurred before removal, Rambam should have said that they hire workers to remove them elsewhere. According to this reading the second view disagrees and requires notification before removal. Examine this carefully.
