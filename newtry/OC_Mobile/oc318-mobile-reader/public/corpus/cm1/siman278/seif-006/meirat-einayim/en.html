Rather, he assesses that increase in money, etc. Meaning, the firstborn takes two portions in the property their father left, while in the increase he takes only one portion like an ordinary son. The value of the additional portion is appraised in money and divided among them.
Unripe grain that became stalks, etc. The Gemara says that the dispute concerns fodder that became stalks and date blossoms that became dates. Rashbam explains: their father left cut green fodder that later developed into stalks, or date palms whose fruit had begun to blossom and later developed into dates. Rabbi holds that the firstborn takes double because the increase occurred by itself. The Sages hold that he does not take double because the item acquired a new name. The halacha follows the Sages.
Upon which silt accumulated. Ran explains this as a field that had not been fertilized, but a river carried straw and stubble from elsewhere over it, creating fertilizer and improving the field.
If it came through expenditure. Meaning, the expenses were taken from the undivided estate.
If they did not make a change in them, etc. The Tur writes: if they did not change them, such as where there were grapes and they harvested them. Meaning, they merely cut the grapes from where they were attached; the grapes themselves did not change.
If they did make a change. Meaning, there were grapes and they pressed them into wine. The ordinary brothers acquire the increase through the change, just as robbers acquire through a physical change and pay according to the value at the time of robbery. The firstborn therefore does not take a double share of the increase beyond what existed at the time he protested against their action.
And not in the loss. Meaning, if the wine spoiled after pressing. The Tur adds that even if they made no change, such as where attached grapes were cut and later spoiled, the firstborn does not bear a double portion of the loss. See there.
