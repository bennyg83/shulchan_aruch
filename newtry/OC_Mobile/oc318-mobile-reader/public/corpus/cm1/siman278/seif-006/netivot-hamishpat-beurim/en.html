If they changed them. See Sema, seif katan 15, who explains the rule through acquisition by change and payment according to the value at the time of the wrongful taking. Why is this law stated specifically regarding a firstborn? The same principle applies between ordinary heirs if one intended to steal from another. The novelty is that here the heirs did not intend to steal the body of the firstborn's property. They merely ignored his protest and sought to preserve the pre-protest law under which the firstborn takes the appreciation but must reimburse the cost of improvement. They expected him to pay them the value of the wine's improvement while the body of the wine itself would remain his. Since they did not intend to acquire the body of the property, they cannot say, "our property appreciated." Nevertheless, because there was a change in the improvement itself, the enactment encouraging repentance can allow them to retain that appreciation. This resembles one who forcibly takes another's cow and performs labor with it without intending to acquire the cow itself. Shitah Mekubetzet explains such a case, and siman 363, Sema seif katan 13, shows that the wrongdoer need not return the labor value because of the enactment encouraging repentance. This also resolves Ketzot HaChoshen's question on Tosafot in Bava Batra 126. Tosafot challenge Rashi's explanation based on change, asking from the view that change remains in place. Ketzot objects that Shmuel and Rav Yosef themselves hold change acquires. But Tosafot's question is not from the dispute about a full robber who changes the body of the stolen item. In full robbery, Shmuel and Rav Yosef indeed hold that change acquires, and Tosafot elsewhere say there is effectively no operative view to the contrary. Their question is from Bava Kamma 95 concerning one who steals a sheep and shears it, where the body of the sheep was not acquired by the shearing and the robber cannot say "my property appreciated." Yet R. Yehudah holds he need not return the wool because the appreciation itself changed and the enactment encouraging repentance applies, while others require return of the wool. That is precisely parallel to our case, where the heirs did not intend to acquire the body of the property. Therefore Tosafot ask correctly that according to the opinion requiring return of the changed appreciation in the wool case, the heirs here should likewise have to return the appreciation. This is the correct explanation.
And not in the loss. See Sema, seif katan 16, who says that even without a formal change, such as where attached grapes were cut and then spoiled, the firstborn does not share in the loss. This is difficult. Siman 363 says that even a full robber need not pay for fruit that partially rotted. It is strained to say the case is only where the grapes rotted completely and thereby underwent a formal change. More fundamentally, if the grapes had reached the time for harvest and the other heirs did not cut them in order to steal the grapes themselves, but only intended to receive compensation for the improvement while the firstborn would receive the body, they were permitted to harvest as partners. Why should they become robbers merely because they harvested? In my view the case is where they cut the grapes before the proper harvest time and thereby caused the spoilage. If the wine spoiled only after they pressed the grapes, the primary rule follows Ran cited by Beit Yosef: they pay the value of the grapes after harvest, because at harvest the firstborn already acquired his relevant share. Perishah's statement that the robbery began at the start of harvest is difficult. This resembles the rule in Bava Kamma 98 concerning one who breaks or consumes after a prior wrongful act: the later damaging act is the operative loss.
