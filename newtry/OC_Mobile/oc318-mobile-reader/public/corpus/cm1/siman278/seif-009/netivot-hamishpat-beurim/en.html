To what is this comparable. Ketzot HaChoshen asks: if the firstborn said he would not take the grapes as a firstborn, he waived his share in the grapes. Once the brothers therefore owned the grapes equally, why should he later not divide the wine equally as well? There is no difficulty. The case is not one where he said he would not take his firstborn share in the grapes. Rather, he told witnesses that even if he happened to divide equally, he did not intend that division as waiver of the firstborn share. As long as he had not actually divided the grapes equally, there was no waiver. Therefore, if the grapes later became wine, the earlier statement still does not establish waiver. Even if the firstborn had not yet protested by saying, "Do not improve the property," so that under the law he does not receive the wine's appreciation without compensating the others, this still does not force him to divide entirely equally. He might have said he was willing to divide the grapes equally only because the grapes themselves were not significant in his eyes. But once they became wine, if he fails to take a double share even in the original grape-value component, that conduct can reveal that he does not wish to claim the firstborn portion at all, unless he protests.
