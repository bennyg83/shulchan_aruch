But if they owe, etc. See Responsa Kehillat Yaakov, siman 5, concerning one who died leaving a firstborn and an ordinary son, and left a fortified house pledged to a nobleman who had leased several villages from the crown, the house serving as security for payment of the installments at their proper times. The question was whether the firstborn takes a double portion in this house, since it is encumbered to the sovereign. He explains that the doubt arises from Bava Batra 55, concerning collectors who sell land for unpaid royal tax, from which some authorities inferred that where royal tax is owed the firstborn does not take a double portion. But the present case is different. There the law of the sovereign permitted the land itself to be taken outright and sold permanently, without appraisal limited to the amount owed. Rashba and Nimukei Yosef make this clear, and Tosafos add that in that system the land was fundamentally regarded as belonging to the sovereign. Darchei Moshe and Sema below, siman 369, seif katan 14 and 15, say similarly. Here, by contrast, the house remains fully possessed property of the owner and the firstborn takes a double portion. This is obvious if current law permits collection only from the annual produce or rent, or permits redemption when the owner can pay. Even if the law allows the property eventually to be forfeited entirely, so long as present royal law requires exact appraisal and collection only according to the amount of the debt, the encumbrance is merely ordinary debt collection. Until the due date arrives and collection occurs, the property remains in the owner's domain, in accordance with the rule that a creditor acquires only prospectively upon collection. He therefore concludes that where the house fundamentally belongs to its owner and is encumbered only up to the amount of the debt and by appraisal, it is certainly possessed property and the firstborn takes a double portion.
