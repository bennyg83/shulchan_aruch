Although the halachah does not follow Sumchus but the Sages, who hold that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, we nevertheless find several matters in which the Sages ruled that in a case of doubt the parties divide the property. So write Tosafot there. See Responsa Maharashdam, siman 375, and Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 66.
His loan. Although at the time of the loan a creditor does not acquire the pledge, nevertheless, since he acquires a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan, a pledge received at the time of the loan is also regarded as collected and presently in his possession, rather than as property merely expected to come to him. The Levush explains that it is considered in his possession because he acquired the pledge, but this is incorrect, since he does not acquire a pledge taken at the time of the loan. So writes the Sema. This is not a valid objection, however, because this is the language of the Rambam and the Mordechai as cited by Beit Yosef, and the Gemara in the chapter Kol Shaah likewise uses this language. Tosafot there already explain that it means that, since a creditor acquires a pledge taken other than at the time of the loan, he is considered to hold the property even when the pledge was taken at the time of the loan. See there. It appears that even according to what I concluded in siman 72, seif 2, that a creditor acquires a pledge only when it was taken other than at the time of the loan, whereas a pledge taken at the time of the loan is not acquired by him at all, nevertheless the law here is correct. For even at the time of the loan he acquires the pledge at least insofar as he has a lien upon it. Thus his lien is in his hand, and for purposes of the firstborn it is deemed property in his possession rather than property merely expected to come to him, even though he did not acquire the pledge completely. Proof is that the Tur writes in the name of the Raavad that even a mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem the land is considered in the creditor's possession when he collects from the land itself. It is obvious that with a mortgage of land the creditor does not acquire the land itself but merely has a lien upon it, as is evident in the chapters HaSholeach and Kol Shaah and elsewhere. Necessarily, therefore, for purposes of the firstborn, whenever the lien is already in his hand the property is not considered merely prospective. This also explains the poskim who give as the reason that a creditor acquires a pledge: they mean that he acquires it for purposes of his lien, not as complete ownership. See below, siman 281, seif 7, at the end of the gloss, and Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 109. Shach.
A non-Jew. The Shach writes that this ruling requires study. Maharik derived it from the Mordechai concerning a gift of a person on his deathbed, but there there is a different reason, namely that the donor does not rely upon collection. Regarding a firstborn, however, even where only the lien is in the father's hands, the property is considered possessed, as stated immediately above. If so, even with a pledge belonging to a non-Jew, the lien is in his hand and should be considered possessed. Although Hagahot Ashri in the chapter Eizehu Neshech likewise writes as Maharik does, one must say that he does not accept the Raavad's view cited above regarding a mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem the land. According to the Raavad, however, this is certainly true with a pledge belonging to a non-Jew. The matter requires study. Nevertheless, if the Jew accepted liability for the pledge, concerning which the Rosh writes in the chapter Kol Shaah that he violates the prohibition of possessing chametz, it appears that even Maharik agrees that it is considered possessed. The present-day law concerning ordinary pledges of non-Jews also requires study, since under their law, if the pledge is lost by unavoidable accident or burned, the debtor is exempt, while the lender is merely not required to pay him the amount by which the pledge exceeds the debt. See Responsa Divrei Rivot, simanim 78 and 178.
