Even if he took the collateral at the time of the loan. Although a creditor does not acquire collateral taken at the time of the loan, since he does acquire collateral taken after the loan, collateral taken at the time of the loan is likewise regarded as though already collected and in his possession. The Levush explains that it is deemed possessed because the creditor acquires the collateral. The Sema objects that this cannot be correct, since a creditor does not acquire collateral taken at the time of the loan. This is not a valid objection. The Levush's language follows Rashbam and the Mordechai, as cited by the Beit Yosef, and it also follows the wording of the Gemara in Perek Kol Shaah. Tosafot there already explain that because the creditor acquires collateral when taken after the loan, the collateral is deemed possessed even when taken at the time of the loan. Even according to what I concluded above, siman 72, seif 2, seif katan 9, that a creditor acquires collateral only when taken after the loan, whereas collateral taken at the time of the loan is not acquired by him at all for any purpose, and one therefore cannot argue that because he acquires it after the loan he also acquires it to some extent at the time of the loan, nevertheless the law here is correct. Since even collateral taken at the time of the loan is acquired at least to the extent that the creditor has a lien upon it, his lien is in his possession, and it is classified as possessed rather than prospective. With respect to the firstborn's double share, whenever the lien is in the creditor's possession, even though he does not fully own the property, it is regarded as possessed rather than prospective. Proof is that the Tur cites the Raavad's view that even a land mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem it is regarded as possessed when the creditor collects from the land itself. Yet it is obvious that a creditor does not acquire land as collateral, and has only a lien upon it, as is evident in Perek HaSholeach, Gittin 37a, Perek Kol Shaah, and many other passages. Necessarily, then, regarding the firstborn, wherever the creditor's lien is in his possession it is not classified as prospective. This also explains the language of Rashbam, the Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot, the Maggid Mishna, the Levush, and the other decisors, who give as the reason that a creditor acquires collateral. They do not mean full ownership, but that he acquires it for purposes of his lien. Examine this carefully. See Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 109, and below, siman 281, seif 7, at the end of the gloss.
Some say that this applies only, etc. This ruling requires study. Maharik derives it from the Mordechai in Perek Mi Shemet regarding a gift by a deathly ill person, where a creditor does not acquire collateral from a gentile. This is no proof. Although a creditor does not acquire a gentile's collateral, as explicitly stated in Perek Kol Shaah and explained in Even HaEzer, siman 28, seif 12, in the gloss, that means only that the collateral does not become his property. It therefore remains merely a loan, and with respect to a gift by a deathly ill person the donor does not rely upon it. Regarding a firstborn, however, even where the collateral is not owned by the creditor, if the lien is in his possession it is classified as possessed, as I wrote above. Thus, even collateral belonging to a gentile should be regarded as possessed because the lien is in the creditor's possession. I did find in Hagahot Ashri, Perek Eizehu Neshekh, concerning a land mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem it, that a firstborn does not take a double share from it. He writes: 'From here there is proof that a firstborn does not take a double share from collateral belonging to gentiles. Since the creditor does not acquire such collateral even when it was taken after the loan, it is inferior to collateral taken from a Jew at the time of the loan, as is evident in Perek Kol Shaah according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak. Although one could argue that regarding a firstborn we require only that the property be in the creditor's possession, and he is indeed in possession of the gentile's collateral, it is comparable to a land mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem it, from which the firstborn does not take a double share. Even though the creditor's possession is stronger there, since he does not acquire it the firstborn does not take. Likewise, gentile collateral may be redeemed whenever the debtor chooses,' end quote from Mahari Chayim. These are indeed the words of Maharik. Necessarily, however, Hagahot Ashri does not accept the view of the Raavad and Tur cited above. According to their view, even a land mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem it yields a double share to the firstborn when the creditor collects from the land itself. The same therefore applies to collateral belonging to a gentile. Even regarding the gift of a deathly ill person there is room to disagree with the Mordechai, as the Beit Yosef writes in siman 253, and as I wrote there in seif 20. The matter requires study. If the Jew accepted responsibility for the collateral, however, so that the Rosh in Perek Kol Shaah rules that the Jew violates the prohibition concerning it, it appears that even Maharik agrees that it is classified as possessed. The status of ordinary gentile collateral today also requires study. Under their law, if the collateral is lost or burned, the debtor is exempt, but the creditor need not pay the debtor the excess value of the collateral above the debt. It is therefore uncertain whether the creditor is deemed to have accepted responsibility. See Responsa Divrei Rivot, simanim 78 and 178.
