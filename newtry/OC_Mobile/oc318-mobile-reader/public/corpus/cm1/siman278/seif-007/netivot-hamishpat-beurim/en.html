With a loan. If it is an iska document, Responsa Divrei Rivot, siman 78, states that it is nevertheless considered property merely due to come into the estate, even though the recipient is forbidden to spend the money except for the business. His reasoning is sound, because collection is still required and these are not the actual coins that the father left, as stated in the chapter Yesh Nochalin.
A gentile's pledge. See Shach, seif katan 8, who questions the ruling and concludes that if the Jew accepted responsibility for the pledge, in which case the Rosh writes that the Jew violates the prohibition concerning chametz, it is considered property in his possession. At first glance this is very difficult. What does chametz have to do with the present issue? Even with a deposit for which one accepted responsibility, he violates the prohibition because Scripture treats an object that can cause monetary liability as property for this particular law, although throughout the rest of Torah it is not treated as his property. How does that prove that he is considered presently possessed for inheritance? It appears that Shach's proof is different. With an ordinary deposit for which the Jew accepted responsibility, although he violates the prohibition against possessing chametz, the chametz is nevertheless not forbidden for benefit after Pesach, as stated in the Yerushalmi cited by Chok Yaakov, siman 440, because it remains the gentile's chametz and only the Jew committed the violation. By contrast, a pledge for which he accepted responsibility is forbidden for benefit, as is clear from the Gemara's discussion of a gentile who lent money. We must therefore say that acceptance of responsibility for a pledge operates because the gentile transferred the pledge to the Jew as security for the debt, so that if the pledge is lost the lien is lost. It is consequently called the Jew's chametz, and the penalty imposed upon a Jew's chametz applies. Since this shows that he acquires the pledge for purposes of his lien to the point that it is called the Jew's chametz, Shach infers that it is also considered property presently possessed. In my humble opinion, however, the Rama is correct that a pledge of this kind is not considered presently possessed. Shach brings proof from a mortgage in a place where the debtor may redeem it, where if the creditor ultimately collects the land itself it is treated as presently possessed, while if he collects money it is treated as property merely due, as the Tur explains. One might ask that once the land is considered presently possessed when collected, then if it is later redeemed with money it should be treated as though the heir had sold it back, as we say in a place where redemption during the term is not permitted. It therefore appears that the primary principle is what I wrote above in siman 72. Even where a creditor does not formally acquire a pledge, he has a property right sufficient for the rule that collection works retroactively, as Rashi writes in Pesachim 30 concerning the Mishnah of a gentile who lent money. Where actual collection is still lacking, retroactive collection does not apply. Therefore, if he ultimately collects this very land, it becomes his retroactively and is treated as presently possessed, whereas if he collects money or another property it is not. According to this, with a gentile's pledge, according to the view that a Jew does not acquire a pledge from a gentile, he necessarily does not acquire it even for purposes of retroactive collection. Otherwise he would violate the chametz prohibition for this very reason, as Abaye holds. Since collection is not retroactive, there is no distinction between collecting from this field and from another. The proof from chametz likewise fails according to what I wrote in my work Mekor Chaim. There I explained that an ordinary deposit is different because the Jew can tell the gentile, "here is your property before you," so prohibiting the chametz creates no monetary penalty to the Jew, and therefore the Sages did not penalize him. With a pledge, however, the gentile is not obligated to compensate the Jew when the chametz becomes forbidden through the Jew's conduct. Forbidding it therefore does penalize the Jew by destroying his lien, so the Sages imposed the penalty.
In a place where the debtor cannot redeem during the term. See the Tur, who states that even in a place where redemption is permitted, if the creditor ultimately collects the land itself it is considered presently possessed. Only if he collects from its fruits by deducting them from the debt, or collects from elsewhere, is it considered property merely due, for the reason I wrote immediately above. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that if the firstborn himself owes his father on a mortgage in a place where redemption is allowed, the firstborn may satisfy the debt with the land itself even if he has money, since he would otherwise suffer a loss, as explained in Ketubot 95 concerning Reuven who sold a field to Shimon and converted the purchase price into a loan. In my humble opinion, here even if the firstborn lacks cash he cannot satisfy the debt with the land until he makes the effort and brings money. Siman 117 explains that an unspecified mortgage has the status of an unspecified apotiki, and with an apotiki the debtor cannot remove the creditor except with money. Since the fruits of the field were designated to the creditor as an apotiki, the debtor cannot stop the creditor from consuming them and deducting their value until he brings money. Bava Metzia likewise states that even if he says, "I will make the effort and bring money," the creditor continues to consume. Thus he must discharge the mortgage either with money or through the creditor's consumption of the fruits, and its law is like an ordinary loan concerning which the authorities disagree.
Provided that the due date has not yet arrived. The Tur cites a dispute between Ri Migash and Rashba. In my humble opinion, each addresses a different case and they do not disagree. Ri Migash speaks where the creditor collects from the fruits of the land. In a place where redemption is not allowed during the term, he may collect even from the fruits. Once the due date has arrived, however, the field is no longer treated as sold to him for its fruits. Since the fruits did not exist at the father's death and are not certain to come, for the mortgagor may redeem them, they are property merely due. Rashba speaks where the creditor collects the debt from the body of the land itself or receives money. Rashba's reason is that money received upon redemption is considered presently possessed because redemption is treated as the debtor repurchasing the land from the creditor. If so, what difference does it make whether redemption occurs within the term or after the term? The field remains transferred to the creditor until the debtor actually redeems it. The mere expiration of the term does not terminate the transfer where no money was paid. It is therefore fully considered property presently possessed.
