Although it was evidenced by a document. Bava Batra says that where the heirs inherited a debt instrument, Rabbi treats the debt as sufficiently held for the firstborn to receive a double portion. Rashbam explains that since the document is in hand and collection proceeds through it, the later collection resembles automatic appreciation of existing estate property. Tosafot asks that even according to the Sages the firstborn should simply take a double share of the documents themselves, and then whatever is later collected on his documents would naturally belong to him. This can be answered according to Rashba, who holds that debt instruments are not divided as tangible property because their bodies are not money. Therefore, before collection the entire matter remains in the category of pre-division appreciation. Rabbi includes such appreciation, while the Sages do not. Rashbam nevertheless limits Rabbi to a documented debt, because an oral loan can be denied by a plea of repayment and lacks a present document in the heirs' hands. A difficulty remains from Bava Kamma, where Rabbi appears to treat even an oral monetary claim as held property for firstborn purposes. The passages therefore require reconciliation.
The father had a loan owed by the firstborn. Ramban's responsum explains that even according to the view that a husband does not inherit his wife's ordinary outstanding loan because it is expectant property, a loan she held against the husband himself is divided differently. The reason is the same doubt found concerning a firstborn who owes money to the estate. We are uncertain whether a debt already held by the heir or husband himself is considered presently held by the deceased. Therefore the husband receives half of that loan, and the firstborn likewise receives half of the additional firstborn portion in a loan that he himself owes.
A pledge from a gentile is not considered held property. A Jew does not acquire ownership of a gentile's pledge merely by holding it. Shach suggested that if the Jew accepted responsibility for the pledge, perhaps it should be considered held property, analogizing to chametz for which responsibility can create a prohibition. That analogy is incorrect. Chametz is exceptional because the Torah treats something that causes monetary liability as though it were one's property for that prohibition. For ordinary acquisition and firstborn inheritance, responsibility alone does not create ownership. Since a Jew does not acquire the body of a gentile's pledge, the firstborn does not take a double portion in it even if he bears responsibility for loss.
A land mortgage in a place where the debtor cannot remove the creditor. Tur cites Raavad that even in a place where the debtor can redeem, if the creditor may collect from the land itself the mortgage can be considered held property. It follows that if the firstborn himself owes his father on such a land mortgage, he may discharge his brothers with land rather than money so that his own double portion remains intact. Even where a debtor who has cash ordinarily must pay cash, one may sometimes insist on land when otherwise he will suffer a loss. The same principle applies here even though the firstborn's loss is technically a lost gain rather than an out-of-pocket loss.
