If he does not wish to take the double share. See Ketzot HaChoshen, who cites a dispute whether a firstborn can renounce his firstborn share during his father's lifetime. He questions why, according to the view that an ordinary heir cannot renounce his future inheritance because it is a Torah right, a firstborn who can renounce before division should not likewise be able to do so during his father's lifetime. According to all the explanations, he argues, there should be no distinction between renunciation after the father's death and during his lifetime. In my humble opinion, however, this is not so. According to Rashbam in Bava Batra 126, who holds that a firstborn has no present right before division and explains that his failure to waive is because one cannot waive something not yet in existence, then all the more so during the father's lifetime, when the firstborn certainly has no right at all, he cannot renounce it. The reason he can renounce after the father's death, notwithstanding that renunciation is generally ineffective against a Torah entitlement, is that the firstborn portion is an obligation imposed upon the other heirs. The Torah obligates them to give this gift to the firstborn. Proof is siman 277, seif 11, concerning a case of uncertainty, where collection cannot be made without authorization. This is explicit in Bava Batra 127. Without authorization, the heirs can defer each claimant. Why can they do so? The disputed share certainly does not belong to the other heirs, and the only uncertainty is which of the two claimants owns it. One would expect the dispute to be resolved between them, either by division or by holding the property in abeyance. What interest do the other heirs have in the share? It must be that the Torah placed upon the heirs an obligation to give the firstborn portion, so that they are like defendants who say, "I do not know whether I became obligated to you," and therefore can defer each claimant. Accordingly, after the father's death, when the obligation has already arisen, the firstborn can waive it. During the father's lifetime, or according to the view that a firstborn has no right before division, meaning the obligation itself does not arise until he seeks division, he cannot waive it because this would be waiver of something not yet in existence. Ketzot brings proof from Rashbam in Bava Batra 126, who says that here the son did not waive, for what benefit would he have in waiving, and even though he remained silent this is not waiver because he did not want to anger his father. Ketzot infers that an actual waiver would work. In my humble opinion there is no proof. The firstborn can waive against the father because the Torah gave the father power to distribute his estate as he wishes, except that it imposed upon him an obligation toward the firstborn not to take away the firstborn share. The firstborn can therefore waive the obligation that the Torah imposed upon the father toward him. He cannot, however, waive in favor of the other sons during the father's lifetime for the reason stated above. Only after death can he do so.
Even if his books were stolen. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this because Mordechai said only that stolen books are not considered property merely due with respect to collection of a ketubah, where even a loan is not treated as property merely due in the same manner. Regarding the firstborn share, by contrast, even a loan is property merely due, so stolen property should likewise be considered merely due because it is not in the owner's possession. It appears that Rama understood Mordechai's statement that such property is not considered merely due as a general rule applying in all areas. Mordechai's statement that stolen books are no worse than a loan was only an additional reason. The underlying reasoning seems to be that the Gemara explains a loan is merely due because these are not the very coins that the father left. With stolen property, however, this is precisely the property the father left. It is comparable to a lost object recovered after the father's death, which also appears not to be considered merely due even though it was outside his possession. The same should apply to other movable property where it is known that the owners did not despair. Ketzot's distinction that books are ordinarily sought after is not found in the authorities. Books too may have been stolen by a Jew, and when in a Jew's possession they are like any other stolen movable property.
It is considered presently possessed. Ketzot HaChoshen seeks to derive from the law of regular priestly recipients, where the gift is treated as presently possessed because the owner may not retract from his promise, which is like a small gift, that the same should apply whenever a person swore to give a gift. On that reasoning, every small promised gift should be considered presently possessed by the recipient. In my humble opinion, the reason is unique to priestly gifts. The gifts fundamentally belong to the kohen, and if a regular recipient kohen seized them, he would not have to return them, as Tosafot explain in the discussion of a kohen who seized disputed gifts. The reason is that priestly gifts are the kohen's property, while the owner possesses only the benefit of choosing the recipient by Torah decree. Once it becomes prohibited for the owner to retract, the benefit of choosing the recipient that the Torah gave him disappears, because the Torah did not grant that benefit in a circumstance where exercising it would violate a prohibition. Since the owner cannot retract and the recipient can seize the gift against the owner's will, and if he does so it becomes fully his for kiddushin and all other purposes, he is regarded as presently possessed. By contrast, with an oath or any other small promised gift, even if the recipient seizes the object it does not become his until the owner transfers it through a valid kinyan. So long as no kinyan has occurred, it is not his and he is not presently possessed. Mahari Weil intended these points in responsum 74; see there.
