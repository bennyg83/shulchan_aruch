That if he wishes to retract, etc. The Tur cites the Raavad: Even regarding an ordinary inheritance share, if an heir says, "I do not want the inheritance and will not pay the debts," he is not compelled to take it. The distinction concerns later retraction. With an ordinary share, if he says, "I will not inherit and will not pay," and his brothers then pay the creditor with money, he may later say, "Give me my share and I will reimburse you the money," because when the father died he immediately acquired the ordinary share. His statement, "I will not pay," was neither a sale, gift, nor valid abandonment. But with the firstborn share, he has not yet acquired until he accepts it, because Scripture calls it a gift. Therefore, once he says, "I do not wish to take it," he cannot later retract. If the brothers paid the creditor with inherited land rather than money, the result differs because an appraisal made by the debtor himself to a creditor does not automatically return, as explained in siman 103. The Mechaber cites only the Raavad's final point, that after the firstborn says he does not wish to take the firstborn share he cannot retract. He does not cite the Raavad's opening view that an ordinary heir may also initially remove himself from his ordinary share, because the Mechaber prefers the opinion of Ri Halevi that an ordinary son stands automatically in his father's place and cannot disclaim the inheritance in that way.

But in a place where the king does not sell land for taxes and merely leases the land for three or four years until the tax is paid, if the landowner dies during that period the firstborn takes a double portion. This is Rama's meaning when he says, "but in a place where the practice is not, etc."
