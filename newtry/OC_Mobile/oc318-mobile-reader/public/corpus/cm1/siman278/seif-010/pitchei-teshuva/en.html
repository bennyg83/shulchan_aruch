But if they owe, etc. Responsa Kehillat Yaakov 5 discusses one who died leaving a firstborn and an ordinary son, and left a walled house pledged as security for a nobleman who leased several villages from the imperial government. The house stood as collateral for payment of scheduled installments. The question was whether the firstborn receives a double portion in the house because it is pledged to the crown. The potential doubt arises from Bava Batra 55, where land sold by tax collectors for unpaid royal tax is treated as a valid sale, and some authorities infer that property subject to royal taxation is not considered presently held for the firstborn. Kehillat Yaakov distinguishes the cases. There the sovereign's law allowed the land itself to be taken and sold permanently, without limiting collection to the amount of the debt, and some authorities additionally treat the land as fundamentally belonging to the king. Here, by contrast, the house remains fully owned by the deceased. If contemporary law permits the government to collect only fruits or rent, or allows redemption when funds become available, it is plainly presently held property. Even if the law permits eventual forfeiture, since collection is made by exact appraisal according to the amount due, the pledge is merely ordinary security for a debt. Until the due date arrives and collection occurs, the property remains in the owner's domain, consistent with the rule that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward. He therefore concludes that where the house fundamentally belongs to the owner and is encumbered only up to the debt and by appraisal, it is presently held property and the firstborn takes a double share.
