He is entitled not to pay a double share. Tosafot explains that even if a firstborn has no vested double portion before division, he may say, "I neither take the double portion nor pay the corresponding extra share of my father's debt." They understand this not as a permanent renunciation, but as saying that so long as he has not taken the double portion the creditors cannot compel him regarding it. This creates a question from the rule that a debtor can sometimes be compelled to pursue a claim so that he can satisfy his own creditor. Why is the firstborn not compelled to demand the double portion in order to pay the estate's creditor? The answer depends on the nature of the debt. In Tosafot's case the debt is the father's debt. The firstborn never personally incurred it. He becomes liable only as heir to the property. So long as he refuses the double share, we do not compel him to become heir to that additional property merely in order to incur the corresponding liability. This differs from a debt personally owed by him, where coercion can be appropriate.
If he wishes to retract, he cannot. Raavad holds that once the firstborn says, "I do not take the double portion," he cannot later retract because the Torah calls the firstborn portion a gift, and a recipient can refuse a gift. I was uncertain whether the firstborn can make such a disclaimer during the father's lifetime. An ordinary heir cannot renounce his future Torah inheritance while the ancestor is alive. Tosafot explains that a person generally cannot disclaim a Torah inheritance, while Ran and others distinguish inheritance that already stands ready to come to him from rights that arise only through a later relationship. The firstborn portion is different because Scripture calls it a gift and allows the firstborn to say after the father's death, "I do not want it." This may support an effective disclaimer even during the father's life. Yet the comparison is not exact, because before death the right has not vested at all. The issue depends on whether the Torah's characterization as a gift gives the firstborn a power of advance renunciation. The matter requires study.
Even if books were stolen from him. Darkei Moshe cites Mordechai that if gentiles stole a book and returned it only after the owner's death, it is not treated as expectant property because owners do not ordinarily despair of sacred books. He also cites Nimukei Yosef concerning stolen land, since land cannot be acquired through robbery. In my view Mordechai's ruling concerned collection of a wife's ketubah, not the double portion of a firstborn. Mordechai himself compares the stolen books to an outstanding loan and says that although a loan is expectant property for a firstborn, a wife may still collect her ketubah from it. Thus the source does not prove that stolen books are held property for firstborn inheritance. The distinction is important. A firstborn needs property that was actually held by the father. Movables wrongfully taken from him, even without despair, can remain outside his possession and therefore be expectant for the firstborn. Land is different because land is never legally stolen away from the owner. Accordingly, Rama's extension from Mordechai to firstborn inheritance is difficult.
It is considered held property. I was uncertain about one who swore to give a particular object to another and the intended recipient died before receiving it. Is that object considered held property for the recipient's firstborn heir? The oath does not effect a kinyan, yet the promisor is forbidden to retract. Tosafot states that recognized priestly recipients are considered held because the owner is forbidden to retract from the small gift, even though technically he still could. This suggests all the more that a gift bound by an oath or handshake pledge should be considered held. A difficulty arises from the law of "after you." The first recipient is forbidden to sell away the property, yet if he does sell, the sale is effective, and the later recipient's right is therefore considered expectant rather than held. The difference may be that in an "after you" gift the later recipient has no present right at all. The first recipient still owns the body. By contrast, an oath to give a specific object can create a much stronger present certainty of receipt, though not formal ownership. The question therefore remains nuanced.
