See Noda BiYehuda, siman 34, who answers a question whether government obligations are considered merely anticipated property or property already in possession with respect to the firstborn son's double portion. He writes that there is no room for doubt: they are certainly considered anticipated property. There is no reason to distinguish one debt from another, and the market value of such obligations itself rises and falls over time. Even according to Raavad, who holds that a mortgage can be considered property in possession in a place where the creditor may collect from it, here too, when the debt is ultimately collected in money, Raavad agrees that it is not presently possessed. Thus the firstborn does not take a double portion from it. He then discusses a second question. The deceased himself owed money to creditors. The firstborn wished those debts paid from the obligations, leaving the property already in possession for distribution, while the ordinary sons argued that the debts should be paid from the possessed property and the anticipated property then divided equally. He concludes that the debt should be paid proportionately from both categories. For example, if the estate is worth three hundred gold coins, of which two hundred are possessed property and one hundred is a loan receivable, and the estate owes thirty, ten should be paid from the receivable and twenty from the possessed property. The firstborn then takes a double portion from the remaining one hundred eighty of possessed property, and the balance is divided equally. His reason is that all the father's assets, both possessed assets and debts owed to him, are subject to the creditor. This is explicit in siman 107, seif 1, which states that heirs must also pay their father's debt from money owed to their father by others. This is not dependent upon Rabbi Natan's lien. Even if one rejects Shach's view in siman 86, seif katan 5, and holds that Rabbi Natan's lien applies only when the debtor has no other assets, a creditor may still collect from his debtor's receivables even when other assets exist, because in our times livelihood and commerce depend principally upon debts, and creditors rely upon them. He adduces proof from two responsa of the Rosh. Accordingly, since the creditor's lien rests on the receivables as well, payment must be allocated to them proportionately. He explains that Responsa Darkei Noam, siman 43, is no contradiction, because there the heirs had to mortgage land immediately for burial expenses before any receivables could be collected, and burial obviously could not be delayed until collection. He adds that his ruling applies where the obligations have already become collectible after the father's death and may be used to satisfy the debt, or where the creditor agrees to accept them at their market value. If they are not yet collectible and the creditor refuses to accept them, the matter depends on a major dispute. According to Mechaber and Rama in siman 101, seif 5, the creditor cannot refuse and must accept notes in payment. According to Shach there, seif katan 3, who rules that he cannot be compelled to accept notes, the question requires study. One might say that since the notes would have been subject to collection if no other property existed, they remain proportionately encumbered and the firstborn's share must be completed from them. Conversely, since the creditor cannot be forced to accept them, perhaps the firstborn bears the loss. He leaves this point requiring study. See also Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 53. There the questioner cited Pnei Yehoshua, siman 1, who rules that the entire debt is paid from possessed property, causing the firstborn to lose. Noda BiYehuda responds that although Pnei Yehoshua is a great authority, he does not retract his own position. Pnei Yehoshua's reasoning was that a creditor can reach another debt only through Rabbi Natan's lien, which does not apply when other assets exist. Noda BiYehuda maintains that collection from receivables exists even independently of Rabbi Natan's lien, as he proved from responsa of the Rosh. Therefore his earlier ruling stands. The preceding responsum there discusses the analogous case of a widow's ketubah, where the firstborn wished to pay his mother's ketubah from debts owed by non Jews so that the possessed assets would remain for his double portion, while the ordinary sons wished to pay it from possessed assets. He refers to his earlier ruling. See also Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 149, who likewise rejects Pnei Yehoshua and agrees in result with Noda BiYehuda that payment should be allocated proportionately between anticipated and possessed assets, though for a different reason. He concludes that payment of creditors, a wife's ketubah, and support obligations toward daughters are all paid proportionately from both types of assets. If the firstborn is an adult and a compromise can be reached whereby he waives something for the benefit of minor orphans, that is preferable. See there.
