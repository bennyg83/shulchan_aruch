He left them a rented cow. The baraita in Perek Yesh Nochalin says that a firstborn takes a double portion in a rented or leased cow. The Gemara initially says, "Whose view is this? Rabbi's." Rav Pappa then states that where a palm grew thicker or land produced alluvial deposits, everyone agrees the firstborn takes a double portion, while they dispute where growth transformed, such as flowers becoming grain. Since we rule like the Sages rather than Rabbi, Rambam's ruling like the baraita seems difficult. Sema answers that Rambam reads, "Whose view is this? Rabbi's," as a challenge, and Rav Pappa comes to answer that the baraita can follow even the Sages because a rented cow resembles a palm that merely grew thicker. The enhancement occurred automatically without consuming estate resources. This also answers Tosafot's question. Tosafot says the baraita seems to follow Rabbi, yet elsewhere Rabbi holds that gifts not yet separated are not regarded as already separated. According to Sema's reading, the Gemara was indeed challenging the attribution to Rabbi, and Rav Pappa reestablishes the baraita according to the Sages. Rambam's ruling is therefore understandable. Tosafot's underlying textual issue remains difficult. In Kiddushin, the Gemara discusses one who steals another's untithed produce. Rabbi says he pays the value of all the untithed produce, while Rabbi Yose son of Rabbi Yehudah says he pays only the value of the unconsecrated portion. One proposed explanation is a dispute whether unseparated gifts are treated as already separated. Rashi there appears to say Rabbi holds they are treated as separated, not the opposite. Rashba preserves an alternative textual version but himself rejects it. Nedarim likewise presents an attempted explanation that Rabbi holds unseparated gifts are not as separated, but the Gemara then rejects that explanation. Rosh in Nedarim comments that the exchange is difficult and that the later questioner did not understand the prior answer. Thus there is no simple established source that Rabbi holds the gifts are not as separated. Tosafot's statement remains difficult.
It was grazing in the meadow and gave birth, the firstborn takes a double portion in it. Rashbam explains "takes in it" as taking a double portion in the rental income. Hagahot Ashri likewise reads it as rental and establishes the case where the lease was documented, because otherwise even Rabbi would agree that the firstborn does not take a double portion in an oral loan type claim. Rambam, however, rules like the Sages that a firstborn does not take a double portion even from a documented loan. Therefore his "takes in it" cannot mean rental income. Sema correctly explains that it means he takes a double portion in the cow's own natural appreciation. At first one might say the phrase refers simply to the body of the cow, teaching that the cow itself is not "expectant" property merely because it is rented out. But the Gemara compares the rented cow to one grazing in the meadow, emphasizing appreciation that comes automatically without depletion of estate resources. This points to increase in the cow itself, not merely ownership of its body. Lechem Mishneh understood Rambam as referring to rental income and asked from Arakhin, where rent payable only at the end can leave a person poor until collection. He suggests either that we hold rent accrues from beginning to end or that rent set during the father's lifetime is not considered expectant. This is difficult. Even if the rent accrues throughout the term, it is no stronger than a loan whose maturity occurred during the father's life, and a firstborn still does not take double in a loan, even a documented one. Therefore Sema's interpretation is primary: he takes double in the cow's natural appreciation, not in the rental debt.
And in its offspring, a double portion. Raavad objects that this is true only according to Rabbi, whereas we rule like the Sages and the firstborn should not receive a double portion in the offspring. Maggid Mishneh explains Rambam that birth is not considered a significant transformation because a fetus is part of its mother, and therefore the offspring is like ordinary appreciation. He acknowledges that the reason requires study but says this was Rambam's intent. In my view Rambam follows his own position in the laws of theft. If an animal was pregnant when stolen and later gave birth, Rambam rules that the thief pays the animal and its offspring. Raavad there objects that birth constitutes a change. I explained elsewhere that Rambam derives from Avodah Zarah that birth is not a halachically significant change. The Gemara asks whether grain that was worshiped while standing can later be used for offerings after being ground. It brings a proof from animals whose offspring can sometimes be permitted even though the mothers were subject to a prohibition, and distinguishes: there, it was an animal before and is an animal now; only the doors of the womb were closed before it. Here, it was grain and is now flour. This shows that birth is not viewed as a change of the object's essential identity. It was an animal before and remains an animal after birth. Rambam therefore holds likewise for firstborn inheritance: if the cow was already pregnant during the father's lifetime, the later birth does not turn the offspring into newly arisen expectant property. The firstborn takes a double portion in it. Raavad is consistent with his own view that birth is a change for theft law and therefore can also make the offspring expectant property for firstborn inheritance. It follows that Rambam's rule applies specifically where the cow was already pregnant during the father's lifetime. If the father left a nonpregnant cow and it conceived and gave birth only afterward, the firstborn does not take a double portion in that offspring. This is clear.
He left them a slave. This applies where the firstborn already declared that he wishes to divide the estate. From the moment he expresses that intention, he immediately acquires his double portion, as Ran explains. But if he had not yet declared his desire to divide, and the heirs hired out or leased the slave after their father's death, the firstborn does not receive a double share of the rent. Tosafot states this explicitly. The same applies if the father left money and the heirs lent it on interest before the firstborn expressed his desire to divide. The later interest is like rent generated by the heirs after the father's death and is not subject to the firstborn's double portion.
