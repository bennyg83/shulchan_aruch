The firstborn takes in it and in its offspring. See Sema, seif katan 6, who explains this as referring to appreciation of the animal, implying that the firstborn does not take double in rental income. Lechem Mishneh writes that he does take double in rental income because we hold that rental accrues continuously. Ketzot HaChoshen objects that rental is still a debt and should be no better than an ordinary loan that matured during the father's lifetime. In my view neither formulation is the core issue. The case concerns rental that accrues only after the father's death, just like appreciation that occurs after death. Whether rental legally accrues continuously or only at the end is therefore not decisive. Still, I believe the primary ruling is that the firstborn does not take double in such rental. There is no greater change than this. What difference is there between a tree producing fruit, where the firstborn does not take double even when unripe dates later become mature dates, and an animal producing value through its labor? Likewise, if an animal becomes pregnant only after the father's death and gives birth, the firstborn does not take double. The underlying reason why the firstborn takes in automatic bodily appreciation but not in changed produce requires explanation. Ran, cited by Beit Yosef, writes that the Torah calls the firstborn share a gift. A recipient of a gift transferred through another does not acquire until he hears and accepts; if he protests on hearing, he never acquired. Ran adds that where such a gift appreciates before final acceptance, the donor receives the appreciation in certain forms. At first glance this is difficult, because acquisition through another usually takes effect retroactively once accepted. The explanation is that the Torah says "to give him," making the firstborn's share resemble a gift with a condition of delivery. This operates like a reservation, akin to "from today and after the condition is fulfilled." In a gift "from today and after death," siman 217 teaches that fruit appreciation at the time of death belongs to the donor, while bodily appreciation belongs to the recipient. The same is true here. Bodily appreciation is treated as belonging retroactively to the firstborn, but fruit-like appreciation occurring before fulfillment of the delivery condition belongs to the estate. Rental from the animal, and offspring conceived and born after death, are therefore fruit-like and belong to the ordinary heirs. The gloss cited by Rama disagrees principally regarding the offspring. As to bodily fattening of the animal itself, where the animal grazed and naturally increased in body, all may agree that the firstborn takes double, comparable to a palm that thickens. Tur, however, implies that even bodily improvement of the animal may not produce a double share. One can distinguish a tree's natural thickening, which is its fixed and expected pattern, from an animal fattening through grazing, which is not certain at the father's death. Moreover, the heirs may expend effort in bringing it to pasture or entrusting it to a shepherd. That can make the gain "expected property" rather than present possession.
