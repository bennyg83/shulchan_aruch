Who are related to one another. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 54, who questions a case where the witnesses were related to one another through marriage when they observed the event, and the relationship later ceased. Is this considered a beginning in disqualification? One can argue that at the time of observation, if they did not intend to testify, each one individually had the ability to go to beit din and testify, and his status would have been that of a single witness. Likewise, according to the Rosh and those who agree with him, who require the disqualifying joinder to occur specifically through testimony in beit din, as long as the two had not yet testified each one remained capable of testifying and had the status of a qualified single witness. Only if both testified together would their testimony have been nullified by their joint testimony. Since they have not yet testified, each retains the status of a qualified witness, and after the relationship ends they should be able to testify together and count as two witnesses. Rabbi Akiva Eiger writes that he found no explicit source for this question except Hagahot Ashri, chapter 4 of Shevuos, siman 15, which states plainly to the contrary, that this is considered a beginning in disqualification. Hagahot Ashri does not even present it as a novel ruling, but only comes to add that even if their wives were dying at the time of observation, this is not considered a beginning in qualification. This requires considerable study. Tosafos there, page 93, s.v. kerovim b'n'sheihem, does not prove the point, because perhaps Tosafos understands 'relatives through their wives' to mean that the witnesses were related to the lender or borrower, as Torat Chayim explains there. Rabbi Akiva Eiger then writes that support for Hagahot Ashri may be brought from the sugya in Sotah 24 concerning a shomeret yavam. See what I write in Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer, siman 166, seif katan 7. At first glance, it appears that the Gaon's uncertainty applies specifically to witnesses in monetary law. There one can say that this is not deemed a beginning in disqualification because each witness, standing alone, was initially fit to function as one witness, and his testimony would have had legal effect by establishing liability for an oath. In witnesses to kiddushin and similar matters, however, where one witness has no effect at all and only the joinder of two witnesses gives legal force, their kinship at the time of observation should plainly constitute a beginning in disqualification. If so, however, the Gaon's proof from the sugya in Sotah would fall away, because there too, with respect to testimony that intercourse preceded the husband's death, a single witness has no effect, and it would not prove the monetary case. This requires study. I later saw in Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 37, that his father in law, Rabbi Akiva Eiger, presented this very doubt to him. Chatam Sofer answered that in the underlying reasoning he too had reached the same idea in his novellae to Shevuos, but he disagreed with the implication that the doubt also applies to witnesses to kiddushin. He copies there what he wrote in his novellae and likewise questions the source of Hagahot Ashri. He concludes that a case can indeed be found in which a woman sues for food and clothing, while the man denies that there was kiddushin, and she administers an oath to the witnesses to the kiddushin. In that case, if at the time of observation the witnesses were related through their wives, they were certainly disqualified, because one witness to kiddushin has no effect. The same applies to divorce and similar matters. See there. Regarding the additional rule that witnesses related to the judges are disqualified, see Sema, seif katan 26, Shach, seif katan 16, and Responsa Noda B'Yehuda, Even HaEzer, siman 72. Some of this is cited in Pitchei Teshuva to Even HaEzer, siman 11, seif katan 16.
