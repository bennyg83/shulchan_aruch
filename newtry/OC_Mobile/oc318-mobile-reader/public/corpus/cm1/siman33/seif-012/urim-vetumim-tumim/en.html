Even though she left him children, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah questions Rashbam in Bava Batra, Perek Yesh Nochalin. The Mishnah says that if a person knew testimony before becoming another's son-in-law, and afterward the daughter died, he becomes qualified again. Rashbam explains: she died without leaving children from him. Nimukei Yosef copies the same limitation. At first glance this seems to rule like R. Yehudah, who holds that if she left children he remains disqualified. There is no difficulty. In Arakhin 17b R. Yehudah derives from "if he is poor" that the person's status must remain the same from beginning to end. The Gemara asks: if so, regarding testimony should we likewise require the witness to remain qualified from beginning to end? It then cites the baraita that if one knew testimony before becoming a son-in-law and later the daughter died, he may testify. That baraita is being used against R. Yehudah. Yet according to R. Yehudah, if she left children he remains disqualified. Therefore the baraita must necessarily be speaking where she left no children. Tosafot state this explicitly there. Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef therefore added "and she had no children from him" only because that is the way the baraita must be established according to R. Yehudah for purposes of the Gemara's argument. It is not a practical ruling in accordance with R. Yehudah.
However, if judgment was already rendered, etc. See Urim, where I explained this law in accordance with Shach, and that interpretation correctly reflects Baal HaItur. Nevertheless, the rule itself requires study. Baal HaItur says that once a relative testified while still related and judgment was rendered, he should not be permitted, after the relationship ended, to repeat the testimony because he is likely to reinforce his original words. But relatives are not suspected of deliberate falsehood. This is why a document signed before the signer became a son-in-law remains valid even if it appears only afterward. We do not suspect that he forged an earlier date now, as we might suspect a robber. If the relative's original testimony was not presumed false, but merely invalid by Torah decree, why should later removal of the relationship be ineffective? His original words may have been entirely true. One can answer that once Torah disqualified relatives, they themselves may not scrutinize their testimony carefully, thinking their words have no legal effect. They may speak from approximation. When they later become qualified, they may then be inclined to support what they said earlier even though the first version was not carefully formulated. This explanation would require that, even if beit din did not know of the relationship, the witness himself assumed the judges knew and would disregard his words. If he knew the court was unaware of the relationship and would actually rule on his testimony, he would presumably be careful, since relatives are not suspected of intentional fraud. Even with this defense, the law appears to depend on the dispute between Ramah and Maharam concerning witnesses who testified outside the litigant's presence. According to Ramah, as cited by later authorities and accepted by Maharshal in responsum 11, such witnesses cannot later repeat the testimony properly in the litigant's presence, because they will tend to reinforce their original words. If so, Baal HaItur's rule follows naturally. But Maharam holds that they may testify again properly and we do not fear they will merely preserve their prior version. Rama in responsum 86 writes explicitly according to Maharam that witnesses who testified while financially interested may testify again after the interest is removed; we do not say they are likely to reinforce their earlier words. If this is true even where financial interest could actually have motivated false testimony, all the more so for relatives, who were never suspected of lying at all. Therefore Baal HaItur clearly follows Ramah and Maharshal's stricter approach. But Rama above, siman 28, seif 15, rules plainly according to Maharam that testimony given outside the litigant's presence can be repeated properly later. According to that practical ruling, the same should apply here: once the relationship ends, the witness should be able to testify anew. It is difficult to distinguish by saying that here both judgment was already rendered and the witness will reinforce his prior words. What source do we have that the combination of those two factors overrides the usual rule? We generally hold that a judgment can be reopened so long as the litigant brings valid proof. Therefore Baal HaItur's ruling is difficult to maintain according to Rama's own practical position.
