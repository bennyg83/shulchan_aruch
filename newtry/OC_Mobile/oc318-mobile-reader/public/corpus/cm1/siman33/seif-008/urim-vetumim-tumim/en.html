And not for her father, etc. There are three views concerning this relationship. Bach and Maharshal hold that he is disqualified only for his wife's father, but may testify for her grandfather, who is his father-in-law's father, even according to Rabbenu Tam and Rosh, who disqualify third with first. Their proof is Tur's ruling in item 23 that "his father-in-law's father is valid to testify for him." They consider it too strained to say Tur rules there against Rosh and Rabbenu Tam. Sema holds that according to Tosafot and Rosh, a wife's grandfather is indeed disqualified because the husband is first with his wife, second with her father, and third with her grandfather. Tur's validation is only according to Rambam and Rif, who validate first with third. Taz goes further and says that even according to Rif and Rambam the wife's grandfather is disqualified. In counting degrees of marital relationship, the husband himself is not counted as a separate generation from his wife. Just as the wife is first with her grandfather's child and second with the grandfather, the husband stands in her place through "a husband is like his wife." Taz proves this from the rule that one's father-in-law's brother is disqualified. If the brother is disqualified, how could the father himself be valid? Sema's distinction based on generational remoteness is forced. Taz therefore emends Tur, as Beit Yosef suggested, to read "the father of his father-in-law's father." In my view, Bach and Maharshal cannot be correct even according to Rabbenu Tam. First, their interpretation of the Yerushalmi is difficult. The Yerushalmi asks, "May a third generation testify for the wife of the first? May Moshe testify for Pinchas's wife?" If even a second-generation relative may testify for the wife of the first, why formulate the question specifically with a third generation? The entire issue would already be resolved at the second generation. Bach answers that Moshe himself is disqualified to testify for Pinchas, and the novelty is only that he may testify for Pinchas's wife. That answer might fit a declarative statement, but the Yerushalmi presents this as a question. One cannot ask whether third with first is valid unless second with first is already understood to be invalid. Second, Tur writes in item 5 that first with first through two marital links is valid according to Rosh, and therefore if one man married a mother and another married her granddaughter, they may testify for one another. Why choose a granddaughter? If Bach were correct, even one who married the man's actual granddaughter would be valid through only one marital link, because that is effectively one's wife's grandfather. The fact Tur bases the case specifically on two marital links is a strong proof against Bach. We therefore return to Sema's approach: according to Tosafot and Rosh, the wife's grandfather is disqualified; Tur's validation would then follow Rambam and Rif. I found the same in glosses to Rif in the name of Riaz: the father of one's father-in-law is valid. This supports Sema. Yet Sema's reading still faces Tur's rule that if one man marries a mother and another marries her granddaughter, they are valid according to Rosh. The wording implies that according to the dissenting view they are disqualified. But the Mishnah says "his stepson alone," excluding the stepson's descendants. Why should the granddaughter's husband be disqualified at all? Bach answers that according to Rif, "his brother-in-law alone" excludes only the brother-in-law's sons and sons-in-law from another wife, and likewise "his stepson alone" excludes only the stepson's stepson, not his actual son or son-in-law. He distinguishes Rambam, who in his Commentary to the Mishnah says "brother-in-law alone" excludes children from another wife, while "stepson alone" excludes the stepson's son and son-in-law. Avodat HaGershuni and Chavot Yair, however, understand Rambam to agree with Rif that "stepson alone" excludes only the stepson's stepson. Tur's wording seems initially to support them: he says simply that one who married the mother and one who married her granddaughter are valid "according to Rosh," implying no other major authority agrees. If Rambam agreed with Rosh, Tur should have said so. Yet Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah clearly supports Bach's reading. On "brother-in-law alone" he excludes children from another wife; on "stepson alone" he says the stepson's son and son-in-law are valid. This creates a broader difficulty. Tur later states simply that "his father-in-law's father is valid," which, if read according to Rambam alone, would ignore both Rif and Rosh. It is unlikely Tur would state a rule without qualification against both principal authorities. If we preserve Tur's text, I suggest another approach. Maggid Mishneh, cited by Kenesset HaGedolah, distinguishes an asymmetry in some marital relatives: one person may testify for the son of his brother-in-law, while the son of the brother-in-law may not testify for him. Mordechai also entertains nonreciprocal disqualifications, explaining one form through "what a woman acquires, her husband acquires," but not necessarily the reverse. At minimum, there is no universal rule that if A is disqualified for B, B must always be disqualified for A in these rabbinic marital relationships. Why would such asymmetry exist? According to Rambam, marital relatives are rabbinically disqualified. The rules "a wife is like her husband" and "a husband is like his wife" are likewise rabbinic extensions supported by Scriptural allusion. The Sages enacted these laws to prevent confusion: if one may testify for the husband, people may mistakenly permit testimony for the wife, and vice versa. Where that confusion cannot arise, the decree need not be symmetric. Thus Maggid Mishneh's distinction is understandable. A man may testify for the son of his brother-in-law. If people infer that his own wife could also testify, no actual testimony would result, because women are not qualified witnesses. But the brother-in-law's son should not testify for him, because people may then mistakenly allow him to testify for the man's wife, who is his aunt and a genuine relative. This is a coherent rabbinic distinction. The same can explain Tur's laws of step-relations. "His stepson alone" can mean that the husband may testify for the son of his stepson, his wife's grandson, because if people extend the rule to his wife there is no valid testimony by her anyway. But the grandson may not necessarily testify for the husband, because people could then allow testimony for the wife, who is his grandmother and is genuinely related to him. This explains Tur's careful phrase in item 5, "they are permitted to testify for one another" where two marital links exist. Tur needs the reciprocal validation because the granddaughter herself might remain disqualified to testify for the husband under the single-link decree, whereas the husband may be valid for her. Likewise in item 8 Tur says, "the husband of his maternal grandmother is valid for him." This can mean that the husband may testify for his wife's grandson. There is no concern people will say his wife may testify too, because she is not a witness. But the grandson might still be disqualified from testifying for that husband if people could confuse it with testimony for the wife, who is his grandmother. This also explains item 23, "his father-in-law's father is valid for him." The wording need not mean reciprocal validity. It can mean the husband may testify for his wife's grandfather, inferred from Rambam's phrase that he may not testify for her father, implying that for her father's father he may. Again, his wife's inability to testify removes the confusion in that direction. The grandfather, however, may still be disqualified from testifying for the husband of his granddaughter, because people might then allow him to testify for his granddaughter herself, and through "a husband is like his wife" the relationship remains prohibited. This yields a sharp but coherent reading of Tur. It also resolves the question in the Gemara: "When we were in Ulla's house, we asked: what is the law regarding his father-in-law's brother, the son of his father-in-law's brother, and the son of his father-in-law's sister?" The answer cites the Mishnah's relatives, "his brother, his father's brother, his mother's brother, they and their sons and sons-in-law." Why was this a question at all? Surely they knew the Mishnah. The uncertainty was exactly whether marital-relative disqualification is reciprocal. They knew a person may not testify for his brother's son-in-law because permitting him might be confused with testimony for his brother's daughter. But perhaps the son-in-law could testify for him, since the son-in-law's wife is not herself in the category of witnesses and the same confusion does not arise. The Gemara answers from the Mishnah's broad, unqualified formulation. Where the relatives are explicitly listed in the Mishnah, the intent is reciprocal disqualification. Otherwise the Mishnah would have needed to specify one direction. But for relationships not expressly listed, such as various father-in-law cases, the rabbinic decree can remain asymmetric. This supports the distinction suggested by Maggid Mishneh and explains why the question was meaningful. It also resolves Rashi and Tosafot's difficulty that "his stepson" seems redundant because "the husband of his mother" was already listed. According to this approach, the two are not identical in direction: the law governing the stepfather's testimony may differ from the stepson's testimony. All of this is an attractive way to preserve Tur's reading "his father-in-law's father." Nevertheless, after all the effort, I think the better textual correction remains Beit Yosef and Taz's: Tur should read "the father of his father-in-law's father." Then his father-in-law's father is disqualified according to everyone, even those who validate first with third in ordinary blood relation. There is some support from Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah. Rambam asks why the Mishnah lists "his father" but not "his son." If the son were obviously included through the rule that all listed relatives include their sons and sons-in-law, the question would be weak. The difficulty makes sense only if the direct son's marital descendants are not simply covered in the way required to validate a wife's grandfather. Therefore, practically, Taz's correction is more persuasive: one's wife's grandfather is disqualified, and only the next generation, the father of that grandfather, is valid.
