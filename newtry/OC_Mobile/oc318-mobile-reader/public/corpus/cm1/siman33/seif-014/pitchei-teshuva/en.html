Those who are related to him but unrelated to his sons. See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 40, concerning a testamentary document consisting of a gift made by a healthy person, effective from the present and after death, with a kinyan. Two disqualified witnesses were signed on it, one because of kinship and the other because of financial interest. The document stated, 'He said to us: Be qualified and trustworthy witnesses for me, and make a kinyan from me,' and concluded that anyone challenging the document should have his words nullified. The testator then signed in his own handwriting, followed by the disqualified witnesses. The questioner wished to validate the document because the testator himself had signed and accepted the disqualified witnesses as qualified, especially since there was a kinyan. Chatam Sofer responded that there is no basis to validate the document. Since the disqualified witnesses signed after the testator's own signature, who says that he intended these particular witnesses? To the contrary, the document begins by calling for qualified and trustworthy witnesses, indicating that he intended qualified witnesses to sign after him, and these people later came and signed on their own. This resembles a guarantor whose obligation appears after the signatures on a document, where the signatures do not relate to his undertaking. Here too, the testator intended the transfer to take effect only through witnesses, yet there is no valid witnessed document, because he never intended these disqualified persons. Furthermore, even if the witnesses had signed before the testator, then had he remained alive and wished to retract, we might have said that he had already accepted the disqualified witnesses as qualified, perhaps even without a kinyan, notwithstanding the two disadvantages. For witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony has already been investigated in beit din, and we rule, as stated above in siman 22, that after judgment is concluded or testimony is accepted one cannot retract his acceptance. Here, however, the property fell before the heirs, and the recipients of the gift seek to extract it from the heirs on the strength of a document signed by disqualified witnesses who testify that the recipients acquired the property during the donor's lifetime. The heirs do not believe the disqualified witnesses. What benefit is there in the donor's having accepted them as qualified? The heirs never accepted them. This is even stronger than the case cited by the Ran in the name of the Ramban in the chapter HaNizakin concerning a document containing an improvement clause, codified in Choshen Mishpat siman 108, seif 18. Therefore, in my view, one cannot extract property from Torah heirs on the basis of this document. End of the substance of his ruling. See there. At first glance, one can say that if the signatures of witnesses disqualified only because of kinship had preceded the testator's signature, and the case were one described here in Shulchan Aruch, where they were related to him but unrelated to his sons, then according to all opinions the document would be valid. At the time the testimony was entrusted to them he accepted them as qualified, and after his death the heirs cannot say that they do not believe them, because as to the heirs they are now qualified witnesses. This requires further study.
