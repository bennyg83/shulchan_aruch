There is an authority who validates. Sema explains that in a deathbed will the principal legal benefit belongs to the heirs, who are unrelated to the witnesses, while the dying person can still retract. Taz therefore says that if the deathbed gift was only partial and supported by a kinyan, so that the dying person cannot retract, the witnesses would be disqualified according to everyone. In my view they can still be qualified even in a gift by kinyan, based upon Ramban's rule in Bava Batra 43. Ramban asks why partners who were financially interested at the time of seeing an event can testify after removing their interest, despite the requirement that testimony begin and end in qualification. He answers that "beginning in disqualification" applies when the person was personally related or otherwise bodily disqualified toward the person for whom he later testifies. But where the defect was merely that the money was his own, once he removes himself from that money he is not testifying for the same person to whom he was previously disqualified. He now testifies for another owner. The money itself has no enduring testimonial personality. Nimukei Yosef explains that the rule follows the person for whose benefit testimony is given, not the object. If one was interested because the money was his own, no true witness status ever attached to him regarding himself. Once the money passes to another, he can testify for that other person. Meisharim therefore rules that a relative who knew testimony about a field owned by his relative can testify after the relative sells or gives the field to someone toward whom the witness is unrelated, provided there is no relevant responsibility returning to the relative. The same should apply when the property passes by inheritance. Accordingly, in our case even if the dying person made a binding gift by kinyan, once he dies and the dispute is now against unrelated heirs, Ramban's approach allows the witnesses to testify for the new owners. The Rosh follows another model, like Raavad, that distinguishes financial interest because while interested the person never had the legal status of a witness at all, whereas relationship creates the status of a disqualified witness and therefore leaves a defect of "beginning in disqualification." This explains why the Rosh disqualifies witnesses to a dying person who were his relatives even though they are unrelated to the heirs. Maharit likewise notes this dispute. For practical purposes, according to Ramban, Nimukei Yosef, and Meisharim, even a gift by kinyan can be testified to against or for unrelated heirs. A further distinction is required between testimony about a specific identified object and testimony about a personal debt. If a relative originally saw the decedent incur a debt, then after death he cannot testify against the heirs merely because he is unrelated to them. The decedent himself was not bound by admissible testimony, and the heirs' assets are only guarantors for his obligation. Since the principal debtor was exempt from this proof, his estate cannot be charged. This follows the rule that if the principal borrower is exempt, the guarantor is exempt. But where the testimony concerns title to a specific field or object, the new unrelated owners can be bound by testimony under Ramban's model. This has practical application to instruments commonly called a shtar chatzi zachar. If the signed witness was related to the giver but unrelated to the heirs, and the instrument created a monetary debt during the giver's lifetime, the witness cannot later establish that debt against the heirs. It is a personal debt of the decedent, and his assets are only guarantors. If instead the testimony concerns an identified object or land passing to the heirs, the Ramban model can allow testimony after the property has passed to unrelated owners.
