And some validate. Sema explains that the will is not for the benefit of the deathly ill person himself but for his children. His wording implies a gift of all the estate by a shechiv mera or one explicitly giving because of impending death, where the giver may retract if he recovers. In such a case the testimony about the will creates no fixed personal consequence for him, since he can change it. But then I do not understand why Rosh would disagree with the Gaon and invalidate the will. If the shechiv mera has no legal interest in the testimony, why should the witnesses be disqualified? Relatives of a murder victim can testify against the murderer where the victim derives no monetary benefit. All the more so here, where the shechiv mera can simply retract. One might say he receives satisfaction from favoring one child over another. But a murder victim surely has even greater emotional satisfaction from the punishment of his killer, because his heart burns for vengeance. Yet that does not create monetary interest. Had Rosh stated the case expressly, I would not argue from reasoning. But I believe the Gaon and Rosh actually follow their respective broader positions in a different case: a partial gift by a shechiv mera accompanied by kinyan, which cannot be revoked. There, the testimony does affect the giver's own present property and is invalid as to him. The question is whether it can nevertheless operate as to his heirs. The Gaon follows the view cited by Rosh in the first chapter of Makkot, according to which if witnesses are related to one beneficiary but unrelated to another, their testimony is not entirely void. We divide the statement. Thus, although the testimony cannot bind the shechiv mera himself, if he dies without retracting, the witnesses can establish how the estate was allocated among the children, to whom they are unrelated. Rosh, however, follows Rif and does not divide testimony in this kind of case. Once the testimony is invalid in relation to the giver, the whole testimony fails, and the will is void. But in a gift of the entire estate by a shechiv mera, where the testimony creates no fixed legal detriment to the giver because he can revoke the gift if he recovers, there is no reason to create a dispute with the Gaon. Therefore, as a matter of halachic theory though not for practical ruling, it appears that everyone should agree that in a full shechiv mera will the testimony is valid as to the heirs. We should not multiply disputes among the poskim without necessity.
