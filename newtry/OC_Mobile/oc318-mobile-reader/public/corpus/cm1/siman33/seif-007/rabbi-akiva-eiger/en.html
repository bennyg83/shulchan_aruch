[Shulchan Aruch] He knew of it before he became his son-in-law. Note: It appears to me that if one saw Reuven deposit an object with Shimon while the witness was Shimon's relative, and afterward, once the relationship ceased, he saw Reuven demand it and say, "This object is mine," and Shimon denied it, he may testify that Shimon denied a deposit, thereby disqualifying him from testimony. The same applies where Shimon swore concerning a loan, although at the time of the loan the witness was his relative: he may testify that Shimon swore falsely, in a manner where Reuven has withdrawn his monetary claim so that this is not testimony part of which became void. This is not considered a case where the beginning of the testimony was in a state of disqualification, because his entire testimony concerns the later time when Shimon denied or swore, and that denial or oath is the complete event concerning which he becomes disqualified. Although the witness must nevertheless know that Reuven lent him the money or deposited the object with him, that knowledge is not itself the subject of the testimony. There is room to question this, however, because one can argue that he must still testify that Reuven lent him the money and that Shimon swore he had not borrowed; since the witness was a relative at the time of the loan, the beginning of his testimony was in a state of disqualification. By way of sharp analysis, it may be possible to resolve the words of Tosafot in Bava Batra 34, s.v. "It is," on this basis. Urim VeTumim asks in the rules of migo: What does he gain by saying, "I seized what was mine"? In any event, the litigant and the witness can combine to testify that he exchanged his fellow's object and thereby disqualify him from testimony. The answer may be that Tosafot follow their own view in Niddah 51, that even monetary interest creates a disqualification at the inception of the testimony and that subsequent removal of the interest does not help. The Gemara's question, "Let two of them withdraw and then judge," is principally a question regarding the judges, that they should judge the laws of that city; see there. Accordingly, if he says, "I seized what was mine," he cannot disqualify him, because with respect to this the litigant's testimony began while he was disqualified, since at the time of the seizure he had a monetary interest in his testimony. But if the other party swears that he did not seize it, then the principal disqualification arises from the false oath, and at the time of the oath the owners had already withdrawn their monetary interest, so it is not considered testimony that began in disqualification. This is clear.
