Or if he claims that he repaid, etc. Shach cites R. Yerucham, following Ramban in Milchamot, concerning a creditor who holds a document while witnesses testify that it was repaid. Kenesset HaGedolah asks why Tur omits this case. I think this is why Raavad did not explain the law that way. If the witnesses testify that the debt was repaid, the borrower is thereby exempted. On what basis would we then obligate the guarantor? We would have to divide the testimony in the most unnatural manner: accept it to exempt the borrower but reject it as to the guarantor, thereby causing the guarantor loss. Raavad therefore explains the reverse case: the witnesses testify that the borrower did not repay. Then there is a real reason one might have thought to divide their words: accept their testimony to obligate the borrower, but reject it regarding the guarantor because the witnesses are related to him, allowing the guarantor to claim repayment. The ruling teaches that we do not divide the testimony in this manner. Raavad follows the view that where a person gives one gift to two people and the witnesses are disqualified regarding one, the entire testimony fails. Once Tur records Raavad's rule that we do not divide the testimony to obligate the borrower while exempting the guarantor, there is no need to add the converse case. All the more so we do not divide the testimony in order to exempt the borrower while imposing liability on the guarantor.
Relatives of the murder victim, and even the murder victim himself, may testify. So write Sema and Shach. Rosh, however, in responsa klal 56, writes that relatives of the murder victim cannot testify, while relatives of the victim's heirs may testify. Maharit in Choshen Mishpat siman 80 struggled greatly to explain what sort of interest is involved and why relatives of the victim differ from relatives of the heirs, but did not reach a clear resolution. I am surprised that Rosh ignored the several answers of Tosafot to the Gemara's question, "Let the murdered person save him." Tosafot explain variously that the victim is an enemy, that he is a tereifah, or that he is a relative. According to all these answers, the victim's relatives are not disqualified merely by being related to him. Rosh instead follows one Tosafot answer that treats the victim as the litigant himself. The other explanations appear at least as plausible, especially since Tosafot in Sanhedrin and Yevamot do not mention the litigant explanation at all. Mordechai, citing Sefer HaChochmah, gives only the tereifah explanation. I am uncertain even if we accept the interpretation that "let the victim save him" means he is the litigant. Why should his relatives be disqualified? There is no ordinary monetary interest. Yalkut on Parashat Masei cites Sifrei Zuta: the repetition of "witness" includes the litigant. Zayit Ra'anan, authored by Magen Avraham, explains that the murder victim himself can testify. This supports the view that "let the victim save him" cannot mean simply that he is always excluded as litigant, nor Maharshal's theory above that such an enemy is universally disqualified. I believe the key lies in Rosh's discussion in the first chapter of Makkot. There, witnesses are related to the guarantor but unrelated to the lender. Raavad asks why their entire testimony is invalid. In Sanhedrin, where a person says, "So-and-so had relations with my wife," he can join another witness to execute the man but not the wife. We divide his statement. Raavad answers that division is different for the litigant himself. Regarding one's own person, Scripture says, "a matter shall stand," meaning through those who establish the matter. A person is not considered a witness concerning himself at all, so there is no rule of "testimony invalid in part is invalid entirely." The self-related part never entered the category of testimony. But ordinary relatives do enter the category of testimony and are disqualified witnesses. Where a testimony is partially disqualified by kinship, we do not divide it; testimony void in part is void in full. Rosh supports Raavad from the Gemara's phrase, "One might have thought a person is not considered related to his wife." That seems impossible, because the Mishnah expressly disqualifies one from testifying for his wife and even her son. The Gemara must mean that perhaps a wife is treated like one's own person, so that the testimony can be divided without the ordinary relative-witness problem. The novelty is that she is indeed treated like his own body for this purpose. Raavan disagrees. He holds that even in testimony involving relatives we can sometimes divide the statement. He answers Raavad's question about the guarantor differently: in "so-and-so had relations with my wife," one can restate the accepted portion as "so-and-so had relations with a woman," preserving the entire substantive accusation against the man. In the guarantor case, the testimony cannot be reformulated without changing the monetary event. According to Raavan, the Gemara's phrase about a person being related to his wife can likewise mean that even though she is a relative, the testimony can still be divided where the accepted portion is independently coherent. This is why Tosafot enter into strained explanations of that Gemara. They do not accept Raavad's categorical distinction between the litigant himself and ordinary relatives. The practical issue concerning relatives of a murder victim therefore depends on this dispute. The victim himself can testify against the attacker by dividing his testimony. To the extent he has a personal monetary claim for medical expenses, loss of work, pain, and similar damages if he survives, his testimony cannot be used for his own benefit. But concerning the murder accusation, the court can accept the independent statement that the accused committed the violent act. This is easier for the victim himself because he is not a witness concerning his own monetary rights at all. The self-interested part is outside the category of testimony, as Raavad explains. Relatives of the victim are different. At the time they witnessed the assault, they did not know whether the victim would die. If he survived, the attacker could owe him medical expenses, loss of work, and other damages. The relatives would then be related to a party with a direct monetary interest. Testimonial fitness requires qualification both at the beginning and at the end. Even if by the time they come to court the victim has died and those personal damage claims no longer apply in the same manner, they were disqualified at the moment of seeing the event. According to Raavad and Rosh, we cannot divide their testimony: "So-and-so killed my brother" cannot be accepted as "So-and-so killed a person" while discarding the relative aspect, because testimony invalid in part through ordinary kinship is invalid in full. According to Raavan and the Tosafot approach, however, we can divide the statement where the accepted core can stand independently. Then the relative can effectively testify, "So-and-so killed a person," without the part attaching the victim to his own family relationship. This explains the dispute among the authorities. Rosh follows Raavad and therefore disqualifies relatives of the murder victim. Mordechai follows Tosafot and Raavan and therefore validates them. But relatives of the victim's heirs are different and are valid according to everyone. Consider the sequence. While the victim is alive, the potential claims for medical expenses and lost work belong to the victim himself. If the witnesses are unrelated to him, they have no kinship problem at that stage. The heirs have no present personal claim while the victim lives. Once the victim dies, those particular living-person claims cease or change, and the relevant consequences pass to the heirs. But the witnesses were never related to the victim, only to the heirs. Thus there is no moment at which they are both witnesses to a claim and relatives of the person holding that claim in the manner that would create the beginning-in-disqualification problem. This makes Rosh's distinction clear. The victim himself is valid through the special rule of dividing one's own statement, because his self-interest is outside testimony. Relatives of the victim depend on the Raavad-Raavan dispute concerning division of testimony where kinship is involved. Relatives only of the heirs are valid according to everyone because no relevant disqualification existed at the time of seeing. This also explains Mordechai's language in Perek Zeh Borer that relatives of the murdered person can testify because the brother is already dead and the consequence belongs only to the heirs. At first glance that seems wrong, because we require the witness to be qualified at both beginning and end. According to what I have written, however, Mordechai follows Raavan and Tosafot: even though at the beginning the witness was related to the victim who might have had personal damage claims, the testimony can be divided and accepted regarding the independent killing accusation. There is an additional practical case. In places where the murderer is fined a fixed monetary amount payable to the heirs as blood compensation, and nothing is payable if the victim survives, witnesses who are relatives of the victim but not of his children can testify. While the victim lives, there is no monetary benefit to him from that fine. The witnesses' kinship to him therefore creates no pecuniary interest in that claim. The children, who might later receive the fine, are not related to the witnesses. Thus there was no beginning-in-disqualification problem. Accordingly, the practical structure is: The murder victim himself may testify according to everyone, with his statement divided as necessary. Relatives only of the heirs may testify according to everyone. Relatives of the victim himself fall into the dispute between Raavad and Raavan/Tosafot whether testimony can be divided where ordinary kinship disqualifies part of it. Rosh follows Raavad and disqualifies them; Mordechai and the Tosafot approach validate them. This resolves Rosh's responsum and the distinction that troubled Maharit.
