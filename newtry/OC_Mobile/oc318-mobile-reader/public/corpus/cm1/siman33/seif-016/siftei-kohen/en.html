The relatives of the murdered person may testify. Even the victim himself may testify while alive, provided he is not a tereifah. The Mordechai says so. The Beit Yosef inferred that a tereifah is categorically disqualified from all testimony due to bodily status. This is incorrect. The Darkei Moshe instead explains that the Mordechai discusses capital cases, and links this to the view that testimony capable of refutation is required only in capital cases. The Sema adds that the Rama omitted the point because many disagree. All of this is mistaken. Sanhedrin 78a expressly teaches that a tereifah who killed outside beit din is exempt because the testimony cannot be refuted through the ordinary punishment of the witnesses. Rava states that witnesses who testified against a tereifah and were refuted are not executed, while tereifah witnesses who were refuted are executed; Rav Ashi says even tereifah witnesses are not executed, because a second set refuting them could not itself be punished. The Rambam rules accordingly. This proves that a tereifah is intrinsically fit even for capital testimony. The defect in capital cases is solely that the testimony cannot receive full refutation, since execution cannot be imposed in the usual reciprocal manner. That problem does not apply to monetary testimony, because refuted witnesses can still be required to pay money. Thus the Mordechai's qualification, 'while alive and not a tereifah,' applies specifically because he discusses capital law. The Darkei Moshe and Sema also misunderstood the Nimukei Yosef and Rabbeinu Yitzchak. The Nimukei Yosef cites the Yerushalmi that witnesses related to judges are disqualified because their testimony cannot be refuted, and his discussion plainly includes monetary cases. When he says monetary law does not require inquiry and examination, he means that beit din need not initially investigate every detail, because of the policy against closing the door before borrowers. Ordinarily we presume that the witnesses know the day and hour. But where it is known that they do not know those details, or where they contradict one another in the essential inquiries, the testimony is invalid because it cannot be refuted. The Baal HaIttur and Rambam should be understood the same way when they validate documents lacking place or time and later-dated notes. They dispense with initial inquiry, not with the substantive requirement that testimony be capable of refutation. Nor does Rabbeinu Yitzchak hold otherwise. Tosafot explain in Ketubot 21b that witnesses related to the judges remain capable of refutation because another beit din can receive the refuting testimony. This proves that Rabbeinu Yitzchak also requires testimony capable of refutation in monetary cases. In my opinion no authority disagrees. The Rif and Rosh cite the Yerushalmi without qualification, the Rosh and Ran apply the principle to validation of documents, and the Gemara in Bava Kamma likewise assumes it. The proof is also clear from the opening of Sanhedrin: by Torah law both monetary and capital cases require inquiry and examination, and the Sages waived initial inquiry in ordinary loans only to avoid closing the door before borrowers. Since the purpose of inquiry is to ensure that the testimony can be refuted, the underlying requirement remains. Therefore, if beit din did conduct inquiries and one witness says, 'I do not know,' or the witnesses contradict one another regarding the essential inquiries, the testimony is void, even in monetary law. The Gemara distinguishes only contradictions in subsidiary examinations. Rashi, the Rambam, Semag, Tur, Nimukei Yosef, Rivash, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and the other authorities all support this. Accordingly, a judge should not conduct unnecessary inquiry in an ordinary case, lest a witness answer that he does not know and thereby cause the litigant a loss. Detailed inquiry should be reserved for a suspicious case or where otherwise needed. The Rambam's wording in the laws of murder that testimony incapable of refutation is invalid 'in capital law' excludes only the need for initial inquiry in ordinary monetary law. In capital law, the testimony must be demonstrably fit for refutation in every respect. The Lechem Mishneh's difficulty therefore arises from overlooking the Rambam's words in the laws of loans and the Ran at the end of Ketubot.
