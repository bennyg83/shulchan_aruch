Or witnesses related to the judges are disqualified because the judges cannot receive testimony of hazamah against them, etc. Meaning: although in civil cases we do not require testimony that is actually capable of hazamah in the full capital-law sense, the testimony must at least be of a type that could properly enter the framework of hazamah. If the witnesses are related to the judges, the same judges cannot receive the later testimony of the zomemim against them, and the judicial process is defective. Shach builds a large structure on this and argues at length in seif katan 15 that even in civil law testimony must be fully capable of hazamah. He therefore rules that if one witness says, "I do not know," in one of the formal interrogations, the testimony is void. He says that when civil witnesses were not asked the day of the month, we simply presume they know it. Despite the length of his discussion, this cannot be correct. If saying "I do not know" invalidates because the testimony cannot be refuted through hazamah, then silence should be worse. What does it help that the witness knows the date in his heart? Unless he states in beit din when the event occurred, later witnesses cannot say, "You were with us at that time," because there is no judicially stated time to refute. Hazamah operates on what was testified in court, not on private knowledge. Below in siman 43, seif 19, witnesses who do not remember the exact date may write that the document was in the first third of the month, second third, and so forth. They expressly say they do not know the exact day, one of the seven interrogations, yet the document is valid. Shach might answer that documents are different according to Baal HaMaor, who does not require hazamah in written testimony. But many authorities disagree, and Shach himself elsewhere requires the possibility of hazamah in documents for certain questions of witness disqualification. Moreover, the seven interrogations include the Sabbatical cycle and Jubilee cycle. Even if witnesses say, "He killed yesterday," Rambam in his Commentary to the Mishnah explains that the formal interrogations still include those questions because Scripture fixed seven interrogations. Rivash likewise writes that we do not follow R. Yosi's reduced system; the interrogations serve to clarify truth. Who among today's witnesses knows in which Sabbatical cycle and Jubilee cycle an event occurred, beyond saying the number of years since Creation? Their inability to answer those Scriptural formulations cannot mean civil testimony is routinely void. The correct rule is that in ordinary civil law the seven interrogations are not required. Shach asks: if so, why does contradiction in the formal interrogations invalidate? There are other explanations. The sages of Lunel, in their compromise between Ramban and Rambam, explain that contradiction in an interrogation is equivalent to one witness saying, "No, the loan did not occur as the other says." The added statement that perhaps a loan occurred on another day does not repair the direct contradiction regarding the event claimed. I explained this above in siman 30. This itself is strong evidence that the invalidation does not arise from incapacity for hazamah. Even according to Rambam there is another reason. Witnesses are expected to be exact regarding the formal interrogations. Therefore contradiction there is treated as a genuine contradiction. Subsidiary examinations concern details people may not notice carefully. Consider Rambam's example: one says the object was a barrel of wine and the other says a barrel of oil. Rambam treats that as contradiction in the formal substance of the testimony and invalidates them. What conceivable hazamah problem exists because one said wine and one oil? The issue is not whether later witnesses can place them elsewhere. The problem is that the witnesses contradict one another about the very event. Thus the reason contradiction in interrogations invalidates is precision and substance, not the technical need for hazamah. What, then, is the proof from the rule that judges may not be related to witnesses because the judges cannot receive hazamah? There is none for Shach's broader claim. Even if civil testimony need not itself include all data necessary for hazamah, the judicial system must still be capable of receiving valid hazamah if the testimony was sufficiently specified and later refuting witnesses actually appear. Suppose the witnesses do identify the day and place, and genuine zomemim then come. If the judges are related to the original witnesses, the zomemim stand in court but cannot be heard. Justice would be distorted: the mechanism exists factually, but the court is personally incapable of administering it. That is why witnesses related to judges are disqualified. This is entirely different from a witness saying, "I do not know" to an interrogation. In that case no actual valid hazamah testimony exists before the court and is being blocked by judicial kinship. Civil law permits the original testimony despite the missing formal interrogation. Shach's entire proof therefore fails. The overwhelming majority of poskim hold that if a civil witness says "I do not know" in the formal interrogations, the testimony remains valid. One should not overturn that settled law. I wrote in Urim that Shach correctly objects to Darkei Moshe and Sema on another point, and that objection is strong. But his criticism of Beit Yosef, who treats a tereifah as an intrinsic disqualification, can be defended. Tosafot in Perek Hayu Bodkin write that according to R. Akiva, if one of a group says "I do not know," this can resemble finding one of the witnesses related or disqualified and may void the testimony. That makes sense when the person actually comes to beit din and testifies in a form that lacks the necessary qualification. But if three people witnessed an event and only two come to court while the third remains absent and happens not to remember the exact day, it is difficult to say the testimony of the two is void merely because an absent third person would not have been capable of hazamah. Otherwise no capital case could ever be decided until every person present at the event was brought to court and questioned about the date, Sabbatical cycle, and Jubilee cycle, lest one had forgotten and thereby invalidate all witnesses. Thus a person who merely lacks the data for hazamah is not intrinsically a disqualified witness. He is simply unable to furnish a fully qualified testimony. His inability does not automatically disqualify other witnesses who do. If so, the same should be true of a tereifah if his only problem were inability to be punished through hazamah. Why should his presence at the event disqualify other witnesses? The Gemara nevertheless asks, "Let the murdered person save him," and Tosafot explain that the victim may be a tereifah. That suggests a tereifah has an intrinsic personal disqualification, not merely a technical inability to be subjected to hazamah. There is additional support. The entire sugya containing the questions "let the murdered person save him" and "let the person sodomized save him" appears in the academy of Abaye and Rava, as indicated by the Gemara's presentation. Tosafot ask concerning the willing sodomy victim that Rava applies the rule of dividing testimony. This shows the discussion is being read according to Rava. But in Sanhedrin 78, Rava and Rav Ashi dispute witnesses who were fit when they testified but later became tereifah before being refuted. Rava holds the refuting witnesses can still be punished; Rav Ashi says not, because the second hazamah process cannot be reciprocally applied. If a tereifah were disqualified only because his testimony cannot be subjected to hazamah, then according to Rava a tereifah should apparently remain capable of testimony in some circumstances. Why, then, would the Gemara ask that the murdered victim, as a tereifah, invalidate the group? One can answer that the victim's tereifah status is an intrinsic bodily disqualification. The later discussion of "tereifah witnesses who were refuted" concerns witnesses who were healthy when they originally testified and became tereifah only afterward, before the hazamah testimony arrived. Their original testimony was fully valid at the time. Rava and Rav Ashi dispute only the later application of punishment to the zomemim framework, not whether an already-tereifah person is intrinsically fit to become a witness initially. This preserves Beit Yosef's view. There is a conceptual basis as well. The Gemara entertains that intercourse with a tereifah might not be treated like intercourse with a fully living person, because a tereifah is, in some respects, like one already destined to die. Though the Gemara rejects that for sexual benefit, it still indicates that tereifah status affects the person's legal classification. Thus the Gemara's question "let the murdered person save him" can rest on intrinsic witness disqualification. Shach nevertheless argues that a tereifah is valid for sacrificial service, as appears in Bechorot. Why, then, should he cease to be a legal "person" for testimony? This remains a strong reason to hesitate before calling tereifah an intrinsic disqualification. I later found nearly explicit support for Beit Yosef from Mordechai. Mordechai says that if the murder victim is not yet a tereifah he can testify, for example where he was given a deadly poison that will ultimately kill him but has not yet perforated his organs, or where physicians assessed him as likely to die though he is not technically tereifah. Consider the dilemma. If he is already so certainly destined to die that the attacker can be executed while the victim is still alive, then the victim himself cannot be subject to ordinary hazamah. Refuting witnesses could say, "You were with us," but he is already a person expected to die. His ability to be punished through the reciprocal hazamah framework is highly questionable. If, on the other hand, he is not considered certainly dying, then the attacker cannot be executed until the victim actually dies, because perhaps medicine will save him. Until death occurs the causation of murder remains unresolved. But once the victim dies, what can be done to him if his testimony is later refuted? He is dead. There is no actual possibility of imposing the reciprocal punishment. Thus the victim's testimony seems never to be fully capable of hazamah in the ordinary sense. Yet Mordechai validates him so long as he is not technically a tereifah. This is powerful proof that mere inability to subject the witness to hazamah does not itself invalidate other civil or even capital testimony in the broad manner Shach proposes. The tereifah problem must be a separate bodily disqualification. One could try to say that refutation could occur while the victim is still alive but technically tereifah, and the court could then execute him before he dies naturally. But this is very strained. Perhaps he could live unexpectedly, as Yam Shel Shlomo notes that not every tereifah dies immediately. And if his entire testimony becomes capable of punishment only after he reaches tereifah status, then at the moment of original testimony the reciprocal structure was not yet complete. Therefore the stronger conclusion is that testimony need not always be actually capable of hazamah in the literal sense Shach demands. The rule about witnesses related to judges means only that the court itself must not be structurally barred from hearing valid refuting testimony when it exists. It does not impose the seven formal interrogations upon ordinary civil witnesses. As for tereifah, there is substantial basis for Beit Yosef's intrinsic-disqualification approach, though practically Shach's ruling regarding the tereifah witness may still be followed, especially because a tereifah is otherwise recognized as a living person for important Torah laws. The central point, however, is clear: Shach's attempt to derive from this law that every civil testimony must be fully capable of hazamah and that "I do not know" in an interrogation invalidates it cannot be accepted.
