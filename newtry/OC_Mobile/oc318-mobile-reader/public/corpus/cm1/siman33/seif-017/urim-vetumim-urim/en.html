Or if they are relatives of the judges, they are disqualified. The reason given is that the judges cannot receive hazamah testimony against their relatives, whereas testimony must be capable of hazamah. Darkei Moshe cites authorities who say that because monetary law does not require testimony fit for hazamah, judges may be relatives of the witnesses. Sema. In Tumim I explain that everyone agrees ordinary monetary testimony need not actually be capable of hazamah in the sense that failure to answer formal interrogations invalidates it. The authorities who disqualify judges related to witnesses do so for another reason: if the witnesses were in fact intentionally discredited through hazamah, there must exist a beit din capable of receiving that judgment. We should not structure a court so that injustice would result if hazamah arose. This is unlike Shach, who disqualifies monetary testimony when witnesses cannot answer interrogations. Beit Yosef also writes that a person who is a *tereifah* cannot testify even in monetary law, which sounds like an intrinsic bodily disqualification. Sema explains this through inability of hazamah, but Shach correctly notes that in monetary law hazamah can still impose monetary payment upon a *tereifah*. Only in capital law, where he could say, “They killed a man already considered dead,” is hazamah impossible. Shach is right on the law itself, though in Tumim I defend Beit Yosef from being treated as having made an obvious mistake.
