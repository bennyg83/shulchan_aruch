Some say that maternal relatives as well are disqualified only rabbinically. The Rama's wording implies that this is a lenient opinion, with practical effect even for witnesses to kiddushin. In my view this is incorrect. The Rambam calls laws derived through the thirteen hermeneutical principles 'of the Sages,' as the Kesef Mishneh and Rivash explain, because they are not explicitly written and are not counted separately among the 613 commandments. He does not mean that their legal force is merely rabbinic. The same is evident from his description of kiddushin by money. Although he calls its derivation 'from the words of the Sages,' he explicitly states that its law is Torah law. The Rashbatz and Megillat Esther explain at length that every law derived through the thirteen principles has full Torah force, though it is not counted as an independently explicit commandment. The Kesef Mishneh likewise writes that one may incur stoning or bring a sacrifice for violating such a law, exactly as for an explicit Torah law. Accordingly, even according to the Rambam, maternal relatives are disqualified with the same Torah force as paternal relatives. The Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe themselves cite the Rashbatz that the Rambam's terminology concerns only classification in the enumeration of commandments, and that kiddushin before such relatives is invalid. I therefore find the Maggid Mishneh's presentation difficult, for elsewhere he himself explains that kiddushin by money is Torah law according to the Rambam. In any event, the halachah is that maternal relatives are disqualified by Torah law. The Rivash rules so, citing the Rashba and a responsum of the Rif; the same appears in the Rashba, Shiltei HaGibborim, Sefer HaChinuch, a responsum of the Ramban, Divrei Rivot, Semag, and the general language of the other authorities. The Bach, Rama, and Levush therefore did not formulate this opinion precisely.
