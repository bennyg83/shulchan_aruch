Except for one who loves him. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur, letter 2, cites Radbaz that household servants are valid witnesses, reasoning that a servant should not be worse than a friend, who is valid. Yet Radbaz elsewhere writes that servants may be interested parties. Tumim finds a proof from Rambam, Hilchot Gezelah 4:7, that a hired worker or household retainer is valid, and says this proof is conclusive and reflects common practice. In my opinion, however, there is little reason to debate the matter as an absolute rule. It plainly depends on the judge's assessment whether the servant has any personal benefit from this testimony. Rambam, cited below in siman 37, seif 21, writes that such matters depend on the judge's understanding and that he must investigate whether the witness derives any benefit, even remote or unusual. Radbaz himself explicitly applies this principle when disqualifying the servants of Shimon from testifying regarding a marriage, because it was advantageous to them that the daughter of wealthy Reuven should become the wife of their master Shimon. The judge must therefore examine each case for any benefit to the witness; this requires study.
And one who hates him. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur, letter 4, writes that were he not hesitant to innovate, he would say that an extreme enemy, like one of a person's pursuers who seeks revenge, is disqualified from testifying. This was the view of Maharshal in Responsa, siman 33, and he suggests support from the Rama and Tosafot in the first chapter of Makkot. It may also be the view of Mahari ben Lev and Maharshal, who disqualified an enemy, although other authorities challenged them. Responsa Knesset Yechezkel, siman 89, cites Maharshal and argues at length against him, noting that Maharit likewise rules that even a great enemy is valid. His proof is from the Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri cited below in seif 16, where no greater enemy can be imagined than the murdered victim, yet the relevant testimony is accepted. Tumim also discusses Maharshal at length and concludes that there is no clear proof from the Gemara and that one should not create a new disqualification from reasoning alone. See Responsa Mayim Chayim, simanim 1 and 2, and what is written below at the end of siman 35.
