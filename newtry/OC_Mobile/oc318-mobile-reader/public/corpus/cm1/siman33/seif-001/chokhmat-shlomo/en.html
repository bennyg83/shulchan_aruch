Seif 2. In the gloss: “Some say that maternal relatives are disqualified only rabbinically.” Note: My sharp and distinguished friend, Rabbi Getzel Margoliyot, asked me from Shevuot chapter 4. The Gemara asks regarding two successive groups of witnesses who denied knowledge of testimony: concerning the second group it is understandable that they can be liable for an oath of testimony, but why should the first group be liable when the second group remains available? The Gemara answers that the second witnesses were related through their wives and the wives were near death, so the second group could later become fit. According to the Rambam, if this particular familial disqualification is only rabbinic, the answer seems difficult according to the opinion that a gambler is still Torah-valid for testimony and therefore can be liable for an oath of testimony. If rabbinic disqualification does not remove the Torah status of a witness, why is the first group treated as indispensable merely because the second group is rabbinically disqualified? There is no difficulty. The opinion that a gambler is liable for an oath of testimony because he is Torah-valid speaks about the gambler’s own oath. Since the Torah still recognizes him as someone capable of testimony, his false oath can generate the korban. But when we ask whether the first group is exempt because there are other witnesses available, the issue is not the abstract Torah status of the second group. The issue is whether those witnesses can in practice establish the claimant’s case. If they are rabbinically disqualified, their testimony cannot be used. They are therefore functionally like witnesses who have gone overseas. The first group remains essential and may be liable. Indeed, according to the opinion that a gambler is Torah-valid for purposes of the oath, the Gemara could have answered more simply that the second group consisted of gamblers. The first group would be liable because the second group cannot actually produce a judgment rabbinically, while the gamblers themselves could nevertheless be liable for their own oath because their Torah witness status remains. The Gemara’s answer involving relatives through marriage is necessary only according to the opinion that even a gambler is exempt from the oath because we follow the practical rabbinic disqualification. This resolves the question.
