Except for a friend and an enemy, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah cites Radbaz and notes an apparent contradiction between his responsa. In one place Radbaz validates household servants as witnesses because they are no worse than a friend or enemy. In another he disqualifies them. I do not have the second part of Radbaz before me, but there is some support for disqualification from Bava Batra 29b. The Gemara asks how continuous three-year possession of a house can be established, since ordinary witnesses know daytime residence but not necessarily nighttime residence. It answers through neighbors or tenants. Why not simply use the possessor's household servants and table-companions? Servants normally remain in the house and would know continuous residence day and night. This suggests they are considered interested witnesses. Even if at the time they later testify they are no longer in his household, during the three years of chazakah they were his servants and dependents. Testimonial qualification requires fitness both at the beginning and at the end, as explained above in siman 37. This is some support for Radbaz's stricter ruling. Nevertheless, since no early authority clearly states this disqualification, we should not create a new rule merely from a Gemara difficulty. Many answers can be given. Indeed, I find a strong proof in the opposite direction from Rambam, Hilchot Gezelah 4:7. Rambam writes that if the homeowner's hired worker or household servant was present during a robbery, they do not take the special oath and collect as the robbed owner himself might, and the owner cannot swear because he was not present. The robber is not made to swear because he is suspected regarding oaths. I explain below in siman 90 that the ordinary oath against the robber arises from the claim supplied through the worker or servant. Rambam holds that even where the claimant himself does not know definitely, a claim based upon one valid witness can impose an oath. But Rambam also holds that a claim based on a relative or otherwise disqualified witness cannot impose that oath. Therefore the worker and servant must themselves be valid witnesses. Mechaber likewise rules below in siman 75 that only a valid witness can create the relevant oath. Thus Rambam clearly treats a hired worker or household servant as fit to testify despite the employment relationship. This is, in my view, decisive proof. The practical custom also follows this: ordinary servants and hired workers are not disqualified merely because they work for or receive support from a party, unless a specific monetary interest in the outcome is established.
And an enemy, etc. Maharshal, responsum 33, rules that one who pursues another with intent to kill him, or the like, creates such extreme enmity that the intended victim is disqualified from testifying against the pursuer. Kenesset HaGedolah writes that many Sephardic authors agreed with Maharshal. Maharshal's proof comes from the Gemara's question, "Let the victim save him," meaning that the murdered person should count among the witnesses and invalidate the testimony. Tosafot explain that the victim is an enemy of the killer. The Tosafot Yeshanim ask: what of it? An enemy is valid to testify. One cannot simply say the Gemara asks according to R. Yehudah, who disqualifies an enemy. Perhaps R. Yehudah follows Rebbi, who requires the disqualified person to have been warned or to have intended to testify before he joins the witnesses. Rashi explicitly says that according to Rebbi the difficulty of "let the victim save him" can disappear. Maharshal therefore answers that this level of enmity is different. Where one person seeks another's life, the victim is so absolute an enemy that everyone agrees he is suspected of false testimony. This is a substantial novelty, and the proof is not compelling. Maharsha in the first chapter of Sanhedrin interprets the Gemara's question as directed according to R. Yehudah and does not adopt Maharshal's new law. At first, however, there is a difficulty with Maharsha's approach. If the question "let the victim save him" is only according to R. Yehudah, perhaps R. Yehudah follows Rebbi and requires actual intent to testify. Why must every murder case fail because of the victim? There is a clear answer. The requirement of warning or intention matters only where warning is itself part of the legal framework. But according to an authority who holds a scholar needs no warning, where the Torah case does not depend on formal warning, the joining of a disqualified participant cannot be made to turn on whether warning occurred. Makkot 6b says R. Yosi holds that an enemy is executed because he is considered forewarned and warned, and the Gemara establishes him according to R. Yosi b. Yehudah, who holds a chaver does not require warning. According to that view, the question "let the victim save him" is strong. Since the killer's victim is certainly his enemy, if enemy testimony is disqualified, how can the remaining witnesses execute? Nor can we say R. Yosi b. Yehudah may agree with the Sages that an enemy is fit to testify. The Sages' reason is that Jews are not suspected of lying merely because of hatred. But if R. Yosi b. Yehudah treats enmity as sufficient to characterize the killing as intentional and forewarned, perhaps he would also treat the enemy more strictly regarding testimony. Still, this line has difficulty from Sanhedrin 29, where the verse "he was not his enemy" is used regarding two Torah scholars who hate each other and therefore should not sit together in judgment. If R. Yosi b. Yehudah already needs that verse to disqualify an enemy as a witness, how can the verse also support the judicial rule? Perhaps according to him the witness disqualification follows from logic: if one is suspected enough in the killing context, certainly his testimony against the enemy is suspect. Then one verse can remain for the judicial rule. But this is not decisive. The more important point is that we need not establish Maharshal's broad practical rule from this sugya. Tosafot in Makkot also discuss the question, "Let the person who was sodomized save him," meaning the victim should be a disqualified participant. They reject the explanation that he is disqualified merely because he hates the attacker after being violated by force, because R. Yosef states explicitly that where a person says, "So-and-so sodomized me by force," he may join another witness to execute the offender. If an involuntary victim were an enemy disqualified according to everyone, R. Yosef's rule would be impossible. Maharshal could answer that attempted murder creates greater hatred than sexual violation. Perhaps one who is about to lose his life is an enemy of a different order. But this itself is a new distinction without clear source. The Tosafot discussion suggests that extreme injury does not automatically create universal testimonial disqualification. There is also the question concerning one who was willingly sodomized and thereby calls himself wicked. Tosafot ask that we divide his statement and accept the portion incriminating the other person. One cannot answer that the question is only according to R. Yehudah, who does not apply splitting of testimony in the same way, because, as explained, the Gemara's challenge can arise under other Tannaitic frameworks as well. The better reading is therefore that the sugya does not establish a new universal category of "extreme enemy." Tosafot themselves offer other explanations for "let the victim save him": the victim may have become a tereifah, or the case may involve a relative of the victim. Mordechai adopts the tereifah explanation as primary, and many authorities follow it. If Tosafot believed that a person pursued for his life is automatically disqualified as an enemy according to everyone, there would have been little need for these alternative explanations. Therefore Maharshal's proof does not establish practical law. This also answers the argument from the victim's evident hatred. Torah did not disqualify ordinary enemies from testimony according to the Sages because we do not assume a Jew will lie merely from hatred. Even severe hatred should not be made into a new disqualification without an explicit source. Indeed, the Gemara asks what the Sages do with the verse "he was not his enemy." If extreme enmity were universally disqualified, it could have answered that the verse refers to such a case. The fact the Gemara does not do so weighs against Maharshal. Kenesset HaGedolah also discusses Mordechai's statement in Perek Get Pashut concerning the custom that two shushvinin sign in the blank space between a ketubah and its witnesses. Mordechai says the document is not invalidated because it is evident that they sign merely to fill the space and because they are identified as shushvinin. Kenesset HaGedolah asks: if a friend is fit to testify, why would the shushvinin's signatures pose any problem? He therefore suggests that perhaps one who is specifically the shushvin for this marriage is disqualified for testimony concerning that event. I think the opposite is more reasonable. They are shushvinin to both bride and groom, so their affection is balanced. More importantly, there is no basis in Gemara or early poskim for creating a special disqualification of this sort. Mordechai's concern is simpler. By custom, the shushvinin are often relatives of the bride or groom. If their signatures appeared near the valid witnesses, one might worry that relatives joined the testimony and invalidated the document. Mordechai therefore explains that their role is visibly different: they sign only to fill blank lines and are identified as shushvinin, so their signatures are not treated as joining the legal testimony. Baal HaItur states this explicitly: in ketubot the custom is sometimes to have relatives and minors sign because the ketubah is a public document, and shushvinin who are relatives of bride and groom may sign for the customary purpose without disqualifying the valid witnesses. This makes Mordechai's intent clear. It has nothing to do with friendship as a testimonial disqualification. I would therefore not derive any new rule from the shushvinin. If I were not hesitant to say so, one might even suggest that some early authorities understood the anonymous Gemara's question "let the victim save him" as reflecting the halachah of R. Yehudah that an enemy is disqualified, rather than the Sages. There is an intriguing indication from Maharam. Maharam ruled concerning a document signed by a man's son-in-law that one must establish that the document was seen before he became related, just as with a robber. Beit Yosef is astonished, because the Gemara at the end of Perek Mi Shemet teaches that only a robber is suspected of falsification; a relative is disqualified by decree of Torah and need not have the document previously established in beit din. Perhaps Maharam holds that this distinction follows the Sages, who say a friend and enemy are not suspected of lying and likewise a relative is not personally suspected. But if the halachah followed R. Yehudah, who treats love and hatred as creating suspicion of falsehood, a close relative such as a son-in-law could be treated more like a robber for this purpose. This would explain Maharam's ruling. Still, the Gemara and baraita in Bava Batra are stated broadly, and creating such a major distinction from this inference is hazardous. For practical halachah, therefore, we should not disqualify a person merely because the litigant pursued him, injured him, or created extreme hatred, unless another recognized ground of disqualification exists. The Sages' rule remains that a friend and enemy are fit witnesses. Maharshal's stricter position has supporting authorities, but Tosafot's alternative explanations, Mordechai, and the broader structure of the Gemara favor the ordinary rule. Accordingly, I would not innovate the disqualification of an "absolute enemy" where the Talmud and primary poskim did not clearly establish it. Maharshal's own proof therefore remains only one possible reading of the Tosafot question. The old Tosafot ask why the victim's hatred should matter when an enemy is valid according to the Sages. If their answer were that this particular hatred is universally disqualifying, that would indeed establish Maharshal. But the later Tosafot do not settle the passage that way. They seek other grounds of disqualification, including tereifah and kinship. That strongly suggests they did not regard extreme hatred alone as enough. The distinction between the murdered victim and the person forcibly sodomized is especially important. One might argue that fear of death creates a more absolute hatred than other injury. But no Talmudic source states such a hierarchy for testimony. Once R. Yosef says the forced victim can join another witness to execute the offender, the burden lies on anyone who wishes to create a new category of hatred so extreme that all Sages agree it disqualifies. The ordinary rule is exactly the opposite: despite natural hostility, Torah trusts an enemy not to fabricate testimony. The Sages' language is broad, "Israel are not suspected of this." Without a text limiting that presumption, we should not create degrees of enmity by intuition. Nor does the fact that a pursuer has shown murderous intent establish that the intended victim will now lie. The pursuer's wicked conduct proves something about the pursuer, not about the moral reliability of the victim. This also weakens the proposed proof from R. Yosi b. Yehudah. Saying that an enemy who kills is treated as forewarned concerns the killer's state of mind and the legal need for warning. It does not necessarily mean every enemy is suspected of false testimony. A person can act violently from hatred and still not be presumed willing to swear or testify falsely in beit din. Thus the inference, "if hatred can bring him to kill, surely it can bring him to lie," is not compelled. Torah frequently treats different transgressions through different presumptions. The verse "he was not his enemy" likewise does not force Maharshal's rule. According to R. Yehudah it supplies the explicit disqualification of enemy. According to the Sages, the verse can be used for another law, such as judges who hate one another. There is no need to carve out an unmentioned middle category of "ordinary enemy valid, extreme enemy invalid." This is why the absence of such a category from the major codifiers carries weight. The shushvinin case supplies a parallel lesson. It is tempting to infer special bias from unusually close social relationships, but the early sources do not do so. Instead they explain the potential defect through concrete recognized disqualifications, especially kinship. Baal HaItur's explicit language confirms that the customary signatures in a ketubah can include relatives and minors because everyone knows those signatures are ceremonial or used to fill space, not part of the legal witnessing. That is exactly the concern Mordechai addresses. If friendship or participation as shushvin itself created invalidity, the sources should have stated that simpler reason. Maharam's ruling about a son-in-law can perhaps be read through suspicion, as suggested, but it is too uncertain a basis for overturning the explicit rule concerning relatives. Beit Yosef's astonishment remains strong, and one should not derive a general doctrine of extreme-enemy disqualification from such an isolated inference. Therefore, even accepting that Maharshal's interpretation is possible, practical halachah should remain with the established rule unless the person falls into a recognized category such as relative, interested party, wicked witness, tereifah in the relevant capital-law framework, or another explicit disqualification. A court may certainly scrutinize the testimony of someone with powerful personal hatred. The judges are always obligated to investigate a case that appears fraudulent and to seek the truth. But heightened scrutiny is not the same as formal witness disqualification. This distinction preserves both principles: severe hatred can make a case suspicious and justify careful examination, yet it does not automatically remove the witness's halachic status where the Sages explicitly validated an enemy. Accordingly, Maharshal's position should not be extended beyond a case where an accepted authority or established local ruling specifically follows it. As a general rule, enemy remains a valid witness.
