Seif 13. See Shach, seif katan 10, who writes that the same rule applies where a man divorced his wife. At first glance it is unclear what he adds, since the Shulchan Aruch expressly requires that a witness have been fit both at the beginning and at the end. One might say that he teaches that this rule applies even to a disqualification created by marriage, but the Mechaber himself speaks of a person who knew testimony while someone was his son-in-law and whose daughter later died, which seems even stronger. It appears that the Shach is teaching a significant point. With a monetary interest, the Shulchan Aruch rules that according to one explanation of the Sema, the witness need not have been fit at the beginning because he could have removed his own financial interest from the outset. The defect is therefore not treated as an intrinsic disqualification. One might similarly argue that a relationship created by marriage is removable because a husband can divorce his wife. Perhaps it should therefore not count as an intrinsic defect and should not require fitness at both endpoints. The Mechaber’s case does not answer this, because where the witness is related through his daughter’s marriage, he cannot force his son-in-law to divorce her. Even where the relationship ends by her death, death is not in his control. The Shach therefore teaches that even when the witness himself later divorces the wife and thus demonstrates that he could in principle have removed the relationship, we still require that he have been fit at the beginning and the end. Marriage-based relationship is treated as a genuine legal disqualification, not merely an external interest. This seems the correct intent of the Shach. Seif 16. “There is no difference whether the borrower seeks exemption by a denial,” etc. I temporarily obtained Machaneh Ephraim and saw that in the laws of testimony he writes that where a person is liable only to a hesset oath, witnesses may exempt him even if they were not fit at both the beginning and the end, because for a rabbinic oath the Sages did not require the full Torah standard of witness fitness, just as adults may testify to certain matters that they saw while minors. In my view this is not convincing. Rabbinic enactments are not automatically analogous to one another. We possess only the leniencies that the Sages actually established. One might have expected the Gemara in Shevuot, when listing differences between Torah and rabbinic oaths, to mention this distinction if it existed: Torah oath requires witnesses fit at both endpoints, while hesset could be defeated by witnesses who were initially disqualified. It does not. This proof can be rejected because the Gemara may have listed only distinctions inherent in the oath itself, whereas witness qualification is a general evidentiary rule. Even so, the underlying argument remains: we cannot infer from one special rabbinic evidentiary leniency to another without a source. Therefore Machaneh Ephraim’s innovation does not appear correct. There, in the gloss. “Relatives of the murdered person may testify against the murderer.” See Shach, seif katan 16, who proves that in such testimony we do not initially require every condition necessary for testimony capable of hazamah, or full formal examination, but if the witnesses actually contradict one another in the core interrogations, or one says “I do not know” concerning an essential inquiry, their testimony is invalid because Torah law fundamentally requires testimony capable of hazamah. He proves this from the Rambam and Mechaber in siman 30, who rule that a contradiction in the interrogations invalidates monetary testimony. Urim VeTumim rejects that proof and explains that contradiction in the interrogations invalidates for a different reason: one witness is then effectively testifying that the event did not occur as described by the other. He gives another reason as well. I initially thought there was proof for Urim VeTumim against the Shach from the first chapter of Sanhedrin, in the sugya concerning concern for reputational harm. The Gemara explains a dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages over whether a case of motzi shem ra requires three or twenty-three judges, in a case where the witnesses contradicted one another in secondary examinations, linking it to the dispute of Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai and the Sages. If, according to Rabban Yochanan ben Zakkai, secondary examinations are treated like core interrogations because of the requirement of hazamah, and contradictions in monetary law invalidate only for that same reason, then even the monetary aspect should collapse and there would be no need for three judges at all. On further consideration this is not a proof. Depending on the interpretation of the sugya, the monetary component may concern the husband’s hundred-sela liability, or the wife’s ketubah, and the effect of the contradiction may not parallel the capital aspect. Rashi’s earlier discussion also shows that when witnesses are refuted or contradicted, the procedural consequences differ depending on whether their falsehood has been affirmatively demonstrated. Thus the challenge can be resolved without rejecting the Shach. Still, the sugya raises another question. The Gemara cites a baraita: if he brought a monetary claim, three judges; if he brought a capital claim, twenty-three. Why not explain the baraita as referring to a husband who betrothed and consummated immediately, so that there is no possible capital consequence and only the monetary issue remains, whereas if there is a possible capital consequence the case requires twenty-three? This is especially significant according to Rava, who holds that one who married a woman on the presumption that she was a virgin and found otherwise may deny her ketubah, relying on the presumption that a man does not exert himself over the wedding meal merely to lose it. The matter requires further study.
