Seif 4, in the gloss. “Nevertheless, initially they should not sign the document together.” Note: See the Sema, who explains that we are concerned lest an erroneous beit din later misunderstand the signatures. This is difficult, for we ordinarily do not suspect that a beit din will err, even in matters of prohibition, as in the laws of miun and chalitzah in Even HaEzer, and certainly not in monetary law. Maharshal’s responsa raise a similar objection to Rabbeinu Yonah’s view, cited in siman 205, concerning a person who asks witnesses to write a declaration of protest. I discussed possible answers there and in Even HaEzer siman 17, but those resolutions do not readily apply here. The matter requires study. There, Seif 12, in the gloss. “If judgment was rendered while he was still a relative.” My young, discerning, and sharp son Chaim Yehudah offered a reason for this ruling. Testimony requires that the witness be fit both at the beginning and at the end. Since the decisive time for testimony is when the claim becomes judicially relevant, if at the moment judgment was rendered the witness was still related, this is treated as though the time for his testimony had arrived while he was disqualified. The later removal of the relationship cannot repair the lack of fitness at the conclusion. There is also an analogy to the rule that once testimony has been given it cannot be given again: when the legally relevant time arrived and he was unable to testify, the later opportunity does not recreate the original testimony. He also explained the opinion in seif 17 regarding witnesses who were relatives of a person on his deathbed but unrelated to his heirs. Some authorities disqualify them. A gift of a person on his deathbed is upheld by rabbinic enactment so that his mind not become disturbed. The effectiveness of the gift gives the dying person satisfaction and prevents distress. Testimony supporting that gift may therefore be viewed as testimony that directly benefits and pleases the dying person himself. Since the witnesses are related to him, they can be disqualified even though the eventual property passes to heirs who are unrelated to them. This is a fine explanation.
