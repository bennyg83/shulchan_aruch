And some say that a third-degree relative to a first-degree relative is disqualified. This is Rabbenu Tam's view, based on the Yerushalmi which says that Moshe may testify for Pinchas's wife, implying that he may not testify for Pinchas himself. This indicates that first with third is disqualified. I heard in the name of my maternal grandfather, the renowned gaon R. Leib Tzintz, that Rabbenu Tam's proof can be rejected. The Yerushalmi may not be addressing third with first at all. It may follow Rabbah, as Rashi also writes on the Torah, that Elazar actually married one of Yitro's daughters. If so, Tzipporah, Moshe's wife, was Pinchas's aunt, and Moshe was disqualified to testify for Pinchas because this is first with second through one marital link. Yet he could testify for Pinchas's wife because that involves two marital links, according to Rosh and those who agree with him. The suggestion is elegant, but one who examines Rosh will see that the Bavli and Yerushalmi themselves disagree whether first with second through two marital links is valid. According to the Yerushalmi it is disqualified, while Rosh rules according to our Bavli. Therefore the Yerushalmi cannot mean the relationship just described, because on its own view Moshe would also be disqualified to testify for Pinchas's wife. Moreover, the Yerushalmi itself asks: "May a third generation testify for the wife of the first?" This plainly implies that testimony for the first himself is disqualified. That cannot be explained through the proposed marital relationship. Thus Rabbenu Tam's proof remains.
And some say that maternal relatives, etc. Shach discusses this at length and objects to Rama. Rama's wording suggests a practical difference for kiddushin: if a man betrothed a woman before maternal relatives, there may be concern for the kiddushin and she may require a get. Shach argues that Rambam often calls any law not written explicitly in Scripture but derived by the thirteen interpretive principles "Divrei Sofrim," as he does regarding kiddushin by money, even though the rule is fully Torah law. Therefore, according to Shach, there is no substantive legal difference between such a rule and one stated explicitly in Scripture. His complaint, however, is really against the source cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 42, where it is explicit that according to Rambam one should be concerned for such kiddushin. Kesef Mishneh also writes so. The deeper question is: from where did Rambam know that the Gemara's derivation, which repeats "fathers" twice and applies one occurrence to paternal relatives and one to maternal relatives, is merely an asmachta? The answer appears from Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah. The Mishnah says: "R. Yosi said: this is the teaching of R. Akiva, but the earlier Mishnah said: his uncle, his cousin..." Rambam explains that the phrase "and anyone fit to inherit him" belongs to R. Akiva's statement, not to the earlier Mishnah. Maharshal agrees. Rambam further understands "uncle and cousin" in the earlier Mishnah as paternal relatives. Nimukei Yosef likewise indicates that if "anyone fit to inherit him" belonged to the earlier Mishnah, it would be needed to exclude the thought that uncle and cousin meant only paternal relatives. Thus the ordinary meaning of those terms here is paternal kin. Now throughout the Talmud, when one Tanna derives a law from a verse, the Gemara commonly asks what the other Tanna does with that verse. Yet here, after the Gemara derives maternal relatives from the second occurrence of "fathers," it never asks what R. Yosi, who preserves the earlier Mishnah, does with that extra word. This indicates that the verse is only an asmachta according to R. Akiva. The earlier Mishnah is the Torah law, and R. Akiva agrees that originally only the paternal relatives were Torah-disqualified. He holds, however, that the Sages later added maternal relatives and relatives by marriage. R. Yosi disputes the later enactment and says the original Mishnah remained unchanged. This also explains the Mishnah's wording: "This is the Mishnah of R. Akiva, but the earlier Mishnah said..." If R. Akiva believed the earlier generation had simply taught the Torah law incorrectly, the phrase "earlier Mishnah" would be strange. Rather, the earlier teaching remained true as Torah law, and R. Akiva reports a later rabbinic expansion. Accordingly, the double use of "fathers" is only an asmachta for the rabbinic extension. I am therefore surprised that Shach rules so readily that kiddushin before maternal or marital relatives may be disregarded. Baal HaMaor also holds that the Gemara does not decisively reject the earlier Mishnah. The Gemara merely suggests that perhaps the halachah does not follow R. Yosi of the Mishnah but R. Yosi of a baraita. That is not a decisive resolution. Ramban disagrees, but his proof is not conclusive. He points to Amoraim who say maternal relatives may not testify. Yet they may have said so because they were themselves uncertain whether the halachah followed the earlier Mishnah, just as R. Yosef was uncertain. If the matter had been clear to them, why was R. Yosef uncertain? In a matter of possible married-woman prohibition, one should therefore be stringent in accordance with Baal HaMaor and Rambam. Perhaps Shach thought that even according to the earlier Mishnah many maternal relatives were already disqualified, because "uncle and cousin" could include maternal relatives, as Nimukei Yosef entertains. But this is strained. There is also support from the Yerushalmi. It derives that judges may not be relatives of the murder victim because they would be blood avengers, whereas Scripture says, "the congregation shall judge." But a blood avenger is only a paternal relative. If maternal relatives were Torah-disqualified equally, the proof would not fit cleanly. Rather, R. Yosi follows the earlier Mishnah, where uncle and cousin mean paternal kin, and thus the same relatives who are blood avengers are the Torah-disqualified ones. Therefore it is clear that one should be stringent like Baal HaMaor and Rambam. Even according to Shach's own theory, that maternal disqualification is derived through the thirteen interpretive principles and is fully Torah law, Rama's requirement of a get is still understandable. Baal HaItur, in the section on receiving testimony, cites Rav Hai Gaon from Kiddushin: a slave released through loss of other limbs requires a get shichrur because the derivation is rabbinic in form and not known to the masses. Rabbenu Tam explains that because people do not know this law readily, Chazal required a get shichrur. The same reasoning applies here. The public does not know that maternal relatives are disqualified. Therefore, even if the legal derivation is Torah-level, one can still require a get in order to avoid confusion. Kiddushin by money is different because that law is universally known. Thus Baal HaItur and Rav Hai Gaon support Rama's caution, and Shach's leniency is difficult. A further question arises in Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah. If the phrase "anyone fit to inherit him" belongs to R. Akiva, what does it add? It cannot mean that a maternal brother who does not inherit is valid, because Rambam explicitly rules that brothers and sisters, whether paternal or maternal, and their children, are disqualified. Nor can we simply say the phrase adds more inheriting relatives beyond those listed, because then a great-grandson could become disqualified whenever the intermediate heirs had died, since he might then inherit in their place. Yet Rambam explicitly validates third-degree relatives to first-degree relatives according to his system. Perhaps Rambam retracted in Mishneh Torah from his Commentary to the Mishnah, but that is difficult. Possibly "anyone fit to inherit him" refers to one who has a son from an Israelite woman, even a mamzer. Yevamot says "any son" includes a mamzer; since he is considered the person's son for inheritance, he is disqualified as a relative. By contrast, a child from a maidservant is not considered his son and does not inherit him even if later freed, so the relationship does not create the same disqualification. This requires further study. I also found a support for Rambam from the episode of the Golden Calf in Yoma. The Gemara says that those who sinned with witnesses and warning were executed by the sword, and explains the verse "one man against his brother, his companion, and his relative" as including maternal grandfather, maternal uncle, and daughter's son killed by the Levites. How could such relatives execute them if witnesses were required? If other witnesses testified, the actual witnesses were absent, yet capital law requires "the hand of the witnesses shall be first." If these same relatives were the witnesses, how could they testify against their relatives? The answer must be that maternal relatives are not Torah-disqualified. Their disqualification is rabbinic, exactly as Rambam holds. I discuss this at greater length in my novellae.
