Brothers with one another. The Mechaber follows Rambam in chapter 13 of Hilchot Edut and begins with brothers and their descendants because this disqualification is expressly rooted in the Torah. The verse says, 'Fathers shall not be put to death because of children.' It cannot mean literal punishment, because the Torah already states that each person dies for his own sin. Chazal therefore interpret it to mean that fathers are not condemned through the testimony of children, and conversely. From the plural wording they derive two brothers and their children, and from the verse's redundancy the remaining familial disqualifications that the Mechaber proceeds to enumerate.
And it is unnecessary to say third degree to second degree. Although the phrase is 'it is unnecessary to say,' the Mechaber still states it lest one think that third degree to first degree is different because three generations separate them, whereas third to second is one generation closer.
Therefore a father with his son's son is disqualified, and with his son's son's son, etc. The text should be rearranged: 'Therefore a father with his son's son is disqualified, because father and son are first degree to first degree like two brothers; and with his son's son's son,' etc. Then 'therefore' is clear: since first degree to second degree is disqualified, a grandfather and grandson are also disqualified. Rambam's presentation likewise supports this structure.
Some say third degree to first degree is disqualified. The Gemara itself contains a dispute. The disqualifying authority derives it from an extra conjunction in the verse. The poskim then dispute whether even according to that view the disqualification is Torah law or rabbinic, the scriptural redundancy functioning only as an asmachta. Rama therefore writes that some hold it only rabbinic. Rif and Rambam, who rule like the Gemara's validating opinion, hold third degree to first degree is fully valid even rabbinically. In the Derishah I explained the broader rules and showed that the same dispute concerns a man's wife's grandfather or great-grandparent relationships.
The practical difference is for witnesses to kiddushin. If kiddushin occurred before such witnesses and the woman later accepted kiddushin from another man, according to the stringent view she may require a get from both.
Some say maternal relatives too are disqualified only rabbinically. This is the correct reading. Perhaps their reason is that the verse expressly mentions 'fathers,' indicating paternal relationship is the primary Torah disqualification. Although the Gemara derives maternal relatives by saying that if the verse is unnecessary for paternal relatives it applies to maternal ones, this view may regard that derivation as an asmachta.
