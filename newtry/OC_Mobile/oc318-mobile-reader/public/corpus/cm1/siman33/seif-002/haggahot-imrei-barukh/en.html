(Shach, seif katan 9) For he will certainly repeat his original words so that he not appear a liar. Note: This requires study, for he can avoid giving false testimony by declining to testify, analogous to the migo that witnesses could have remained silent. He can also say that he no longer remembers the matter. One can further say that the principle that the prior ruling remains in force applies only where the original ruling on that same matter would still be legally proper now, but the claimant seeks a different route through an either-or claim. Here, by contrast, the witnesses were then ruled invalid because they were relatives. The law now returns to validate them once the relationship has ceased and they have left the disqualification. Even when the earlier ruling was issued, it was already known that once they ceased to be relatives they would be valid for this testimony. Thus the earlier ruling that they were then invalid stands together with the rule that they are now valid. This resembles the rule that so long as one later brings proof he can overturn the judgment: the first ruling was based on the absence of witnesses, while the later ruling changes because of the new evidence. This differs from the doubtful yavam in Yevamot 37b, where the same doubt previously adjudicated for the benefit of the yavam's children is later sought to be overturned without new evidence, merely through a newly framed either-or claim. The same applies to the Terumat HaDeshen's ruling regarding a found object.
