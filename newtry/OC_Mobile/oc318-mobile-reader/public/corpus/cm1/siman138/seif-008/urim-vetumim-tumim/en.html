The burden of proof rests upon the claimant. One might ask why, according to this, the Gemara needs to explain that a claimant is not believed to say, I rented it to you, because we say, Until now you suspected him of theft, and now you rented it to him without witnesses? Even if the object were unquestionably the claimant's and now emerged from another person's possession, why should he be believed to say, I rented it to him, and thereby extract it? All the more so here, where it has not yet been established that the object was ever entirely his. Necessarily, the Gemara teaches that one should not say he is believed through a migo that he could have claimed, You snatched it from me. The special presumption, Until now you suspected him of theft, defeats that migo. According to Rambam, however, who holds that even the plea, You snatched it, is not believed, the Gemara's reason initially appears unnecessary. We must say Rambam's novelty concerns an item ordinarily lent or rented. One might think that half the item remains presumptively the claimant's and therefore he can say, I rented it to you, even without proof. The Gemara teaches that relative to this opposing party the object is treated as one not ordinarily lent or rented, because the claimant himself previously treated him as a suspected thief and it is implausible that he then rented it to him privately. This also explains the Maggid Mishneh's statement in the name of Ramban and Rashba, cited by the Rama, that if the claimant says, I rented it to you before named witnesses who have gone overseas, he is believed through a migo that he could have claimed seizure. Why is a migo needed? The Gemara implies that but for the reason, Until now you suspected him of theft, he would be believed to say, I rented it to you, without witnesses. Where he says he rented before witnesses who are now abroad, that reason disappears because he did act with witnesses. One could answer that Ramban and Rashba speak even of objects not ordinarily rented. There a claim of rental would not ordinarily be believed, even if the object is known to be his, and therefore they need the migo of seizure. According to this, with objects ordinarily rented no migo of seizure is needed at all. He is believed to say that he rented them before witnesses who went overseas. Rambam can agree, since the presumption, Until now you suspected him of theft, does not apply where he in fact rented before witnesses. Thus there may be no practical dispute here except concerning objects not ordinarily rented. Examine this carefully.
And if he did not bring proof, he swears. The Shach in seif katan 12 cites Rabbenu Yerucham concerning two men who return from overseas with a woman and a bundle. Each man says, She is my wife and this bundle is mine, while the woman says, The bundle is mine and these two men are my slaves. Rabbenu Yerucham records a dispute whether, after each man gives her a get, she has any right to the amount remaining after her ketubah, and concludes that she receives nothing from the excess. The Shach says his words require study and appear mistaken. He cites Ritva on Kiddushin that when they voluntarily give her gittin, she certainly collects her ketubah from the bundle through a mima nafshach: either the bundle is mine, as I claim, or according to your claim that it is yours, pay me my ketubah from it. Rabbenu Yerucham must be discussing the later custom that a ketubah is collectible from movables, since the Gemara established the case according to Rabbi Meir, who allows ketubah collection from movables, and the halachah is not generally like Rabbi Meir. Ritva continues that the remaining amount is governed by the law of two persons holding a garment. I do not know why the Shach treated Rabbenu Yerucham's words as mistaken. Rabbenu Yerucham can fully agree that she certainly collects the ketubah through the mima nafshach. His statement that there is a dispute concerns only the excess. Rashba in his novellae to Kiddushin cites Rav Barzeloni that because the woman accepted two gittin and thereby contradicted her own claim, she has no share in the bundle. His intent must be the excess, since the Gemara itself clearly gives her the ketubah by mima nafshach. Rashba disagrees there, but that is no objection to Rabbenu Yerucham, who from the strength of his own analysis ruled like Rav Barzeloni rather than Rashba. Hence he accurately says there is a dispute and concludes that she receives none of the excess. There is no difficulty. From this bundle case we can derive a rule for two people holding a garment where one says, The whole garment is mine, and the other says, It is mine, but you have a lien upon it because I owe you one hundred. The latter creditor should receive one hundred through a mima nafshach: either the whole garment is mine, or according to your own version I have a lien of one hundred upon it, just as the woman says, According to my claim the bundle is mine, and according to your claim I have my ketubah lien upon it. Yet a difficulty remains. We rule that where one claims wheat and the other admits barley, the admission does not create liability because they are different matters. All the more so here, a debt of money and ownership of a physical object are different claims. Why is this not treated as wheat and barley, resulting in exemption from both? Likewise, when the woman claims, You are my slaves, she effectively denies the marriage debt and thus appears to waive the ketubah, just as a claimant who demands wheat is deemed to waive the admitted barley. The answer must be that the wheat and barley rule applies where the defendant has a presumption of possession. Against a possessor, a weak or inconsistent claim cannot extract money. See above siman 88 regarding seizure by the claimant. Here, however, no one has exclusive possession of the bundle. In such a case we do not apply the wheat and barley exemption, because the defendant lacks the possessory advantage. It is as though the claimant had already seized, in which case we rule above that the admission remains effective. Further study is required.
