Ramban and Rashba say that one who claims, "I rented it to him before so and so," can be believed because he has a migo that he could have said, "I seized it." It follows that according to authorities who do not believe a direct seizure claim, the rental claim likewise cannot be believed merely through that migo. The Gemara's reasoning that one who previously suspected another of robbery would not later rent the item to him without witnesses seems to imply that if he says he rented before witnesses, the rental claim has independent force. The explanation is that once the court says a sensible owner in such circumstances would rent only before witnesses, the item is no longer treated like an object ordinarily rented without documentation. Whether something is "ordinarily lent or rented" depends upon the relationship between the parties and their circumstances, not only the physical type of object. Where a person would rent only before witnesses, the class of ordinary rental objects becomes effectively like property not ordinarily lent or rented in this relationship. The claimant's assertion that he rented it before witnesses can therefore stand on its own. Talmudic labor law likewise recognizes that arranging witnesses for payment is burdensome and not ordinarily done. Thus a rental that by its nature would require witnesses is itself exceptional. Tumim writes that only property not ordinarily lent or rented requires the migo, while property ordinarily lent or rented supports the witnessed rental claim independently. In my view the better distinction is as above: once witnesses are required because of the parties' distrust, the particular property ceases to be "ordinarily rented" between them in the ordinary undocumented manner.
