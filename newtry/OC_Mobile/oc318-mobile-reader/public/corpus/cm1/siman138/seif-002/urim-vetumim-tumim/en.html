And if one says, The whole is mine, etc. See Sema seif katan 9, who writes that one who claims only half cannot take half by oath through a migo that he could have claimed the whole, because that would be a migo used to extract property. The Sema notes that in siman 82 the Rama cites authorities who do recognize a migo to extract. It is possible, however, based on Tosafot at the beginning of Bava Metzia, that even Rabbah, who in Bava Batra concerning a forged document accepts a migo that the claimant could have remained silent and relied on the document, agrees that an ordinary migo to extract is ineffective. In the forged document case the claimant could simply have remained silent, which is different. If so, even those who disagree in siman 82 may agree here. Tosafot also give other answers, including that we do not say a migo from one item of property to another. Sefer HaKeritut states this rule in its laws of migo. The Shach, however, in his rules of migo, siman 12, argues that according to Hagahot Mordechai this answer would imply that a person may not retain one item belonging to his fellow as security for a different debt, and we do not rule that way. I do not understand the Shach. He writes the same in siman 150 seif 4, but it appears that he combined two separate discussions in Hagahot Mordechai. On Rabbenu Tam's answer that no migo operates from one property claim to another, Hagahot Mordechai gives the example of one who holds Reuven's land as a mortgage for several years and then claims that the house in which Reuven lives is his. He is not believed through a migo that he could conceal the mortgage document and claim to own the mortgaged land. That is one rule. Later, regarding the theory of an old loan claim, Mordechai cites authorities that a person may not seize his fellow's garment merely because the fellow owes him money, and Hagahot Mordechai distinguishes between money and garments. These are entirely different rules. The first concerns using a possible claim to property in one's own possession as a migo to extract different property from another. The second concerns retaining another's property already in one's hands because of a separate debt. They are unrelated. Even if we do not follow the latter rule, we find no authority rejecting the former. It therefore remains practical law, and the Shach's words are surprising.
