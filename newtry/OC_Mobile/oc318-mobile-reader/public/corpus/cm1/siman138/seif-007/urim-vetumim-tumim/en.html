They divide as at first. The Sema in seif katan 14 cites Hagahot Mordechai that if one party consecrated the object while it was still in his possession, the consecration does not later lapse. The Shach in seif katan 9 questions this, arguing that Tosafot and the decisors said so only in the case of a bathhouse governed by the rule kol d'alim gevar, where one who seizes with beit din's permission seizes lawfully. Here, by contrast, the seizure was without beit din's permission. I do not understand his objection. Where one snatches the garment and the other was initially silent but later protested, the Gemara leaves unresolved whether the silence was an admission. If the garment were then placed in an alley after the seizure and no one presently held it, the law in this unresolved monetary dispute would be kol d'alim gevar, as explained below at the end of siman 139. If one then seized it and consecrated it, all would agree the consecration is effective. Can the fact that the same person maintained continuous possession from the original seizure somehow make his legal position weaker than if he had first set the garment down in an alley and then seized it again? Plainly not. The consecration therefore takes effect. It is true that the Shach follows his own position, for below he also questions whether kol d'alim gevar applies to an unresolved question of law. See what I wrote there.
