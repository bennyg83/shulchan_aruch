His words imply that if the first person who seized it remains completely silent when the second person later seizes it, we do not remove it from the second. This is difficult. The Rashba, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, writes that silence is treated as admission only in the case of a garment that both parties originally held. Where an object is in the sole possession of one person and another seizes it from him, the second is considered a robber even if the first remains completely silent, and the object is removed from the second. The Rashba proves this from the case of the metal ingot of Rabbi Abba, where no distinction is made between silence and protest. Here, once the garment was established as belonging to the first seizer because the other party remained entirely silent during the first seizure, even witnesses would not later undo that status in the manner explained above. Since it has already been established as belonging to the first and the second then removes all of it from his possession, the second seizure cannot be effective even if the first remains completely silent. This is exactly the case described by the Rashba, and the matter is clear.

An explanation can be given for the rule that if the first person seized the object and consecrated it, a later seizure by the second person is ineffective. Why should consecration be stronger than the first seizer himself? A transferee cannot have greater power than the transferor. The reason may be understood from the Rosh at the beginning of Bava Metzia, who explains that the rule, "whoever is stronger prevails," rests on the assumption that the person who truly owns the property will exert himself to bring proof or will risk himself for it, whereas the other will not, knowing that tomorrow his opponent may produce proof and remove it from him. Accordingly, when the second person succeeds in seizing the object from the first and we do not remove it from his possession, we can reason that had the truth been with the first seizer, he would have exerted himself to bring proof. But once the first has sold or consecrated the property, or died and bequeathed it, we plead on behalf of the purchaser or heir that had the first still been alive he might have produced proof, similar to the rules in siman 62 and siman 133, seif 4. Moreover, the first seizer has something comparable to the chazakah of an original owner, as Tumim writes in siman 139. The only reason to strengthen the second seizer is that we see him risking himself more than the first, and infer that if the challenger truly knew the property to be his, he too would risk himself, as Tumim explains. That reasoning does not apply to a purchaser or heir, who themselves claim only uncertainty and for whom beit din advances the definite claim. Since they do not personally know the matter with certainty, their failure to risk themselves proves nothing. We therefore leave the property in the status created by the first seizer, which resembles that of an original owner. With movable property there is also a simpler explanation. A seizer is at least no better than a robber, and an ordinary act of robbery generally causes the owner to despair of recovery, as explained in siman 368. Even according to the Shach, who disagrees there, it is possible that where there are no witnesses and beit din has already released the robber from liability, even the Shach agrees that there is ye'ush. The robber himself does not acquire through ye'ush alone. But if he then consecrates or sells the property, there is both ye'ush and a change of possession, which certainly effects acquisition. A later seizure by the second party is therefore ineffective.
