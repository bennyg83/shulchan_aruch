Shach explains that seizure can help where the dispossessed party was initially silent and only later protested because that is an unresolved Talmudic doubt. If he remained silent from beginning to end, however, the Gemara treats the silence as a clear admission, and therefore if he later reseizes the garment it is removed from him. This is difficult according to Rambam. Rambam generally treats every Talmudic "if you say" as a resolution of the prior doubt. Here the Gemara says, "If you say that when one seized before us we do not remove it, what if he consecrated it?" That would imply the seizure question was resolved in favor of leaving it with the seizer. Yet Rambam rules that if the second party later reseizes it from the first, even if the first had remained silent from beginning to end, they return to division. Kesef Mishneh explains that the original question of silence followed by protest was never conclusively resolved, and therefore the court leaves the garment with whoever currently holds it. He then adds that the Gemara's "if you say" supports that approach. But if Rambam treats "if you say" as resolution, the explanation appears self contradictory.
Lechem Mishneh answers that even though Rambam normally treats "if you say" as a resolution, Maggid Mishneh says here that we still do not remove the garment from the first seizer because the original question remains formally doubtful. This too is difficult, because every case with "if you say" begins as a question. Under Rambam's usual method, the later formulation resolves it. The correct explanation follows a view recorded in Shitah Mekubetzet. The real question concerning initial silence followed by later protest is not simply whether silence definitely means admission. Everyone agrees the silence creates some doubt. The question is what legal force that doubtful admission has. Since the garment itself was already in a state of dispute, perhaps even a doubtful admission is enough to leave it with the seizer. Or perhaps the original joint possession was itself treated as a kind of certainty, and a doubtful admission cannot remove that certain half right. Another formulation is that the later protest may reveal that the initial silence was not admission at all but confidence that the court saw the seizure. When the Gemara says, "If you say we do not remove it," Rambam reads this as deciding that the silence creates at least a doubtful admission sufficient for the current seizer to keep the garment. But it does not decide that the silence was a definite admission. Therefore, if the first party later reseizes it, his original half right is still substantial enough that the court does not remove it from him either. The result returns to division. Rambam's ruling is therefore fully consistent: the "if you say" resolves only the effect of the doubt in present possession, not the underlying fact as a certain admission.
