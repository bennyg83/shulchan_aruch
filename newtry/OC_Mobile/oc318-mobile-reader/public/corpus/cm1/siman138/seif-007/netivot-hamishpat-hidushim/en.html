They divide as at first. This applies specifically where the first party remained silent and only later protested, which is an unresolved question. But where he remained silent from beginning to end, the Gemara states plainly that his silence is treated as an admission. If he later seized it back, however, we remove it from him and return it to the first party who seized, provided he then protested. Shach. See the Beurim. Even if the first party remained completely silent when the second party seized, we nevertheless remove it from the second party. Even where he was initially silent and later protested, if the first party seized and consecrated it, the second party's later seizure is ineffective. Sema. The Shach writes that one can distinguish: consecration after seizure is effective only where the rule is "whoever is stronger prevails," but here, where the law is merely an unresolved question, the first party's consecration should not help. Tumim is surprised by this. In an unresolved question, where the property is lying in a public side area, the practical law is also "whoever is stronger prevails," and certainly so when it was already in his possession when the uncertainty arose.
