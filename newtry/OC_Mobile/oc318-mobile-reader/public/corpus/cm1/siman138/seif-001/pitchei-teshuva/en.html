It is presumed to belong to the one in possession of the house. See Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 121, concerning a widow whose ketubah was settled by beit din and who was allowed to keep her orphaned children with her, reside in her late husband's house, and administer the children's property as guardian. She later married Shimon, who undertook to support the children in return for a fixed annual payment and to reside with his wife and her children without charge in the house belonging to the orphans. After the woman died, a dispute arose between the orphans' guardians and Shimon concerning the contents of the house. The guardians claimed that everything found there belonged to the orphans, since the entire estate of their father had remained under their mother's control, and since the house itself belonged to the orphans while Shimon occupied it only in consideration of his support of them, the orphans were deemed in possession of everything in it. Shimon answered that he was the principal occupant of the house and the children were merely dependent upon him, so he should be deemed in possession of the house and its contents. He relied on the rule concerning Reuven who lives with Shimon in Shimon's house, arguing that a tenant who occupies the house alone is deemed its possessor because a lease for its term is treated as a sale, whereas the case in siman 138 concerns one who merely allowed another to live with him gratuitously. The responsum rules for the orphans. First, many authorities hold that the statement that a lease is a sale for its term applies only to the law of overreaching and not universally, as the Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 313. This also defeats Shimon's distinction between gratuitous and paid occupancy. Further, even according to those who generally regard a lease as a sale for its term, this case is different. That principle applies where the landlord conveyed the use of the body of the house by effective language, so neither party may retract and even the tenant's waiver does not return the tenancy without a new conveyance. Here the marriage agreement merely stated that the couple would live in the orphans' house without charge. Such wording by itself does not effect a conveyance, and the orphans or their guardians could in principle retract. Even if his continued residence is binding because the matter was stipulated at marriage, he has acquired no proprietary interest in the house, so his occupancy is not treated as a sale. Nor does his claim that he may sublet prove that he is the principal possessor. The responsum therefore concludes that Shimon is not deemed the possessor of the house and the law follows the orphans. See there and Netivot HaMishpat.
