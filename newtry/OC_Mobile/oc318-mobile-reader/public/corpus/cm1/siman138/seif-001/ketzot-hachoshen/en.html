Tosafot distinguishes the rule of two people holding a garment, where they divide, from the case of a third disputed maneh held by a custodian, where the money remains deposited. In the latter case, division cannot represent the truth because the custodian already knows that one of them deposited the whole amount, and there is no ordinary way for the depositor later to have transferred half while it remained in the custodian's hands. Rosh gives another explanation. In the third maneh case, before the litigants ever disagreed, the custodian already knew the money did not belong to both of them, because he knew that one person alone deposited it. Tumim asks against Rosh from the sale case in siman 222. There, the seller holds the item and accepted payment from two parties, one willingly and one under compulsion, and the law can be division. Yet the seller knows before their dispute that he intended the sale for only one buyer. That appears exactly like the third maneh case. The question can be raised against Tosafot as well. Just as in the third maneh case we say division cannot be true because the custodian knew one person deposited the whole and there is no ordinary later transfer of half, so too the seller says he consented to sell to only one and there is no ordinary later transfer of half while the item remains in his hands. The two cases therefore appear structurally identical.
According to what the Shach writes in siman 300, however, where there is no fraudulent claimant even the third maneh can be divided. The sale case in siman 222 is then readily understood, because neither buyer is necessarily lying. Each may sincerely believe the seller consented to him. Since there is no known fraud, division is possible under the Shach's approach, whether one follows Tosafot's explanation or Rosh's.
