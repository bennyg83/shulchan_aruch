That he has in this item, etc. Rif, Rambam, and Tur quote only the Mishnah's language that he swears that he has no less than half of it. The Rosh's words likewise indicate this. He infers from the requirement that one who claims three quarters must swear specifically to that amount that wherever a matter can be clarified in order to avoid possible fraud, we clarify it, even though all oaths are administered according to beit din's intent. If the Rosh held that the ordinary oath includes the words, I have a share in it and I do not have less than half, as Tosafot explain, he should have made his inference from that formula itself. It is therefore clear that he requires only the Mishnah's phrase, I do not have less than half, and does not accept the suggested evasion that these words could mean he has no share at all. Linguistically, I do not have less than half does not naturally bear that meaning. The Gemara's question is instead as follows. In either event, if he swears concerning what he possesses, meaning the amount to which he is entitled, he should simply swear, I have half, because that is precisely what he receives. If the oath is about what he does not possess, meaning that he must also deny the part he will not receive so his words do not contradict themselves, then he should swear that the entire garment is his. The Gemara answers that the Mishnah deliberately chose the phrase, I do not have less than half, because it can accommodate both sides: I have an interest in it, and I do not have less than half. Yet its primary sense remains that he has at least half. The alternative formula, The entire garment is mine, would be improper because beit din is not giving him the whole garment, while swearing simply, Half is mine, would weaken his original claim that all of it is his. Therefore the Mishnah chose a formulation that does not weaken his claim and does not make him swear to more than he receives. It is as though he says, The whole is mine, and according to your ruling I have no less than half. This is what the Beit Yosef and Rabbenu Ovadiah of Bartenura intend. I saw a printed explanation in the name of a Gaon that since we explain why one suspected concerning money is not automatically suspected concerning an oath by saying perhaps he has an old uncertain loan claim against the other, how can he evade by swearing, I do not have less than half, if in truth he has nothing? These words have no force. Precisely because he is uncertain whether an old loan is owed, his oath can truthfully be phrased this way in either event. If the old loan truly exists, then half is legitimately his and the statement that he has no less than half is true. If no old loan exists, then he has nothing in the garment and his language remains literally true only in the technical sense proposed by the Gemara. Thus the wording is chosen to avoid a definite falsehood in either scenario. The proposed objection has no substance.
And they divide it. The Sema in seif katan 6 cites Rashi and Ramban that where one of the litigants is certainly fraudulent, the property is left until Eliyahu comes. According to them, Tosafot's question from two persons holding a document, where the Mishnah says they divide although one must be lying, is no difficulty. There they are holding only the document, while the borrower is holding the loan money. If we say the document should remain deposited until Eliyahu comes, but not the money in the borrower's hands because the competing claimants do not possess it, the borrower benefits completely from the dispute and achieves exactly what he wants: the document is neutralized forever while he keeps the money without payment. Beit din cannot remove the money from the borrower and deposit it until Eliyahu comes, because with respect to the money the borrower is the possessor. The Sages therefore required the claimants to divide the monetary value of the document so that the borrower does not gain entirely and the creditor does not lose everything. When the Gemara compares the document case to two people holding a garment, its challenge is against the first Tanna, who says the document should be authenticated and the creditor collects. The question is proper: why should the creditor's position be stronger than the claimants in the garment case, especially here where the borrower actually possesses the money? The Shulchan Aruch and Tur, however, follow Tosafot and Rosh that even where one party is certainly fraudulent the litigants divide. Tosafot distinguish the case of three hundred deposited by two persons, where the disputed hundred remains deposited, because there the division cannot possibly reflect truth. There is no normal possibility that after depositing the money the owner transferred half to the other while it remained in a third party's custody. The Rosh gives a different reason: before the dispute arose, the depositary already knew that the disputed sum did not belong jointly to both and that only one of them deposited it. According to that reason there is a difficulty from below, siman 222, where the Tur and Shulchan Aruch rule that if merchandise remains in the seller's hands, and he received money from two purchasers, one willingly and one against his will, but cannot remember which, the purchasers divide. There too the seller knew before their dispute that only one transaction was voluntary and the item did not belong jointly to both. This seems exactly like a depositary who knows from the outset that the item is not jointly owned. If the Rosh intended Tosafot's distinction, namely that in a sale a division could conceivably be true while in a deposit it cannot, why did he formulate the entirely different consideration that the depositary knew the facts before the dispute, without stating Tosafot's principal reason? Pilpula Charifta appears to force Tosafot's language into the Rosh, but the Rosh's words do not naturally indicate that. The matter requires serious study, especially because in the Rosh's general discussion of purchases the case where the seller remains in possession and cannot remember whom he accepted willingly is not expressly addressed.
