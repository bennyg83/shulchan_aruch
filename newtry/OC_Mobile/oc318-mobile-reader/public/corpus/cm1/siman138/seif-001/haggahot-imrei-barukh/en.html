(Shach, seif katan 8) At the end of the passage: a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Note: See Shitah Mekubetzet on this sugya. The Ran explains that even if we conclude that one who was silent and only afterward protested cannot recover, this is not because his silence is treated as a certain admission. Rather, a doubtful admission is enough in the case of two people holding an object, because each one's original possession is itself not a powerful presumption. Therefore, if one later has the object in his sole possession, the doubtful admission is sufficient to weaken the other's original right based on joint possession. The Gemara's question is whether joint possession is treated as a full presumption, in which case a doubtful admission does not overcome it, or only as a doubtful possession, in which case the doubtful admission arising from silence followed by protest is enough. According to this view, the Bach's words are correct: witnesses can overcome such a doubtful admission. But where he remained silent from beginning to end, that is a certain admission and it defeats any testimony. This also resolves the Derishah's question here concerning the Rambam's rule that if the other later seizes it back they divide. According to the Ran, even if the Gemara's doubt is resolved in favor of treating the silence as an admission, it is still only a doubtful admission, and later proof or renewed seizure can affect the outcome.
