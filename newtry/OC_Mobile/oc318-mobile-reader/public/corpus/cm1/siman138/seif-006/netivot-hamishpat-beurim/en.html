Taz and Tumim rule like Bach that the statement that witnesses do not help applies only where he remained completely silent, but if he was silent and afterward protested, witnesses are effective. They bring a clear proof from the Gemara, which establishes the rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant in a case of silence followed by protest, showing that proof can indeed be effective. As for Taz's implication that even complete silence can be overcome by proof, Tumim is correct to reject it. Taz argues that even after an explicit admission a person can claim, "I was mistaken," as shown in siman 126. But there the admission was made before witnesses. An admission before beit din in response to a claim cannot afterward be retracted by saying, "I was mistaken," as shown in siman 81 and in siman 88 concerning one who is claimed against for wheat and admits barley, where later witnesses do not help. Likewise in Bava Metzia 36, where the Gemara says that if the other party remains silent, silence is like an admission, even though it is objectively clear that he cannot owe both claimants. This is also evident in many places. Still, if one seized the object before witnesses and the other remained completely silent, although Shach in seif katan 30 writes that witnesses are equivalent to beit din, it appears that this is only when the silent party does not claim that he was mistaken but merely says that his silence was not an admission. That claim has no force, because we regard such silence as an admission. But if he brings witnesses and claims that he was mistaken, he can certainly say so, as explained in siman 126, where even one who has a migo may claim mistake, all the more so when he has witnesses. The reason witnesses help after silence followed by protest, despite the Gemara leaving unresolved whether such silence counts as an admission, is as Ran explains, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. The Gemara's uncertainty is only whether it is a doubtful admission sufficient in a case where both are holding the object and ownership itself is doubtful. Where witnesses later establish the truth, a doubt cannot displace certainty. Ketzot HaChoshen writes the same, and the matter is straightforward.
