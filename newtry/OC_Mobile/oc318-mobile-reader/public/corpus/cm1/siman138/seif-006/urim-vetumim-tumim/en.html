From his fellow in our presence. The Shach in seif katan 6 writes that the decisors dispute whether seizure before witnesses is equivalent to seizure before beit din, and he says he ruled in his own work that it is treated as though done before beit din. We have not merited seeing that work, and I have found no authority equating witnesses with beit din. The Gemara's precise phrase, the Rabbis saw him, plainly indicates beit din, and no one disputes that when the seizure occurs before beit din, later protest does not restore the first possessor automatically. Some authorities, however, hold that where the seizure occurred only before witnesses, a protest made afterward is ineffective because the special factor that the Rabbis observed the seizure is absent. There is no difficulty from the incident of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba, where one seized from another and the Gemara does not state that the victim protested. The inference of admission from silence applies where both parties were already holding the item. Where one alone held it and the other snatched it, silence is not automatically admission. Ritva states this explicitly in the name of Ramban. Rashba disagrees and does not distinguish whether both initially held the object. He therefore infers from the incident of Rabbi Abba that when witnesses saw the seizure, even silence from beginning to end is not an admission. Nimukei Yosef and Ritva in Bava Metzia cite both views. But I have found no opinion saying witnesses and beit din are entirely equivalent, and the Gemara's wording does not support it. The Shach's ruling therefore does not appear correct. Shitah Mekubetzet records that Ritva and Ramban ruled against Rashba and held that before witnesses, silence followed by protest can still amount to admission because the element of the Rabbis seeing the act is absent. This is also reasonable. Although the Ritva before us leaves the matter unresolved, the testimony of the author of Shitah Mekubetzet is reliable, and Ritva's language itself leans against Rashba. It is true that the Maggid Mishneh writes, all the more so before witnesses, which sounds as though witnesses and beit din share the same rule. But this is not conclusive. He holds that even where one was silent from beginning to end and the other seized, the silence is a complete admission, yet if the first later seizes back we do not remove it from him. Under that approach there may be little practical distinction between witnesses and beit din. According to our ruling, however, that where the second party re-seizes after such uncertainty we may remove it from him, important consequences remain if the initial silence and later protest occurred only before witnesses. Examine this carefully.
For a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. The Bach holds that where the first possessor was silent from beginning to end, witnesses cannot help him, but where he was initially silent and later protested, even if we treat the initial silence as an admission, witnesses can prove that it was not a true admission and that his later protest was genuine. The Shach in seif katan 8 argues that once the initial silence is deemed an admission, what can witnesses accomplish, since a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses? His argument is incorrect. The Gemara initially attempts to resolve the question from the baraita stating that if the garment is found in one person's hands, the burden of proof rests on the other. It establishes this as a case where the first party was initially silent and later protested, and proposes that the silence is an admission. But if so, how can the baraita say the burden of proof rests upon the claimant? Even a thousand witnesses should not overcome the party's own admission. Necessarily, in the case of initial silence followed by later protest, external evidence can still show that the silence was not intended as an admission. Ritva expressly says that the Gemara did not establish the attempted proof as silence from beginning to end because then the phrase burden of proof would make no sense, since a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. This shows that if the case is initial silence followed by protest, the phrase burden of proof is coherent even under the proposed interpretation that the silence may count as admission. Thus everyone agrees that where a later protest exists, evidence can rebut the inference from earlier silence. The Bach is correct. Examine this carefully.
