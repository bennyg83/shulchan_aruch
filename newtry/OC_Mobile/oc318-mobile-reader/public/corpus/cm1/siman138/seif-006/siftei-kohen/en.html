[“We are like us. "And if it were before witnesses there was a dispute between the occupiers and the main thing seemed to us as if it had been before B.D. and a review of the books."
Although he later cried out, etc. The case where he later cried out remains an unresolved question in the Gemara. Where he remained silent from beginning to end, however, the Gemara states plainly that his silence constitutes an admission. It follows that even if the original possessor later seizes the article back, it is removed from him and returned to the first seizer when that seizer had cried out. The Rambam's and Mechaber's ruling in the next seif that they divide as at the outset applies only to the latter case, where he eventually cried out. See Responsa Maharan Sasson, siman 132, Responsa Rabbi Yosef of the House of Levi, siman 23, and Responsa Maharit, end of siman 106.
Others say, etc. The Bach disagrees with the Rama and maintains that the Rosh's statement that witnesses do not help applies only where the party remained silent from beginning to end, not where he later cried out. This is not a valid objection. If the Tur refers only to the first clause, the Rama likewise refers only to that clause, since the Tur places the Rosh's ruling in the same location as the Rama. Yet the plain meaning of both the Tur and Rama is that even in the latter case witnesses do not help. The reason appears to be that since the current possessor may say, 'He admitted it to me,' subsequent witnesses cannot overcome that admission, for a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. See Responsa Maharam Padua, siman 40, and Even HaEzer, page 87a.
