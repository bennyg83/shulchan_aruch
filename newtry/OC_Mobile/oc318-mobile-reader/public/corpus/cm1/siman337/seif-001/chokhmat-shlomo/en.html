Seif 1. A worker who performs labor for an employer. Note: Shach, seif katan 1, writes that the Gemara derives from 'your fellow's vineyard' that the worker's Torah right to eat applies to a fellow Jew's vineyard and not consecrated property, and similarly not to a non-Jew's property, according to the view that theft from a non-Jew is prohibited. My student, Rabbi Yaakov Segan HaKohen, asked me that our printed Gemara appears to state the opposite. I answered that the Shach evidently did not have our reading. He either omitted entirely the phrase, 'this is why the verse was needed to permit the worker,' or had a different reading and interpreted the Gemara as follows. The baraita's phrase 'your fellow's vineyard, but not that of a non-Jew' means that in a non-Jew's vineyard even the worker's eating is forbidden because the Torah's special permission does not apply. The Gemara asks: this works according to the opinion that theft from a non-Jew is prohibited, because a verse is then needed to tell us that the worker may not eat. But according to the opinion that theft from a non-Jew is permitted, why would the verse be needed, since the worker could eat no less than an ordinary person could take? This must be the Shach's text and interpretation. Thank God, I found precisely this in Shitah Mekubetzet in the name of Ritva, who explains that according to the view prohibiting theft from a non-Jew, the Torah can tell us that the worker may not eat because the non-Jew himself is not subject to the mitzvah, whereas according to the view permitting such taking the exclusion becomes difficult. Shitah Mekubetzet further cites Ritva that 'your fellow' excludes both a non-Jew and consecrated property. This is exactly the Shach's position. See also what I wrote in my work on Orach Chaim, siman 637, regarding this sugya according to the Yere'im.
