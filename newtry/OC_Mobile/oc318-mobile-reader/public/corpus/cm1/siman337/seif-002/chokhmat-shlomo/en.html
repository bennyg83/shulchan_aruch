Seif 8. This applies where the worker knows that the produce has already been processed, but if he did not know and thought that it had not yet become obligated in tithes, the owner must tithe it and feed him. Note: See the Sema, who explains this on the basis of mistaken agreement, and see Tosafot Yom Tov to Bava Metzia, chapter 4, where the source of the law is clearer. I am uncertain about its precise scope. If the basis is indeed mistaken agreement, then ordinarily either party to a mistaken bargain may retract. Perhaps, therefore, the rule that the owner must tithe and feed the workers applies only where the owner wants to hold them to their employment and compel them to finish, in a situation where the workers otherwise could not legally withdraw. The workers can then say, 'We agreed to this wage only on the assumption that we would be entitled to eat.' If the owner insists on performance, he must therefore tithe and feed them. But if the owner does not insist that they continue and merely says, 'If you wish, work under these terms without eating; if not, leave,' perhaps the workers cannot force him to tithe and feed them, because a mistaken agreement is voidable by both sides. Likewise, if they already performed part of the work, perhaps the rule means only that if the original wage agreement remains in force, then the owner must add the food right because that was part of the workers' understanding. But if the true value of the work is now less than the agreed wage, the owner could perhaps say that he contracted according to the actual legal rule that they were not entitled to eat. If they were mistaken, the wage agreement falls away and the work should be appraised at its fair value. On the other hand, perhaps the owner has no such right to retract. Since ordinary workers in such produce are entitled to eat, the contract is implicitly made on that basis, and because an employer ordinarily cannot withdraw after hiring, he remains bound and must specifically tithe and feed them. I have not yet found an explicit source resolving this. I further believe that even if the workers can compel the owner to tithe and feed them, that should apply only where, according to ordinary experience, the majority of cases support their assumption, as Tosafot Yom Tov explains in the name of Maggid Mishneh. Most produce is not fourth-year produce, and most produce has not yet completed the stage that obligates tithing, while the exceptional cases are uncommon. Workers are not expected to anticipate unusual circumstances, so the ordinary contract is understood to include their right to eat and they did not need to stipulate it. But where the two possibilities are both common, the workers cannot simply say, 'We assumed the favorable case.' In such a setting it was for them to stipulate, and without a stipulation the burden is against them. This law therefore should not be generalized beyond cases where the ordinary majority supports the workers' understanding. This appears correct to me.
