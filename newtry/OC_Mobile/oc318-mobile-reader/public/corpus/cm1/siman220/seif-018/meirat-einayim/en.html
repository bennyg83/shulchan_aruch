If there were mothers and daughters in it at the time of sale, etc. This is the correct reading, exactly as in the Rambam, chapter 23 of Hilchot Mekhirah. The rule comes from the Mishnah and Gemara in HaMokher Et HaSefinah, where the textual versions and interpretations of Rashbam, Tosafot, and the Rif differ. In the Derishah and Perishah I explained them at length, and there I wrote that the Rambam follows the Rif's text. Here I will state only what is needed to explain the Rambam and Mechaber and to show that the Ir Shushan did not correctly preserve their wording. The Mishnah says: “One who buys the offspring of a dovecote from his fellow allows the first brood to fly.” The Gemara asks from a baraita that says, “the first and second brood,” and Rav Kahana answers: “There is no contradiction, one refers to her and one to the mother.” According to the Rif's version, both the Mishnah and baraita speak where, at the time of sale, the dovecote already contained mother birds and daughter birds, while the seller sells only their future offspring, not the birds themselves. Enough offspring must be left with each existing group for companionship so that the dovecote will not be deserted. The Mishnah concerns what must be left with the mother birds. The purchaser leaves them one additional brood, which will remain with the mother together with the daughter birds that already existed at the time of sale. Once the mother has two companion groups, later offspring can be taken by the purchaser without fear that she will flee. The baraita concerns the daughter birds already present at the sale. From the offspring of those daughters, two broods must be left for their companionship, just as a brood was left for the mothers, because otherwise the daughters may flee. The Gemara then asks why the mother is satisfied with the daughters and the additional brood left for her, while the daughters are not satisfied with their mother and that additional brood. It answers that a mother is content with her daughter, but a daughter is not content merely with her mother, and therefore the daughters require their own two broods. This, with minor expansion for clarity, explains the Rambam and Mechaber. When the Mechaber concludes, “and everything born after the two broods of the daughters and the first brood of the mothers belongs to the purchaser,” the word “after” governs both clauses. It means: everything born from the daughters after the daughters have produced the two broods left for their companionship, and everything born from the mothers after they produce the additional first brood left for them, belongs to the purchaser. The Rambam and Mechaber do not state whether the offspring later born from those retained companion broods themselves must also be partly left or may all be taken. They follow the abbreviated Mishnah and Gemara, which do not address that issue. Rashbam, according to his approach, says no additional companion offspring need be left for those broods, because after so many births they are content with the mothers already present. Tosafot, according to their approach, say the first offspring of those retained broods must also be left for companionship. The Ran, explaining the Rif, expressly says the matter is doubtful, and the Rambam's intention is likewise uncertain because he follows the Rif. The Maggid Mishneh appears to understand the Rambam in the manner I have explained. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who omitted the crucial word “after” and wrote flatly that all offspring born from the two broods of the daughters and the first brood of the mothers belong to the purchaser. That alteration is contrary to Tosafot, ignores the Ran's doubt, and does not faithfully explain the Rambam. Although the Tur also cites the Rambam without the word “after,” his own surrounding discussion shows that he understood the Rambam differently and reconciled him with Rashbam's version. The Ir Shushan, however, gives only the Rambam's formulation and therefore cannot be defended that way.
