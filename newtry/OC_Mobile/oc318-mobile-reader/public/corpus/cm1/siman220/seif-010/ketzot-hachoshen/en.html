If the animal is pregnant, the fetus is included in the sale. Sema notes that although Rosh states this, Tur appears to distinguish between saying "this cow" and merely "a cow." Where he says "this cow," the buyer sees the animal before him and its pregnancy is included. But where he says simply "a cow," the seller can choose whichever cow he wishes to provide, and the fetus is not necessarily included. Taz raises a difficulty from the Mishnah concerning one who exchanges a cow for a donkey, where the dispute is only when the birth occurred. If the seller had said merely "a cow," then under the rule that the fetus is not sold the time of birth should be irrelevant. If he said "a pregnant cow," then under the opposite rule the fetus should be included even if born before the later dispute. The early authorities already addressed this. Shitah Mekubetzet, citing Rabbeinu Yonatan and others, establishes the Mishnah where the seller indeed sold a pregnant cow but later says that he discovered the cow had already given birth before the sale. He offers either to provide another pregnant cow or to cancel the sale. The buyer claims that this very cow was still pregnant at the time of sale and that the calf was born in his domain. Since a fetus in utero has a different value than a born calf, the dispute remains meaningful. This establishes a real disagreement among the authorities and answers Taz's question.
There is strong proof that even without saying "pregnant," if one says "this cow" or "this maidservant," the fetus is included. The Mishnah in Temurah discusses one who says, "The offspring of this animal is an olah and the mother a shelamim," or first says, "This animal is a shelamim," and afterward says, "Its offspring is an olah." Rabbi Yosei distinguishes whether the second designation was intended from the outset or arose only afterward. If saying merely "this animal is a shelamim" did not include the fetus, then the fetus would remain unconsecrated. When he later says, "Its offspring is an olah," that later consecration should unquestionably work. This would be analogous to Rabbi Yochanan's rule that one who separates a pregnant sin offering can choose whether to use the mother or the fetus because the fetus is not legally identical with the mother's body and can be separately reserved. The fact that Rabbi Yosei can treat the fetus as already consecrated with the mother shows that saying "this animal" includes its fetus. One cannot distinguish consecration on the ground that one consecrates generously. A gift is given more generously than consecration, yet Tur and Rambam distinguish generosity chiefly in land, where attached appurtenances can be included. With movables there is no general rule that consecration includes more than sale. Thus the proof stands: one who sells "this cow" includes its fetus. If he sells merely "a cow," however, he may deliver whichever cow he chooses, as Sema explains.
