In all of these cases the owner shall swear, etc. And the possessor shall swear that he owes him nothing for the produce, etc. There is room to question what exactly the owner claims regarding the produce. If he claims, "you consumed the produce for three years unlawfully," as the Rambam's language in chapter 16, halachah 8, appears to indicate, then the owner's own admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses: he has admitted that the possessor occupied for three years. Why, then, should the land be returned to him? One might suggest that because there are no witnesses to the chazakah, the owner has a migo to say that he never sold the land, since he could have denied that the possessor consumed for all three years. But we rule like Rabbah regarding land that wherever it stands it remains, meaning that the current possessor is treated as in possession of the land. Such a migo for the owner would therefore appear to be a migo to extract property. One could instead say that the owner claims only, "you consumed for two years," and on that claim he may impose an oath. But regarding the third year's produce he could not impose an oath, because he himself says the possessor did not consume that year. If so, what of the case where one witness testifies that the possessor consumed for three years? How can the owner impose an oath, when that witness assists the possessor by saying that he possessed the property properly? This is no difficulty. The witness did not testify that the possessor purchased the land. He testified only that he consumed for three years, while the owner claims with certainty, "I never sold it to you." The witness therefore provides no assistance regarding the owner's claim for the produce itself. There remains an ordinary oath of denial, as the Bach and Knesset HaGedolah write. It seems to me, however, that the Tur and Mechaber's phrase "in all of these cases" does not refer to the preceding case involving the third year's produce, because there the possessor is not liable to pay that produce and should not even need an oath concerning it, as just explained. Yet this is strained in the phrase "in all of these cases." I therefore prefer the following. Although we rule like Rabbah that with land the possessor's migo is not considered a migo to extract because the land remains where it presently stands, that does not mean he is an absolute and unquestioned possessor. The very issue being litigated is whether his chazakah is valid, while the original owner has definite ancestral title. We nevertheless do not classify the possessor's migo as one used to extract because he is physically holding the land. All the more so, if the owner has a migo, it is certainly not a migo to extract, because he stands upon his established ancestral title and there is doubt only regarding whether the current possessor acquired a valid chazakah. Thus, both parties' migo claims, that of the owner and that of the possessor, fall outside the category of migo used to extract: one holds the land physically, while the other holds the established ancestral title. Accordingly, the owner can indeed require an oath even regarding the third year's produce. He may say: "It is true that you consumed it, but you consumed it unlawfully." Nevertheless the possessor does not acquire the land, because the owner has a migo. One should not object that the owner seeks to impose an oath concerning the third year's produce, and if he instead said that the possessor never consumed for three years there would be no oath. Regarding the land itself, the owner's present claim, that the consumption was unlawful, is accepted by migo, as the Ramban writes concerning the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. Moreover, if the owner is lying, the possessor will merely swear and the owner gains nothing improper from his formulation. Consider this carefully.
