He may strike him. See Mishneh LaMelech and Tumim, who ask that in siman 421, seif 6, in the gloss, it is written that only regarding a servant, where one fears that he may steal and there is a potential loss, is one permitted to strike him, but otherwise it is forbidden. It therefore appears that Rambam was troubled by the question raised by Mishneh LaMelech from Bava Kamma 28, where the Gemara says: although you have permission to remove him, you do not have permission to injure him. In my humble opinion, the explanation is as follows. It is obvious that where the other person has a claim and says that he is rightfully holding the property, that he seeks judgment, and that he will obey the ruling of beit din, the owner may not strike him. This is a case where the owner can recover his property by another means, and it is forbidden to strike him, as explained in Bava Kamma 28 regarding a case where it was possible to pull the item away from him. Even if the other person offers no claim at all, however, it is still difficult to say that one may strike him initially where there is no potential loss. Even beit din itself does not immediately use force against the body of one who refuses to comply. Rather, beit din sends its agent to remove the property against his will. If he does not allow the agent of beit din to act, they place the recalcitrant person under a ban. Only if he then disregards the ban is it certainly permitted to strike him until he complies. Why, then, should the owner himself be permitted to strike him immediately? This too is a case where he can save his property another way, namely by going to beit din. If the other person does not obey the ruling of beit din, they will place him under a ban, and Jews are not suspected of disregarding a ban. It is also possible that beit din will send its agent and remove him from the house without any striking at all. Furthermore, perhaps the other person has a claim that he wishes to reveal only before beit din, and he will obey its ruling. See Nimukei Yosef. The private owner is no better than an agent of beit din. Nevertheless, if he already struck him and beit din is uncertain whether even they could have recovered the property without striking him, he is exempt for the injury. Initially, however, it is difficult to permit striking. It therefore appears that Rambam and Shulchan Aruch distinguish between land and movable property. With movable property there is a concern of loss, and even initially he may strike the person in order to recover it. With land, where there is no concern of loss, he may not strike initially. There is also a distinction between the rule initially and the rule after the fact. When the Gemara says that in a place of loss everyone agrees that a person may execute judgment for himself, it refers to a definite loss, such as the case of the water vessel discussed there, where no water remained for the other person to draw and there would be certain damage to his field. Beit din would not collect that damage for him under the law because the other party is merely an indirect cause and is exempt. There, it is certainly permitted to act even by striking, because he will unquestionably suffer damage and beit din cannot compensate him for it. Where there is no certain loss but there is nevertheless a concern of loss, such as where another person has seized his movable property, then even though beit din will eventually collect it from the possessor, there is still a concern that he may hide it or damage it. That is the dispute in Bava Kamma, and we rule that even in such a case a person may execute judgment for himself. Where, however, he can certainly recover his property through beit din without any concern of loss, such as the case described in siman 421, where his house will certainly not be harmed merely because the other person remains there for a short time while he goes to beit din and beit din sends someone to remove him, Rambam and Shulchan Aruch hold that he may not strike him. Since there is no concern of loss, it is a case where he can recover by another means, and where another means is available it is forbidden to strike, as explained in that sugya. If he already struck him, however, and after he comes before beit din they are uncertain whether perhaps even beit din itself could not have effected the removal except through striking, such as where the person would not have obeyed beit din until they too were compelled to strike him, then he is exempt. But if it is clear that beit din could have accomplished the result without striking, so that the owner could have recovered by another means, he is liable. This is the case discussed in Bava Kamma 28, as is evident from the sugya there. According to the opinion that a person may not execute judgment for himself, he is liable after the fact in every case. Therefore, since the baraita in Bava Kamma teaches only that he is exempt after the fact, the Gemara challenges only the opinion that a person may not execute judgment for himself. According to the opinion that he may do so, there is no difficulty, because the case must necessarily be one where he could not save his property by another means. If he could have done so, he would be liable, as is evident there. As to acting initially, Rambam and Shulchan Aruch therefore correctly rule in siman 421 that it is forbidden. Why should a person rely upon his own assessment and thereby risk violating a prohibition by striking his fellow and rendering himself wicked when there is no concern of loss? Where the other party has a claim and says that he wishes to obey the ruling of beit din, it is certainly forbidden to strike him in the absence of a definite loss, as explained above.

This is difficult, for the source of this ruling is Maharik, and Maharik states the opposite. Tumim likewise questions Sema. In my humble opinion, the words of the Rama may be explained by first considering the Gemara in Bava Kamma 28. The Gemara challenges the opinion that a person may execute judgment for himself from the Mishnah concerning one whose field contained a public path and who took that path for himself and gave the public another path at the side. The Mishnah says that what he gave remains given, while his original path does not become his. The Gemara asks that if a person may execute judgment for himself, why does he not take a staff and sit there to prevent the public from using his original path? Tosafot explain that the initial assumption is that his original path does not become his because he cannot summon every passerby to litigation. Two matters are difficult to me. First, since we rule in siman 106 that collection may be made even in a person's absence, and certainly stolen property may be recovered in his absence, and the case here must be one where the owner can prove his right, since otherwise all agree that he may not execute judgment for himself, why should an agent of beit din not take a staff and sit there? Alternatively, beit din could authorize the owner himself and appoint him as their agent to do so. Second, according to the opinion under discussion that mere seizure without striking is permitted, even according to the view that a person may not execute judgment for himself, although taking a staff would be forbidden, why could he not block the road without striking, for example by fencing it or digging a trench? Surely passersby can be prevented without the use of a staff. The first difficulty may be answered from Rambam, Hilkhot Shekhenim chapter 4. Regarding a house and upper story that collapsed, Rambam rules that if the owner of the lower house is absent, the owner of the upper story builds, and so forth. Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam follows the Yerushalmi, which asks whether one collects from a person in his absence. Nimukei Yosef and Beit Yosef likewise write that Rambam holds that collection is not made in a person's absence. Tumim, siman 106, seif katan 1, explains that the rule permitting collection in a person's absence is an enactment so that a person should not take another's money and travel overseas. That rationale does not apply to a house, because such conduct is uncommon. Here too, the landowner himself caused the situation by exchanging the paths and effectively adjudicating for himself against people whom he cannot summon to beit din. Everyone agrees that one does not collect from an absent party in such a case. It is also possible to say that because the only obstacle to summoning all of them is the burden involved, collection in their absence is not permitted. The second difficulty can likewise be resolved. Maharik permits seizure without striking only when the seizing party thereafter immediately goes to beit din, as the Rama writes. In the case of the public road, he will never ultimately litigate with all of them. Therefore, even mere seizure is forbidden. Once we have established that mere seizure is prohibited when the seizing party does not immediately proceed to litigation, it follows without question that where he does seize and immediately goes to beit din, seizure is permitted. Tumim proves this from Ketubot 85, where Rav Pappa and Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua seized property belonging to orphans, although their claim was not to the specific objects but arose from another debt. This is the intent of the Rama in the gloss. He first writes that a person may execute judgment for himself only regarding an object clearly established as his, meaning that he claims ownership of the very object. He then writes that some say an act is called executing judgment for oneself only when one harms the other person, such as by striking him. Therefore, he may not do so unless he can prove that the object is his. The meaning is that he must have a claim to the very object as his own. The Rama mentions the ability to prove the claim because if he cannot prove it, then even if he claims the very object, he may not execute judgment for himself. The principal point, however, lies in the words "that it is his." His intent is that when he harms the other party, he may execute judgment for himself only with respect to something that is itself his, whereas he may not harm the other person in order to collect a different debt. The Rama then concludes that mere seizure, where he takes property as security, is permitted in every circumstance. This means even where he does not claim the specific object itself, but seizes it for an obligation arising, for example, from theft. Such conduct is not considered executing judgment for oneself because he seizes the object and immediately goes to beit din. If, however, he does not go to beit din and instead retains what he seized as his own collection, that too is considered executing judgment for oneself, and it is permitted only where he has a claim to the very object. This is precise in Maharik's language. He writes that to hand property over to non Jews who are prone to make false accusations is certainly an act of executing judgment for oneself, and one may not do so unless the property is known to be his, and so forth. There is an evident omission in Maharik's words, and they should read: "It further appears that the question whether one may execute judgment for oneself applies only when he harms or causes loss to the other person, as I wrote above." The entire sugya in HaManiach concerns cases where the defendant suffers bodily or monetary loss. Where the debtor suffers no loss from the seizure, the principle that one may not execute judgment for oneself does not apply, for what act of judgment is involved? It is merely seizure. Striking another person, by contrast, can properly be called executing judgment, because it resembles the act of a judge, who has authority to pursue and compel a party, whether physically or financially. Likewise, if we could say that even where the seized object is not itself the subject of the claimant's right, but he is owed different property, he may nevertheless take the object for himself as collection, meaning that he collects for himself without beit din, then the question whether one may execute judgment for oneself would properly apply. There he too acts as a judge who descends to the property of the liable party and collects it for the party entitled to payment. Maharik therefore had to prove that in such a case we do not say that a person may execute judgment for himself, since his claim is not to that particular property. It follows explicitly from Maharik that whenever a person seizes property as actual collection, meaning that he does not go to litigation but collects for himself without beit din, this is called executing judgment for oneself, and it is forbidden unless he claims the very object itself. Mere seizure, by contrast, meaning that he takes possession and immediately goes to beit din, is not called executing judgment for oneself at all. It is only seizure, not an act of a judge, and it is permitted in every circumstance, meaning even where he has no claim to the object itself and his claim arose because the other person became indebted to him through theft. Nevertheless, seizure is certainly permitted only where it will be legally effective before beit din, such as where there are no witnesses to the seizure, or where it will be effective because he can assert a claim of kim li, as in Maharik's case. To seize in the presence of witnesses when he will not be believed before beit din is certainly forbidden. Indeed, it appears even more strongly that although he knows he could later be believed before beit din through a migo that he returned the object, he still may not seize it in the presence of witnesses. By doing so he will become established as a robber and thereby render himself disqualified, as explained in Bava Batra in the case of the metal ingot of Rabbi Abba. How could one imagine that this is permitted? Once he becomes established as a robber, he is subject to lashes. The incident of Rav Huna in Berakhot, cited by Mordechai, must therefore have been either without witnesses, or Rav Huna knew that the sharecropper would never go to beit din, or he knew that the sharecropper would admit the matter before beit din. It is impossible to say, Heaven forbid, that Rav Huna acted in a manner that would cause him to be established before beit din as a robber and a disqualified person. The impropriety there was that he took other branches and did not go to beit din. That was executing judgment for himself because he acted like a judge who collects for himself. Such self judgment is permitted only where he claims the very object as his, as Maharik writes. Maharik's words can now also be explained in a way that answers the questions raised against him by Maharshal and Tumim. In Maharik's case, the community had seized sacred utensils in order to assert a kim li claim. The questioner argued that their seizure was unlawful, because one may not execute judgment for oneself where the matter is not clearly established, and he sought to prove this from Mordechai, who writes that the rule applies to an object that is clearly established, and so forth. The questioner understood this to mean that the claimant must have proof. Maharik answered first that mere seizure is not called executing judgment for oneself. Even if it were so called, he further answered that Mordechai's phrase "an object that is clearly established" does not mean that the claimant possesses proof. If that were its meaning, Mordechai's proof from the prohibition against entering the debtor's house to take a pledge would be difficult. Rather, Mordechai means, as Maharik explains, an object with respect to which the claimant asserts that the very object is his. This excludes a case where he seeks to collect that object on account of a different obligation. In that situation he may not execute judgment for himself. Mordechai can therefore properly prove the point from the prohibition against taking a pledge, for the Torah teaches that in such a case a person may not act as his own judge. Maharik then brings another proof from Mordechai's answer that Rav Huna took different branches. If the questioner's interpretation were correct, Mordechai could simply have answered that Rav Huna had proof. Maharik afterward writes that one might bring support for the questioner from another passage of Mordechai, that without proof a person may not execute judgment for himself, but he rejects that proof because action through non Jews is different. Maharik then challenges the questioner, according to the textual correction I wrote above, that even if Mordechai meant as the questioner understood, the principle of executing judgment for oneself applies only to striking, or to conduct such as Rav Huna's, where one collects a different item for himself without beit din and never goes to litigation, thereby acting like a judge who descends to the liable party's property and transfers it to the entitled party. The same is true where one takes the property as actual collection for oneself. But taking possession and then proceeding to beit din is not executing judgment for oneself at all. It is merely seizure, and it is permitted. Tumim understood Maharik differently and therefore questioned him, but the clear explanation is as I have written. To seize property on account of an ordinary loan debt is certainly forbidden by the verse, "You shall not enter his house." Whether one may seize for a debt arising from an iska, half of which has the status of a deposit, will be explained in siman 97, with Hashem's help.
