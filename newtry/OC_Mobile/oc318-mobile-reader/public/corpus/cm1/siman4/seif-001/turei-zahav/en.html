In the Shulchan Aruch, gloss to seif 1. “Some say this applies specifically to his debt,” etc. The dispute concerns the interpretation of the baraita in the chapter HaMekabel, which says, “You shall not enter his house.” The Rivash understands that one may enter the house of a guarantor, and that whenever the matter is not an actual debt, one may act even without permission of beit din. By contrast, with an actual debt, even permission of beit din does not permit entry. Here, no permission of beit din is required at all, so there are two grounds for leniency. This is explicit in the Rivash, who writes that by virtue of the rule that a person may execute justice for himself, he may retain what belongs to him even though he could obtain relief through beit din. The latter opinion, that of the Mordechai, holds that in a matter that is not a debt, permission of beit din is nevertheless required. He proves this from the first chapter of Berachot, where Rav Huna was punished for withholding the tenant farmer's vines and thereby taking the law into his own hands, although the prohibition “You shall not enter his house” did not apply there. Accordingly, the baraita's statement, “but you may enter the house of a guarantor,” means with permission of beit din. A matter that is not a debt is therefore preferable to a debt in only one respect. The Sema strained unnecessarily to emend the text because he did not examine the sources carefully.
