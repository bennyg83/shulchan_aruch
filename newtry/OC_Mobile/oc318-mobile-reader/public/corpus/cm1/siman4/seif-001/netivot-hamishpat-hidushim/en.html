That he can establish it. Meaning, if he cannot prove his claim, then even without striking the other person he may not seize in the presence of witnesses. If he seizes without witnesses, however, he may do so, because he will be believed through a migo, and this is treated as though he can establish the claim. See Beurim, seif katan 1, that striking is permitted only where there is at least a concern of loss, such as movable property which might be concealed or removed. But if someone enters his house and refuses to leave, and there is no concern of loss, he may not strike him and must instead go to beit din. If he already struck him, then if it appears that even beit din could not have rescued the property without force, he is exempt for the striking. If rescue was possible without striking, he is liable. See further there that even where a creditor may seize and has already seized, the debtor may satisfy him with other property and remove the seized item from his possession. It therefore appears that the text should be emended to read, "It is permitted for one to enforce his own right," meaning even by striking. The novelty is that self enforcement by force is permitted. Yet the Rama's subsequent statement, "Some say this is not called self enforcement," giving as an example striking him, implies that the preceding discussion concerned mere seizure. We must therefore say that this latter "some say" refers back to the beginning of the siman, where it states that self enforcement is allowed only when the claimant can prove his right. This implies that where he cannot prove it, even mere seizure is forbidden. Against this, the Rama records another opinion that simple seizure is always permitted. Thus far the Sema. In every case. The Sema writes that there is a distinction between one who seeks to seize the very item that was stolen from him, where the other party is presumed to deny the theft, and one who claims that the other owes him from a different matter and fears that the debtor will leave or later claim insolvency. In the first case, where he cannot prove the theft, he may not seize even without witnesses. In the second, he may take a pledge because it is not certain that the debtor will deny the debt. See Beurim, seif katan 3, that in truth one may never seize in the presence of witnesses where he cannot prove the claim. He may seize only without witnesses. The distinction is instead as follows: where he can prove the claim, the first opinion permits self enforcement only for recovery of an object taken by theft, not for collection of a debt. The latter opinion then teaches that without striking he may seize in every case, meaning even for a debt that did not arise from a loan, provided he immediately submits the matter to adjudication. Where he cannot prove the claim, he may seize only without witnesses. In the presence of witnesses, seizure is forbidden even if he would later have a migo of having returned the seized item. For a loan debt, seizure is forbidden. The law of a business investment debt will be explained below in siman 97:
