But mere seizure to hold as collateral. Maharshal, Perek HaMeniach, siman 5, strongly disagrees with Maharik and concludes that a person may not seize another's property as collateral even in matters where taking collateral is not independently prohibited, such as for robbery or guaranty, unless the seized object is itself clearly and indisputably his. It appears, however, that this restriction concerns actively seizing the other's property. If he already holds the other's collateral or deposit, he may withhold it even initially in order to save his own money. This is not considered taking the law into his own hands, because he does not actively seize anything, but merely remains passive and withholds what is already in his possession. The Shach in Tekfo Kohen disagrees and rules that where a person's claim is clear to himself, he may seize property in a manner that gives him a migo. This, however, is certainly only where there is no Torah prohibition against taking collateral, such as wages, guaranty, or similar obligations. With an ordinary loan, where the Torah prohibits entering the debtor's property to take collateral, seizure is prohibited. Even taking it in the street is forbidden. Although one passage in Tekfo Kohen might imply otherwise, the Shach himself ultimately places the relevant source in a case where taking collateral is independently prohibited, which implies that in a loan one may not seize merely to create a migo. This is also reasonable. Even though one may generally enforce his own rights where the matter is clear, the Torah specifically restricted self enforcement in loan collection. The same must apply even where the debtor denies the debt and the creditor fears loss. I have seen many people act cleverly by seizing an object from another before litigation merely to create a migo. They do not act properly, and where the obligation is a loan their conduct approaches a Torah prohibition. There is some possible leniency discussed in siman 97, but upright people should not rely upon it. Regarding a deposit already in one's possession, Maharshal implies that there is no prohibition in withholding it. The Zohar, however, treats withholding a deposit because of an unrelated debt as a grave violation of trust. There is also support for this in revealed halachah from Ritva, who holds that one who received an item specifically as a deposit must perform a complete return. Therefore one who fears Heaven should distance himself even from withholding a deposit.
