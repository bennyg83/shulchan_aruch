Seif 1. He may take it. The Sages said, "Steal from a thief and taste the taste," and Rashi in the first chapter of Berachot explains that there is a prohibition in this. It appears that many distinctions are necessary. Just as we rule that a person may sometimes execute judgment for himself, according to the details in siman 4, so too there are cases where he may take his own property secretly. The prohibition against stealing in order to annoy the owner or in order to pay double does not apply where, at the moment of taking, he is taking what is rightfully his under the rule permitting self-help. He could even have seized it openly in a manner resembling robbery, yet it would not be robbery because he is rescuing his own property. Likewise, where he takes it without the other party's knowledge, the act does not acquire the legal name of theft or robbery from the outset. Nevertheless, the dictum about "stealing from a thief" remains relevant where he lacks certain knowledge, established by actual observation, that the sharecropper took what belonged to him. One may not adjudicate on mere circumstantial assessment except a leading beit din of the generation, as explained in siman 15, and all the more one may not rely upon his own assessment where his own interest is involved, since a person does not perceive his own liability. Error is exceedingly easy in such assessments, and the result may be a grave transgression. One might also argue that he should initially have selected a trustworthy sharecropper, although that is not decisive, because the wrongdoer bears the duty to distance himself from causing harm. If one knows he can protect his property simply by not giving this person the opportunity, without thereby placing a stumbling block before him, that is preferable. If he will not be this man's sharecropper, presumably he can find employment elsewhere. The essential point is that one may not rely on an unproven assessment to permit what would otherwise be theft. This requires further study. Even if the person who suspects his partner is presently in possession, we do not say that monetary doubt is ruled leniently for the defendant merely because the burden of proof lies on the claimant. Even where the partnership began under compulsion caused by danger to life, as can occur where a governmental concession protects partners from military conscription, this creates no leniency. Every Jew has a presumption of fitness that outweighs mere suspicion, so there is no genuine doubt on which to act. If the partner has a notorious and established reputation for dishonesty, it is obviously improper to enter a partnership with him, and one should find another arrangement. After the fact, however, it appears that even regarding such a suspect the principle of "stealing from a thief" applies and it remains forbidden, because a Torah doubt is treated stringently. I have written elsewhere that the reason the burden of proof rests on the claimant is that just as there is a possible prohibition of robbery upon the defendant if he withholds money, there is a possible prohibition upon the claimant if the court removes money from the defendant incorrectly. The beit din therefore remains passive in an evenly balanced doubt. This is why one who says, "I do not know whether I became obligated," may nevertheless have an obligation to satisfy Heaven. For himself, since he can give the money voluntarily even if he was not strictly liable, a Torah doubt calls for stringency. Even there, however, it is not always an absolute enforceable obligation, because money that came into his hand permissibly and that he temporarily withholds with intent to pay later does not necessarily fall under the prohibition of withholding wages or property, particularly where the other party has not demanded it and he is waiting to clarify the doubt. A partnership is different. The moment one partner fails to place what he must into the common fund, or fails to disclose it to his partner as their agreement requires, and all the more if he denies the matter or tells a falsehood to conceal anything from his partner, this is actual theft. A Torah doubt under the prohibition of theft is certainly treated stringently. Since one's own financial interest blinds him, he must be especially careful, and should also gently warn his household not to cause any concealment wherever this can be prevented.
