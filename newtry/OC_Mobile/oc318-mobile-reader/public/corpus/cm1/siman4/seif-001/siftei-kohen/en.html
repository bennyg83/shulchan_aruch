If he cannot save it in another manner. The Rosh writes likewise, and so it appears in the Gemara, Bava Kamma 28a.
If he waits until he brings him to judgment, etc. See the Tur, who writes, "If the opposing litigant complains against him, etc." Its plain meaning is that the opposing litigant says: I am prepared to submit to judgment. Return my property to me, and I will immediately obey the ruling before you and remove it from his possession by other means. There may also be other practical differences. The ruling teaches that we do not overturn the first party's self help action. The Bach explains differently, but in my humble opinion the explanation I have given is correct. Examine it carefully.
Provided that he can establish it, etc. Even without witnesses he may seize it, because before beit din he will be believed by virtue of a migo with respect to his claim. So writes the Sema in seif katan 2, and so appears in Responsa of the Rosh, rule 64, siman 1.
For the reason that will be explained below, etc. See Responsa Menachem Azariah, siman 51.
Nevertheless, if he transgressed and acted through gentiles, etc. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 3, siman 6.
If he could not save it in another manner. Gloss: If he could have saved it otherwise, but went before gentile courts, recovered what was his, and they imposed an additional accusation upon the other party, he must pay the resulting damage under the law of a moser. Maharshal, chapter 3, siman 6. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 145.
For example, when he strikes him, etc. He thereby acts as his own judge. See Maharik there.
But mere seizure, where he seized it as collateral, etc. See Yam Shel Shlomo, chapter 3 of Bava Kamma, siman 5, where Maharshal argues at length against Maharik on this point. See Sema, seif katan 4, and see my Sefer Tekfo Kohen on this matter. The Sema's position accords with the Bach's explanation.
And he is one of the townspeople. It appears that the Sema wrote this because Maharik, shoresh 1, states that the public are not considered to be in possession against an individual unless that individual is one of their townspeople. I examined Maharik there, however, and it appears that only with respect to exercising self help did he rule in shoresh 1 that the individual must be one of the townspeople. With respect to the public being considered in possession, he rules in shoresh 2 that this applies even against one who is not a townsman. It appears that this is the Rama's view in the gloss, contrary to the Sema. See there carefully.
The community is considered to be in possession. The Sema writes that this also allows them to say, "We maintain that the law follows this authority," where there is a dispute. This applies specifically to matters of taxation. In other matters the community is not deemed to be in possession for purposes of asserting kim li. This is explicit in Terumat HaDeshen, siman 341: Maharam held that we do not validate their testimony on behalf of the public merely because there is a dispute among the authorities. Thus, the public is not considered actually in possession for all matters, but only regarding taxation. Examine this carefully. See Responsa Rashdam, simanim 361 and 364; Responsa Mahari ben Lev, part 3, siman 77, page 45a; and Responsa of Rabbeinu Nissim, end of siman 10.
