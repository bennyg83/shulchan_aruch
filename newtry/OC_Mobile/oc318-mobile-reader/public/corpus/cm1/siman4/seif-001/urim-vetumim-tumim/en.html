To strike him, etc. Mishneh LaMelech, Hilchot Avadim 3:5, was troubled by Rambam's ruling concerning a pierced-ear eved Ivri. Rambam writes that where the master had given him a non-Jewish maidservant, the Yovel arrived, and the servant nevertheless refused to leave, the master may strike him and is exempt, because the servant is now prohibited from remaining with the maidservant. Mishneh LaMelech asks that the Gemara in Bava Kamma 28 establishes this baraita as a case involving a maidservant only according to R. Yehudah, who holds that a person does not ordinarily execute judgment for himself. The Gemara therefore says that everyone agrees one may act personally in order to separate another from a prohibition. But since we rule like Rav Nachman that a person may execute judgment for himself, why did Rambam need to establish the baraita as involving a maidservant? It should apply simply because the servant refuses to leave the master's domain. Mishneh LaMelech therefore concludes that Rambam follows the conclusion of Bava Kamma 48 concerning one who enters another's courtyard without permission: if the owner knows of his presence and injures him, the owner is liable. Although the owner has authority to remove him, he has no authority to injure him. Thus, even though a person may execute judgment for himself, that permits him to remove the intruder but not to strike and wound him, unless the blow is needed to separate him from a prohibition, as in the case of the maidservant. If that were Rambam's view in this broad form, Mechaber should not have ruled here without qualification that one may strike the other person, since Rambam would disagree. In truth, however, the pierced-ear servant does not prove this. Rambam there need not be speaking of a servant who is physically remaining in the master's property and whom the master seeks to eject. That law of self-help in one's own property was already stated in its proper place, in Hilchot Sanhedrin. Hilchot Avadim can instead be speaking where the servant is no longer in the master's house, but remains attached to the maidservant and refuses to accept his freedom by separating from her. Rambam then teaches that the master may strike him in order to separate him from the prohibition, even though the matter no longer concerns the master's own property. This accords with the Gemara's principle that although the Amoraim dispute how to explain the baraita, they all agree to the underlying rule that any person may strike another when necessary to separate him from a prohibition, even where the matter does not involve that person's own money. We do not say that only beit din bears the duty to prevent the prohibition. Maharshal appears to have understood Rambam this way in Yam Shel Shlomo on this sugya. There is nevertheless a difficulty from Mechaber below, siman 421. There he rules that one who enters another's courtyard without permission may be removed, but the owner may not injure him. Mechaber then cites as "some say" the view that if the intruder refuses to leave, the owner may injure him. By presenting that second view as "some say," Mechaber appears to understand Rambam as not accepting it, precisely as Mishneh LaMelech says. If so, why here does Mechaber rule simply that one may strike him, without presenting the matter as disputed? The answer emerges from Mordechai in the chapter HaManiach. Mordechai writes regarding the pierced-ear servant that the master may strike him and remove him, and that the same applies to a servant even before the agreed term if the master fears him because of theft. All the more so, the same applies to any outsider if he has acquired a reputation as a thief. This wording is difficult. Regarding the servant, there is an obvious reason to require that he be suspected of theft, since otherwise the master cannot remove him before the end of his term. But why, regarding an ordinary outsider, must he have a reputation as a thief? Since a person may execute judgment for himself, the owner should be able to remove any unwanted outsider from his property. Yam Shel Shlomo noticed this and answered that the reference to a thief is merely the ordinary case. That is strained. The better explanation is that where the owner can remove the person by some other means without striking him, he must do so, just as the Gemara says that if he can withdraw his property without causing injury, he should. If the outsider is not a thief and the owner's only concern is that he does not wish him in his house, why strike him? The owner can prohibit his property to him by a neder. We do not suspect an ordinary Jew of deliberately violating a Torah prohibition. If the intruder then refuses to leave and violates the prohibition, the owner may certainly strike him afterward, because he is then separating him from a prohibition, like the case of the maidservant. This is why Rambam rules that one who merely entered another's courtyard without permission may not initially be injured. The owner can first prohibit the premises to him rather than use force. Likewise, regarding the pierced-ear servant, Rambam understood that the reason for the blow could not simply be removal from the master's property, because the master could create the prohibition and compel departure without injury. The case therefore concerns separation from the maidservant, where the prohibition itself is already present. The Gemara initially thought, according to R. Yehudah, that a person may not execute judgment for himself at all, even for a matter of prohibition, because Hashem has established judges whose task it is to separate people from transgression. The answer is that R. Yehudah too agrees that where an immediate prohibition is involved, an individual may act directly. But even according to Rav Nachman, the baraita must concern an actual prohibition, because otherwise the injury itself would not be justified. The alternative establishment of the baraita as involving a thieving servant is likewise clear. There is no need first to prohibit the premises to him. The owner has an evident and immediate concern for loss, every moment presents a danger of theft, and one should not create a neder that the suspected thief may violate in pursuit of stealing. Doing so could itself place a stumbling block before him. Therefore the owner may strike him in order to remove him. This also explains why the baraita says that the servant's term had ended. If the issue is the maidservant, only after the term ends does continued relations become prohibited. If the issue is theft, then during the term the master's authority over the servant creates fear and reduces the concern that he will steal. Mordechai's wording is now exact. An ordinary outsider may be struck immediately only where he has a reputation as a thief. If he is not suspected of theft, the owner must first use the nonviolent means of prohibiting the property to him. Accordingly, Mechaber is consistent. In siman 421, involving an ordinary unauthorized entrant, he cites as "some say" the view that one may injure a person who simply refuses to leave, because Rambam may disagree and require the owner first to use another available method. Here, however, the case is one in which the owner sees his own clearly identified property in the other person's hands. That person is comparable to a thief. There is an immediate and evident monetary wrong, and the owner may use force to recover his property. There is therefore no real dispute, and Mechaber properly states the rule here without qualification.
Only with a clearly identified object, etc. Because Sema's words here are somewhat unclear, it is necessary to set out the source of the law at some length. Mordechai in the chapter HaManiach writes that we rule that a person may execute judgment for himself. Maharam limits this to a clearly identified object that belongs to him and which the other person refuses to return. In other matters this rule does not apply, for Rav Nachman certainly does not dispute the Mishnah that a lender may not enter the borrower's house and take a pledge. Sefer Yereim likewise explains that Rav Nachman's rule concerns a robber who is holding the owner's actual property and seeks to keep it, where the value of the robbery has not been converted into an ordinary debt. The verse prohibiting a creditor from taking a pledge concerns a loan or a liability that has become a debt. Maharam brings proof from Berakhot, where Rav Huna took branches belonging to his sharecropper after the sharecropper had stolen from him, and the sages rebuked him with the expression, "One who steals from a thief also tastes theft." R. Kalonymus asked: why was Rav Huna criticized? He merely took what was legally his, and we rule that a person may execute judgment for himself. He answered that if Rav Huna had taken the very branches the sharecropper stole, he would indeed have been permitted to take them wherever he found them, like Ben Bag Bag's statement that one who takes his own property is taking what is his. Rav Huna, however, took different branches. Maharam derives his rule from this episode. Rav Huna certainly knew that the sharecropper had stolen from him, for Heaven forbid we suspect Rav Huna of seizing merely on doubt, and the Gemara's language supports this. Yet because he took other branches rather than the identical stolen branches, he was rebuked. Maharik, shoresh 161, raises a difficulty. The Rav Huna episode plainly was not one in which witnesses had established the theft. Rav Huna knew it himself, but the matter was not judicially established. Nevertheless, R. Kalonymus and Maharam imply that had Rav Huna claimed that the branches he seized were the very branches stolen from him, he would have been permitted to retain them. But Mordechai in HaGozel discusses two people disputing a certain item, each saying, "It is mine." One of them forcibly obtained it for himself through non-Jews. Mordechai says that although he should not have done so, because the rule permitting self-help applies only to an object clearly known to be his, he is not treated as a moser merely because he used non-Jews to obtain it for himself. There the claimant says of the very object itself, "This is mine," and is personally certain of his claim, yet because it was not established by witnesses he may not execute judgment for himself. Maharik therefore distinguishes between taking possession through violence, non-Jewish authorities, striking the other person, or comparable injury, and mere seizure without such harm. From Maharik we learn the following structure. If a person claims that the very object before him belongs to him and this is established by witnesses, he may seize it even with force, just as a judge may strike a recalcitrant litigant in order to enforce a judgment. If the matter is not established by witnesses but the claimant himself is certain, he may not use violence or other harmful means, but according to Maharik he may seize that very object without striking or injuring the other person. If, however, he does not claim the object he is taking is itself his, but seeks to seize some other property because the other person previously stole something else from him or owes him money, he may not do so at all. That is precisely the lesson of Rav Huna's branches: one may not become a thief after a thief by taking substitute property. Maharik's language implies that even where the seizure occurs before witnesses, so that the claimant has no migo, he may nevertheless seize the very object he says belongs to him, provided he does so without violence. This must also be how Maharik understood Rav Huna's case. If Rav Huna had seized without witnesses and therefore had a migo, there would have been no reason for Maharik to distinguish between seizure with force and seizure without force, nor would the Mordechai case of the disputed item have posed a difficulty, since there everyone knew the item was disputed and there was no migo. Therefore Maharik must understand Rav Huna as having seized before witnesses, without a migo. Even so, had those been the very branches that Rav Huna said were stolen from him, he could have seized them. Maharik therefore had to reconcile this with Mordechai's case by distinguishing violent seizure from simple taking. Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo strongly challenges this. How could one even entertain that a person may seize an object before witnesses merely by saying, "It is mine," when there are no witnesses supporting him? If that were allowed, society could not function. People would continually seize property in front of witnesses without violence and say, "I know this is mine." This is indeed a powerful objection. Shach in Tekfo Kohen, siman 114, attempts to answer that Maharik would certainly agree in an ordinary case that such seizure is impermissible. Maharik spoke only of the specific case before him, where the community had seized sacred utensils and, once in possession, could invoke a kim li argument in a dispute among authorities. According to the view that possession prevails in a safek among poskim, such seizure can be effective. Where no kim li claim exists, Maharik would agree with Maharshal that one may not seize. This cannot be Maharik's intent. If so, the Rav Huna case must have involved seizure without witnesses, so that Rav Huna had a migo, because there was no question of kim li there. But then Maharik's entire distinction between violent seizure and simple seizure becomes unnecessary. Rav Huna's case would differ from Mordechai's disputed item for the obvious reason that Rav Huna had a migo while the other litigant did not. Moreover, Maharik concludes that where the claimant has a definite claim to the very money or object itself and the other person suffers no injury, everyone agrees that the question is not governed by the dispute whether a person may execute judgment for himself. He derives this from R. Kalonymus's treatment of Rav Huna. If Rav Huna's seizure occurred only because he had a migo, how can that prove anything about seizure before witnesses? Maharik could have stated his position from independent reasoning, but then R. Kalonymus's case would provide no proof. The correct explanation of Maharik appears to be as follows. Maharik certainly agrees that if a person seizes an object before witnesses, has no proof that it is his, and the other party sues him in beit din, he must return it. His mere claim does not prevail. The narrower question is whether, when the seizer knows that the other party will never sue him in beit din because the other party is ashamed to expose the truth, and the seizer himself knows with certainty that the item was in fact stolen from him, he may take the object initially and hold it unless and until a beit din orders return. There is room to say that even then the initial taking is prohibited, because "the wicked man borrows and does not repay," and an improper seizure may itself make him a wrongdoer from the moment he takes it. Maharik resolves this question from Rav Huna. Rav Huna seized before witnesses. Nevertheless, he knew that the sharecropper would never bring him to beit din because the sharecropper was ashamed of his own theft. That is exactly what occurred: the sharecropper did not sue; rather, the sages themselves rebuked Rav Huna. R. Kalonymus therefore says that had Rav Huna taken the identical stolen branches, he would have acted properly in taking them, because he would simply have been reclaiming his own property. True, had the sharecropper later sued him in beit din, Rav Huna might have had to return the branches for lack of proof. That would be because Rav Huna could not establish his claim judicially, not because his own act of taking what he knew was his would have been intrinsically wrongful. As long as no such judicial claim was brought, he was holding his own property and was permitted to do so. But because Rav Huna took different branches, the seizure was wrongful from the outset. Taking another person's property as substitute for a claim is itself an act of seizure requiring legal valuation and authority. Even if the sharecropper never comes to beit din, Rav Huna has no right to retain unrelated property merely because another property debt is owed. Thus the sages said, "One who steals from a thief also tastes theft." Maharik then reads Mordechai's case of the disputed item similarly. There the prohibition was not merely that, if sued, the possessor would have to return the item for lack of proof. The very method of seizure, through non-Jewish force and coercion, was itself forbidden where his title had not been established. Mordechai even considers whether such conduct might make him a moser, and says only that the full law of a moser does not apply because he intended to obtain the item rather than injure his opponent. Maharik therefore distinguishes precisely: where the claimant has no witnesses, harmful or coercive self-help is forbidden; simple taking of the very object he knows to be his can be permitted until the matter is brought to beit din. This resolves Maharshal's objection. Maharik does not say that a seizure before witnesses creates judicial ownership merely because the seizer claims certainty. If the other person sues, the seizer must return the object absent proof. Maharik addresses only whether the initial act itself is prohibited where the seizer knows the truth and knows the other party will not litigate because of his own wrongdoing. The same interpretation fits Maharik's actual case. Had simple seizure been intrinsically forbidden, the community could not have seized the sacred utensils and then relied on a kim li position, because the very possession needed to support that argument would have been created through an unlawful act. Once Maharik establishes that simple seizure of the very disputed property is not intrinsically prohibited in such a situation, the subsequent possession can support the kim li argument. This, in my view, is Maharik's true intent, and the difficulty raised by Mishneh LaMelech and others disappears. Several later writers tried to infer from Maharik's wording that if even the very object itself could not be seized, R. Kalonymus had no basis to criticize Rav Huna specifically for taking different branches. They were troubled that the Gemara itself indeed implies that the branches Rav Huna took were different. But this is exactly Maharik's proof. R. Kalonymus asked why Rav Huna was rebuked despite the rule of self-help, and answered specifically that Rav Huna took different branches. If there were no legal difference between the identical stolen object and substitute property, that answer would be meaningless. He should simply have distinguished between a claim proved by witnesses and one known only to Rav Huna. The distinction between the particular branches is therefore intentional and decisive. Sema, seif katan 4, asks against Rama, who cites Maharik that even where a claimant cannot prove the object is his, he may take it as a pledge without striking the other person. Tur and Mechaber, however, say that self-help is permitted only where he can establish that he is reclaiming his own property. Sema answers that if the claimant seizes the object itself saying, "This is what you stole from me," and it is certain the alleged thief will deny the claim, seizure is permitted only where he can prove it. But where he merely takes a pledge for another debt and it is not certain the debtor will deny the debt, he may take the pledge. According to this explanation Sema appears to allow seizure even of a different object as security. That is not stated in Maharik. To the contrary, Maharik indicates throughout that a different object may not be seized at all. This is especially clear according to the explanation above. Rav Huna knew the sharecropper would not sue him, and knew his substantive claim was true. Yet the sages still rebuked him for taking different branches. If substitute seizure were permitted merely because the other party might not deny the underlying debt, why was Rav Huna's act forbidden? Even if one forces a distinction, Rama would still be puzzling. He begins by citing Maharik, yet omits Maharik's central distinction between taking the identical object with force and taking it without force, and replaces it with an entirely different distinction between reclaiming stolen property and taking a pledge for another debt, a distinction not found in Maharik. It is therefore clear that Rama's word "as a pledge" is not precise in the sense Sema understood. Rama means, as Darkei Moshe itself says, that one may take from the other person the object that was stolen from him. His point is Maharik's distinction within the identical object: violent or coercive seizure is prohibited without proof, whereas simple taking can be permitted. Tur and Mechaber's condition that the claimant be able to prove ownership refers to the case they are discussing, namely seizure through striking or force. For simple nonviolent taking, proof is not necessarily required in the limited circumstances described above. Likewise, Darkei Moshe's statement that nonviolent seizure is permitted "where the seizure helps" does not mean a hidden seizure giving the claimant a migo, as Sema seems to understand. Maharik is not discussing that situation. It means a case where possession will produce a legal benefit, such as enabling a kim li claim in Maharik's own responsum, or Rav Huna's case where he knew the sharecropper would never sue him. Otherwise there is no purpose in seizing today only to return tomorrow when beit din orders it. We have therefore clarified that Maharik appears to allow even initial seizure where the claimant has a migo, and Sema and Taz appear to understand him similarly. Otherwise the entire difficulty discussed above could have been resolved simply by saying Rav Huna seized without witnesses and therefore had a migo. Yet this seems to contradict Rosh, responsa, klal 64 siman 1. Rosh discusses a woman who seized an object belonging to her husband. He writes that even if it was not hers, although one may not initially execute judgment for oneself in that way, after a person seized another's object for a claim owed to him he is not necessarily called a robber. This plainly implies that even where the seizer has a migo, initial seizure is not permitted. Maharshal likewise holds that a migo helps only after the fact for credibility, but does not give permission initially to seize another person's property unless the object itself is clearly established as the seizer's. It therefore seems preferable to say that Maharik agrees with Rosh. Suppose Rav Huna's seizure had been without witnesses and he therefore had a migo. Even then, initial seizure would have been forbidden. Nevertheless, once he had seized, he would not be treated as a robber and might be permitted to retain it because of the migo. In that situation it would have been inappropriate for the sages to rebuke him with the sharp expression, "One who steals from a thief," because after the seizure he would not have the legal status of a thief. The fact that they used that expression shows that Rav Huna must have seized before witnesses, so that legally he would have to return what he took. This supports the interpretation above. Rivash, responsum 396, however, states that a person may retain another's deposit and say he is holding it for a separate debt owed to him, because a person may execute judgment for himself even where he could protect his right through beit din, such as where he has witnesses and no danger of loss. Rivash also permits seizure of movables in another person's possession where the prohibition "you shall not enter his house to take his pledge" does not apply. This appears to contradict Maharik, who holds that a different object may not be seized even without force. Darkei Moshe explicitly says Rivash disagrees. Shach, in Tekfo Kohen simanim 115 and 116, attempts to reconcile them. He suggests that Maharik's distinction between the identical object and other property applies only where taking the other property would violate the Torah prohibition against entering to take a pledge. Where that prohibition does not apply, as with a deposit already in the claimant's hands or property held by a third party, everyone agrees seizure is permitted. Shach himself recognizes a problem. If so, why did R. Kalonymus object to Rav Huna's taking different branches? No prohibition against entering a debtor's house for a pledge applied there. Shach attempts to answer that once the sharecropper stole from Rav Huna, the stolen property's value may have become fixed as an ordinary debt. This does not resolve the matter. First, the Gemara itself calls the sharecropper a thief and says he committed wrongdoing. There is no indication that Rav Huna and the sharecropper had made an accounting converting the theft into an ordinary loan. If they had agreed, "You owe me such and such," why would the sages still call the sharecropper a thief in this episode? Second, even if the obligation had become a debt, Rav Huna was withholding from the sharecropper's share that was already in Rav Huna's possession. The prohibition against entering a debtor's house to take a pledge plainly does not apply to property already in the creditor's hand. The same difficulty exists in Maharik's actual case, where the community seized sacred utensils which, according to their claim, had been donated by the deceased. There was no issue of entering a debtor's house to take a pledge. If Shach's reconciliation were correct, Maharik would not have needed his lengthy discussion. Likewise, in Rosh's responsum about the wife who seized an object belonging to her husband for what she claimed he owed her, the prohibition of entering a debtor's house does not explain the problem between husband and wife. It is therefore clear that these authorities hold a broader principle: even where the Torah prohibition concerning a creditor's pledge does not technically apply, the rule that a person may execute judgment for himself does not allow him to take unrelated property for a separate claim. When Maharam and the others say the rule applies specifically to "that object itself" but not other property, and add that Rav Nachman certainly does not dispute the Mishnah forbidding a creditor's self-help pledge, their meaning is not that the Mishnah is the sole source of prohibition. Rather, the Mishnah proves that Rav Nachman's statement cannot be literal and unlimited. If Rav Nachman meant every monetary claim, one could mistakenly apply it to a creditor entering for a pledge, contrary to the explicit Mishnah. His rule must therefore be restricted. The episode of Rav Huna then supplies the positive proof that the proper restriction is to the very object claimed as one's own. This explains why Mordechai, after mentioning the pledge-Mishnah, still adds the Berakhot proof. If the Mishnah itself established the complete rule, the second proof would be unnecessary. Rather, the Mishnah merely proves that Rav Nachman's general wording is not absolute, while Rav Huna's episode identifies the actual boundary. Yam Shel Shlomo also appears to understand the matter this way. There remains a difficulty: how can Rivash disagree with Maharam, R. Kalonymus, and Rosh, who seem explicit that a different object may not be seized? And there is another question. Rosh's responsum appears to say that apart from a clearly identified object belonging to the claimant, a person may not execute judgment for himself even where there will otherwise be loss. Maharik too seems to hold this. In his case, the community's entire possibility of prevailing depended on a kim li claim that required possession. Without seizure they would lose, which is certainly a form of monetary loss. Yet Maharik still carefully analyzes whether they were permitted to seize. How then do we understand Ketubot 85, where Rav Pappa and Rav Huna seized property of orphans even though their claim did not concern the particular object seized but a different debt? The seizure was made precisely because otherwise they could not collect from the movable property of the heirs. If seizure of unrelated property is forbidden even in a case of loss, how could they do so? One cannot answer that Torah scholars are different because Moed Katan 17 says a Torah scholar may execute judgment for himself. If that rule applied broadly to money, Rav Huna's earlier conduct in Berakhot would have been unobjectionable, since he was a Torah scholar. The authorities therefore explain the Moed Katan passage as dealing with excommunication for one's honor, not with monetary seizure. That is why the poskim cite it in Yoreh Deah, in the laws of nidui, and not here in Choshen Mishpat. Nor does the Gemara imply that Rav Pappa and Rav Huna first obtained specific authorization from beit din to seize. It is possible to resolve this by adopting a distinction suggested by Maharik himself between "seizure" and "executing judgment for oneself." If a person seizes property and then simply keeps it, without promptly placing the matter before beit din and saying, "This is what I seized; adjudicate between us according to Torah," he is exercising judgment for himself. That is permitted only under the restrictive conditions discussed above. But if a person seizes solely to preserve his possible right and immediately comes before beit din, openly states what he seized and why, and asks the court to rule according to law, this is not truly "executing judgment for oneself." He has not decided the law for himself. He has merely preserved the property so that the court's eventual decision will not be frustrated. That is what Rav Pappa and Rav Huna did in Ketubot. Immediately after seizure they came before beit din and said, in effect, "We seized this so it will not fall under the rule of movable property of heirs. Now judge us according to Torah." Such provisional seizure can be permitted. This also explains Maharik's case. The community's argument depended upon possession and a kim li claim. If ordinary unilateral seizure were prohibited, their possession could not support the legal position. Maharik therefore had to determine whether the particular seizure fell within a permitted form. It is possible that Rivash too speaks of a case in which the claimant retains a deposit or property already within reach and then promptly submits the matter to beit din, rather than secretly deciding the debt for himself. If so, the apparent dispute between Rivash and the other authorities can be narrowed. Nevertheless, this final distinction requires study, because I have not found it stated clearly by the earlier authoritative writers. I therefore do not present it as certain, but it provides a plausible way to reconcile the passages.
But an individual against the community, etc. There is room for careful analysis here. Rosh, responsa, klal 6, ruling 26, derives this law from the statement that a Torah scholar may execute judgment for himself in a matter whose law is clear. Rashi explains that where the law is clear to the scholar, he may adjudicate for himself and extract payment from the debtor against the debtor's will. Rosh reasons that all the more so, where the correct law is known to the majority of the community, their position is comparable to that of a Torah scholar. This proof works according to Rashi. But Rosh himself, in his halachic rulings, accepts Rabbeinu Tam's explanation that in monetary matters a Torah scholar has no special power to execute judgment for himself. The statement concerns excommunication and matters of personal honor. I have already explained above that the rulings of Maharam and R. Kalonymus support Rabbeinu Tam. Otherwise Rav Huna, who was certainly a Torah scholar, could have executed judgment for himself without the restrictions discussed in the case of the branches. Tur and Mechaber likewise do not cite the special rule concerning a Torah scholar here in Choshen Mishpat. They cite it only in Yoreh Deah in the laws of nidui. This too shows that the rule has no special application to monetary seizure. If so, the proof supporting Rosh's responsum appears to have fallen away. We must therefore say that the real basis of Rosh's ruling was communal custom. The accepted practice was that where an individual litigated against the community, the community could proceed according to the rule stated there. Rosh supplied a rationale for that existing custom by noting that those who established it could rely on Rashi's interpretation. Thus, although Rosh himself preferred Rabbeinu Tam's interpretation, he did not invalidate a settled communal practice founded on Rashi, because those who established the practice had a significant authority upon whom to rely. The matter nevertheless requires further study.
Before they descend to litigation. Sema, seif katan 5, cites Darkei Moshe, who notes an apparent contradiction in Maharik. In shoresh 1 Maharik writes that the defendant must specifically be a member of the city, while in shoresh 2 he writes that the rule can apply even where he is no longer a member of the city. Shach, seif katan 9, answers that shoresh 1 concerns the community's power to execute judgment for itself, which applies specifically to one who is a member of the city, whereas shoresh 2 concerns the rule that the community is treated as the party in possession, which need not depend on present city membership. I am surprised by this answer. In shoresh 1 Maharik writes that the community of Würzburg had no authority to pressure the opposing party through non-Jewish authorities. Ordinarily, when a person refuses to pay communal taxes, the custom is that the community may first compel payment and only afterward litigate, because the person is required to pay first and then contest the assessment. One who refuses to do so is treated as one who refuses to obey the law. But in the case before Maharik, R. Moshe had already separated from them, their association had been dissolved, and the partnership had ended. Maharik therefore says that he need not pay before the litigation, because the rationale of "a pot belonging to partners" no longer applies. They no longer rely upon him as part of a common fiscal partnership. Yet in shoresh 2 Maharik rules expressly that even after a person leaves the city, the rule requiring payment first and litigation afterward can continue. How does Shach's distinction between self-help and presumptive possession answer this contradiction? The issue in both responsa is precisely whether payment must precede litigation. The correct explanation, in my view, lies in the facts of shoresh 1. There, when R. Moshe separated from the community of Würzburg, the residents of Würzburg themselves also ceased to function as a single tax partnership. Each individual's share of whatever R. Moshe allegedly owed was separately allocated to that individual. The money was not destined for a common community treasury. Once that happened, the concern described as "a pot belonging to partners" disappeared. Each person could sue R. Moshe for his own defined share, and no one had reason to rely on someone else to pursue the common debt. This is indicated by Maharik's exact wording: "R. Moshe had already separated from them, their bundle was separated, and the partnership was nullified." The repetition is meaningful. Not only had R. Moshe left; the townspeople's common fiscal bundle itself had been dissolved. Otherwise the language "and their bundle was separated and the partnership was nullified" would be redundant. Maharik then says that the "partners' pot" concern does not apply because "they do not rely upon him." Without the explanation just given, this is difficult. Why should the community members rely on each other any less merely because R. Moshe moved away? The natural concern that each person will assume another will pursue the communal claim would remain. Rather, once each community member has his own separate portion of the claim, each is responsible for pursuing his own money. There is no common pot and no mutual reliance. Shoresh 2 concerns a different situation. The townspeople themselves remain a single fiscal community and the communal partnership continues. The fact that the defendant has left the city does not dissolve the partnership among the community members. The "partners' pot" concern therefore remains, and the defendant can still be required to provide payment or security first and litigate afterward. Thus the two responsa are fully consistent.
He need not provide security. I do not understand the reason for this ruling. The accepted rule is that in every dispute between an individual and the community, the individual must provide security because of the concern expressed by "a pot belonging to partners." If so, what difference should there be between a Torah scholar and another person? Maharam of Rothenburg, whose ruling is the source here, may have held like the authorities who require security only in disputes concerning communal taxes. On that view the ruling is understandable. A Torah scholar is exempt from the relevant taxes, and therefore no tax security is required from him. But Rama rules that the individual must provide security in all disputes with the community, not merely tax matters. Once that broader rule is accepted, why should a Torah scholar be different from anyone else? It is forced to say that just as there is concern that a community will be negligent because each person relies on the others, so too a Torah scholar may be too occupied with his learning to pursue the litigation. If that were the reason, then the Mishnah's rule concerning a jointly owned tree should also be divided: where the tree belongs to a Torah scholar, perhaps he should not be required to cut it before receiving payment because he is occupied with learning. No such distinction exists. The matter therefore requires study.
