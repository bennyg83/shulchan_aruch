He may strike him, etc. See Tumim. At first glance Rambam appears to disagree with this, and the Mechaber himself in siman 421:6 cites the rule as "some say." I explained there, however, that in the present case there is no dispute and all agree. If someone entered another person's courtyard and refuses to leave, whether the owner may strike him is discussed below in siman 421:6 and in Tumim.
Provided he can prove that it is his. Sema says that if he cannot prove ownership, not only may he not take his fellow's property by force, but even without striking him he may not seize it. See Tumim, where I wrote that this is not compelled. According to Maharik, as cited by the Rama, if he takes without force the very object that he claims was stolen from him, he may seize it even without presently proving ownership.
Or where he need not take a pledge, etc. Sema asks why this is introduced as "some say," since the Gemara in Perek HaMekabel explicitly permits entry into the house of a guarantor and similar situations to take collateral, and the same should apply where the property is a deposit or the debt is not a loan. Sema forces the explanation that the dissent concerns only the Mechaber's requirement that the claimant be able to prove his claim. The source is Rivash, however, and his responsum does not speak of inability to prove the claim. The actual dispute is with Maharik. Maharik holds that a person may not seize some other object by self help merely because money is owed him. The Gemara's permission to take a pledge for wages means with authorization of beit din. Entry into a borrower's house is forbidden even with beit din authorization, while entry for wages or into a guarantor's house is permitted. But without beit din authorization, self help remains forbidden. Rivash disagrees and therefore the Rama properly says "some say." Taz also appears to read it this way. See Tumim.
But if he already became obligated to him from another matter, he may not. Sema and Taz say that if he seized without witnesses and therefore has a migo, he may retain it. See Tumim. The Rosh implies that even initially such seizure is forbidden, even where there is risk of loss, because he may not execute judgment for himself in this manner. Maharshal writes likewise. I suggested that if immediately upon seizure he comes to beit din, says exactly what he seized and why, and places the matter in their hands to decide according to Torah law, the act may be permitted because he is not really judging for himself. He seized only to prevent loss and immediately submitted to beit din. This still requires study.
But it is forbidden to do so through non Jews. This appears to apply only where the other party is not a violent person. With a violent recalcitrant, above in siman 2 we rule that beit din may have him struck through non Jews. Since a person may execute for himself whatever beit din could lawfully do, he should be able to use that means against such a violent party. In any event, the rule cited from Terumat HaDeshen has no firm basis. Its proof is from Mordechai in Perek HaGozel, which says that even where the law is "whoever is stronger prevails," one may not seize through non Jewish authorities. But there the person delivered the property to a powerful official in a manner that would prevent Jewish beit din from recovering it even if the other side later proved its case. If the non Jew merely takes the item and delivers it into the Jew's hand, so that Jewish beit din can return it if proof later appears, Mordechai does not forbid it. In such a case, according to everyone, it may be permitted.
But mere seizure as collateral may be done in every case. This means, according to Sema, even where he cannot presently prove that the seizure was lawful. Sema asks how this fits the Tur and Mechaber's earlier rule that one who cannot prove his ownership may not seize. He distinguishes between claiming the very object as stolen, where the other person will certainly deny and cannot be branded a thief without proof, and taking another object as collateral for an admitted separate debt, where perhaps the debtor will acknowledge the obligation. See Tumim. This does not fit the view being cited, which is Maharik. Maharik holds that an object not claimed as one's own may not be seized at all merely for some separate obligation. The case therefore concerns the very object the claimant says is his. The distinction is between taking it through force, which requires proof, and mere peaceful seizure, which can be done even before proof. The Rama's word "collateral" is not literal, as his Darchei Moshe indicates. The Tur's earlier statement that one who cannot prove the claim may not seize concerns seizure through force, because that is the context there. Peaceful possession is different. Maharshal writes that if a person cannot prove ownership, goes to a non Jewish court and obtains the object, causing the other party substantial expenses and accusations, then although he acted improperly and must return the object because his claim is unproved, if he truly believes his own claim he is not automatically a moser liable for all consequential damage. He intended to recover what he believed was his, not to harm the other. But if he could have established his right in Jewish beit din and nevertheless chose foreign courts and caused damage, his intent to harm is evident and he must pay under the law of a moser.
But one individual against the many, etc. The Rama abbreviated Responsa Rosh. It appears that specifically when most of the townspeople or most of the communal leaders know the facts may the community act for itself. If only a few townspeople know, they are interested parties and cannot extract money on their own testimony. See Tumim.
And he is one of the townspeople. Sema says Maharik's responsa appear contradictory. In one place he limits the rule to a resident of the town, and elsewhere appears to apply it even to an outsider. This is not a difficulty for the Rama. For the special rule that a community can act for itself and rely on testimony of interested townspeople, the opposing party must indeed be one of that community and subject to the accepted regulation. Against a resident of another city, their interested testimony cannot be validated merely by local acceptance. Shach. See Tumim, where I reconciled Maharik's texts. The practical point is that even one who no longer currently lives in that city or under that ruler may still be required to pay or first give security if the underlying obligation arose there.
And he must give them collateral. Since communal property is "a pot of partners," everyone may delay responsibility and the individual could keep the community's property indefinitely. Therefore the individual must first give security so that he becomes the claimant and has incentive to pursue the matter. In my view the same applies when several individuals oppose the community. We do not say they too are a "pot of partners" and may delay. Public regulation is the concern, not their private convenience. The proof is from the rule that where a tree creates a public hazard it is cut first and payment is litigated afterward. Even if the tree belongs to ten partners, the public still removes the danger first. Knesset HaGedolah cites a dispute about this, but in my view the law is clear. Maharik adds that since the individual must first pay and only afterward litigate, if he refuses, the townspeople may compel him through foreign authorities as one refusing a beit din ruling, without obtaining a new authorization from beit din.
And this is what it means that they are considered in possession. Meaning, the individual is treated as the one seeking to extract and bears the burden of proof. In a dispute among authorities the community can plead "kim li." Where there is factual denial and an oath is needed, some representatives of the community may swear, or they may shift the oath to the individual. Sema writes that this leniency in the oath exists because of the public interest. Ordinarily, partners liable for an oath require one to swear and the others to affirm that they agree, as Sema explains below in siman 87. Here the entire public need not stand and repeat the oath. He also writes that in doubts concerning taxes, so long as the individual has no clear argument, the individual must pay because the community stands in the place of the sovereign and dina d'malchuta applies. Mordechai in the name of Maharam says likewise, while Responsa Ran implies the opposite: in a doubtful tax obligation, the community is not automatically considered in possession.
But not in other matters. In other matters the community is not considered the possessor merely because it is the community. Nevertheless, because communal property is a "pot of partners," the individual may still have to give security first. The special possessor status in tax matters rests upon the sovereign's right and therefore applies specifically to royal taxes. Darchei Moshe cites Maharam of Rothenburg that once the community has already paid the tax to the sovereign, it no longer has that special possessor status against an individual because the sovereign's immediate claim has ended. Sema.
A Torah scholar whose Torah is his occupation. Terumat HaDeshen writes that although the special monetary status of a Torah scholar regarding a litra of gold is not applied nowadays, the exemption of Torah scholars from communal taxes still applies. In my view, even in places where, due to the decline in honor for Torah, communities unfortunately impose taxes upon Torah scholars, this present rule still matters: the community should not enjoy special possessor status against the scholar. Strict law exempts him, and we should not expand the communal advantage beyond its source.
