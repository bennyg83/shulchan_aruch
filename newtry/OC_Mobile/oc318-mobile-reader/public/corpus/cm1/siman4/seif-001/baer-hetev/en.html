That he can. Meaning that if he cannot prove the property is his, not only is he forbidden to strike the other person and take it from him, he may not even take it without striking him. This applies, however, specifically where he takes it in the presence of witnesses. Without witnesses he may seize it, since before beit din he will be believed regarding his claim through a migo. Sema.
Below. There it is explained that one may take security from a guarantor and from a renter for the rent due. If so, Rama seemingly did not need to cite this here in the name of "some say." The Sema therefore suggests amending the text to read that one may execute judgment for himself, meaning even by striking the other party. This fits well, since the novelty is that self-help is permitted even through force. The Sema then questions Rama's next statement, where some say that merely taking possession is not called executing judgment for oneself, while striking him is. This implies that the preceding discussion concerned mere seizure. He answers that this second "some say" refers back to the Mechaber's opening rule, which permits action only where one can prove his claim and implies that without proof even mere seizure is forbidden. Rama therefore records an opinion that simple seizure is permitted in every case. Thus the Sema.
To save. If he could have recovered his property himself but instead went to a non-Jewish court, recovered what belonged to him, and also caused an accusation to be made against the other party, he must pay the resulting damage under the law of one who hands another over to coercive authorities. Maharshal, chapter 3, siman 6. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 145. Shach.
Matter. The Sema writes that a distinction applies. If he seeks to seize the very object that was stolen from him, where the other party will presumably deny the theft, he may seize it only without witnesses, for only then can a migo assist him. But if he claims the other party owes him for some separate reason and fears that he will leave or claim insolvency, he may take security even in the presence of witnesses, since it is not certain that the defendant will deny the debt. In that case the one who acts promptly is commendably protecting his claim.
The city. This is not limited to residents of one city, but includes those subject to one ruler and paying taxes to him, as Maharik writes. In another responsum, however, Maharik writes the opposite, that even against a single outsider the community is considered the party in possession. The Sema writes that this may be why Rama did not state that when the opposing party is not a resident of their city the community is not considered in possession, since the matter is doubtful. The Shach writes that Maharik implies the requirement that he be from the same city applies specifically to exercising self-help, whereas the rule that the community is regarded as the party in possession applies even against outsiders. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 14, and Responsa Shev Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 3.
Taxes. The reason is that since the community collects the taxes in order to give them to the king, they are regarded as acting with the king's authority, and the individual must establish his right before the king. Sema.
