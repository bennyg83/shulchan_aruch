If he sees his property in another's hand after that person stole it. The Tur adds: or if the other is coming to steal it and he stands over him and saves his property. Rama likewise writes this below. See the Perishah.
Provided he can establish that it is his. If he cannot establish ownership, not only may he not strike the possessor and take it from him, he may not even remove it without force. This is evident from the Tur. This applies where he takes it in the presence of witnesses. Without witnesses he may seize it and later be believed in beit din through a migo supporting his claim. What Rama writes below, that he may seize property as collateral in every circumstance, is explained there.
For the reason explained in siman 97. There it is explained that one may take a pledge from a guarantor and from a renter for rent. Since this is explicit in the baraita, Rama's subsequent statement in the name of 'some say' that if the debt does not arise from a loan he may take a pledge seems unnecessary. I therefore suggest emending the text to read that he may 'take the law into his own hands,' meaning even by striking the other person. Likewise, if he seeks to recover a deposit by force, he may strike the possessor until he releases it. This also appears in Darkei Moshe. Rama's later statement that some say this does not apply to a debt already arising from another source would then mean that he may not strike and seize in that circumstance. The cited baraita concerns seizure without striking. The later 'some say' thus qualifies the Mechaber's opening rule: simple seizure may be allowed even where he cannot prove the claim, but bodily force is more restricted.
He may act in every circumstance and afterward descend with him to judgment. This wording implies that even without witnesses to ownership he may seize in the presence of witnesses. Yet the Tur indicates that where he claims the very object was stolen, he can take it only without witnesses if he cannot otherwise prove the theft. Darkei Moshe cites Maharik that he may act for himself only where his seizure would legally help. The distinction is that when he takes the very object he says was stolen from him, the possessor certainly denies his ownership, so seizure with witnesses would not help him. When he takes something merely as collateral for a separate debt, however, the debtor may not deny the debt at all. The creditor may therefore take collateral even before witnesses, fearing that the debtor may leave or become insolvent, and then litigate. If the debtor admits the debt, the pledge remains until payment; if he denies it, the creditor returns it and the claim is adjudicated.
And he is one of the townspeople. Rama writes this because Maharik says that the many are considered in possession against an individual only where the individual is of their city, meaning under the same ruler and tax structure. Darkei Moshe cites another responsum of Maharik that sounds broader. Rama therefore does not expressly state that an outsider is excluded, because the matter is uncertain.
They are considered in possession. Meaning, the individual must bring proof, because the burden of proof rests on the one seeking to extract. If he has no proof, some members of the community swear and are exempt, or they may shift the oath to the individual, as the ordinary rights of one in possession allow. They may likewise say 'kim li' in a disputed question. Terumat HaDeshen discusses these rules, including tax cases. The community's position is even stronger because they may require the individual to pay the tax immediately or give collateral; if he later proves his right, the money or collateral is returned. Any uncertain claim of tax exemption is treated as though the king is in possession until exemption is proven.
Nevertheless the individual must give collateral to the community. The reason is to make him the claimant in subsequent litigation. Otherwise the matter may be indefinitely delayed because no particular member of the community will undertake the suit, as with the principle that a jointly owned pot is neither hot nor cold.
And they may compel in tax matters, etc. The Tur likewise writes this below in siman 97.
