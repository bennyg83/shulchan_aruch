When a Person May Enforce His Own Rights. One seif. A person may enforce his own rights. If he sees his property in the possession of someone who robbed him, he may take it from that person; if the other resists, he may strike him until he releases it, provided that he cannot recover it in another way. This applies even where waiting until he can bring the other party to court would cause no loss, provided that he can prove in court that the property he took was his. Nevertheless, he may not seize collateral for a debt owed to him. {Rama: The reason will be explained below in siman 97, seif 6. Some say this restriction applies only to an actual loan. If the other person owes him for a matter other than a loan, or if no new seizure is necessary because the object is already in his possession as a deposit or he found it in another's possession, he may retain it (Rivash, siman 396). Others say that a person may enforce his own rights only regarding a specific object clearly known to be his, as where it was stolen from him, another seeks to steal it, or another seeks to damage it; he may then protect what is his. But if the other party merely incurred a monetary obligation to him through theft or otherwise, he may not do so (Mordechai and Nimukei Yosef, chapter HaManiach). Only the person himself may enforce his rights; he may not do so through non-Jews (Terumat HaDeshen, siman 304). If he nevertheless acted through government officials, and he could not otherwise have recovered his property, what he did is effective (Mahariv, shoresh 161). Some say the restriction on self-enforcement applies only when he harms the other person, such as by striking him; he may therefore do so only when he can prove the property is his. Mere seizure as security, however, is permitted in every case, after which he litigates the matter in court (Mahariv, shoresh 161). All this concerns one individual against another. When an individual who is a resident of the city disputes with the community, the community may enforce its own rights if it knows the law is on its side, even though its members cannot prove this in beit din because they are all interested parties (Responsa of the Rashba, volume 7, siman 95). See siman 7, seif 12, and siman 37. If there are disputes and claims between them, the community is considered to be in possession against the individual, and he must give security before they litigate (Mordechai, chapter HaMocher Peiros and end of chapter Lo Yachpor). The community is considered in possession against an individual only in tax matters, not other matters; nevertheless, he must give security before litigation (Terumat HaDeshen, siman 341). This applies where the individual is not a Torah scholar. If he is a Torah scholar whose Torah study is his occupation and the case concerns taxes, he need not provide security, and the community is not considered in possession against him (Maharam of Risburg). In tax matters it is permitted to compel him through non-Jews and cause him financial loss if the tax cannot otherwise be collected (Mahariv, shoresh 17 and 27; see also simanim 10 and 40).}
