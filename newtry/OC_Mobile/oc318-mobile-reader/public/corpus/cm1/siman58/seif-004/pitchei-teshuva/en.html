If the creditor said: This never occurred. Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes in his gloss that although the creditor's denial could be understood to mean merely that the borrower never repaid him and that the money witnessed was given as a gift, nevertheless where the borrower specifically said, 'Did I not repay you before so-and-so and so-and-so?' the creditor should have recalled what occurred in their presence and answered in beit din that the money was a gift. If the borrower did not identify particular witnesses but merely said, 'I repaid you one hundred,' and the creditor denied it, after which witnesses testify that the borrower counted out money to him, the creditor may still claim that it was a gift and is not established as a liar. This is exactly analogous to siman 79, seif 4; see the Sema and Shach there. According to this, the Sema's ruling at the beginning of siman 294, that if one is sued for a deposit, denies that it ever occurred, and witnesses later testify that the item was seen in his possession, he may no longer say that it came to him by purchase because he has been established as a liar, appears incorrect. Why not understand his denial to mean that no deposit was ever made, while the later sighting is consistent with a purchase? The Taz there supports the Sema from the law here, but in Rabbi Akiva Eiger's view that support fails, because here the borrower named the witnesses to the alleged repayment, so the creditor should have remembered and explained that the transfer was a gift. Indeed, siman 79, seif 4, is a contradiction to the Sema. End quote.
