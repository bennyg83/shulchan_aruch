One should not object to Rosh's explanation that the set-off claim is weak because a person does not pay an oral debt while leaving a written debt outstanding. What could the borrower have done? He truly intended to pay the written debt, but the creditor chose to apply the money to the oral debt. The answer is that the borrower should have anticipated this. A creditor naturally prefers to receive payment on an oral debt rather than on a debt secured by a note. If the borrower owes both and pays without demanding return of the note, he should realize the creditor may apply the money to the oral debt. If that is not what the borrower wants, he should refuse payment until the creditor returns the note. His failure to do so indicates that he knew there was no other oral debt. Thus Rosh's presumption remains sound.
However, Rosh's rationale creates a difficulty where the creditor says the money was applied to another written debt and that he actually returned that other note. In that case there is no weak claim of preferring an oral debt to a written debt. Why should witnesses to payment weaken the present note? Baal HaTerumot himself was initially uncertain whether a claim of another written debt is stronger. Tur in siman 30 nevertheless rules broadly that separate payments can combine and the creditor cannot say they related either to an oral debt or to another written debt that he returned. Rosh's halachic rulings can be understood by distinguishing the source of the weakness. When witnesses actually saw the payment, even a claim of another written debt is inherently poor unless the creditor said before the witnesses, "I am receiving this for the other note." If he knew the borrower owed him under another document and was now paying before witnesses, why remain silent and allow ambiguity? If he truly returned another note, he could have declared so. Rosh's specific statement that a set-off claim needs a migo because people do not leave a written loan while paying an oral loan addresses the Gemara's case with no witnesses. There, one might ask why any migo is needed at all. If no witnesses saw payment, what external weakness exists? Rosh answers that when the asserted other debt is oral, the claim itself is weak because of the normal preference for paying written obligations first. Thus a migo is needed. If the creditor instead claims another written debt and no witnesses saw the payment, that intrinsic weakness may not exist, and perhaps no migo is needed. But when witnesses saw the payment, a separate evidentiary weakness arises from his failure to identify the other note at the time. Therefore, in that setting, even a claim of another written debt can weaken the note. This also explains why an undated general receipt is effective. Suppose the creditor later produces a note and says the receipt referred to another written debt. The receipt itself mentions a note. Once the written receipt identifies the payment as relating to a documentary obligation, the creditor cannot simply say it was some other note without proof. Otherwise receipts would be practically useless. More fundamentally, Rosh's question cannot be read as asking why the creditor should be believed to extract money merely because he says "set-off." Without a migo, the general rule is that one seeking to extract money bears the burden of proof. Rosh's point is rather that there is some circumstantial support for the creditor because the borrower paid without demanding return of the note. That conduct suggests the payment may have related to another obligation. This support is enough to prevent complete cancellation, though not necessarily enough to permit collection without a migo. Accordingly, if the borrower expressly mentioned the note while paying, the circumstantial support disappears. The creditor cannot later say the money was for a different obligation, whether oral or written. This also explains why a receipt that identifies the note defeats a later set-off claim. Thus the cases divide cleanly. If the note was expressly identified during payment, there is no set-off defense. If no note was mentioned, an oral-debt claim has some plausibility because the borrower failed to protect himself, while another-written-note claim has less of that particular presumption but can still be weak before witnesses because the creditor failed to identify it. This resolves the apparent difficulty and confirms Maharik's ruling in a case of partial payment on the very note: the creditor cannot say the balance related to another note once the relevant note had been identified.
