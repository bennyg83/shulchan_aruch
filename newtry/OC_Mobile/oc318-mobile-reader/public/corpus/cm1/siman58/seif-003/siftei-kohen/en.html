[" Since he admitted, etc., if there are witnesses, etc. The Sema writes that where there are witnesses, the instrument is void even without the lender's admission that the debt arose from the price of the ox, as stated above in seif 2. He therefore explains that the Mechaber retained the Rambam's wording, 'since he admitted,' even though according to the opinion requiring witnesses the admission is unnecessary. This is strained. The Tur likewise writes, 'since he admitted,' although the Tur expressly rejects the Rambam's ruling, and Ir Shushan uses the same wording without mentioning the Rambam's view. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi himself also uses the language 'since he admitted,' as Baal HaTerumot quotes him. It therefore appears that the witnesses here do not testify that the lender received the price of the ox expressly as payment from this borrower. Rather, they testify that he received the proceeds of the ox from the buyers who paid for its meat. Thus, the phrase 'if there are witnesses that he repaid him' is not precise and means that there are witnesses that the purchasers paid him the price of the ox. In such a case, although above in seif 2 Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and the Mechaber allow the lender to claim that an unspecified payment was for another debt through a migo that it was a gift, here, once the lender admits that the instrument itself arose from the price of the ox, the instrument is weakened according to all views, because it is presumed that the money he received from sale of the ox was received as payment. This also explains the repeated reference of the Tur and Mechaber to 'the price of the ox' without resorting to the strained explanation of the Sema and Bach."]
[" If there are witnesses that he paid him the price of the ox. The Sema and Bach say that 'the price of the ox' is not literal, and that it suffices for witnesses to testify that the borrower gave money as payment, as above in seif 2. In my view this is strained, because the Tur repeats the expression 'the price of the ox' several times. It is also difficult according to the Bach's preferred explanation, for if the Tur already holds above that even testimony to an unspecified receipt of money weakens the instrument, there would be no need here to bring the Rambam and Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and then decide between them. Rather, the words should be understood literally. The Tur understood the Rambam to mean that even without witnesses that the purchasers actually paid over the price of the ox, the instrument is weakened once the lender admits that the debt in the instrument was for the ox and it is established that he collected the proceeds of the ox. Since the lender admits the source of the debt and received the proceeds, there is a presumption that he received them toward the instrument, and his migo is a migo against a presumption. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, however, maintains that even then the lender has a migo unless there are witnesses that he actually received the proceeds from the purchasers. Those witnesses need not know whether he received them as payment of this borrower's debt, but only that he sat at the butcher shop and received the price of the ox. Ordinarily Rabbi Yosef HaLevi allows the lender to claim another debt if the witnesses merely saw an unspecified transfer, through a migo that it was a gift. Here it is different because the witnesses testify that the money was specifically the proceeds of the ox and the lender himself admits that the instrument was for that ox, so it is presumed to have been received as payment. The Tur rules like Rabbi Yosef HaLevi. Even though the Tur holds above that testimony to an unspecified payment by the borrower himself can weaken the instrument, here the money is received from third party purchasers, and without testimony identifying it as the proceeds of this ox the lender could say those people owed him money or gave it to him as a gift. Once the witnesses identify the money as the ox proceeds, and the lender admits that the instrument represents the ox price, his own admission joins with the testimony and removes his migo. This resembles the Gemara's case of the hired donkey where the party's admission combined with external facts made his migo a migo against witnesses. So too here, although the witnesses do not know expressly that he received the money in payment of this debt, his admission joins with their testimony and weakens the instrument."]
[" That he repaid him, etc. The Sema writes that according to Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh, who hold in the preceding seif that even testimony to an unspecified transfer of money weakens the instrument so that we neither collect with it nor tear it up, in this case, where the lender admits that the instrument was for the ox, the instrument is completely void. It appears that the Tur's phrase 'the instrument is void' led him to this. In truth, however, the Rosh and Ran explicitly indicate that even in this case the instrument is only weakened to the extent that we neither collect with it nor tear it up. They reject the Rif's proof from the case of the ox that the instrument is completely void, because it is possible that the lender advanced money by instrument to purchase oxen and later collected other oral debts from the proceeds, while the debt represented by the instrument remained outstanding. The fact that the borrower did not take back the instrument supports that possibility. Therefore the Tur's expression 'void' is not exact and relies on what he wrote earlier. The Bach likewise understands the Tur to treat the two cases alike. It further appears that even where witnesses testify that the lender expressly received the ox proceeds as payment, according to the Rosh and Ran the instrument is not torn up unless the witnesses testify expressly that it was payment of this instrument."]
[" And the principal ruling is like the first view. It is close to my view that even the Rambam agrees to this. When he writes, 'although there are no witnesses that he repaid him from its proceeds,' he means that there are no witnesses that the money was expressly received as repayment. A proof is that immediately beforehand the Rambam writes that if witnesses saw the borrower give the lender money but did not know whether it was payment, the lender is believed with an oath and may collect on the instrument, 'for he did not repay him before witnesses,' meaning that the money was not given expressly as payment before witnesses. His next case, where the borrower says that the instrument was for the price of the ox, continues that same discussion. Thus his statement that there are no witnesses that he repaid him from the proceeds likewise means that there are no witnesses that the proceeds were given expressly as repayment."]
[" If it is known, etc. This ruling requires study. It is a novel distinction not found in the other authorities, and Maharik himself did not decide it independently as law, but merely joined it with additional reasons in the case before him. See there."]
