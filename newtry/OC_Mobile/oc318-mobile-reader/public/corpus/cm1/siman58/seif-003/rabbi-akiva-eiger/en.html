[Shulchan Aruch] Or as a gift, if the lender said. Note: Although one might explain his statement as meaning that the borrower never repaid him and that the money was instead given as a gift, nevertheless, because the borrower said to him, "I repaid you before so-and-so and so-and-so," he should have recalled what happened before those witnesses and responded in beit din that the maneh was given as a gift. But if he did not mention specific witnesses and merely said, "I repaid you a maneh," and the lender said, "It never happened," after which witnesses came and testified that he counted out money to him, one may say that the lender can claim that the money was a gift and is not established as a liar. This is exactly analogous to the rule below, siman 79:4; see the Sema and Shach there. According to this, what the Sema writes at the beginning of siman 294, that if one was sued for a deposit and said, "It never happened," and witnesses afterward testified that they saw the object in his possession, he cannot then say that it came into his possession by purchase because he is established as a liar, appears difficult. Why should we not say that his words "it never happened" meant only that the other never deposited anything with him? The Taz there supports the Sema from the law here, but in my humble opinion that proof does not stand. Here, because he said, "I repaid you before so-and-so and so-and-so," he should have recalled and answered that it was a gift. On the contrary, the rule below in siman 79:4 contradicts the Sema. It appears to me that there is a proof for this from Bava Batra 45a, "but if he delivered it to him without witnesses, since he could say, 'It never happened.'" Necessarily, even where he admits that the object originally came into his possession as an artisan but claims that he later purchased it, he is believed through a migo that he could have said, "It never happened," meaning that it came into his possession as a purchase, as Tosafot explain. Thus, the phrase "it never happened" can mean that no object of the other person ever came into his possession as that person's property, because it came to him by purchase. Why should we invent a distinction between the language of the Sages and ordinary speech? However, Tosafot on page 46, s.v. Shapir, do not imply so. They say that if he first said, "It never happened," it is obvious that he cannot later create a case of seeing the object, because if witnesses recognize it by visual identification he will not be believed, since he already denied and said, "It never happened." This means that although, had he not first denied, he could have shown it to the witnesses and said, "I purchased it," at the time he displayed it, through a migo that he could have withheld it from them, nevertheless, once he denied it by saying, "It never happened," he is not believed. It follows that he cannot explain his earlier words as meaning, "I purchased it." Examine this carefully. The practical law therefore requires study.
