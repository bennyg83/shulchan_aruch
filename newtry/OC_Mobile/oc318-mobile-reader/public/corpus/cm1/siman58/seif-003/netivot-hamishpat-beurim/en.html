Since he admitted, etc., if there are witnesses. Shach's text requires correction. Rambam holds that even if witnesses merely saw the lender receive money from those purchasing the borrower's ox meat, without knowing whether those very coins were the sale proceeds or from another transaction, the lender cannot redirect the payment once he admits that his debt arose from the ox. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi is more lenient. In his view the lender can rely on a migo unless witnesses specifically establish that he received the actual proceeds of the borrower's ox. Then the situation is equivalent to seeing the borrower himself pay. Even where witnesses see the lender receive money from the borrower without an express statement that it is payment, Rabbi Yosef HaLevi can allow a separate-debt plea through a migo of gift. In the ox case, however, once the lender admits that the debt itself consisted of the ox proceeds, the migo becomes a migo against the operative presumption. Tur's ruling is consistent: when money comes directly from the borrower, people do not ordinarily give a gift to a creditor before paying the debt. When it comes from third-party purchasers who owe the lender nothing, a gift explanation is more plausible unless witnesses establish that these were specifically the sale proceeds owed through the borrower. Why is the lender's own admission that the debt was the ox proceeds also needed? Because if witnesses merely saw both parties at the place of sale and the lender alone collected the money, the lender could say the ox itself was his and there was never any repayment of a debt. Even if the ox had previously been seen in the borrower's possession, animals that move about do not create an ordinary possession presumption. Once the lender admits that the ox price had already become a debt owed by the borrower, however, the receipt of those proceeds is treated like receipt of any other debt payment. Nor is the lender believed through a migo that he could have denied that the debt arose from the ox. In ordinary separate-debt cases the lender says that even though the borrower intended the payment for the documented debt, he may retain it against another real debt. That resembles seizure without witnesses. Here the payment occurred before witnesses and the ordinary presumption treats it as directed to the document; his alternative explanation would contradict that presumption. There is a further reason. Where payment occurred before witnesses, even if the witnesses no longer remember whether it was before or after the date of the debt document, the lender cannot normally redirect it. A receipt may be written without a date when the witnesses remember payment but not its exact date, and such an undated receipt cancels the document. Oral testimony to the same payment should be no weaker. But if the witnesses saw only an unspecified transfer and do not know whether it was payment, gift, or deposit, the rule cancelling the document rests on the presumption that a debtor does not give gifts before paying. That presumption applies only if the witnesses know the transfer occurred after the debt arose. In the ox case they do not remember the time, so the lender's own admission that the debt was the ox proceeds is necessary to tie the transaction to the debt. This is the clearest explanation.
If it is known that he gave him part of the money. This speaks specifically where the payment was made before witnesses. The novelty is that the creditor cannot say that the remaining money was intended for a separate debt in a manner that would allow seizure. In Maharik's responsum, although the issue was not formally one of seizure, the same principle was necessary because the case involved two partners and the entire amount due from one partner had already been paid. Without this rule, the creditor could have said that the amount paid in excess of the known obligation was for a different debt. The presumption that a person does not leave a written debt unpaid would not apply there, because that partner had already paid his full share under the document. By contrast, where payment was made without witnesses, the creditor can still say that he retained part for a separate debt. Even if circumstances indicate that both portions were intended for the documented debt, an unwitnessed retention can operate like seizure not before witnesses. This is also implied by Maharik, who proves the point from the later seif where Rama requires witnesses. According to this, Urim VeTumim's question from Responsa Rosh is answered, because that responsum concerns payment without witnesses. On the basic rule, I agree with Urim VeTumim that this applies when the payments were given together. If they were paid at separate times, then even if one payment was expressly toward the document, the creditor can say the later payment was for a separate oral debt.
