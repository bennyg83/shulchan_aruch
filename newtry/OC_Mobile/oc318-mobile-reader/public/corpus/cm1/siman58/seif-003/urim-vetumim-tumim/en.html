If there are witnesses, etc. The later authorities ask: if there are witnesses, why is his admission needed? With witnesses he is not believed to say that the payment was for another debt. The Sema writes that in truth his admission is unnecessary, and that the Mechaber merely followed Rambam's wording, which mentions admission because witnesses are not required. The Shach challenges him from the Tur, which requires both admission and witnesses. This is not a difficulty according to the Sema's approach. The Tur ruled above that the document is not entirely void, as the Rama states, but where there are both witnesses and his own admission, everyone agrees that the document is completely void. The Shach answers that the case is one where the seller received money from the purchasers as payment for the ox, but they did not know whether the seller had perhaps given that money as a gift to the person who had bought the ox on credit. He therefore invokes the Ri Migash's position above that a migo is effective in such a case, and for that reason an admission is needed. This answer does not suffice according to the Tur, who does not follow the Ri Migash. One would need the additional forced explanation that even the Tur concedes here, as stated in siman 129, because perhaps the purchasers gave him the money as a gift. These explanations are strained. In truth, I do not understand the difficulty. The Rosh writes that the reason one needs a migo, and that a creditor is not believed to say that a payment was for another debt without a migo, is because this is an inferior claim: a person does not ordinarily leave a documented debt outstanding while paying an oral debt. That reasoning applies when the borrower pays from his own pocket. But here the situation is different. Shimon owed Reuven by document, and Reuven additionally gave him an ox to sell for profit, but on condition that the price of the ox would be collected directly from the purchasers so that Shimon's debt to Reuven would not increase. This arrangement is common. Accordingly, the creditor can say: "You already owed me by document, and when I sold you the ox I did so only on condition that I would collect the price from the purchasers, so that your debt to me would not increase." This is a plausible and ordinary claim, and he is believed without a migo. Therefore, witnesses that he received the price of the ox do not suffice until he himself admits that the document represents the price of the ox. Once there are both his admission and witnesses, he can no longer make any alternative claim, and the document is void. Why is his admission alone insufficient? Because without witnesses he has a migo that he could have denied receiving the price of the ox. But where witnesses establish receipt, we do not say that he has a migo based on the fact that he could have refrained from admitting that the document was for the ox, because once he says that the document is for the ox and the witnesses establish payment, the document has already become a mere shard, and such a migo would be a migo used to extract money. There is also another answer. The Rosh writes in Perek Chezkas Habatim regarding the forged document case that where a person had to begin by stating a falsehood, we do not apply a migo. Here too, he would have had to say falsely at the outset that the document was not for the price of the ox when in fact it was. Therefore there is no migo at all. This resolves the matter.
That he paid him, etc. The Tur writes that the document is void. The Sema infers from this that even according to the Tur, who follows the Rosh that an ordinary document is not completely void in such a case, here everyone agrees that it is completely void. The Shach objects that the Rosh cites the proof of those who interpret "the document was weakened" to mean complete invalidation from the case in which payment for the ox was made toward the ox and the ox was taken, where the Gemara says that the document was weakened. There, the phrase would seem to mean completely void. The Rosh rejects this and says that even there it may not be completely void, because perhaps the money received for the ox was applied to another debt and the price of the ox itself was converted into a loan, so the document could still secure another obligation. The Derishah itself sensed the difficulty and gave a very strained answer. It is also difficult because the Rosh's entire reason for saying that the document is not completely void is that the Gemara says "weakened" rather than "void." Yet in the ox case the Gemara also says "weakened." If there the meaning is complete invalidation, the Rosh's linguistic proof falls away. Nevertheless, the Tur's wording strongly supports the Sema, and the Shach's suggestion that the Tur merely used habitual wording is strained. It is possible to explain the matter as follows. Everyone agrees that the ox case is substantially worse than an ordinary claim that a payment was applied to another debt. Therefore, one descends one level. According to the opinion that in an ordinary case with witnesses the document is not weakened at all and may still be collected upon, in the ox case it is enough to say that the document is weakened and cannot be used for collection, though it is not torn, because one can still make the Rosh's possibility that the ox payment was applied to another debt and the ox price was converted into a loan. Rav Papa, who says in the ox case that the document was weakened, follows his own position that an ordinary payment does not weaken a document at all. The earlier authorities therefore sought to prove from Rav Papa's language that "weakened" means completely void, because in this unusually defective ox case logic would support complete invalidation. The Rosh properly rejects that proof: even here "weakened" may mean only that the document cannot presently be collected upon, because Rav Papa follows his own position that ordinarily the document is not weakened at all. But according to our ruling against Rav Papa, that in an ordinary case with witnesses the document is already weakened, the ox case contains an additional defect. With two defects combined, there is good reason to invalidate the document entirely. Thus the ox case before Rav Papa is described as merely "weakened," while according to our practical ruling it is completely void. This appears to reconcile the language of the Tur and the Sema. Although the abridged rulings of the Rosh appear more like the Shach, because they omit the special ox case and thereby imply no distinction from other cases, the Tur itself supports the explanation given.
He cannot say, etc. The Shach questions this ruling because Maharik has no clear colleague for it, and Maharik himself stated the principle only together with other supporting considerations. There is also a partial difficulty from Responsa Rosh, rule 86, at its end. In that case a person received one thousand gold coins and said that 276 were for this debt and the remainder for another debt. The Rosh writes that it is true that he can ordinarily claim that the payment was for another debt, but only without witnesses; where there are witnesses he is not believed. Why is the presence of witnesses decisive? Even without witnesses, since the 276 were expressly applied to this debt, should we not infer that the remainder was as well? Perhaps the distinction is that in the Rosh's case the payments were received separately, while the written acknowledgment was made as one combined statement. Maharik may concede that where the payments themselves were separate, a person can say, "this payment was for this debt and that payment for another debt," even if the later acknowledgment was combined. Maharik speaks where the payment itself was made as one undivided act. In such a case he holds that it is unreasonable to split one payment into two different debts. This requires study. Below, seif katan 13, I offered some conceptual support for Maharik's view.
