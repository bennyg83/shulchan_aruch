The reason is that where the custodian will have to pay, the seizer is not regarded as seizing from the creditor's debtor, but rather from the custodian, while the custodian himself is not obligated to the seizer at all. This is why the law distinguishes whether the custodian was negligent. Mishneh LaMelech asks why, in a case where Reuven lent utensils to Shimon and Shimon then lent them to Levi, if Levi seizes them on a claim against Reuven, Shimon should be liable only where he was negligent. Since Shimon is a borrower, he is liable even for unavoidable accidents. The question is not difficult. A borrower's liability for unavoidable accident concerns ordinary accidents such as breakage or death. Here Levi claims that Reuven actually owes him the value and he has a migo that he never seized the utensils. That migo may protect Levi as against Reuven, but it does not establish the truth of the claim vis a vis Shimon. Reuven can proceed against Shimon and say, "You are liable to me as borrower. I deny owing Levi anything." The migo protects only Levi himself. It does not permit Shimon to say, "Perhaps Levi is telling the truth and therefore I am exempt." Had there been witnesses to the seizure and no migo for Levi, and Levi then gone overseas, Shimon would certainly have to pay. The same is true even where Levi has a migo.
But if he seized it from the depositor and has a migo. It appears to me that all these distinctions concerning seizure from a custodian, where the custodian would be forced to pay, apply specifically where the seizer claims only that the depositor owes him money. A creditor ordinarily acquires only from the time of collection onward and has no right specifically in this object. Therefore we say: go collect from your debtor, for what you are holding is effectively taken from the custodian who must now pay. But if the seizer claims that the deposited object itself belongs to him and he has a migo, then according to everyone he is believed. He is identifying his own property in another person's possession. The fact that a custodian accepted responsibility for that object does not force the true owner to return it merely because the custodian may thereby become liable.
