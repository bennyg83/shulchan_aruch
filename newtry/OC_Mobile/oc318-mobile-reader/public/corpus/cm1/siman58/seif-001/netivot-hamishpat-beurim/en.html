The agent must repay the sender. This is explicit in Ketubot. At first glance it seems to conflict with Sema in siman 125, who in a similar case can exempt the sender based on the agent's migo. There is a difference between a migo that merely removes personal interest from a witness and a migo that constitutes substantive proof. In Kiddushin the agent does not become a witness merely because he could have said, 'I returned it to the borrower.' That alternative claim is no stronger for him than saying, 'I paid the lender.' The migo does not itself prove truth; it only shows that he could have avoided liability and therefore is not financially interested. Once hesset was enacted, he is still a witness who needs an oath and cannot testify in that way. Sema's case in siman 125 relies on a stronger type of migo. The agent chooses a worse claim, one that leaves him exposed to dispute with both sides, instead of the simpler claim that he returned the money. That conduct can function as substantive evidence that he is telling the truth even if he were not otherwise a valid witness. It can therefore help the sender remain exempt. Why then is the agent liable here when the lender also has a migo supporting the claim that the sender owes him another debt? Because that lender's migo cannot independently extract money. A migo is not used to extract except together with the debt document, and the document itself does not prove the alleged separate debt. The agent's negligence in leaving the document outstanding is what converted the lender from a claimant who would otherwise have had to prove his case into one who can retain or collect through the existing instrument. The agent therefore caused the sender's loss and must reimburse him. In any event, as I explained in siman 56, the broader rule of Sema is not accepted in practical law. A migo belonging to another person does not ordinarily help unless it belongs to a litigant, to the person charged with carrying out the matter such as a shalish or agent, or to one who has actual power to do what he claims.
To repay the sender. One should not object that the agent's only negligence was leaving the document with the lender, while a person who later returns a paid document to the lender is ordinarily exempt because that is only indirect causation. Here the agent departed from the sender's instructions in the very act of payment. His deviation caused the money to be paid in a legally defective manner and directly exposed the sender to renewed collection. That is treated as negligent damage to the sender's money, not merely as later indirect harm caused by the existence of a paper.

Likewise, one who deposited. Shach explains this according to the view that because the agent will have to reimburse the depositor, a creditor cannot seize the entrusted item and thereby cause loss to the agent, unless the agent says the deposit was placed with him on behalf of the debtor in a way that removes his own liability. The underlying reason is not a penalty for the agent's false statement. A custodian can himself have a property-like interest in the deposited item because of the liability he accepted. Under Rabbi Natan's lien, a creditor ordinarily reaches property owed by the third party. But where collection would cause the custodian a direct loss, the deposited movables resemble encumbered movables. A creditor cannot foreclose them merely because the underlying debtor owes him, especially where the debt is oral. This is supported by the rule that when a custodian pays for a stolen deposit, he can acquire the deposit retroactively or from the time of the theft. His responsibility therefore gives him a real contingent proprietary interest. More broadly, the Sages made custodial transfer effective by meshichah, and the object can be treated as held in his domain like collateral securing the responsibility he undertook. The creditor therefore cannot seize it in a way that destroys that interest. If, however, the custodian admits that he held the item only as agent of the debtor and that his own interest has ended, his admission removes his competing right. Even if witnesses show that his version was false, his own admission can operate against his proprietary interest and prevent him from claiming the object as encumbered to himself. A practical distinction follows. If the item never entered the custodian's possession through an act of acquisition, and he merely undertook personal responsibility while the object remained in the owner's domain, a creditor of that owner may seize it because the custodian has no property interest in the object itself. Likewise, among partners, a creditor may seize the debtor-partner's share from the debtor's possession even if that causes some indirect loss to the other partner, because the latter did not acquire a lien on that share merely by partnership. But the creditor may not seize from the innocent partner's own possession where that partner's custodial or proprietary rights intervene. When the creditor claims the object itself was stolen from him and truly belongs to him, the entire encumbered-property rationale falls away and seizure is permitted according to all opinions.
For he was negligent. Sema writes that a borrower is liable even without negligence. Mishneh LaMelech explains that in this case the only true unavoidable accidents are classic accidents such as breakage or death; where Levi says the value is actually owed to him and has a migo, liability of Shimon arises only because Shimon negligently handed property to someone with whom the owner cannot litigate effectively. That explanation is difficult. If Levi's migo is not accepted strongly enough to treat him as owner, why would an ordinary borrower not be liable even without negligence? The explanation can be reconciled with Sema in siman 125 and with Maharam of Lublin. According to their approach, one who voluntarily pays another's debt can lose his money because the debtor may say, 'I would have appeased the creditor and settled for less.' But where the creditor or beit din forcibly seized the property, that argument is weaker. Bava Kamma teaches that where beit din first seized property, Rabbi Natan's lien can justify the result. The same should apply where the creditor himself seized through coercion and where the law gave him a legitimate claim against the person holding the debtor's assets. Where the person is seized merely on another's account and has no legal obligation at all, he may be exempt only where the seizure was wrongful. But if secular or Torah law itself authorizes proceeding against him, as with taxes under the law of the kingdom or Rabbi Natan's lien, the loss can properly fall on the debtor. Thus Mishneh LaMelech's distinction is sound in this limited sense: voluntary payment can create a claim that the payer caused his own loss, whereas compelled seizure can remove that objection. This does not mean every person who seizes for a creditor becomes liable as a tortfeasor. Once he seizes on the creditor's behalf, the creditor acquires the property. The seizer's liability would then be only for causing damage, and where the underlying loss is uncertain he is exempt. A custodian is different because he has an affirmative duty of return; uncertain proper return can leave him liable. I write this only to explain Mishneh LaMelech and Maharam of Lublin. Practical law does not follow them in every respect, as explained in simanim 128 and 180, nor do we follow Sema's broader rule in siman 125.
