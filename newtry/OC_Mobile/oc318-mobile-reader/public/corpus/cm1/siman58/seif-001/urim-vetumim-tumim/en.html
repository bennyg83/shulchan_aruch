He collects only with an oath. Sema and Bach understand this to mean that an oath is required even if the debtor does not say, "Swear to me," because there is an evident weakness in the document. According to Tur, who generally accepts migo to avoid an oath, this requires explanation. The creditor could have denied receiving any payment at all, in which case he would not have had to swear unless the debtor demanded an oath. Why, then, should admitting partial payment force an oath without such a demand? One could say that the migo is weak, or that had the debtor demanded an oath even under the denial, the creditor would have had to swear. Alternatively, once the document itself has been weakened, it no longer has sufficient force to support an extracting migo without an oath. Shach disagrees. He holds that the note has the same law as any ordinary document: unless the debtor explicitly demands an oath, beit din does not impose one. He brings proof from Ramban in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 19. Ramban holds that even if a single witness saw payment but the creditor says the payment was for another debt, there is no Torah oath generated by that witness, because the witness is not directly testifying that this document was paid. All the more so there should be no automatic oath where there is not even one witness. Neither side cites Maharik, responsum 120, where the issue is discussed directly. The questioner there took the same position as Shach and brought proof from Rabbenu Chananel and Rabbenu Tam, who interpret "the document was weakened" to mean only that an oath is required. From that he argued that where there is a migo and the note was not actually weakened, no oath should be required. Maharik rejected the proof because those authorities were discussing a document containing an explicit credibility clause. Where such credibility exists, saying the document is weakened means the special credibility is removed and an oath becomes necessary. Without a credibility clause, perhaps an oath is needed even according to the view that the document itself is not weakened. Maharik supports this from Rif's wording: some explain that where the note contains credibility, the creditor's special credibility is weakened and he collects only with an oath. Maharik infers that the mention of a credibility clause is deliberate. But this proof from Rif is not decisive. The "some explain" cited by Rif is Rabbenu Chananel, as Ran records. Rabbenu Chananel introduced the credibility-clause case specifically to answer the difficulty from Ketubot, where a father's personal knowledge weakens a note through one witness. If every ordinary single witness already imposed the same oath, there would have been no novelty in that episode. Rosh explicitly cites Rabbenu Chananel this way. Thus the mention of credibility does not prove that an ordinary note always requires an oath. The questioner's proof remains valid, though not merely from Rabbenu Chananel and Rabbenu Tam, who were discussing credibility. The stronger proof is from Tosafot in Ketubot 85b and Shevuot 42a. Tosafot do not explain the case through a special credibility clause, yet they treat "not weakened" as meaning the document stands without any oath. We do not find a clear authority disagreeing with this point. Therefore, Shach's ruling is correct. Tur's phrase that the creditor "must swear" uses the standard language for a case where an oath is demanded. It does not mean beit din imposes an oath automatically without the debtor's request.
And likewise one who deposited, and so forth. Shach says Rama's presentation is disordered. In his reading, Rama's first opinion holds that where the agent would be liable to reimburse the principal, the recipient must return the money to the agent. Thus, if someone deposits property with another without telling him it belongs to a third person and then directs him to transfer it, the transferee cannot seize it for a debt owed by the true owner, because the intermediary would have to reimburse the depositor. The second opinion, however, allows seizure even where the intermediary suffers. Shach therefore says Rama's later phrase "and likewise one who deposited" belongs only to the first view. Shach also rules like the first opinion based on Responsa Rosh, klal 110, and says the contrary Mordechai view is merely a marginal gloss or student error. This is not convincing. Ritva rules explicitly that the recipient may seize the money even though the messenger will become liable, and Responsa Maharam, Prague printing, siman 811, says the same. Rosh himself appears to take that position in the case of the cattle keeper discussed below. The view cannot be dismissed as a student's mistake. More importantly, Rama's two opinions concern a different issue. Rashbam explains the case of the people of Bei Chozae as follows. If the messenger would be liable to reimburse the sender, why can the recipients keep the money? He answers that they have a migo: they could deny receiving anything, and therefore they can say the messenger expressly told them to apply the money to a different debt. Under Rashbam, mere silent delivery is not enough. The recipient needs an explicit claim that the messenger designated the money for another debt. Maharam gives a different answer. Since the money was handed over without the messenger demanding the note, the recipients may presume it was intended for another oral debt. Even if the messenger said nothing, the circumstances themselves permit seizure. These are the two opinions in Rama. The first requires the recipient to say expressly that the messenger gave the money for another debt. The second says that even a silent, unspecified transfer can be applied to another debt. Once this is understood, Rama's later rule concerning a deposit is independent of those two views. Where Reuven's deposit is in Shimon's hands and Shimon transfers it to Levi as Reuven's property, Levi cannot seize it for Reuven's debt if doing so will make Shimon reimburse Reuven. The special inference used in the Bei Chozae case does not exist. There is no omission of a demand for a note showing that the money was meant for another debt. Therefore all agree the recipient cannot impose loss on the intermediary in that ordinary deposit case. There is nevertheless room to question the deposit rule if Shimon lied to Levi and said, "Reuven instructed me to give you this property," when witnesses establish that Reuven never gave such instructions. Why should Levi lose merely because Shimon told a falsehood? If Levi otherwise has a valid right to seize Reuven's property, what halachic penalty transfers the burden to him merely because Shimon lied about authorization? Rivash's proof comes from the cattle keeper case: after the owner's death, a creditor seized an animal and the keeper became liable to the heirs for allowing the seizure. Yet the creditor retained the animal. Bach explains that the keeper claimed the seizure occurred only after death, while the creditor said it occurred during the owner's lifetime. The keeper's loss resulted from his own assertion. The creditor can say: "Had you told the truth according to my version, you would not have been liable. Your loss is self-inflicted." That distinction is significant. Where Shimon's lie is unrelated to Levi's right of seizure, why should Levi bear the cost? If Shimon would have been liable whether he spoke truthfully or falsely, the recipient should not necessarily lose his seizure merely to save the messenger. Maharam's approach supplies the solution. The messenger's liability is a form of dinei de-garmi. Where he himself contributes to the loss by making an unnecessary statement or transfer structure that enables the recipient to seize, his conduct is part of the causation. Once the supposed injured intermediary participated in creating the situation, the law of garmi may not operate to shift the entire loss away from the seizing creditor. Thus Rama's language is coherent. The two earlier opinions concern whether an unspecified transfer can be treated as payment of another debt. The later deposit rule applies independently and ordinarily prevents seizure where the intermediary would thereby suffer. But where the intermediary's own conduct materially created the loss, the exact result can depend on the laws of garmi. In any event, Rama's structure is not contradictory, and the second opinion is a major position supported by Maharam and Ritva.
But if he seized it, and so forth. This is Ritva's view, and Rosh also appears to hold it. Responsa Rosh, klal 106, deals with Reuven who deposited Shimon's property with Levi. Levi wished to seize it because Shimon owed him money. Rosh ruled that since Reuven would have to reimburse Shimon, Levi would effectively be seizing Reuven's property rather than Shimon's and therefore could not do so. But in Rosh's halachic rulings, Perek HaKotev, he addresses the cattle keeper. In our law that movable property of heirs is subject to creditors, if a creditor seized an ox of the heirs after the owner's death without witnesses and claimed a debt against the estate, the seizure can be effective through a migo that he could have denied taking it. The cattle keeper is nevertheless liable to the heirs for negligence in allowing the seizure. Maharit asks: if the keeper is liable to reimburse the heirs, why should the creditor's seizure be valid? This clearly indicates that Rosh accepts Ritva's principle that the seizing creditor is not automatically deprived merely because another person will suffer reimbursement liability. Shach argues from the responsum that Rosh cannot agree with Ritva, but that inference is not decisive. Rosh's statement there that "since Reuven must pay, Levi is effectively seizing Reuven's property" means something more specific. Reuven himself was the person who entrusted the item to Levi. He can say: "I placed this property in your hands on the understanding that you would not seize it from me. Since I am personally liable to restore it to Shimon, your seizure is effectively from me, the person who delivered it to you." That creates a direct claim between depositor and recipient. In the cattle keeper case, the creditor never received the animal from the keeper. He seized property that, in truth, was subject to his debt. He can say to the keeper: "Why should your reimbursement obligation concern me? The property is encumbered to me. Why should I lose what is mine in order to save you from a loss?" This is especially strong if, as many authorities hold, garmi liability does not apply where one acts for his own legitimate benefit rather than with intent to harm. Thus there is no contradiction in Rosh. Where the person seeking return is the one who originally delivered the item to the seizing party and remains liable for it, he can demand its return. Where the creditor independently seized property subject to his claim, we do not undo the seizure merely because a custodian will have to reimburse another person. I discuss this further below in siman 125. In practical halachah, Ritva's approach is primary, as Bach also rules.
