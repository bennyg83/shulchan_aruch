An authenticated note, etc. The same rule applies to a note that is not yet authenticated. If the creditor later authenticates it, he is believed concerning his prior claim that the payment was applied to a different debt, because at the time of the claim he could have denied receiving any payment. This follows from the beginning of siman 82. One cannot distinguish this case on the ground that his claim rested only upon that alternative claim. The Ramban’s discussion at the end of siman 83 concerns a creditor who admits that the document is presently defective; there the later authentication does not revive an alternative claim that did not exist when he spoke. Here, according to the creditor’s own account, the note was valid throughout and merely lacked formal authentication. Once it is authenticated, his earlier claim is established.

If it contains no clause of trust, he collects only upon taking an oath. The Sema and Bach say that even if the borrower does not demand an oath, beit din raises the demand for him because the creditor admitted receiving money, creating an impairment comparable to one who partially impairs his note. This does not appear correct. Since the note itself has not been impaired, it should be no worse than any other note. The creditor is believed that the money was for another debt because he could have denied receiving it. Just as a creditor who denies receipt need not swear unless the borrower expressly says, “Swear to me,” so too here he need not swear absent such a demand. The rule that beit din itself requires an oath applies only where the instrument is objectively impaired, as where the creditor admits partial payment of this very note. Here the question is which debt the admitted payment satisfied. Therefore, unless the borrower demands the oath, the creditor collects without it. If the note contains a trust clause broad enough to believe the creditor concerning payment, he collects without an oath even upon the borrower’s demand, according to the scope of that clause.
Whether he told him, “Take the note,” etc. If he did not tell the payer to take the note, then even if he informed him that the debt was evidenced by a note, the payer is exempt from responsibility for leaving it outstanding. He may say, “I assumed that you relied upon the creditor’s trustworthiness.” The Beit Yosef cites this from the Baal HaTerumot. The Bach’s interpretation of the Tur is not compelled, particularly his statement that where the payer was merely informed of the note he is exempt only if he paid before witnesses. The Baal HaTerumot implies no such limitation. The decisive point is whether he was instructed to obtain the note, not whether witnesses saw the payment.

Likewise, one who deposited, etc. The Rama’s presentation is not arranged properly. This clause follows the first cited opinion. When a messenger deposits the principal’s funds with another person without telling that person that the deposit is being made on the principal’s behalf, the principal cannot seize those funds from the depositary. Since the messenger remains liable to the principal, seizure would effectively take the messenger’s property rather than property belonging to the third person against whom the principal claims. The later words, “Some say this applies only where it came into the depositary’s possession,” follow the dissenting view. The governing distinction is whether the person from whom the property was taken remains personally liable if the possessor is allowed to keep it. A creditor may not seize property when doing so will merely shift the loss onto an innocent depositor or messenger who acknowledges the true owner’s claim. The possessor cannot say that he is collecting from the third party when, by his own admission, the immediate transferor will have to reimburse that third party. If, however, the immediate transferor denies liability or the possessor has an independent claim that would prevent reimbursement, the analysis differs. The earlier authorities cited by the Rama must be read according to this principle, and not as a general license to seize any property that happens to come through a debtor’s hands.
But if he seized it from the depositor and has an alternative claim, etc. I have already written that the Rosh and the Rivash disagree. The Rivash states expressly that there is no difference between property voluntarily deposited and property forcibly seized. Whenever the possessor admits that the person from whom he took it will be liable to reimburse another if the seizure stands, he may not retain it, for he cannot cause that person a loss in order to collect his own claim. The Bach is therefore difficult: he first rules in accordance with the Rivash and afterward cites the Ritva’s contrary ruling without qualification.
For he was negligent. Meaning that he failed to safeguard the deposit properly, and for that reason the creditor was able to seize it. Had he guarded it properly, seizure would not have been possible. The Ir Shushan explains instead that the depositor is liable because he was negligent in depositing it without witnesses. That is an unnecessary forced explanation. It seems he wrote so because he regarded the Rivash’s view as primary, under which a possessor who admits the owner’s claim cannot retain the property if the immediate holder would thereby become liable, except where that liability arises solely through the holder’s own admission. The simpler meaning, however, is negligence in the actual safekeeping of the item.
