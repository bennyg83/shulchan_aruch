Likewise, one who deposited. This follows the first opinion cited above. Where the agent would suffer loss, another person may not seize the property because that is effectively seizing from the agent himself. Therefore the creditor likewise cannot seize from the depositor. But if the custodian says the item was deposited with him on behalf of that creditor, and he is believed through a migo that there was no deposit, then the depositor's denial does not matter. The custodian can say, "Why should I suffer because you falsely obligate yourself by your own admission?" The Beurim explains the main reason differently. Every depositor is treated as though he says, "If it is stolen and you reimburse me, it will be acquired to you," as explained in Bava Metzia. The deposited object is therefore like movable property subject to the custodian's potential right and cannot be seized by the creditor. But if the custodian says it was deposited on behalf of that creditor and is believed through a migo, then by his own admission the item never became potentially his, so the creditor can seize it because liens are Torah law. Accordingly, one may not seize from the custodian's own domain where the custodian will thereby suffer loss, but one may seize the debtor's own property even if another person who accepted responsibility will suffer loss. Likewise with two partners, one may not seize partnership property to satisfy one partner's debt where that harms the other partner, but one may seize from the debtor partner's own property. If the claimant says, "This object is mine because it was stolen from me," he may seize it even if another person will suffer loss. Market protection does not require reimbursing the custodian, because that enactment was made for purchases, not deposits, and loss here occurs only through the custodian's negligence.
