A validated debt document. The same applies if it is not yet validated but he is presently able to validate it, Shach.
Orally. The same applies to another written loan where he claims, "I returned the document to you." The Mechaber mentions an oral loan to teach the stronger point that even where his migo is weak, because a person generally does not leave a written loan unpaid while paying an oral loan, as Rosh writes, he is nevertheless believed when he has a migo, since the fact that the document remains in his possession supports him.
And I accepted it for that debt. Even if the payer gave the money specifically toward this written debt, the lender may say, "I did not accept it toward this debt, but toward another debt," Sema.
If it contains no clause of trust, etc. Sema holds that even if the borrower did not say, "Swear to me," we raise that claim for him because there is some indication of irregularity. Shach disagrees and holds that its law is the same as all other documents. See Tumim, where I agree with Shach, although Maharik appears to support Sema.
The lender cannot retain it. Since the agent would suffer a loss, the recipient must prevent that loss. He is not seizing property belonging to the principal, but property belonging to the agent.
Others disagree, etc. Shach holds that this dissent is the view cited in Mordechai that a person who seizes property need never consider whether the agent will suffer a loss. He therefore rules against it, because most poskim disagree. He also explains that the following clause, "Likewise one who deposited, etc.," does not follow this dissenting view, because there, if the agent did not say that the principal instructed him to deliver it, the recipient could not seize it when the agent would thereby suffer a loss. This is correct, unlike Sema. In Tumim, however, I established that this dissent is Maharam's view. He too generally agrees that the agent should not suffer loss. This case is different because the agent delivered the money without specification and did not mention the document, which is treated as though he expressly delivered it toward that document. The agent therefore caused his own loss and we do not consider the resulting harm to him. In an ordinary case, however, where the agent did not cause the loss through his own negligence, the recipient may not cause him loss. Thus, where one deposited property and the agent did not say that he acts on the depositor's instructions, he did not cause loss through his words, and according to everyone we do not cause him a loss. See Tumim, where I concluded from most poskim that this dissenting view is unquestionably primary and the first view is not the law.
And the custodian said, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that even if the custodian lied, and witnesses establish that the depositor never instructed him to deliver the item to that person, once the custodian falsely said that he had been instructed, the recipient's retention is no longer classified as actionable causation. The recipient is exempt and we do not consider the custodian's resulting loss. See there.
As a deposit. In that case he may not seize property belonging to the agent when the agent will thereby suffer a personal loss. That would be like seizing the agent's own property. A custodian who received a deposit must make a proper return, and there is no proper return where he must compensate from his own pocket. He cannot say, "Why should I care about your loss? I am seizing for myself property already subject to my debt, so why should I be more concerned for your loss than mine?" This argument does not apply to a deposit, because proper return is required. See Tumim, where I explained that this also reconciles Rosh and removes any contradiction. This is the primary law, as I wrote above in siman 125.
For he was negligent. Meaning that he allowed the property to be seized. If he was an unpaid custodian and the recipient took it by theft, he is exempt. If he was a paid custodian and it was taken by force, as explained below in the laws of custodians, that is an unavoidable event and he is likewise exempt. A borrower, however, is liable in every case because he is liable even for unavoidable events. Yet even though the borrower must pay, since he did not voluntarily deliver the item into the recipient's possession, the recipient may say, as above, "What concern is your loss to me? I must protect myself from loss." See below in siman 125.
Since he did not say, etc. Responsa Mabit is inclined to say that where the principal is a gentile and the agent is paid, his responsibility is greater, and because "their mouth speaks falsehood," the agent is negligent if he delivers without witnesses even though he was not told to do so. Shach writes that this requires study because the reasoning is not compelling. Shach is correct. We do not extract money based on such an inference.
