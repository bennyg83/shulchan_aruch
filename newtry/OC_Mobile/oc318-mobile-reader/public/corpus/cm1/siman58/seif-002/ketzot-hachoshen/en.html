But in the presence of one witness we do not say that the document was weakened. This is not like Rabbi Abba's piece of metal, where the defendant is subject to a Torah oath and, being unable to swear, must pay. Here the creditor faces only the rabbinic oath imposed where one witness says the document was paid. Ramban therefore rules that inability to swear does not create payment in the same way. Many authorities questioned Ramban because in the episode of Avimi, Ramban himself indicates that where denial of the messenger would require an oath, the creditor lacks a migo and the document is weakened. The Shach resolves this by distinguishing before and after payment: after the money has already been paid, the matter can become a Torah oath and thus one obligated to swear but unable to swear must pay. Yet Rama's unqualified formulation implies that one witness never weakens the document, and if repayment after payment automatically required return, the entire distinction would be practically pointless. It appears instead that after payment there is still no rule of inability to swear according to Rama. Even if two witnesses say there was an unspecified payment, Rama allows the creditor who retained the money to say it was applied to another debt. If so, one witness certainly does not force return merely by creating an oath. The creditor's claim that the money was applied elsewhere is itself a valid claim of possession. According to the Mechaber, who does not allow retention against two witnesses to payment, one witness may create greater difficulty after payment. But even there one must distinguish whether the witness says merely that money was paid, or says it was paid specifically on this document. An unspecified payment can still be explained as payment of another debt. If the witness says that payment was expressly made on this document, and the creditor admits that fact but says he mentally applied it to another debt, then after payment the creditor can indeed become one obligated to swear but unable to swear. The episode of Avimi was precisely such a case. Avimi sent money for that document, and the creditors nevertheless claimed it was applied to another debt. If two witnesses had established that specific payment, even Rama would not allow retention. Therefore one witness can create the Rabbi Abba structure after payment in that setting. Before payment, Ramban can still say that the creditor faces only the rabbinic oath attached to collection on a document. Another way to understand Ramban follows his discussion in Bava Batra: the migo in the "other debt" claim is not a migo to collect the money written in the document; it is a migo concerning the money already received. Once the money is already in hand, that claim can stand independently. Therefore an unspecified one witness payment does not weaken the document. The practical conclusion is this: if payment was made before one witness without mentioning this document, the document is not weakened either before or after payment according to Rama. If the payer or messenger expressly identified this document and the creditor acknowledges that identification but claims another debt, then after payment there is a genuine inability to swear issue, and even before payment the creditor's migo may fail under Ramban's analysis. See siman 92, seif katan 10.
We do not say that the document was weakened. Bach writes that this remains so even where the payer expressly mentioned this document, because with only one witness the creditor has a migo that he could deny the payment entirely. The Shach notes that Tosafot, Ran, and Ramban appear to disagree. This issue depends upon the two explanations in Tosafot of Rabbi Abba's case. Rabbeinu Yitzchak says that one has no migo to contradict a witness when doing so would require the audacity of swearing directly against him. Under that approach, whenever the creditor must swear against the witness, even a rabbinic oath can prevent the migo, since the practical audacity is the same. Rabbeinu Tam gives a different explanation: even if a migo exists, Torah law itself says that when one witness stands against him he must either swear or pay. Under that approach, only a Torah oath creates the rule of inability to swear. A rabbinic oath does not. Ramban's discussion in Bava Batra first cites the approach that no migo exists against the witness and then notes the difficulty from Avimi. Another explanation says the Torah gave one witness the force of two until he is contradicted by oath. This fits Rabbeinu Tam's structure more closely. Accordingly, Ramban's responsum cited by Baal HaTerumot, that one witness does not weaken the document, can be understood as following the second explanation. This also clarifies the episode of Avimi. According to Rama in seif 1, if the messenger expressly applied the payment to this document, the creditors must deny the messenger's account in order to keep the money for another debt. If the messenger is then regarded as a witness, under Rabbeinu Tam they must either swear or pay. But if they deny him in the very fact that he applied it to this document, their present claim is already a direct contradiction of his testimony and they can swear against him. The remaining testimony is merely that an unspecified payment occurred, which by itself does not weaken the document under Rama. Thus Avimi's case can coexist with the rule that an ordinary one witness payment does not weaken the document. Rashba in Kiddushin likewise explains that once the cause of interest is removed, a formerly interested agent can become a witness, and he asks from Avimi. He answers that the agent there is not testifying about the document itself in a way that necessarily defeats it, but only about the money transferred, and the creditor may still answer with the law of another debt. The precise application again turns on whether the agent expressly designated this document and whether the creditor admits that designation.
