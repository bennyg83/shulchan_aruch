His reasoning is that even if the creditor contradicted the witness, he would only incur a rabbinic oath imposed to appease the opposing party; in such a case no oath is necessary at all. This differs from simanim 89 and 90 above. Although the claim that the payment was applied separately is believed only through a migo that he could have denied receiving it, the underlying denial itself would require only a rabbinic oath. This requires study, because they themselves write in Bava Metzia 3b in accordance with the Ramah. See Bava Batra 31a, s.v. "is it not," where they write that here he is not obligated to swear because, etc., even though a rabbinic oath applies, as stated on 33a, s.v. "migo," etc. I examined the root of the matter. It was derived from Bava Metzia 5, where the Gemara says, "I mean that the opposing party should take an oath," and this is because of the rule that one who cannot swear must pay. The Sages merely instituted that the opposing party swear because otherwise a person would not desist, etc., as Tosafot write there. The Gemara says there that this was Rav Nachman's enactment, etc., and does not explain that because the obligation is rabbinic we do not apply the rule that one who cannot swear must pay and therefore the Torah law remains in place. We rule that with all who swear and collect, one enactment is not layered upon another, so where the claimant cannot swear he cannot collect and the defendant instead takes a hesset oath. The same would apply with one witness. All of this is according to the Rambam. Those who disagree, however, hold that with an authenticated document the creditor collects without an oath, because the plain meaning of the Gemara is that it is an enactment, etc., and the matter is restored to Torah law, as stated in the chapter HaKotev: "Rava said, by rabbinic law," etc., "the Rabbis imposed," etc., and as written in siman 82, seif 8, and siman 92, seif 9. Accordingly, the rule that one who cannot swear must pay does not apply here. This is also the Ramban's view in Milchamot, like those who disagree. This is what the Ramban writes here: "It is not comparable to the case of the piece of metal," etc.; rather, it is an oath enacted by the Sages, etc., and where he cannot, etc. See the Shach, seif katan 25, who cites him.
