[" And he said, have I not repaid you before so and so and so and so, etc. The Sema writes that if the instrument states that the borrower trusted the lender as two witnesses, then even if he paid him before witnesses the lender is believed to say, 'Those payments were for another debt.' The Sema reasons that even without such a claim the lender can contradict witnesses who testify to payment, since the borrower granted him greater credibility than witnesses. Mahara Sasson, responsum 74, argues that where witnesses testify to payment there is a proof from Tosafot that a migo is weakened when the alternative claim would directly contradict a witness, and he cites the Rosh regarding even disqualified witnesses. He therefore distinguishes between testimony that the lender received money merely as payment and testimony that he expressly received it in payment of this very instrument. I do not find this convincing. Tosafot there speak specifically where the alternative claim would require an oath against the witness. Where no such oath would be required, Tosafot themselves state that the migo remains valid even though the alternative claim would contradict the witness. Moreover, there the present claim is not independently strong and depends on the migo. Here, however, where the borrower expressly trusted the lender as two witnesses, the lender's claim that the payment was for another debt is itself a strong claim. Since the borrower granted him the credibility of two witnesses to say that this instrument was not paid, the borrower should have had the payment written on the instrument. Maharshal likewise writes that once such enhanced credibility is written into the instrument, there is no defect until the lender admits before beit din that the instrument was paid, and no migo is needed. This also answers the proof from the Rosh, since there the claim itself required a migo, unlike here. The Rama in siman 70, seif 4, likewise indicates that even where witnesses testify to an unspecified payment, we do not say that the lender prefers the claim of another debt merely because it avoids contradicting the witnesses. I questioned the Rama there only in the case where the borrower was told to pay solely before designated witnesses, according to those authorities who hold that other witnesses were not thereby disqualified. That differs from a case where the lender was trusted as two witnesses, which disqualifies all contrary testimony. This is also the ruling of Divrei Rivot and Gedulei Terumah, that whether the witnesses testify expressly to payment of this debt or merely to payment in general, the lender may claim that it was for another debt if he was trusted as two witnesses. The Bach also clearly indicates this, and this is the correct ruling. The Bach nevertheless writes that even with such enhanced credibility the lender must swear, because a migo does not exempt one from an oath and because the alternative claim would be brazen. Divrei Rivot and Gedulei Terumah likewise require an oath. In my view this is incorrect. The rule that a migo does not exempt from an oath applies only where the claimant is otherwise obligated to swear by law and seeks release from that oath by virtue of a migo. Here, one who was trusted as two witnesses is treated as though two witnesses support his assertion that the money was due on another account, and there is no underlying oath obligation at all. Indeed, where one is trusted as two witnesses, even two actual witnesses contradicting him do not impose an oath. Further, wherever the claim of another debt is accepted through a migo, the present claim assumes the force of the alternative claim. Thus, where payment was made privately, the lender may say that it was for another debt without an oath unless the borrower specifically demands one. Certainly, according to what I have explained, once the lender was trusted as two witnesses, the claim of another debt is independently strong even without a migo. Nor is the Bach's concern for a brazen migo applicable, since that problem arises only where the alternative claim itself would require an oath. It is therefore clear to me that where the lender was trusted as two witnesses and claims that the payment was for another debt, he need not swear at all. As for the Sema's statement that even without the claim of another debt the lender may simply contradict witnesses to payment, I have already shown in siman 71, seif katan 3, that this is incorrect. Without the claim of another debt he cannot contradict the witnesses. The Sema further writes that even if the borrower possesses a handwritten acknowledgment from the lender, this does not defeat the lender's credibility, citing the Tur and Mechaber in siman 71 and the Rama in siman 70. This too is not compelling. The lender can claim another debt only where he has a migo to deny receipt, but the lender's own handwriting is like one hundred witnesses and removes that migo. Moreover, by writing the acknowledgment he effectively nullified the special credibility granted to him. The Bach and Gedulei Terumah rule so explicitly. The cited proof from siman 71 is no proof, for there the text does not say that the lender admitted payment of this particular instrument, and that passage states the preferred procedure for the outset. It also does not discuss the claim that payment was for another debt. The proof from siman 70 is likewise inapposite, because there the borrower violated an instruction to pay only before designated witnesses, which itself makes the lender's claim that the payment was for another debt plausible even without a migo. From the Sema and the later authorities cited above it follows that the rule that payment before witnesses weakens the instrument applies even when the instrument contains ordinary credibility in favor of the lender. This is also explicit in Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 19, and again at the end of part 2 of sha'ar 66."]
[" And they testified that he repaid him, etc. See Maharshal, responsum 51, who wishes to say that this applies only when the witnesses testify to payment of the full amount of the instrument, not a lesser amount. This does not appear correct to me, and neither Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 66, nor the Tur above, siman 30, seif 8, implies such a limitation."]
[" It was another debt that you paid me. Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 66, part 2, considers a case where the lender does not say that the payment was for another debt, but instead says, 'From what you paid before these witnesses, I returned the instrument to you.' Since where payment was made before witnesses the lender cannot claim another debt without a migo, perhaps the same should apply to the claim that he returned the instrument, so that the lender is not believed and we do not abandon certainty in favor of doubt. Baal HaTerumot notes that Rabbi Yosef HaLevi appears to contradict this, since he writes that even with witnesses the lender may say that the money was a gift when the witnesses do not know whether it was a gift or repayment. In his view, the witnesses would therefore need to testify that the money was given specifically in payment of this instrument. Otherwise, just as the lender can say that an unspecified transfer was a gift, so too when they testify that it was payment he could say it was payment of a different debt. Beit Yosef cites this briefly at the end of this siman. In my view the difficulty is unfounded. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi agrees that only where the witnesses do not know whether the transfer was a gift or payment may the lender claim another debt through the migo that it was a gift. Where there is no such migo, he is not believed. This is explicit in the Rosh in Shevuat HaDayanim, in the other authorities, and in Baal HaTerumot himself at the beginning of sha'ar 19, where he records Rabbi Yosef HaLevi as saying that the instrument is weakened specifically when the witnesses testify that the money was given as payment, but do not know whether for this instrument or another, because then there is no migo. The Tur also proves this, for in siman 30, seif 8, he writes that the lender cannot say that the payment was for an oral loan or another written debt which he later returned, whereas here he brings Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's position and the dispute concerning it. It follows that Rabbi Yosef HaLevi agrees in this case, and this is the principal ruling."]

[" The instrument is void. This applies where both claims belong to the same claimant. But if with respect to the debt in the instrument he acts through a power of attorney, while the money that he admitted receiving belongs to his own personal claim, the instrument is not thereby void. The reason is that he could return the authorization to its owner and would then remain only with his personal claim concerning the money he received. As to that money, once he admits receiving it, he is believed with an oath. This is the Sema's explanation, based on the responsum of the Rosh at the end of klal 86. The Rosh adduces proofs from the laws later in simanim 119 and 148. It appears that the oath mentioned here is a heset oath."]

Since we do not tear up the instrument, we do not require the borrower to swear a heset oath while the lender still holds the instrument against him. If the lender wishes the borrower to swear, the lender must first return the instrument, and only then do we administer the heset oath. So writes the Ran, cited by Beit Yosef. [" He is established as a denier and the instrument is void. It appears that all authorities agree to this rule. Once he has been established as a false denier, the instrument is completely void and is torn up."]
[" He is established as a denier, and the borrower shall swear. The borrower swears a heset oath and is exempt. Baal HaTerumot cites an opinion that once the lender has been established as a false denier, no oath at all is imposed on the borrower. Gedulei Terumah questions why Baal HaTerumot presents the first view anonymously and the other merely as 'some say,' since ordinarily when a claimant is established as a false denier the opposing party need not swear. He further cites sha'ar 11, where one who first denied ever borrowing and later claims that he paid twice cannot require the lender to swear, because the initial denial constitutes an admission that no payment occurred. He argues that here too, when the lender initially says that he never received anything, he has thereby admitted that the money was not received on another account, and once witnesses testify that the borrower gave him that money as payment, it necessarily paid the instrument. In my view this comparison is incorrect. In sha'ar 11, a person who says, 'I never borrowed,' necessarily says, 'I never repaid,' because repayment is impossible without a loan. Here, although the lender is shown to have lied when he says he never received the money, and this suffices to void the instrument, he has not been shown to lie concerning his claim that the borrower owes him another debt. That latter assertion may still be true. Nor is the general rule about one established as a false denier applicable, because that rule concerns the very same money. Here the alleged additional debt is a different monetary claim. The Gemara and later authorities expressly state, and the Tur rules in siman 79, seif 8, that one established as a false denier regarding one sum is not thereby established as a false denier regarding another. Therefore the first view is correct: the borrower must swear a heset oath."]

[" For even in such a case, etc. The reason is that a gift is uncommon. A person ordinarily repays a debt he owes before giving his property away as a gift. See Maharshal, responsum 36 and 374."]
[" But before one witness, etc. The Rama states this simply because in Darkei Moshe he cites Beit Yosef as saying that no authority disputes the Ramban on this point. In my view the law requires study. Tosafot in Ketubot and Bava Batra, as well as the Ran and even the Ramban in his novellae to Bava Batra, explicitly indicate that when the lender would have had to swear against the witness had he denied the payment, there is no valid migo and the instrument is weakened. One might reconcile the sources by saying that Tosafot, the Ran, and the Ramban in Bava Batra refer to the situation after payment, where a prudent borrower can bring the lender to a Torah level oath. In that case, if the lender then claims that the money was for another debt, he becomes one who is obligated to swear but cannot, as in the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba. Baal HaTerumot in the name of the Ramban, the Tur, and the Rama here could instead mean only that before payment one witness does not weaken the instrument to the point of preventing collection. They might nevertheless agree that after payment the borrower may sue the lender, at which point the lender may become obligated to swear and unable to do so. This would reconcile the Ramban's writings. Still, the plain language of Tosafot, the Ran, and the Ramban does not sound this way, since they do not invoke the separate sugya concerning a prudent litigant, and their discussion implies that even before payment the lender cannot rely on the claim of another debt. The matter therefore requires study. Maharam of Lublin rejected a questioner's attempt to rule in accordance with Tosafot, but he apparently overlooked the Ran and the Ramban himself. One should not object from the rule that inability to take a rabbinic oath does not ordinarily create the law of one who is obligated to swear and cannot. That rule applies where the present claim is independently strong, or where the migo alternative would not have required a more severe oath. Here the claim that the payment was for another debt is intrinsically weak and works only through a migo of outright denial. Since outright denial against the witness would require a severe oath resembling a Torah oath, while the present claim would require at most heset, the migo falls away and the instrument is weakened. The sages instituted an oath modeled on a Torah oath with its attendant consequences. This also explains the Ran elsewhere, who writes that an oath against one witness who testifies to payment is generally rabbinic and that where the party cannot know enough to contradict the witness he does not lose merely because he cannot swear. There the party's substantive claim is independently valid. The Ritva in Ketubot first cites the approach of Tosafot and then another answer that because the oath is rabbinic, the rule of one obligated to swear and unable does not apply; he concludes that the latter is correct."]
[" Before one witness, etc. The Bach writes that the rule that one witness does not weaken the instrument applies not only where the witness does not specify that the payment was for this instrument, but even where he does specify it, because there is only one witness and the lender has a migo to deny the witness and say that no payment occurred at all. Nevertheless, according to Tosafot, the Ran, and the Ramban's novellae cited above, the instrument is weakened even in that case. If the lender actually contradicts the witness and says that there was no payment at all, however, all agree that he must swear against the witness, which is the Mishnah's rule of one witness testifying that an instrument has been paid."]
[" One witness, etc. This is unlike the case of Rabbi Abba's silver ingot. There the witness testifies directly that the ingot is in the defendant's possession, thereby generating a Torah oath; if the defendant does not contradict the witness, he is one obligated to swear who cannot. Here, however, the witness initially comes to testify only that the instrument was paid. One witness saying that an instrument was paid does not impose a Torah oath, but only an instituted rabbinic oath. Where the claimant cannot swear, he may collect without an oath. Accordingly, the law of Rabbi Abba's ingot does not apply. At most, one might require an oath and permit collection, but in the Ramban's view there is no defect in the instrument and no oath is imposed because of the witness. So writes Baal HaTerumot in the Ramban's name. I have already written above that other authorities disagree, and that the Ramban himself wrote otherwise in his novellae."]
[" We do not say that the instrument is weakened. The Sema writes that if the instrument contains a clause of credibility in favor of the lender, he need not even swear. Darkei Moshe and Gedulei Terumah likewise understand Baal HaTerumot in the Ramban's name as speaking specifically of an instrument containing such credibility, because otherwise, even where there is a migo and no witness at all, Baal HaTerumot writes that the lender must swear and collect. In my view this is incorrect. As I proved above, Baal HaTerumot in the Ramban's name speaks even where there is no credibility clause. His earlier statement that absent credibility the lender must swear and collect refers to a case where the borrower specifically says, 'Swear to me,' like the general law of instruments in siman 82."]
[" And see above, siman 70. In seif 4 I explained there several laws concerning the claim that a payment was for another debt. See also Maharam of Lublin, responsum 114, concerning a lender who sent an agent to collect money. The borrower instructed the agent not to transmit the money until the lender wrote a receipt on the instrument, but the agent failed to do so, and the lender then claimed that the money was for another debt. Maharam ruled that if the lender appointed the agent before witnesses, or by a writing whose handwriting is recognized, then because the lender cannot deny that he appointed the agent, he cannot claim that the payment was for another debt. If the appointment was not before witnesses and the handwriting is not recognized, the lender may make that claim through a migo that he never appointed the agent, while the agent must reimburse the borrower. According to what I concluded above, one must understand the case here as one where the lender claims that the agent also delivered the money to him on account of another debt. Otherwise, since the agent is liable to reimburse the borrower, the lender cannot retain the money and claim that it was for another debt."]
