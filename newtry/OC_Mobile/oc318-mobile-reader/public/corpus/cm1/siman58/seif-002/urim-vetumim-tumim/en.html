And these witnesses came, and so forth. Sema says that if the debtor gave the creditor credibility equal to two witnesses, and below in siman 71 we rule that such credibility can outweigh two witnesses, then the creditor may even claim "set-off against another debt." He has a migo: he could have denied the witnesses entirely and said no payment occurred. Shach agrees, and Knesset HaGedolah cites Maharshal and other authorities similarly. Mahara Sasson disagrees. What migo is there? It is more comfortable to make a claim that does not directly contradict witnesses than to say that the witnesses are lying. If the witnesses merely testify that money was paid, while the creditor says it was payment of another debt, he does not contradict their factual testimony. The supposed alternative, "there was no payment," would directly contradict them. This is a weak migo. His conceptual argument is persuasive. Nevertheless, Responsa Rosh, klal 71, siman 9, implies explicitly that where the debtor granted credibility equivalent to two witnesses, the creditor is believed even to say the payment was for another debt. The better explanation is not migo at all. This is the very substance of the special credibility clause. Why would a debtor authorize a creditor to contradict two kosher Jewish witnesses? We do not assume witnesses will lie, and the debtor cannot meaningfully have intended to license outright theft. Rather, the practical meaning of the clause is that even if witnesses testify to a payment, the creditor may explain that the money related to a different obligation or account and not to this particular note. That is precisely the "set-off" claim. Thus, when the creditor says the money was received for another debt, he is not merely using a weak migo to contradict witnesses. He is exercising the exact interpretive authority the debtor granted him through the two-witness credibility clause. Therefore he is believed, and Rosh's ruling is readily understood.
And they testified that he paid, and so forth. Responsa Perach Mateh Aharon, part 2, siman 2, rules that where two groups of witnesses contradict each other, one group saying the money was payment of this note and the other saying it was applied to another debt, we set the two against the two and leave the note in its original presumption of validity. He distinguishes this from the rule above in siman 46, where a document's handwriting is independently established and two witnesses say the signatories were disqualified. There, once two witnesses stand against two, we treat the matter as if no valid witnesses remain on the document, and the borrower is exempt. Here, he says, the note itself remains in its original presumption. I do not find this distinction sound. Tosafot in Ketubot 20a discuss a receipt signed by witnesses, with two other witnesses saying those signatories were disqualified. The testimony becomes two against two, yet the borrower remains exempt because the claimant bears the burden of proof. We do not say the original loan note remains in its presumption of validity and therefore defeats the receipt. There is no principled difference between a receipt whose witnesses say the debt was fully paid and our case where one pair says the money paid was for this very note while the other says it was for another debt. In both cases there is direct evidentiary contradiction over whether this obligation remains unpaid. Once the testimony is balanced, the creditor who seeks to extract money bears the burden of proof. Therefore, in such a two-against-two case, the borrower is exempt. The rule is simply that one who seeks to extract money must bring proof.
The document is void. Sema cites Responsa Rosh, klal 86, that where a person holds one debt through a power of attorney and there is proof he received payment from the borrower, he may still say, "That payment was for another debt owed to me personally," even if he lacks an ordinary migo. Since he can return the authorized note to its owner, it is treated as though already returned, and he can say the money he received was for his own claim. Rosh's reasoning invokes the principle "if you remain silent," as in Bava Kamma concerning property sold to three people at once. If the holder could restructure the situation by returning the note, the law can treat matters as though that step had already occurred. According to Shulchan Aruch below in siman 119, seif 5, following Rambam and Rashba, we do not always apply this "if you remain silent" mechanism at the conclusion of the sugya. Knesset HaGedolah therefore suggests that this ruling of Rosh depends on the dispute there, and perhaps this is why Mechaber omitted the responsum. However, as I explain below in siman 119, even the authorities who reject the principle there may agree in a case like this. The situation there has special features not present here. More fundamentally, Rosh may not need the "if you remain silent" device at all. The usual reason a creditor needs a migo to say "set-off against another debt" is the presumption that a person does not leave a loan secured by a note unpaid while instead paying an oral debt. But that presumption is strong only where both obligations are owed to the same creditor. If one note belongs to one person and the other debt belongs personally to the authorized agent, the borrower may prefer to pay the agent's personal oral debt first. One creditor may be easier for him to deal with while the other is harder. Everyday experience confirms that people choose which creditor to pay based on circumstances rather than simply preferring written over oral debt. Accordingly, where the payment was made to an authorized agent who also has his own independent claim against the borrower, his assertion that the payment was for his personal debt can stand even without a classic migo. The ordinary presumption favoring payment of the written debt does not apply with the same force across two different creditors. Therefore, Rosh's ruling is correct even according to those who limit the "if you remain silent" principle. The agent may say that the payment was for his own debt, and the authorized document remains unaffected.
He has thereby been established as one who made a false claim. Baal HaTerumot cites two views whether the borrower must nevertheless take a hesset oath. One view requires it; the other holds that once the claimant has been established as a false claimant, no oath at all is imposed upon the borrower. Gidulei Terumah rules that there is no hesset oath, as we generally rule where the claimant has been established as false. Shach distinguishes this case: although the holder of the note was shown false regarding the note, it can still be true that the borrower owes him money from some other source. Therefore, if the lender makes that separate claim, the borrower must either pay or swear that he owes nothing. In my opinion, if the lender claims an ordinary definite independent debt, this is obvious and cannot be the dispute in Baal HaTerumot. Remove the note entirely. Even if witnesses say explicitly that this particular note was unquestionably paid, that does not prevent the lender from saying, "You owe me another debt; swear and be exempt." What uncertainty would there be? Rather, the doubtful case is where the alleged other claim is of a kind that cannot itself be collected through beit din. For example, the lender says that the money he received was for avak ribbit, which is not recoverable through court, or for a profit-sharing arrangement where the borrower was trusted to say there were no profits. The lender says: "What you gave me was for that avak ribbit, or for profits which, once paid, you cannot reclaim by saying there had been no profit. This note remains unpaid." The borrower responds that the payment was on the note. There is then no independent monetary claim that can generate an oath, because avak ribbit is not judicially collectible and the profit claim is barred by the borrower's agreed credibility. Baal HaTerumot's doubt is whether the lender can nevertheless impose an oath by claiming that the note was not paid and that the money received related to that collateral matter. Gidulei Terumah correctly rules that he cannot. Once the lender has been established as false concerning the note, and there is no independent actionable monetary claim, there is no basis for an oath.
But before one witness, no, and so forth. Shach writes at length that many authorities, including Tosafot and Ramban in his chiddushim to Bava Batra, treat this like the case of R. Abba's ingot: since the claimant cannot deny the single witness and say no payment occurred, his assertion that the payment was for another debt makes him one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and therefore he loses. Shach himself notes a difficulty. We rule that the doctrine "obligated to swear and unable to swear" does not apply to a rabbinic oath. Here the oath required to contradict a single witness against a note is only rabbinic. Shach nevertheless argues that there is still no migo, because if the claimant denied receiving the money he would have to take a severe oath while now, by saying the payment was for another debt, he would avoid it. This is not correct. Shach follows the explanation in Shevuot that the reason there is no migo in the case of R. Abba's ingot is that the alternative claim would force him to contradict the witness under oath. Tosafot in Bava Batra reject that explanation. They hold that such a migo can exist despite the oath. The real reason there is no migo is that where Torah law says an uncontradicted single witness is treated like two because the defendant cannot swear, the claim fails against testimony equivalent to two witnesses. Most authorities accept this explanation, as I establish at length in my rules of migo below in siman 82. We likewise rule that one who was hired before one witness may still possess a migo to deny the hiring, even though doing so would require an oath against the witness. Accordingly, where only a rabbinic oath is involved and the doctrine of "unable to swear, must pay" does not apply, a migo can still be used against the single witness. Tosafot and Ramban in their chiddushim must therefore follow the view that even with a rabbinic oath one can apply the corresponding loss where the claimant cannot swear, as Tosafot write in Shevuot and Bava Metzia concerning a Mishnah-level oath. Ramban, however, in his responsum cited by Baal HaTerumot follows his position in Milchamot to Shevuot that a suspected person who cannot take the Mishnah-level oath may nevertheless collect on his note; we do not say that inability to take a rabbinic or Mishnah oath causes loss. According to that view, the migo remains available here. This is our halachah as well: with a Mishnah-level oath we do not apply the rule that inability to swear causes automatic loss, as seen below in siman 82 where a suspected creditor can collect his note. Therefore the law stated here is correct, and Shach's objection has no force.
We do not say, and so forth. If after payment the creditor sues again and one witness testifies, "You already paid him," Shach holds that this is exactly like R. Abba's ingot. The creditor cannot deny the witness and say, "I was never paid," because he admits receiving the money and says only that it was for another debt. He is therefore obligated to swear but cannot swear. Indeed, I found Rashba in Kiddushin 45 writing explicitly concerning the incident of Avimi and the people of Bei Chozae that there is a migo to deny receipt even though Avimi has one witness, because the resulting oath is only a Mishnah-level oath and we do not apply "unable to swear, must pay" to it. Rashba then says that if one is clever, he should pay again before that witness together with another witness and afterward sue with one witness, thereby creating a Torah oath and the rule of inability to swear. However, this is true only according to the opinion that once the note is weakened, it is completely void and even money already seized is removed. Rama rules like Rosh and those who hold that if the creditor has already seized, we do not remove it from him, even if the seizure occurred before witnesses. Under that view, what does it matter that he cannot contradict the single witness? The entire force of "unable to swear" is that an uncontradicted single witness is treated like two. But even if two witnesses testified that he received money without specifying this note, once he already holds the money he can say it was for another debt and we do not remove it. All the more so with one witness. Even according to those who hold the note is completely void, Baal HaTerumot shows that the issue is disputed. He cites proof from Ketubot: where one witness says a ketubah was paid, the Gemara says a clever husband should bring the case into a Torah-oath framework by paying again before that witness together with another witness and then suing. The Gemara asks that perhaps the woman can say she had two ketubot. This means that even after she already received the money, she can say the payment related to another document and there is no Torah oath against her. Baal HaTerumot answers that perhaps this is because she is already in possession and merely seeks to retain what she received; the burden rests on the claimant to prove the payment was not for another debt. Thus even two witnesses may not extract the money from her without proving the particular debt. This also answers Gidulei Terumah's question against Ramban. Ramban says that with one witness against a claim of set-off there is no application of R. Abba's rule because the oath is rabbinic. Gidulei Terumah asks: but the Gemara explicitly says one can transform the case into a Torah oath, and still worries about two ketubot. There is no difficulty. Baal HaTerumot already distinguished between a creditor using a note to extract money and a person who has already been paid and merely retains the money. In the latter case, even two witnesses do not necessarily extract, because the possessor can say the payment related to another debt and require the claimant to prove otherwise.
Nevertheless, I am puzzled by Ramban if his words are read as saying that with one witness and a claim of set-off there is no oath at all. The whole basis for believing the set-off claim is a migo that he could have denied receiving payment. But had he denied, he would have been subject to the Mishnah-level oath. If saying "set-off" removes even that oath, one could always fabricate the set-off claim merely to escape the oath. What sort of migo is that? Therefore, Ramban must agree that an oath remains necessary. His point is only that the oath generated through this migo does not acquire the full legal force of the original Mishnah-level oath for purposes of "unable to swear, must pay." This resembles what Shach writes in siman 296 concerning a deposit documented in writing. A custodian who says, "I returned it," is believed with an oath through a migo that he could have said it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Although the alternative claim would have required a Torah oath, the oath arising only through the migo is not itself treated as a full Torah oath for purposes of the doctrine of inability to swear. The same applies here. Even according to those who say inability to take a Mishnah-level oath causes loss, that applies to the oath itself established by the Mishnah. Where the oath arises only secondarily because it is needed to support a migo, its force is weaker and the doctrine of inability to swear does not apply. Ramban's sequence is therefore: first, the set-off claim is not governed by the R. Abba rule because the underlying oath against the document is only rabbinic; and even if one holds that inability to take a Mishnah-level oath can cause loss, this particular oath is not the formal Mishnah oath instituted to reassure the opposing party. It is only an oath needed to sustain the migo. Hence it is not strong enough to trigger the rule that inability to swear causes loss. Thus Ramban does require an oath. This is why Tur did not record any exemption from oath here. I consider this clear. Tosafot in Ketubot 85b, s.v. "I weaken it," also support this understanding. They ask why Rav Pappa's statement that a note is weakened matters if one witness already requires an oath, and answer that Rav Pappa was a relative of the holder of the note. One might ask why Tosafot did not raise the same question from the earlier incident where Rav Pappa sat before Rava. The answer can be that there the holder may have claimed set-off; if Rav Pappa had only the force of one witness, the resulting migo-based oath would not necessarily have the ordinary force. But where Rav Pappa is believed like two, or joined by another witness, the set-off claim is weakened until oath. In the later statement, Rav Pappa himself holds that a set-off claim does not weaken the note at all, so Tosafot had to answer that he was a relative. This reading somewhat supports Ramban. Even so, the conceptual reading just given is preferable to saying there is no oath at all. It also fits Rosh's citation of Rabbenu Tam that reversal of an oath can function like destruction of the note. The matter is difficult, but the conclusion remains that Ramban requires an oath, though not one carrying the full "unable to swear" consequence.
