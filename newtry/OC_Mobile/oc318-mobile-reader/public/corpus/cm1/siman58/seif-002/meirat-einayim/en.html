And he said, 'Did I not repay you before So-and-so and So-and-so,' etc. If the document states that the borrower granted the lender credibility equal to two witnesses, then even where payment was made before witnesses the lender may say, 'I applied it to another debt.' Even without that claim he is believed to contradict witnesses who testify to payment, and the borrower cannot recover from the lender even if he now pays again before witnesses, as the Tur and Mechaber write at the beginning of siman 71. One might say that the lender prefers to claim, 'I applied it to another debt,' which the witnesses cannot know to be false, rather than directly contradict them. But this is strained, for once he was granted greater credibility than witnesses, he was trusted concerning whatever he says. Even if the borrower holds the lender's handwriting acknowledging receipt of a certain amount, this would not help where the lender was given credibility like two witnesses. This is also implied by the Tur and Mechaber in seif 3, where only an acknowledgment made before witnesses that this particular document was paid overcomes an ordinary credibility clause. There is additional proof from Rama's gloss in siman 70, seif 4.
And those witnesses came and testified. The same applies if the borrower possesses a handwritten acknowledgment by the lender that he received money. The lender cannot then say, 'I applied it to another debt,' because a litigant's own admission is like one hundred witnesses. This defeats an ordinary credibility clause as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 71, seif 9, unless he was specifically granted credibility equal to two witnesses, as explained.
The document is thereby nullified, etc. This applies where both claims belong to the creditor personally. But if he sues on the documented debt as an authorized representative for another, while the money he admitted receiving belongs to him personally, the document is not nullified, because he could return the authorization to its owner, leaving only his personal claim concerning the money he received. With respect to that money, his admission is accepted subject to an oath. So writes Darkei Moshe, seif 3.
And so appears to me to be the primary ruling. See Darkei Moshe at the end of this siman, where he discusses the matter at length and does not rule like Rif and Rambam, primarily because most later authorities disagree with them.
For he did not repay him before witnesses. Meaning, he did not explicitly designate the payment as satisfaction of this debt in the witnesses' presence.
And this view holds that even in such a case the document is impaired, etc. The reason is that gifts are uncommon, especially where the recipient is owed money. People do not ordinarily give him a gift before first paying their debt.
But before one witness, etc. If the document contains a credibility clause, the lender need not even take an oath. See Derishah and Darkei Moshe.
