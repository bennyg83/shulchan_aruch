Seif 2. The borrower claims, “Did I not pay you before so-and-so and so-and-so?” The Shach cites the Rosh that we do not accept a migo against two invalid witnesses. This appears to raise a difficulty with Ritva in Ketubot, cited in the Shitah Mekubetzet in the episode of Avimi. Ritva first asks why the agent should be treated as one witness so that the creditor becomes obligated to swear and, if unable to swear, must pay. He answers that the case occurred after Rav Nachman instituted the oath of hesset. He then asks why the Gemara requires two witnesses to the payment, since one additional witness should suffice. Ritva concludes that regarding a rabbinic oath we do not apply the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot must pay. The difficulty remains: why are two witnesses necessary? With one additional witness, that witness joins the agent. Even if together they are not two fully valid witnesses because the agent has a financial interest, they should be no worse than two invalid witnesses, against whom a migo is not accepted. Moreover, according to our ruling that a supporting witness exempts from an oath, once the agent says he paid the creditor, the agent is exempt from his own oath by virtue of the supporting witness. Although they cannot be regarded as two regular witnesses, because the agent’s fitness itself depends on that other witness and part of the money is effectively established through a single witness, they should at least be equivalent to two invalid witnesses and defeat the migo. The answer is that the rule concerning two invalid witnesses applies where the witnesses have no personal stake in the outcome. Although technically disqualified, ordinary people naturally assume that when two disinterested individuals tell the same story it is likely true, and the litigant is therefore not bold enough to deny them. But where one witness has a direct financial interest, observers themselves remain uncertain whether he is speaking for his own benefit, and even the second disinterested person may be suspected of supporting him falsely. A litigant who denies them is therefore not necessarily regarded as brazen or dishonest, so his migo remains. Tosafot in Kiddushin write that once the agent swears that he paid the creditor, he is no longer considered financially interested because he could instead have said that he returned the money to the borrower. He remains disqualified only as a witness whose testimony requires an oath. That is true when no other witness supports him. If there is another witness, however, then by saying that he paid the creditor the agent is exempt from an oath through that supporting witness, whereas if he claimed that he returned the money to the borrower he would have to swear. He therefore has a direct interest in choosing the former account and is fully an interested witness. The litigant can consequently be bold against them and retains his migo. This also yields a proof from the Gemara that a supporting witness exempts from an oath. If such a witness did not exempt, the agent would have to swear even with the additional witness. He would not then be financially interested, but merely a witness requiring an oath, and one additional witness ought to suffice to undermine the migo as two invalid witnesses do. The Gemara’s requirement of two witnesses therefore supports the rule that a supporting witness exempts. After searching I found that Maaseh Choshev on Shevuot also raises this question. What I have written provides, with Hashem’s help, a proof.
