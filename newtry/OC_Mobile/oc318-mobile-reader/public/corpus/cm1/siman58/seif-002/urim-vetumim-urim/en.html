And these came and testified, etc. Even if the document contains an ordinary clause of trust, that trust does not prevail against witnesses. But if the document contains a clause granting credibility even against witnesses, Sema, Shach, and most authorities hold that the lender is believed to say, "I applied it to another debt," through a migo that he could have contradicted the witnesses and would have been believed. This is so even though contradicting witnesses is a brazen migo. Mahara Sasson disagrees. See Tumim, where I defended Sema and those who agree with him. If the lender has his own handwriting acknowledging that the payment was toward this debt, Sema holds that if the borrower granted him credibility equivalent to two witnesses he still has a migo. Shach disagrees and says he is not believed against his own handwriting, and Shach is correct.
Another debt, etc. Even R' Yosef ibn Migash, who holds in this seif that when money is given without specification the recipient may say, "I accepted it as a gift," because he has a migo, agrees that if witnesses testify that they saw the money given as payment, the lender cannot say, "I accepted it toward a different debt," even if the witnesses did not hear which debt was being paid. Shach concludes this, correctly.
It is thereby void, etc. If two witnesses testify that the payment was toward this document and two others contradict them and say it was toward another debt, Perach Mateh Aharon holds that the document retains its presumptive validity. In Tumim I wrote that this is incorrect. The borrower is exempt because the claimant must produce proof.
The document is void. If the lender also holds another person's debt by power of attorney, and witnesses testify that the borrower paid him, the lender is believed to say that the payment was toward a different debt personally owed to him, and the document later produced by him is not thereby weakened. So writes Rosh. See Tumim, where I concluded that everyone agrees and there is no dispute.
If he seized. Even if he seized in the presence of witnesses. This applies only to movable property, not land. See above in siman 46, seif 37, and what I wrote in the treatise on seizure.
We do not remove it from him. Therefore the lender cannot impose hesset on the borrower. The borrower may say, "If I swear today, tomorrow you may seize from me in front of witnesses and keep it. Either return my document and I will swear, or I will not swear," Ran.
He has been established as a denier. See Tumim. If the lender says he accepted the money toward another debt and that other debt was a fully collectible debt, he may at least impose hesset on the borrower that no such debt existed. But if the alleged other debt was not judicially collectible and was merely a claim where, if collected, the holder could retain it, and the lender says that he collected that debt while the documented loan remains unpaid, whereas the borrower says the payment was on the documented loan, there is no oath, not even hesset. The lender was established as a false claimant, the document is certainly void, and the other asserted claim lacks sufficient monetary basis to impose hesset.
For he did not pay him before witnesses. Meaning that he did not give it expressly as payment, but only without specification, and the lender can therefore say it was given as a gift.
And he holds, etc. Because it is uncommon for a person to make a gift to someone to whom he owes money. One would ordinarily repay his debt before giving a gift. We therefore do not suspect that it was a gift.
In the presence of one witness, etc. Not only does the rule of R' Abba's piece of silver not apply so as to say that because he cannot swear he must pay, since even had he contradicted the witness his oath would only have been rabbinic, and the rule of "because he cannot swear he must pay" does not apply to a rabbinic oath. He does not even require the mishnaic oath. The enactment of an oath was made where the borrower contradicts a witness regarding payment, to appease the claimant. But where the lender admits receiving the payment and merely says, "I applied it to another debt," there is no need to appease the borrower and no special oath was enacted. So writes Ramban in Baal HaTerumos, gate 19. Shach argues at length from poskim who applied the rule of R' Abba's piece of silver. See Tumim, where I concluded that Ramban is primary, unlike Shach. As for Ramban's ruling that there is no mishnaic oath at all, Sema explains that the case contains a clause of trust, because otherwise Ramban holds at the beginning of the siman that the lender must always swear even without a demand. Shach, following his own view that no oath is required absent a demand, explains Ramban as discussing a document without a trust clause. This is correct, because Ramban does not mention trust at all. See Tumim, where I concluded that an oath is nevertheless required in every case, only we do not apply the rule that inability to swear creates payment liability, even according to those who apply that rule to a mishnaic oath.
We do not say, etc. If after payment the borrower sues him and one witness says that payment occurred while the lender says, "I applied it to another debt," see Tumim. I concluded that according to Rama, who rules that the document is not entirely nullified, it is obvious that the rule of inability to swear and therefore pay does not apply. The lender swears and is exempt. Even according to the Mechaber, who treats the document as entirely void, Rashba and Baal HaTerumos dispute whether that rule applies. See Tumim, unlike Shach.
