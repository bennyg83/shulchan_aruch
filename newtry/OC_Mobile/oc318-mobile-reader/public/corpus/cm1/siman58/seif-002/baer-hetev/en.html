That he paid him. See Responsa Rashdam, which understands this to mean specifically payment equal to the amount of the document, not a lesser sum. The Shach disagrees and writes that Baal HaTerumot and the Tur in siman 30, seif 8, do not imply such a limitation.
Void. The borrower takes a shevuat heset and is exempt. See above, siman 30, for additional laws concerning a claim that a payment was applied to another debt. Shach. The Sema writes that this applies specifically where both claims belonged to the same creditor. If the debt in the document is being pursued under authorization from another person, while the creditor personally claims that the money he received was for his own separate debt, the document is not invalidated. He could return the authorization to its owner, leaving only his personal claim regarding the money received, as to which he is believed upon oath. If the document gives the creditor credibility equivalent to two witnesses, then even if payment was made before witnesses he is believed to say it was for another debt. The Shach agrees with this point. He explains that we cannot say the creditor would prefer to make the "other debt" claim because the witnesses cannot directly prove it false, rather than contradict them. That reasoning matters only where he would otherwise have to swear against the witnesses. If the document credits him like two witnesses and he need not swear, that rationale disappears. This is unlike the Bach, who nevertheless requires an oath and argues that migo does not exempt from an oath. That principle applies only where the person is independently obligated to swear. Here there is no oath obligation at all, as the poskim explain at the beginning of siman 71. The Bach's further argument that this is a brazen migo is also inapplicable for the same reason. The Sema's statement that even without claiming another debt the creditor may simply contradict the witnesses is incorrect, as explained in siman 71. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 111. The Sema's further statement concerning a handwritten acknowledgment is likewise not compelling. The Shach rejects the supporting proofs. He concludes that the later authorities make clear that where payment before witnesses weakens the document, this is true even if the document contains an ordinary credibility clause. See the Taz, who distinguishes between two forms of the "other debt" claim.
Primary. Since the court does not tear the document, it also does not tell the borrower to take a shevuat heset. If the creditor wishes, he returns the borrower's document and then the court can administer the oath. So writes the Ran. See below, siman 75, and the beginning of siman 82. Shach.
Before witnesses. Meaning that the debtor did not expressly give the money to him as payment of this debt. Sema.
Was weakened. The reason is that a gift is uncommon. It is unlikely that a person would give money as a gift while leaving his debt unpaid. See Responsa Rashdam, simanim 36 and 374. Shach.
But. The Sema writes that if the document contains a credibility clause, the creditor need not even swear. The Shach notes that Darkei Moshe indeed cites Baal HaTerumot this way, but in his view this is incorrect. Rather, the law applies even without such a clause. The statement that where the document lacks credibility the creditor must swear and collect means only where the borrower specifically demands, "Swear to me," like any other document under siman 82. This does not resemble the case of the metal ingot in which a person is obligated to swear but cannot and therefore must pay. That rule applies only to a Torah oath. Here the creditor's oath is an enactment and rabbinic. Nevertheless, several poskim hold that the claim "I applied it to another debt" is inherently weak and works only where the creditor has a migo that he could have denied receipt. Here, because one witness would have required him to take a severe oath resembling a Torah oath, while on the alternative claim he would need only heset, the migo may be lost. Since the Sages instituted an oath resembling a Torah oath, they may have instituted it with all its consequences. See the Shach's discussion.
