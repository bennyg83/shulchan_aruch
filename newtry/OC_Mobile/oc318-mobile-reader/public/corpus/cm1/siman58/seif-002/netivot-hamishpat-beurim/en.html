The document is void. See Sema, siman 70, seif katan 31, who writes that even if there is a known oral loan, or another documented loan known by witnesses, the creditor still cannot say that the witnessed payment was for another debt. Rather, the debtor is believed to say that the payment made before witnesses was on the document now produced, and the creditor may afterward pursue the oral loan, or the written loan whose document he is not now producing. Mishneh LaMelech, chapter 14 of Hilchot Malveh, writes that this depends on the dispute cited by Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 20, and Tur, siman 83. If so, according to the Mechaber there, in the case of two documents, one with a guarantor and one without, where the creditor may say even after payment before witnesses that the payment was on the document without the guarantor, Sema's ruling would not stand. In my humble opinion the cases are entirely different. There, both documents are presently produced in beit din, and the creditor may apply the payment to whichever debt he wishes, for the debtor cannot prevent him from doing so. Here, by contrast, where the other debt is a known oral loan, Sema is certainly correct, because there is a presumption that a person does not leave a documented loan outstanding and pay an oral loan, as Rosh writes. An unspecified payment is therefore as though the debtor expressly referred to the document. Even where the other debt is also documented, since the creditor is not presently producing that document in beit din, the witnessed payment establishes that one document in the creditor's possession has been discharged. This is exactly like one who says, "I have a paid document in your possession," where, if the creditor has two documents, he may collect on one only when he produces both. That rule applies specifically when he produces both documents in beit din. If he produces only one, it does not help him to bring witnesses that they once saw two documents in his hands after the debtor told him, "You have a paid document of mine." The debtor may say that he later paid one debt, recovered that document, and that the document which witnesses knew to have been discharged is the one still in the creditor's hands. He relied on the fact that the creditor could not collect without producing both documents, because witnesses knew that one document in his hands was paid. There is no difference between his admitting before witnesses that one document was discharged and the witnesses themselves knowing that the debtor paid before them while a discharged document remained with the creditor. In either case he cannot collect unless he produces both documents. Sema's ruling is therefore clear. There is also proof from Ketubot 96b, Rashi s.v. mai lav beha peligi. Rashi explains that when she ultimately claims her ketubah, they will say that what she sold was sold toward the ketubah and that she already received her ketubah. If she says, "Then give me maintenance," they will answer that maintenance was already given. Thus, although the maintenance debt is known, since the payment through sale of the land is known, the ketubah document is discharged and she cannot say that the payment was for maintenance. This is explicit support for Sema. Mishneh LaMelech further writes that if the document is still within its due period, the creditor may say that the payment was for another debt even when payment was made before witnesses, because of the presumption that a person does not pay within the term. He attributes this to a responsum of Rosh. I searched and did not find it in Rosh's responsa, and this ruling requires study, for the authorities state the rule without distinction. He further writes that if the document is for one hundred and the debtor paid one hundred fifty, the creditor may claim that the payment was for another debt. This too requires study, because the creditor can say that the additional fifty paid an oral loan that he had against the debtor. See also what I write in the next seif katan. In Sema, seif katan 10, there is a scribal error. It should read as Tur and Shulchan Aruch wrote in siman 71, seif 50.
That we do not collect with it. See Shach, seif katan 13, through his statement that one should not abandon the certainty and rely on the doubt. Rosh gives a different reason, namely that a person does not leave a documented loan and pay an oral loan. According to that reason it would seem that if the creditor claims, "I returned the document to you," he may claim that the payment was for another debt. It appears, however, that Rosh agrees with the former reason as well. He stated his reason only to answer his question concerning a payment without witnesses. The Gemara there explains the creditor's credibility by a migo that he could have denied receiving anything. Rosh asks why a migo is needed, since a claim that the payment was for another debt should itself be a valid claim. He therefore needed his reason. Baal HaTerumot's reason, that we do not abandon a certain payment for a doubtful debt, applies only when payment was made before witnesses and there is a certain payment before us. Without witnesses there is no certain payment at all, so Rosh needed his additional reason. If the debtor expressly mentioned the document when paying and the creditor claims, "From what you paid me I returned the document to you," it is obvious that he is not believed. Even later seizure in the presence of witnesses does not help him. It is as though the creditor admits, "I have a paid document of yours," and he cannot sue on one document unless he produces both documents, as explained in siman 65, seif 23. This is also explained in Responsa Rosh, kelal 18, siman 19, and explicitly in siman 54, seif 3, in the gloss, see there.
That we do not collect with it. See Sema, seif katan 12. If, for the debt in the document, the claimant comes as an authorized agent, while regarding the money he admitted receiving he claims in his own right, he may say that the payment was for another debt, because he could return the document to its owner. See Shach, seif katan 15, who cites Responsa Rosh as deriving this rule from simanim 119 and 148, from the principle that one may say, "If you remain silent, remain silent," and so Rosh writes explicitly. Urim VeTumim objects that we rule in siman 119 like Rambam that we do not apply that argument, and therefore this ruling should not stand. Knesset HaGedolah likewise rejects it for that reason. Urim VeTumim answers that Rosh stated the argument only for added strength. Even without it the agent may claim that the payment was for another debt, because the principal reason that such a claim ordinarily requires a migo is the presumption that a person does not leave a documented loan and pay an oral loan, whereas here the debts concern two different people, and the agent may say that he preferred the first debtor, and the like. This is very forced. Moreover, even if he claims that he returned the document, where Rosh's reason likewise does not apply, he still cannot claim another debt because of Baal HaTerumot's reason, cited by Shach, seif katan 13, that a doubtful loan cannot displace a certain payment. That reason applies equally to an authorized agent. Rosh's separate reason was stated only for the reason explained above. In my view the matter is as follows. Rosh's statement that the agent could return the document to its owner is itself difficult. What would returning it accomplish? The creditor himself should then be unable to collect, because it is established that the document was paid to his authorized agent, and Shach, seif katan 13, explains that we do not abandon the certain payment and seize upon a doubtful loan. The debtor was authorized to pay the agent. How then could the creditor collect? Further, if Rosh's basis is the argument of "if you remain silent" from siman 119, the same should apply to a purchaser of a debt document, since siman 119 applies even to a sale. Yet there is plainly no reason to say this of a purchaser of a debt document, and even the seller could not collect with that document after taking it back. The answer is that an authorized agent differs. At first glance, even where the debtor paid without witnesses, the agent should be unable to claim that the payment was for another debt, because if the agent simply denies receipt, the debtor is believed against the creditor. Once the creditor authorized payment to his agent, the creditor is like one who says, "I do not know whether I was paid," as explained in siman 121, seif 8, Shach there seif katan 41, and Shach here seif katan 47. Necessarily, we distinguish between a creditor who tells the debtor to pay his agent without obtaining the document, and one who appoints an agent and places the document in the agent's hands. In the first case the debtor may say to the creditor, "You trusted him," similar to the storekeeper on his ledger. In the second case the creditor did not trust the agent to receive the money while leaving the document in his possession. If the debtor pays without taking the document, the agent could deny receipt, and the creditor never consented that payment be made in that fashion. Therefore if the debtor paid and failed to take the document, and the agent says that he never received payment, the debtor must pay again because he paid in an unauthorized manner. For the same reason the agent may claim that the money was for another debt. Accordingly, even where the debtor paid before witnesses, if the agent says that the money was for another debt because the debtor did not mention the document and did not take it from him, the agent can exempt himself by that claim. Even though receipt of the money was witnessed, he may say that he received it on another obligation. Since he returns the debtor's document, the debtor has no claim against him except an oath of hesset. The debtor must therefore pay the creditor again, because he lacked authority to pay the agent without taking the document. It is as though he gave the money to someone who was not authorized. The agent, meanwhile, can retain the money by claiming that it was paid on his own debt, because in relation to the agent the debtor is now a claimant seeking to recover money, rather than a defendant seeking discharge through this payment. Since the debtor gave him the money without specification, the agent may say, "You gave it to me as payment of my debt," as explained in siman 79, seif 7. Moreover, according to Rama in the gloss, even payment made to the creditor himself can support seizure in such a case. Here the agent is already in possession and the debtor is the claimant, so the agent is certainly treated as having seized. However, where the agent himself now seeks to collect from the debtor by virtue of language authorizing him to litigate, acquire, and collect for himself, it appears that he cannot do so. Since the agent himself is the claimant, the debtor is defending himself by asserting this payment, and the agent cannot claim that the payment was for another debt when witnesses saw the payment. Rosh therefore needed the reason that because the agent can return the document to the creditor, we regard him as though it had already been returned and as though the creditor were suing. In this setting all agree, and it is unlike siman 119. There the purchaser is himself the principal litigant and cannot say, "I will return it." Here the agent even now sues only by the creditor's authority, so all agree that we view the document as already returned to the creditor. There is no reason to reverse the transaction pointlessly. If the agent had already returned it and the creditor then reappointed him, he could certainly sue. A purchaser of a debt document, by contrast, certainly cannot claim another debt, because the debtor had authority to pay him even without taking the document, and payment to him is exactly like payment to the original creditor. Yet if the debtor expressly mentioned the document and still did not take it from the agent, then if the creditor cannot recover the money from the agent the debtor must pay the creditor again, because he lacked authority to pay without obtaining the document, as explained above. Nevertheless, the agent himself must return the money to the debtor, because the debtor expressly delivered it for a different purpose before witnesses. The agent's retention on an unproven oral debt is then a seizure before witnesses, which is ineffective. Where the debtor did not mention the document, by contrast, it is not treated as seizure before witnesses on an unproven debt, because money given without specification cannot be recovered from the recipient, as explained in siman 79. If the debtor later sues the agent and the agent claims, "I returned your document from what you paid me," the agent is exempt with an oath even if the debtor had mentioned the document.
Or as a deposit. It appears to me that this applies specifically where witnesses saw him give money. If they saw him give an object without an appraisal, he may say, "I had an object deposited with you and you returned it to me," since they saw it given without valuation.
He was established as a denier. See Shach, seif katan 50, who concludes that the debtor takes an oath of hesset, because one who has been established as a denier regarding this money is not thereby established as a denier regarding other money. Urim VeTumim writes that Baal HaTerumot, who exempts him, speaks where the debtor gave him the equivalent of rabbinic interest, which may be retained if seized, so the debtor does not swear, because until he actually gave the rabbinic interest he was not obligated to do so. I do not understand this. In the case of prohibited rabbinic interest, seizure is ineffective unless the money was expressly given as interest, as explained in Yoreh Deah, Hilchot Ribbit. Here the money was given without specification, so it is certainly credited toward the documented debt and cannot be retained as interest.
In the presence of one witness. See Shach, seif katan 23, who writes that after payment the creditor is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, and must therefore pay. This is astonishing, as Tumim also objects, because even with two witnesses Rama ruled above that later seizure can be effective. Certainly with one witness, after payment, the creditor should at least be no worse than one who has seized. The matter appears straightforward. Rama rules that where payment was made before witnesses and the creditor claims another debt, we do not tear up the document, and even later seizure before witnesses can be effective. Yet since one payment was made before witnesses, why is this not seizure before witnesses on an unproven debt, which is ineffective? The reason is that where the debtor paid without specifying the document, there are competing presumptions. One may say that since he paid without mentioning the document, he presumably paid another debt. One may also say the opposite, that a person does not leave a documented debt and pay an oral debt. It is therefore a doubt concerning the document, where seizure is effective. If the debtor expressly mentioned the document and the creditor seizes the money for another debt, however, this is actual seizure before witnesses on an unproven debt and is ineffective. It is well known that where seizure is effective, it is effective only with movable property, not with land. Accordingly, if payment was made before witnesses, the creditor later sued when those witnesses were not present in beit din, and beit din believed his claim that the payment was for another debt, then awarded him land, and afterward witnesses came and testified that they had seen the first unspecified payment, he must return the land. The doubt arose from the unspecified payment, and the judicial collection is equivalent to seizure. Since it was land, and seizure of land is ineffective, it must be returned. Therefore, where there was one witness and land was awarded, the creditor falls under the rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear. This is not barred by the rule that one does not swear concerning land, because the parties' dispute concerns the first payment, which was money. The creditor is believed to say that the second payment was for another debt only by a migo that he could have denied the first payment. Had he denied the first payment, he would certainly have owed a Torah oath because that payment concerned movable property. Thus, if he is astute, he will seek collection in land, and the case becomes one of an oath obligation that cannot be satisfied. There is another possible basis. These authorities may follow Ramah, cited by Rosh in Ketubot 20, that even where seizure ordinarily helps, property delivered by beit din does not help, because beit din says, "We raised him into possession and we will remove him." Tosafot there also hold that where two pairs of witnesses contradict each other concerning land, the rule of whoever is stronger prevails because of possession, as explained in Urim VeTumim, seif katan 8, and siman 139, seif katan 1. Nevertheless, if beit din placed one party in possession based on witnesses and another set later contradicted them, we say that beit din which raised him removes him, as explained in siman 146, and this is not treated as ordinary seizure. It follows that perhaps the same rule applies to all cases of seizure through beit din. Rosh disagrees with Ramah there because he understands the rule of whoever is stronger not as a rule of seizure, but as based on the assumption that the true owner will exert himself more strongly, as Rosh writes in several places. Accordingly there is no proof from the case of whoever is stronger to ordinary seizure. We, however, rule like Rosh on that issue. It follows that where beit din awarded land, after payment there is a case of one obligated to swear who cannot swear. Where beit din awarded movable property, it is treated as effective seizure and he need not return it. This applies specifically where the creditor was astute and did not bring the witness to beit din when he claimed that the payment was for another debt. Before payment, the principal ruling follows Rama that the document is not impaired. After payment as well, the document must not have entered beit din before the payment. Where movable property was awarded, the law with one witness after payment is that it is treated as seizure if the debtor did not mention the document. If the witness testifies that the debtor did mention the document, seizure is ineffective and the creditor must swear to contradict the witness, as explained. If, according to the creditor's own present claim, he still contradicts the witness and owes an oath against him, one might ask why, according to what Shach writes here and in siman 51, where he still contradicts the witness there is no rule of one obligated to swear who cannot swear, the document should be impaired even according to Tosafot and Ran. There is no difficulty. In siman 51, where one says, "Yes, I seized it, but I seized my own property," and the witness testifies both that he seized and that it was not his, he must swear that it was his even if the robbed party makes no separate claim of seizure and even if the witness merely testifies that the item does not belong to him. Since he still must swear against the witness concerning ownership, Shach correctly says that he is not one who cannot swear. Here, however, there is no independent need to swear that the debtor did not mention the document. Even an unspecified payment ordinarily impairs this document, because the presumption is that one does not leave a documented loan and pay an oral debt. The creditor's only answer is that he is owed another debt and may retain this payment against it through a migo. Where one witness stands against that migo, the migo is ineffective. Thus after payment he is one obligated to swear who cannot swear, where land was awarded, as explained above. If the witness also testifies that the creditor himself expressly acknowledged at the time of payment that it was on the documented debt, then the witness is directly testifying that what was seized was not the creditor's, and the matter resembles Shach's case in siman 51. Yet even there the principal ruling follows Tumim, that it is a case of one obligated to swear who cannot swear. The resulting rules are these. Where the debtor expressly mentioned the document in the presence of one witness, after payment all agree that the creditor is one obligated to swear who cannot swear. If the document was not mentioned and land was awarded, he is likewise one obligated to swear who cannot swear. If movable property was awarded, the award is treated as seizure. Before payment, the principal ruling follows Rama that the document is not impaired. After payment, the document must not previously have come before beit din. Where movable property was awarded and the debtor did not mention the document, the one witness does not undo the seizure. If the witness testifies that the document was expressly mentioned, seizure is ineffective and the creditor must swear against the witness. If the debtor paid through an agent, expressly instructed the agent to obtain the document, and witnesses saw the agent make an unspecified payment without mentioning the document, all agree that the document is completely discharged. The witnesses know that these funds belong to the principal and were placed in the agent's hands only on the condition that he obtain the document. This is exactly like Reuven seizing before witnesses money known to belong to Shimon and claiming an unproven debt, which is ineffective. It is specifically when the debtor himself makes an unspecified payment that the creditor has some basis to say that another debt existed, because the debtor paid without mentioning the document. Likewise, if an agent made an unspecified payment and there are no witnesses to the terms on which the principal gave him the money, we apply the presumption that an agent does not deviate from his principal's instructions. But when witnesses know the terms on which the money was entrusted to the agent, the agent has no power to remove the principal's money from its established status. It is actual seizure of the principal's known money on an unproven debt and is ineffective.
We do not say that the document has been impaired. The preceding analysis explains the circumstances in which the document is, or is not, impaired. Where the payment itself creates only a doubt and the creditor remains in possession of a document that was valid when produced, we do not impair it merely on the basis of an unsupported claim that the payment was on that document. But where the manner of payment, the testimony of a witness, or the form of judicial collection removes the creditor's basis for treating the payment as another debt, the document is impaired according to the distinctions stated above.
