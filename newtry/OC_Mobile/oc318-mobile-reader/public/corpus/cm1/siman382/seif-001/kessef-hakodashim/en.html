See Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 2. Ketzot HaChoshen in siman 382 discusses the proof brought by Tevuot Shor from eating sacrificial meat. One may answer that Tevuot Shor's point is that mitzvot were not given for personal benefit, and therefore such performance is not comparable to an ordinary prohibited benefit. The same may be true of yibbum even according to those who do not follow Abba Shaul's concern regarding improper motive, because the act is fundamentally a mitzvah rather than ordinary pleasure. As to chalitzah, the inability to perform it through an agent may stem from the fact that the principal herself must perform the prescribed act; an agent cannot substitute where the Torah assigned the physical action personally. This requires careful study. The distinction cited from Tosafot Rid has broader implications. If no agency were possible in any mitzvah requiring personal performance, it would be difficult to understand the widespread practice of fulfilling the mitzvah of writing a Sefer Torah through a scribe. One might say that the mitzvah is fulfilled through ownership of the completed Torah, but that works only according to approaches that emphasize the purpose of study and therefore understand the mitzvah today as extending to acquisition of Torah literature. According to those who do not adopt that approach, the mitzvah may be fulfilled through completion of the writing even if the finished scroll is then transferred. If fulfillment requires that the scroll actually be one's own, one would need to ensure that the materials, parchment preparation, ink, quills, and writing are all acquired for the principal, perhaps through agency and beneficial acquisition from the outset. It would then be preferable to use forms of kinyan recognized at the Torah level rather than relying solely on commercial custom, because a rabbinic kinyan may not suffice to displace a Torah-level concern. Such prior acquisition would also avoid the issue of a craftsman acquiring rights through the improvement he creates. Perhaps, however, the Torah's command applies only to each written component as it is produced, and a transfer of an earlier completed portion before later writing could alter the analysis. If so, agency must remain available except where Scripture clearly excludes it. There may be a distinction between acts whose essence is personal benefit and acts that are service of Hashem. Yet that does not fully explain yibbum, and it also leaves the question why a woman could not appoint an agent for chalitzah. Perhaps the Torah's many detailed requirements in the chalitzah procedure demonstrate that the act must be personal. Circumcision, by contrast, is universally performed through an agent, and even fathers who are competent mohalim often honor another mohel. Where agency is valid but there is merely a preference that one perform the mitzvah personally, established competence can justify appointing another qualified person. The Rosh cited there concerning circumcision may mean that because the mohel acts as the father's agent, the act is legally attributed to the father for purposes of who owns the mitzvah. Ketzot's inference that payment should therefore go to the father need not follow, because compensation for performance is ordinarily awarded only to the person who physically performed the act, even though agency attributes the mitzvah to the principal. More generally, where the principal participates in part of the mitzvah, agency may be available except where the Torah expressly requires each individual to act personally, as in certain counting obligations. Regarding the Shach's view in siman 386 that indirect causation is exempt, he nevertheless agrees that ordinary monetary liability can result from sufficiently direct causation. He argues only that the special claimant's enactment was not extended to every indirect cause. If a person refuses to comply with a beit din ruling requiring an oath, the resulting obligation is already monetary and not merely a second-order cause. Likewise, where one must swear in order to collect, a wrongful obstruction can directly prevent receipt of money. Finally, the Shach's discussion in seif katan 25 requires study. Since the law of overcharge does not apply to a non-Jew, one who causes a completed transaction to be undone and thereby definitively deprives another of money may be treated as an informer even if the price was excessive. This differs from mere evasion of a debt to a non-Jew, where the debt itself may remain conceptually outstanding unless and until circumstances such as desecration of Hashem's Name arise. Responsa of Maharam of Lublin regarding property of an informer and inheritance may also suggest that property already removed from the deceased during his lifetime does not automatically pass to his heirs. Whether a duty to notify heirs exists after such a forfeiture requires further study.
