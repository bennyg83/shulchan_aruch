And it is ten gold coins. In the chapter of covering the blood, the Gemara says that one who deprives another of a mitzvah can owe ten gold coins, and that for Birkat HaMazon the amount can be forty gold coins. Tosafot asks why it is not fifty, since there is also the blessing over wine, and answers according to the view that one does not recite a new blessing over the cup of blessing beyond the first cup. The Shach, following Maharshal, suggests that ten gold coins are paid only for a Torah mitzvah, not a rabbinic mitzvah. Since *HaTov VeHaMeitiv* was instituted at Yavneh and is rabbinic, only four relevant blessings remain. I find this difficult, because the blessing over wine is also rabbinic. Almost all blessings are rabbinic, apart from Birkat HaMazon and, according to some authorities, the blessing over Torah study. If rabbinic status exempted payment, why would one pay for the wine blessing? It therefore seems that even according to Maharshal, his distinction applies to performance of a rabbinic mitzvah, but compensation for taking away a blessing can apply even when the blessing itself is rabbinic. The blessing recited over covering the blood proves this directly, for that blessing too is rabbinic and yet the Gemara treats it as compensable. Even so, the fundamental basis of Maharshal's distinction between Torah and rabbinic mitzvot remains difficult once we find compensation for a rabbinic blessing. The distinction requires further explanation.
He had a son to circumcise. Rosh rules that if a father told a particular mohel to circumcise his son and another mohel came first and performed the circumcision, the second mohel does not owe the designated mohel ten gold coins. The father did not confer proprietary ownership of the mitzvah merely by verbal designation. This differs from covering the blood, where the Torah itself says that the one who slaughtered should cover, and from a father who personally intended to circumcise his son and was preempted by another. The Shach infers from Rosh that a father who is himself capable of circumcision should not honor someone else with the circumcision. The mitzvah rests upon the father personally, just as the one who slaughtered is charged with covering the blood. The Shach therefore criticizes the common practice of capable fathers assigning the circumcision to others. Tevuot Shor objects that this proves nothing. The mitzvah may rest upon the father, yet agency should still work, since a person's agent is like himself. Darkei Moshe similarly cites Or Zarua prohibiting a capable father from giving the circumcision to another, but objects that throughout halachah agency can allow another to perform an act on one's behalf. In my view Rosh does support the Shach. If agency were fully effective for the father's personal mitzvah of circumcision, then appointing a mohel would leave the father's mitzvah intact through the agent. If an outsider then preempted the appointed agent, he would have deprived the father of his mitzvah just as surely as if he had preempted the father himself, and he should owe ten gold coins. Rosh's exemption therefore indicates that the father's personal mitzvah is not fulfilled through ordinary agency in the same way. One might answer for Rama that once the father has shown that he wishes the act performed through an agent rather than personally, an outsider who performs it can himself become the father's agent by virtue of the father's disclosed wish. But this is difficult. Agency does not ordinarily arise automatically merely because the principal revealed a general desire that some act occur. In Gittin, where a husband tells two people to write and give a get and they tell a scribe to write, the scribe does not become the husband's agent merely because the husband's wish is known. The rule that verbal instructions cannot be delegated shows that actual appointment matters. The cases where another person can act after the principal merely revealed his intent, such as a matchmaker who completes kiddushin after the man expressed interest in a certain woman, rest on the doctrine of acquiring a benefit for another. That doctrine applies where one acquires something new for the beneficiary. It does not apply where the act must be performed as an exercise of the principal's own authority. For circumcision of the father's son, ordinary agency, not acquisition on behalf of another, is the relevant concept. Tosafot RiD gives the key principle. Agency works where the principal remains the legal actor despite the agent's physical act, such as kiddushin, divorce, and offering a Paschal sacrifice on behalf of its owner. But where the Torah commands a person to perform a bodily mitzvah himself, another person's bodily performance does not become his. One cannot appoint another to sit in a sukkah, wear tefillin, take a lulav, or wear tzitzit in his place. He can appoint someone to build his sukkah, but he himself must sit in it. This answers the proofs from mezuzah and similar mitzvot. One can appoint another to affix the mezuzah because the homeowner then lives in the house containing the mezuzah. By contrast, covering the blood is imposed upon the one who slaughtered, and if another covers it, the slaughterer himself did not perform the bodily act. Circumcision of one's son is comparable. Nor is yibbum a proof for agency. The mitzvah applies to all the brothers, with priority to the eldest. If the eldest declines, the mitzvah passes to the others in their own right. One brother is not performing yibbum as the eldest brother's agent. Kol Bo likewise distinguishes mitzvot that cannot be performed through an agent, such as tzitzit and, in its discussion, covering the blood. This supports the view that covering the blood and the father's circumcision obligation are personal acts for which agency does not substitute. Accordingly, a father who is himself a qualified mohel should ideally perform the circumcision himself. The suction, which is a medical protective measure rather than the mitzvah act itself, can be assigned to another. If the father cannot circumcise personally, then according to Darkei Moshe he should at least fulfill his obligation through a legally valid adult agent. A minor cannot serve as an agent. Although the Shulchan Aruch says that a minor, woman, or slave can in certain circumstances perform a valid circumcision, that concerns validity of the circumcision itself and whether blood must be drawn again, especially where the father is absent. Where the father's own mitzvah is to be fulfilled through agency, the mohel should be someone halachically capable of agency. For a Torah matter, one should therefore ensure that the appointed mohel is known to have reached halachic majority.
