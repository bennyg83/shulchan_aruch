Rambam and the Mechaber imply that this is a penalty rather than ordinary damages, for otherwise the fixed payment of ten gold coins would follow automatically from Rabban Gamliel's ruling concerning covering the blood. Semag similarly says that he pays as the judges see fit. Rambam's later wording, 'some ruled that he pays a fixed penalty,' and 'they likewise ruled regarding all commandments,' also supports this. Shiltei Gibborim understands Rambam as ordinary law, but the inference is not compelling. My learned son-in-law answered Rif's objection by noting that Rav holds that one may derive one penalty from another, whereas Shmuel does not. Nevertheless, even if the award is penal, the court assesses the circumstances. The same framework applies to one who destroys another's opportunity to perform a commandment, though the precise amount may depend upon whether the lost act was itself a Torah commandment or only its blessing. The final practical measure is therefore left to the judges unless the fixed ten-gold rule clearly applies.

And it is ten gold coins. The Gemara in Chullin concludes that the ten gold coins compensate for the blessing over covering the blood. For Grace after Meals the amount is forty gold coins for four blessings. The Ran holds that compensation also applies for loss of the commandment itself. This is not contradicted by the fact that only ten coins are paid for covering the blood, because where the blessing cannot exist apart from the commandment the two are not assessed separately. Tosafot asks why Grace after Meals is not worth fifty coins, including the blessing over wine, and answers that the view in question does not require a new blessing over the cup. I suggest instead that only Torah-level blessings generate this fixed liability. The fourth blessing of Grace after Meals was instituted at Yavneh and is rabbinic, so together with the blessing over wine the count remains four. This also reconciles Rif and Rosh, who require a blessing over the cup yet record only forty coins. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites Rav Tzemach similarly, though elsewhere he explains that the wine blessing is omitted because it is a blessing over enjoyment, in which case the fourth blessing is included. Either explanation may accord with Rif and Rosh.
If he had a son to circumcise, etc. He is exempt. The Sema distinguishes between the father and another mohel regarding answering Amen, but this distinction is not persuasive. Rabbeinu Yerucham states that where the displaced person could answer Amen, the one who performed the circumcision is exempt even when the father himself was obligated. If the father assigned another mohel and a third person performed the circumcision first, the designated mohel acquired no proprietary right in the commandment; even a formal act of acquisition would be merely an acquisition of words. This is explicit in the Rosh and is the basis of the ruling in Yoreh Deah. The father himself, however, should circumcise his son when capable, just as the one who slaughtered should cover the blood. The Gemara and Rambam place the obligation first upon the father, and those who routinely honor another while able to perform it themselves forgo a great positive commandment. Maharashal argues that even if the father answered Amen, the interloper remains liable because the commandment itself was taken, but Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, Ran, and the other authorities correctly hold that answering Amen gives equivalent reward and removes the loss. Rabban Gamliel's case must therefore involve circumstances in which the displaced person could not answer Amen, such as a blessing recited quietly. The same analysis applies to Grace after Meals and Torah blessings. The common saying that honoring another with Grace after Meals gives him forty gold coins is imprecise where the one honoring can answer Amen; at most it gives precedence in reward. If one actually prevented him from hearing or answering and thereby took the public blessing, the fixed payment may apply. Regarding purchased synagogue honors, Maharashal suggests that one who preempts the purchaser may owe ten gold coins if the purchaser seized them, but this is not conclusive. See Maharashal for additional distinctions.
