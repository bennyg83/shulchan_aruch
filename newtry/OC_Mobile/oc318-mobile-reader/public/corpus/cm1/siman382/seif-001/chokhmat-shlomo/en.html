Seif 1. "An ox that was standing to be killed, etc., and a tree standing to be cut down." Note: See Shach, seif katan 1, who asks that the Tur's words contradict one another and also contradict the Rosh, who holds that the ten-gold-piece payment is actual law and not merely a fine. In my view the matter can be explained through the Sema, who interprets "until the judges see" to mean that the judges determine how beloved the mitzvot were to this person. We rule in siman 363 that where a courtyard is not ordinarily rented but the owner suffered some loss, the beneficiary can be charged for the entire benefit. The Sages' valuation that the opportunity to perform a mitzvah is worth ten gold pieces applies specifically to a mitzvah such as Birkat HaMazon, which everyone is commanded to perform. The Gemara teaches that one who is commanded and performs is greater than one who is not commanded and performs. Thus, in a case such as covering the blood, where the chapter Kisui HaDam teaches that every Jew is obligated to cover it, the judges need not investigate specially. Even if the slaughterer does not cherish mitzvot greatly, every Jew values a mitzvah at least somewhat. Once some value is established, the entire benefit can be assessed against the one who grabbed the mitzvah, for by grabbing it he revealed that he personally cherishes the mitzvah and derives the ten-gold-piece benefit. By contrast, with an ox standing to be killed and a tree standing to be cut, the Rambam in Hilchot Rotzeach chapter 11 appears to derive the obligation from the law of a parapet and the verse, "Do not place blood in your house." The Rambam likewise lists as a positive mitzvah the obligation of beit din to stone the condemned ox. It therefore appears that the duty concerning this particular ox or tree rests only upon its owner, or upon beit din, but not upon every bystander. A stranger who destroys the danger performs a mitzvah but is not personally commanded in it, and his own benefit from the mitzvah is not automatically worth ten gold pieces. Although he caused a loss to the owner, who was commanded and could have gained the ten-gold-piece value by performing the mitzvah himself, the judges must determine how much the mitzvah actually mattered to that owner and therefore how much loss was caused. According to this, the Tur can agree in principle with the Rosh that the payment is compensatory law rather than a fine. He simply could not derive the exact amount directly from covering the blood, because there everyone is commanded, while here the outsider is not commanded. The Gemara cites covering the blood only to establish that if the judges see that the owner cherished the mitzvah, depriving him of it can amount to a ten-gold-piece loss. The Tur accordingly holds that the duty regarding the ox or tree rests on the owner and not on every person. This appears correct to me. Examine carefully.
