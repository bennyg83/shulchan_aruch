An ox, etc., because, etc. This does not refer only to an ox that killed when the Sanhedrin had capital jurisdiction, for then one may not kill it without adjudication before twenty-three judges. Rabbi Eliezer's dispute concerns only the five dangerous species. Rather, the rule applies even nowadays to a habitually goring ox, as stated at the end of the first chapter in the discussion of Rabbi Nasan.
And he slaughtered it. "Killed" is not literal. Otherwise the owner could say, "I would have slaughtered it," for if an ox killed before its sentence was completed and someone preceded and slaughtered it, the meat is permitted, as stated in chapter 4. Yam Shel Shlomo.
As they see fit, etc. The Rif cites an opinion that this is a discretionary fine and cannot serve as precedent for other law. The Rif challenges that view from Rav's derivation there. The passage on 117a, "if it is law," etc., may indeed be a dispute, and Rav follows his own view in Gittin 52a. This also answers the Rif's question. Likewise, the Rosh's proof from Chullin 87a can be understood according to Rav. The Rambam therefore rules like Rav, as explained above in siman 385 seif 2, and explicitly does so at the beginning of chapter 7. Rav Hai Gaon and the Ramban, however, agree with the Rif that one does not derive ordinary law from a discretionary fine, following Shmuel. This is the basis of the later "and there is one who says," etc. See the Shach.
(Collected note) As they see fit. The Rashba likewise writes in Bava Kamma 107a that the conclusion indicates that one may derive law from such a fine, as shown by the Gemara's reversal from its initial assumption. (End of note.)
But other, etc. The Gemara there.
Even if there is, etc. Otherwise the Gemara could have established the case that way. See Hagahos Maimoniyos, siman 12.
Like a migo, etc. See Tosafos Bava Batra 5b at the end of s.v. "who." Tosafos explains that a migo is not accepted against an especially strong chazakah.
But, etc. See Shach.
And nowadays, etc. Tosafos there, s.v. "and he obligated him," according only to the latter opinion. The practical consequence is where the claimant seized property. See Tosafos Chullin 87a, s.v. "and he obligated him," and the Rosh there concerning a mohel: when the father had assigned the mitzvah and another preceded him, the circumstances determine liability, for the verbal designation itself did not transfer a monetary right, as stated in the gloss.
