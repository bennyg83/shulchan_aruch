As the judges see fit. It appears that judicial discretion here, and likewise regarding one who prevented another from performing other positive commandments, depends upon the character of the person deprived of the mitzvah. If he is someone known to be careful about performing mitzvot, the judges should impose a larger penalty. If he is less careful, the penalty should be smaller. They should also consider the difficulty of performing the mitzvah. Sometimes a person refrains because of the effort involved and is content for another to do it. See the Derishah.

And another person came and circumcised him, he is exempt. In Yoreh Deah, at the end of siman 28, the ruling concludes that even if the person entitled to the mitzvah seizes money, we remove it from him, because he answered Amen to the blessing. The Sages said that one who answers Amen is greater than the one who recites the blessing. If he heard and failed to answer, he caused his own loss. But he is not exempt merely because he can offer him another chicken to slaughter. That is a different mitzvah. The first mitzvah is gone and cannot be repaired. I wrote there that exemption by answering Amen applies specifically to circumcision, where the blessing is ordinarily recited publicly and aloud, so he certainly heard it. This is unlike the blessing over slaughter. I also wrote above that if one person was given a chicken or animal to slaughter and another preceded him and slaughtered it, we do not impose a penalty because slaughter is not a personal mitzvah uniquely incumbent upon that individual. It appears, however, that if someone precedes a father and circumcises the father's own son, even though the father can answer Amen, we do not exempt the interloper on that basis. The mitzvah is uniquely incumbent upon the father more than upon anyone else. The Ir Shushan gives other reasons for exemption and did not take account of the ruling in Yoreh Deah just cited.
