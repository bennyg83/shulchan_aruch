If it were strict law, it would be obvious that he must pay ten gold coins, just as Rabban Gamliel imposed ten gold coins for taking another's mitzvah of covering the blood, and the authorities indicate that the cases share one rule. Rather, Rabban Gamliel imposed a penalty according to his assessment. The Semag likewise writes that he must pay as the judges see fit. This is also implied by the Mechaber's later wording concerning covering the blood, "There is one who ruled," and "they likewise ruled regarding every mitzvah." Shiltei Gibborim inferred from the Rambam that this is strict law because the Rambam extended the ruling to all mitzvot, but in my view this is not implied. In any event, it is clear that "there is one who ruled" applies to all the cases mentioned earlier, including the ox and the tree. The Rif and Rosh at the end of HaChovel likewise indicate one rule for all of them, with ten gold coins paid in each case. Maharshal rules so in siman 60. The Rashba, in his novellae at the end of Kisui HaDam, also writes that the reward for a blessing is ten gold coins, and the same applies to a mitzvah without a blessing, as shown by the case in HaChovel of the ox standing ready for slaughter. Mitzvot are not valued differently according to their relative importance. The Ran writes likewise. Shach.

The Tur in Yoreh Deah, end of siman 28, concludes that even if the claimant seized property, it is removed from him when he answered amen, since one who answers amen is greater than the one who recites the blessing; if he heard and failed to answer, he caused his own loss. The Sema suggests that answering amen exempts the person who preempted another only for circumcision, whose blessing was instituted publicly and aloud, so the father certainly heard it, unlike the blessing over slaughter. He therefore says that if another person preempted a father and circumcised his son, the father's ability to answer amen does not exempt the one who preempted him, because the mitzvah is especially incumbent on the father. The Shach rejects this distinction. Rabbenu Yerucham, the source of the ruling, explicitly indicates that whenever the person could answer amen, even a father upon whom the mitzvah rests is not entitled to payment. The Rama here must therefore refer to a case in which he could not answer amen, such as where he was pushed away or the blessing was recited quietly. The Rosh at the end of Kisui HaDam indicates that one who knows how to circumcise is himself obligated to circumcise his son and may not delegate it to another, and the Rambam at the beginning of Hilchot Milah and the plain meaning of Kiddushin 29a imply the same. I write this because I have seen many people honor others with circumcising their sons even though they themselves are able to do so. In my opinion they thereby neglect a positive commandment and the great mitzvah of circumcision, and beit din should abolish this practice. The Shach further writes that people commonly say that one who honors another to lead Birkat HaMazon thereby honors him with forty gold coins. Shiltei Gibborim, Rabbenu Yerucham, and the Gemara concerning Rabban Gamliel support liability for one who snatches Birkat HaMazon from another. Yet since Birkat HaMazon with zimun is recited aloud, the displaced person can answer amen. It therefore appears that the reward of ten gold coins for a blessing applies only when the blessing is recited before others, not when one blesses privately. The cases in the Gemara and authorities can thus be understood where the snatcher took the cup, expelled the other person, and prevented him from answering amen until the meal ended. Although the other can recite Birkat HaMazon himself, he loses the special reward because he now blesses alone. According to this, the popular statement that merely honoring another with Birkat HaMazon gives him forty gold coins is not literally correct, since he can in any event answer amen. Perhaps the honor lies only in allowing him to be the first to receive the reward. This still requires study. Regarding a Torah reading blessing, see the Rosh and Mordechai, who cite Rabbenu Tam as exempting the one who preempted. Maharshal writes that nowadays, when honors are purchased, if one bought the third, sixth, or seventh aliyah and another preempted him, the purchaser may retain ten gold coins if he seized them. His conclusion is not compelled. See Maharshal there for further distinctions. Shach.
