Ketzot HaChoshen cites Tashbetz III, 147, that early document forms left four zuz to the heirs, and a Gaon explained that this was done so that the Sages would be pleased with the testator. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat 151, was asked about a wealthy man with no sons who wished, by an inter vivos testamentary instrument, to devote most of his fortune as a permanent charitable fund while leaving only a minority to his heirs, and whether a Torah scholar should assist in preparing it. The source is Bava Batra 133b: one who writes his property to others and leaves his children with nothing does not meet the approval of the Sages. The Gemara includes one who consecrates property, and Ketubot 53b indicates that the concern is not limited to transferring the entire estate. Although the Gemara speaks of children, Rambam and Shulchan Aruch speak more broadly of heirs, since the concern that worthy descendants may arise can apply generally. Chatam Sofer therefore initially understands that even giving only part of the estate to charity, and even where there are no direct descendants, may be problematic. He then discusses Tashbetz's report that leaving four zuz was considered sufficient to satisfy the Sages, but argues that the Ittur cited there may only distinguish the more severe condemnation found in the Yerushalmi from the lesser statement that the Sages are displeased. He notes the widespread custom that one without sons establishes charitable funds. One justification is the teaching that the Torah's expression 'you shall transfer' regarding one who has no son is associated with divine anger, so a man may seek spiritual protection through charity, and his own spiritual welfare precedes remote heirs. Another possible distinction is between a deathbed gift, which takes effect only when the estate is already falling to heirs, and a genuine inter vivos transfer of the corpus from today with fruits retained until death, because during life one may dispose of one's property. Ketubot 53b creates difficulty for that distinction. Chatam Sofer concludes that if a substantial portion is left to the heirs, a Torah scholar may assist. Pitchei Teshuva questions whether Chatam Sofer's stringent inference regarding one who has sons is necessary. Bava Batra proves that consecrating the whole estate is included, and Ketubot proves that an improper transfer to a private person may involve only part, but neither necessarily proves that consecrating only part is objectionable. The case of Mar Ukva in Ketubot 67b may suggest otherwise. The matter requires study.
