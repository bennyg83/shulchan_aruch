Anyone who gives his property to others. Tashbetz discusses two cousins who executed reciprocal dispositions that whoever died first would leave all his property to the survivor. He questioned whether, where both reciprocal gifts were written in one document, the entire document might fail because both gifts could never be fulfilled together, invoking the principle that anything incapable of operating successively cannot operate simultaneously. I do not see the analogy. That principle applies where one act purports to create two mutually incompatible acquisitions at the same time, such as simultaneous kiddushin of two sisters. Here each cousin makes a separate conditional gift: if I die first, my property belongs to you. The two conditions are alternative, and only one will ever be fulfilled. This is no different from many ordinary conditional transactions where only one branch can occur. Nor is it like "you and a donkey," because each donor intends a full valid gift to an existing recipient subject to a condition. Bereirah is likewise not the issue. Conditional acts such as "from today if I die" are valid without invoking bereirah. Therefore there is no reason for one document rather than two to invalidate the reciprocal gifts, provided the document clearly contains two independent conditional transfers.
