Where a father fixes a specified amount for his firstborn in exchange for the firstborn's double share, it appears that the concern of improperly transferring an inheritance does not readily apply. I have written elsewhere, based on what my father in law reported in the name of Maharanach, that a gift or an admission does not fall under the same prohibition of "do not be among those who transfer an inheritance." I also wrote that where all brothers consent there are grounds for no concern at all. In the present case the father is arranging his sons' shares and, in exchange for the economic value of the firstborn right, sets a fixed payment regardless of the ultimate size of the estate. Even without the other sons' knowledge, this resembles a purchase of the firstborn's contingent economic right. Where the firstborn himself agrees, there is especially strong reason for leniency, because the arrangement is effectively a sale of his own prospective benefit for a fixed price payable after the father's death. I have also written that instruments commonly used in these lands, such as a shtar chatzi zachar, are structured as gifts or present obligations and therefore do not directly violate the Torah law of inheritance. Nevertheless, some practice a measure of piety by limiting such instruments to movables rather than land. Movables can be concealed or dissipated and are therefore less firmly attached to the eventual inheritance. Even debts can be affected by collusion or competing claims. Land, by contrast, ordinarily remains clearly in the estate. Some therefore avoid writing a shtar chatzi zachar against land, though others expressly include it, and we have not heard that the great pious authorities universally refrained from doing so. The fact that land might be sold during life is not a complete answer, for there is ordinarily a presumption that existing land will remain with its owner, especially where the instrument itself undertakes not to evade the daughter's rights. Similar caution is sometimes shown regarding sefarim, since sacred books are often publicly known and not readily concealed, and it is improper to sell them except for limited purposes. Still, where the mechanism is a genuine present gift or obligation, there is no Torah prohibition in the writing itself. The scribe is not in the classic position of facilitating a transgression that could not otherwise be performed, since the parties can have such a document written elsewhere. A personal measure of piety cannot properly be used to cause loss to others. More fundamentally, a legitimate legal device is not necessarily prohibited "evasion." In matters between a person and Hashem, inner intent can matter even where formal words might appear sufficient, because Hashem desires the heart. In monetary law between people, however, legal acts are ordinarily judged by the acts and formulations Chazal established. If a person first undertakes a real obligation wholeheartedly and only afterward conditions the daughter's half share upon that debt, this is a genuine legal arrangement rather than a sham. One must distinguish between an artifice that merely disguises a forbidden result and a transaction whose very legal structure is permitted. Similar principles appear in accepted devices used to avoid ribbit. Where the mechanism itself begins in a permitted manner and the parties genuinely undertake it, it is not the sort of evasion condemned by Torah law. By contrast, where a supposed sale is transparently inconsistent with the parties' conduct, such as certain artificial sales involving chametz, the visible conduct may reveal that the transfer was never genuinely intended. The customary shtar chatzi zachar was established by earlier authorities specifically to operate in a fully permitted manner, and we presume that they did not knowingly substitute a prohibited route for a permitted one. In a new arrangement not grounded in an established communal form, however, the person drafting it should ensure that the obligor truly accepts the underlying obligation with a whole heart. A bare gift taking effect only after death may not always avoid the concern of transferring inheritance, because the Torah prohibition concerning the firstborn demonstrates that a disposition labeled as a gift can still be problematic when its substance is to alter inheritance after death. A present obligation is therefore a stronger and cleaner mechanism. A case recently came before me in which a father wished to provide his firstborn with 800 specified gold coins for the firstborn portion, regardless of whether the eventual estate was larger or smaller, and even designated part of a house for that purpose. I hesitated because of Chazal's warning not to participate in transferring inheritance. Since the parties pressed me to draft the instrument and local custom was that such documents be written by me, I concluded that a personal stringency should not cause them loss. There is a real possibility that the arrangement does not diminish the firstborn at all, because the estate may be small, and we do not generally make decisions based on the speculative possibility that a person will become wealthy. Movables in particular can disappear, and even land values fluctuate. Most importantly, the firstborn himself consents, and he is entitled to deal with his own eventual right. The only remaining concern is that if the estate is small the ordinary brothers might receive less, but that is not identical to the Torah's rule prohibiting reduction of the firstborn portion. It resembles a shtar chatzi zachar, and in some respects is more lenient because the potential reduction is only to ordinary brothers. I therefore inclined to draft the obligation without specifically charging land, and to remind the parties that the obligation must be accepted sincerely. Still, I later considered that a kinyan by which the firstborn merely waives future rights may not suffice. Although siman 209 seif 8 discusses renouncing rights in property not yet in existence, Even HaEzer siman 92 indicates that mere withdrawal, even with a kinyan during the relevant lifetime, may not be effective for future inheritance rights. The sounder method is a present personal obligation by the firstborn to return to his brothers any amount by which the eventual double portion exceeds the fixed sum. If this obligation is written in a witnessed instrument, it can bind even his heirs, since they take only through him and his documented obligation can be collected from property subject to the debt. This is the preferable structure.
