A beit din of three laymen. This follows Rivash. Shach argues that only a widow, because of special concern for her social position and ability to remarry, may sell through three ordinary laymen. In ordinary cases, at least one member of the panel should be learned and competent. His proofs do not undermine Rivash. Rivash's foundation is Tosafot Bava Metzia 31, who ask why the Gemara compares a partner dividing jointly owned property to a widow selling estate property. The widow received a special leniency, yet the partner case has no such rationale. Tosafot answer that the partner is taking what is already substantively his, and therefore resembles the widow sufficiently for an informal three-person appraisal. A collateral creditor is similar. The creditor has a proprietary interest in collateral strong enough for several laws: he can use it for kiddushin in some cases, Shemittah does not cancel the corresponding debt, and collateral can give him strong possession. Rashba even treats him as a substantial possessor. He is therefore not an outsider selling another's property. He is realizing an interest already held in the object. That is why Rivash writes expressly that because the creditor is a possessor and acquires the collateral, the "beit din" required for sale can be three ordinary laymen. Ritva likewise explains the widow leniency in terms of collecting something treated as substantially hers. Shach cites Tosafot Pesachim as requiring experts for a deposit or collateral, but the Tosafot passage he invokes does not actually mention collateral in the relevant way. Nor does Rashba's responsum about mortgaged real estate prove otherwise. Land does not have the same creditor acquisition as movable collateral, and in that responsum the debtor explicitly authorized sale "through beit din." Contractual language of ordinary people usually means a genuine competent court, not any three unlearned appraisers. Here, by contrast, we are interpreting the Mishnah's technical phrase "sell through beit din," where Tosafot allow an informal panel unless a full court is clearly required. Kesef Mishneh even questions whether formal beit din is needed at all and whether witnesses could suffice. Responsa Rosh likewise speaks simply of three appraisers without requiring a separate judicial panel. Therefore Rivash's ruling is well founded: three suitable lay appraisers suffice for the collateral sale.
