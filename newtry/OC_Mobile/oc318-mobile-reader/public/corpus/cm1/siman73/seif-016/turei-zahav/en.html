(Seif 19) “An object in another’s possession cannot be waived.” The Sema says the same applies to money, and the true distinction is between a loan and a deposit: waiver applies only to a debt. Maharik likewise implies this. It appears to me, however, that money deposited with a moneychanger or shopkeeper may be waived, because the depositary is permitted to use it. It resembles a Hebrew servant, whose body is not owned and whose monetary claim may be waived. The glossator strongly disagrees. A deposit remains the owner’s property wherever it is and can be transferred to others until actually spent. Accidental loss, currency invalidation, inheritance rights, and Sabbatical cancellation all follow the depositor so long as the money remains unspent. Tosafot show that according to the accepted view, under which the moneychanger is only a paid custodian, the depositor may demand the same coins before they are spent. The Shach misunderstood Tosafot in saying substitute coins may be given. That inference applies only to the view that the depositary is a borrower and liable for accidents. Under the accepted paid-custodian view, the coins remain an ordinary deposit and must be returned intact. Therefore the Taz’s ruling is incorrect: permission to use does not convert the deposit into a debt before use, and waiver language is insufficient; a gift expression is required.
