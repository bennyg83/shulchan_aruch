Seif 18. All of it belongs to the borrower. Suppose collateral is held by a third party rather than by the lender. Three experts appraise it at ten gold coins, and the lender then sells it on credit for eleven. If he did not clearly explain that the additional gold coin was solely for the extension of credit, there is room to say that the entire eleven belongs to the borrower. The three appraisers merely authorized a sale at ten, and the excess realized in the sale may therefore be part of the value of the collateral. On the other hand, if the lender intended to sell the object itself for ten and charge one additional coin only for the deferred payment, the buyer presumably understood the transaction in the same manner. Because the parties did not expressly say, "ten for cash now and eleven for credit," the ordinary ribbit concern may not arise in the same form. Nor is this the rule of "who informed the owner of the wheat," because the object was presumably sold in accordance with its appraisal. If the lender first acquired the collateral for himself at eleven, either personally or through an agent, and then resold it, it appears that after the fact his acquisition can be effective if he genuinely took it for himself. Initially, however, such self-dealing raises a concern of suspicion. The matter requires further study.
