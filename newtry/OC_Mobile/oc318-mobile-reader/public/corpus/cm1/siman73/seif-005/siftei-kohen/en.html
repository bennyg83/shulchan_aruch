Reuven swore, etc., not to pay any debt, etc. The Tur and the Rosh's responsum state more narrowly that he swore not to sell property in order to pay Shimon. One could therefore argue that the oath fails only where Shimon's debt preceded an oath directed specifically against paying him. If Reuven swore generally not to pay any debt, the oath may take effect through inclusion, like an oath not to eat matzah or not to sit in shade, which applies even on Pesach night or in a sukkah because a comprehensive oath can take effect upon a positive commandment. The concluding language of the Rosh and Tur can be read in more than one way. The Mechaber, however, understood it plainly: where Shimon's debt preceded the oath, even a general oath not to sell property for any debt does not take effect. This may be distinguished from matzah and sukkah because there the immediate obligation has not yet arisen at the time of the oath. Alternatively, even if the repayment date had not yet arrived, the debt and lien arose from the loan, and early payment would discharge it; thus the obligation already existed and the later oath cannot take effect. This latter explanation appears primary.

If the first oath preceded Shimon's debt, the second is an oath in vain. When he later swore to pay by a fixed date, that oath necessarily included selling his property if required by law to obtain payment, although he had already sworn not to sell. The Rosh compares this to conflicting oaths. The text of the responsum as printed is defective, because the comparison and conclusion are reversed. It should read as follows: one who first swears not to eat a loaf and then swears to eat it has made the first an oath of expression and the second an oath in vain; he is told not to eat, for it is preferable to violate only the oath in vain rather than both the oath in vain and the oath of expression. So too here, he should not sell, thereby preserving the first oath, and the second oath is in vain. With this correction the Rosh's proof is clear.
The second is an oath in vain, etc. The Bach objects that the second oath is not in vain because, if he sold and paid, he would fulfill it, just as one who swears generally to eat fulfills his oath even by eating forbidden food. He also asks how release of the first oath can help, since liability for an oath in vain arises immediately. These objections are not correct. Since the second oath to pay necessarily includes selling property if no money is otherwise available, it directly conflicts with the earlier oath not to sell and is like swearing first not to eat a particular loaf and then to eat it. The case of a general oath to eat differs because it can be fulfilled through permitted food. Here the intended obligation necessarily includes the forbidden sale. Nor is there difficulty from the immediate liability for an oath in vain. That remains only while the first oath stands. The Rashba expressly rules, following Rava, that if one obtains release from the first oath, the second takes effect in its place and ceases to be an oath in vain, becoming an oath of expression. The Rosh rules like Rava. Because annulment uproots the first oath retroactively, it is then revealed that the second was not an oath in vain. This is what the Sema intended, and the matter is clear.
