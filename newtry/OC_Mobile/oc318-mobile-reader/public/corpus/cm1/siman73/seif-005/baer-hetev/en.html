Takes effect. The Tur writes that such an oath is an oath to nullify a mitzvah. The Sema rejects the suggestion that he can keep the oath not to sell by simply giving the creditor all his property in payment, because transferring all his property to the creditor is itself a sale in substance. The Shach concludes that even if the debt had not yet reached its due date when the oath was taken, the oath still does not take effect. This differs from an oath concerning matzah or sukkah taken during the year, where no current obligation exists and performing the act then would not discharge the future mitzvah. Here, although the due date has not arrived, the debt and lien already exist from the time of the loan, and early payment would discharge the obligation. Therefore the duty exists from the outset and the oath cannot take effect. See Responsa Maharshach II, 82. The Taz and Chacham Tzvi discuss the matter at length and challenge both the Sema and Shach.
Preceded. Even though he swore not to sell his property to pay any debt, if at that time he did not yet owe this debt, the oath took effect before the obligation arose. Sema.
The second. The Mechaber's sequence is as follows. If the first oath preceded Shimon's debt, Shimon cannot collect until the borrower obtains release from the first oath. The creditor's practical course is to apply to beit din to punish the borrower for having taken the second oath in vain, and through the punishment the borrower may regret the first oath and seek its annulment. If a valid opening is found to annul the first oath, he is no longer punished for the second, because it is retroactively revealed not to have been vain. The Bach disagrees and maintains that the second oath remains punishable even after annulment of the first. The Shach sides with the Sema. He also rejects the Bach's argument that the second oath was not vain because the borrower could technically pay without selling property. Since his first oath prohibited selling property to pay any debt and his second oath obligated him to pay Shimon, with the intent to sell if necessary, the second is indeed an oath in vain. See Shach, Yoreh Deah 238, seif katan 22.
In vain. The Bach writes that beit din nevertheless has authority to descend to the debtor's property and collect for the creditor without requiring the debtor personally to sell it. Shach.
Rabbinic lashes. The Sema explains that because the oath was taken without prior warning, Torah lashes do not apply. The Shach notes that this explanation is unnecessary, because in any event all lashes administered by contemporary batei din are only makkat mardut, as the Rambam writes concerning batei din outside Eretz Yisrael. Even in Eretz Yisrael Torah lashes require semichah, which does not exist today.
