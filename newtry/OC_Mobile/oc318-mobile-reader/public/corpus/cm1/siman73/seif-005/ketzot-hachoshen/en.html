Reuven swore. The Shach concludes that if Reuven swore generally not to repay any debt, the oath can take effect through inclusion even where Shimon's debt preceded the oath. He compares it to one who swears not to eat matzah or not to sit in a sukkah generally. He rejects the distinction that here the debt already existed before the oath, because its due date had not yet arrived. Chacham Tzvi strongly attacks this. He says nearly all the major authorities teach that an oath can take effect through inclusion even where the mitzvah obligation already exists at the time of the oath. Tosafot, Ritzba, Ri the Elder, Ramah, Ramban, Ran, Rivash, Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and others all allow inclusion of permitted and prohibited matters. A man already obligated in procreation can nevertheless, according to Rivash and Rama, swear not to marry another woman because the oath includes women toward whom the mitzvah does not apply. Likewise, one who says simply, "I will not eat matzah," while standing on the night of the festival, is forbidden in all matzah through inclusion. Thus, says Chacham Tzvi, the timing distinction cannot stand. Since the Shach's position appears correct to me, I must explain it at length. I found an early authority supporting him: Raavad in Temim De'im on Pesachim. Raavad distinguishes whether the oath preceded the mitzvah obligation. If the oath came first, the permitted and prohibited matters were included before the obligation arrived. But if one swears during the period of obligation itself, there is no effective inclusion because the oath begins immediately with the prohibited matter. Rivash, who validates the oath of an older wife that the husband not marry another woman, relies on inclusion: there are women toward whom no mitzvah of procreation applies. Rabbi Chasdai disagrees and points out that even an oath not to eat matzah includes matzah that cannot fulfill the mitzvah, such as rich matzah or products that do not become chametz. Yet the Yerushalmi says that an oath not to eat matzah on the festival does not take effect. Rabbi Chasdai therefore understands that mere implicit inclusion does not help where the oath begins in direct conflict with the mitzvah. This accords with Raavad. The principle is that if one swears during the festival, "I will eat no matzah," even though his words include rich matzah, the oath emerges immediately as a vain oath because it also includes the required matzah which he must eat now. This resembles one who swears not to eat for seven days. Such an oath contains one or two days during which fasting would be permitted, yet because it necessarily includes the period in which he must eat to preserve life, the oath is vain from its inception. There never was an independent valid oath upon the permitted portion. So too one who swears during the festival not to eat any matzah has made an oath that from the first moment includes prohibited nullification of the mitzvah, and the whole oath is vain. By contrast, one who swears before the festival not to eat matzah generally creates a valid oath immediately on ordinary weekdays. Once it validly takes effect, it continues into the festival through inclusion. The same explains the Shach's statement concerning one who swears to repay a debt. If the obligation already demands immediate payment and the person swears in terms that necessarily include refusing that payment, the oath emerges as vain rather than taking effect through a merely implicit permitted component. This also explains Tosafot's challenge from the Yerushalmi. Tosafot discusses an express formulation that separately includes permitted and prohibited foods, such as, "I swear not to eat slaughtered meat or nonkosher meat." Where the person explicitly names the permitted item as an independent object of the oath, the oath validly takes effect on that permitted matter and can extend by inclusion to the forbidden matter. The Shach's case is different. He speaks of a general undivided oath whose first practical content directly conflicts with an existing mitzvah. Thus the major authorities need not disagree with Raavad. The case of an oath not to marry another woman also fits. Where the wording is, "I will not marry another woman over you," the oath need not nullify procreation because he can divorce the first wife, pay her ketubah, and then marry another. The mitzvah does not require him to keep this particular wife while marrying another. Therefore the oath is not necessarily an oath to nullify a mitzvah. This is how the Rosh's responsum should be understood. He explains that the condition does not contradict Torah law because if she fails to bear children he can divorce her and fulfill the mitzvah with another. It is not necessary to invoke inclusion. Mahari of Trani tried to infer from this responsum that the Rosh rejects Rivash's inclusion theory. He argued that if inclusion were enough, the Rosh would not need the answer that he can divorce the first wife. That inference is not decisive if the case was framed as a contractual condition rather than an oath. Yet after careful review, Mahari's reading has substantial force because the responsum speaks of written confirmations and oaths in the ketubah, not merely an abstract condition. If the oath had been, "I will never marry another woman under any circumstances," it would indeed directly nullify the mitzvah and implicit inclusion should not save it under Raavad's rule. The Rosh therefore needed the practical possibility of divorce and remarriage. Thus the only clear disagreement is Rivash's broader view. The Shach follows Raavad and Rabbi Chasdai: implicit inclusion helps where the oath first takes valid effect on a permitted matter and later reaches the mitzvah, or where the permitted matter is expressly separated in the oath. It does not help where the general oath emerges from its first moment as an oath to nullify a presently binding mitzvah. This resolves Chacham Tzvi's objections and preserves the Shach's distinction.
If the oath preceded it. Knesset HaGedolah in Yoreh Deah siman 236 explains the Rosh's case where the oath preceded Shimon's debt as an oath not to sell one's property for any future debt, whether owed to a Jew or gentile. Only then, he says, is there inclusion. If the oath referred only to Jewish debts, it would still be an oath to nullify a mitzvah, since repayment of a debt is a mitzvah. He compares Rambam's ruling that one who swears not to testify for another has taken a vain oath because there is a mitzvah to testify. Knesset HaGedolah then asks: if inclusion with gentile debts works, why should it matter whether Shimon's debt preceded the oath? Even if Shimon's debt existed first, the oath includes gentile debts and should extend to the Jewish debt. He distinguishes that the testimony case can arise without the person's choice, whereas here the person can avoid borrowing and thereby avoid the mitzvah obligation. He adds that perhaps the Rosh simply disagrees with Rambam. I prefer to say that the Rosh disagrees rather than make such a distinction. Rambam himself says that one who swears not to wear tzitzit has taken an oath to nullify a mitzvah. He cannot mean that a person is commanded always to wear a four cornered garment. Rather, he means that if he wears such a garment he may not leave it without tzitzit. Yet he could avoid the obligation by wearing a garment without four corners. Thus Rambam's law itself shows that an obligation that depends on one's future choice can still make an oath one that nullifies a mitzvah. Rivash similarly rules concerning one who swears neither to perform chalitzah nor yibbum. The mitzvah is to release the widow from the levirate bond through one of those acts. An oath refusing both is a direct oath to nullify the mitzvah. Rivash adds another reason: the mitzvah obligation is owed to the widow, and the oath harms her by leaving her bound. An oath cannot take effect to harm another person, just as one cannot validly swear to strike, rob, or oppress another. This actually supplies a good answer to Knesset HaGedolah's original question. Where the oath precedes Shimon's debt, there is as yet no harm to Shimon. Through inclusion with gentile debts the oath can take effect. When the Jewish debt arises later, the already valid oath remains. But if Shimon's debt already exists, inclusion cannot make the oath effective insofar as it harms him by withholding payment. Inclusion does not validate an oath to wrong another. Therefore the timing distinction remains. This still requires further study, but it provides a principled basis.
