Seif 5: "Reuven swore." It is difficult to understand why the creditor cannot take the relevant property in payment of his debt. Sema writes that this would be like a sale. But what concern is the debtor's oath to beit din? Beit din can descend to his property and give the creditor assets equal to the debt, because a person's property guarantees his obligations and the lien of a creditor is by Torah law. It is also difficult that the ruling says, "if he regrets," etc., "he should sell," implying that unless he regrets his oath the sale should not take place. Sema indeed writes that there is no way to collect the debt until the oath is released. This is extremely surprising. Can a person nullify his fellow's debt by means of an oath? Is this stronger than a sale made in violation of a prohibition, such as one who swore not to sell and nevertheless sold, where siman 208 rules that the sale is valid? It appears to me that beit din certainly has authority to satisfy the creditor against the debtor's will. The issue here is only how to navigate the conflicting oaths. If the debtor himself sells, or beit din sells as his agents, he violates the first oath; if he does not sell, he violates the second. The ruling is that it is preferable for the violation to occur passively, by not selling. This is explicit in the responsum, which brings proof from one who swore, "I will eat this loaf," and afterward swore, "I will not eat it." The proposed method of payment therefore avoids a sale. The debtor thereby violates the second oath passively and is subject to lashes of rebellion rather than Torah lashes, because the violation is passive. The editor objects that passivity is not the relevant point, since at the moment he swore falsely he would be liable for a vain oath; although a prohibition without an act normally receives no lashes, the Gemara expressly excludes an oath, substitution of a korban, and cursing another with Hashem's Name, and Rambam rules accordingly. In the era of the Rosh, however, ordinary judicial lashes were in any event not administered, so the practical point is simple. Through fear of the disciplinary lashes the debtor may regret his conduct, giving an opening to release the first oath and allowing him to fulfill the second. But if the creditor's debt preceded the oath, the oath does not take effect at all, because payment of an ordinary debt is a mitzvas aseh and a debtor who refuses to perform a positive commandment is coerced until he complies. The Gemara in chapter HaKosev explicitly applies "we compel him until his soul departs" to payment of debts; mere lashes do not suffice. Were debt payment not a positive commandment, such extreme coercion would not be justified. The later discussion in the responsum concerning punishment for the oath does not mean to prescribe the debtor's punishment for failure to pay; there, they tell him to remain passive. The point is rather that because payment of a debt is such a weighty obligation that a recalcitrant debtor may be coerced until he complies, an oath cannot override it. This is apparent to anyone who studies Responsa Rosh, kelal 8, siman 3. Therefore, if he does not regret the oath because of the disciplinary lashes, beit din ultimately must do its duty and satisfy the creditor against the debtor's will. Sema understood the Rosh's phrase "we compel him until his soul departs" as prescribing the punishment of this particular debtor, but the explanation I have given appears correct. One matter still requires study. In that responsum the Rosh rules not to sell, so that the violation remains passive, and cites the case of a man who first swore to eat a loaf and then swore not to eat it, saying he should refrain from eating. Yet in his rulings at the end of chapter 7 of Shevuos, the Rosh rules that it is preferable for him to eat. This appears to be a retraction, and since the rulings are later, Tur should have followed them. Indeed, in Yoreh De'ah, siman 238, Tur rules that he should eat. It appears to me that Tur nevertheless rules here not to sell in accordance with the Rosh, but not because of the responsum's comparison to the loaf. On the contrary, with the loaf we rule that he should eat, as stated in Yoreh De'ah, and that very reasoning yields here that he should not sell. In the loaf case, once he swears the second oath, "I will not eat," he has already uttered a false oath and incurred the existing transgression of a vain oath. If he now refrains from eating, he begins a new violation of the first oath. It is therefore preferable to remain with the transgression already incurred and not create a new one. Here, similarly, if he refrains from selling he is already in violation of the later vain oath from the moment it left his mouth, but if he sells he initiates a new violation of the first oath. Thus Tur's ruling is correct according to the later halacha, though not for the precise reason stated in the responsum.
The editor says: I saw that Shach wrote here words that do not appear correct. In seif katan 11 he says: perhaps the Rosh's rule applies only in such a case, but if a person swore generally not to pay any debt, even though Shimon's debt already existed, the oath takes effect. This is very surprising. Even a general oath not to pay any debt certainly does not take effect where all the included debts are already obligatory; there is no inclusive oath covering both permitted and prohibited matters, but only obligations that he is already commanded to pay. What difference is there between Shimon's debt and debts to others? He must pay all of them. The end of the Rosh's responsum itself contradicts Shach, as Shach noticed and attempted to emend, but his emendation is not convincing. In my humble opinion, the Rosh's words are straightforward: only if the oath preceded the debt to Shimon, for example he generally swore that he would not sell his property in order to pay any debt he might later incur, can the oath take effect. If he already owed Shimon and then swore generally not to pay any debt, the oath certainly does not take effect, because there is no inclusive element at all. He is equally obligated to pay Reuven and Shimon. If after owing Shimon he swears, "I will not pay any debt that I will hereafter incur," there is no question at all, because Shimon is not included in the wording; the oath covers future debts, while Shimon's debt already exists. Thus Shach's proposed emendation, "or where after incurring the debt he swore generally not to sell any of his property for any debt he might incur," is a textual error and has no place. Shach further says that the Mechaber's reading appears strained and therefore he explains the Rosh simply, but on that reading one would have to distinguish this case from matzah and sukkah, and ultimately say that the Rosh's case involved a debt that arose before the oath. Any fair reader will see that this cannot be said, for the Rosh expressly writes that the oath preceded the debt, and he must then explain how an oath can precede a debt at all, such as by swearing generally not to pay future debts. If an oath concerning already existing debts were sufficient, there would be no need for that construction. We have also shown that if Shimon's debt already exists and the oath speaks only of debts he will incur, Shimon is plainly not included. Shach further suggests that matzah and sukkah are different because during the year there is not yet an active mitzvah obligation, whereas a debt already exists and could be paid early. I say that all the great authorities disagree with this distinction: Tosafos in the name of Ritzba and Ri HaZaken, Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Ran, Rivash, Beit Yosef, Rama, and the other poskim. From all of them we learn that even regarding a mitzvah already incumbent now, if the oath includes permitted matters together with the prohibited matter, it can take effect upon the prohibited matter as well. Tosafos on Shevuos 24a, s.v. "rather yes," ask from the Yerushalmi's case of one who swears not to eat matzah against our Gemara's discussion of an inclusive oath covering kosher meat together with neveilah and treifah. Were Shach's distinction correct, neveilah and treifah would be different because their prohibition applies at all times. The very question and answer of Tosafos disprove his theory. Baal HaMaor rejected that Yerushalmi from the halacha because of the case of neveilah and treifah; if Shach were correct, the Bavli and Yerushalmi would not be comparable. Ramban, Ran, and Rivash accept Ri's resolution, and Beit Yosef and Rama codify it in Yoreh De'ah, siman 239, concerning one who marries an elderly woman and swears not to take another wife. The oath takes effect because it includes matters on which an oath can take effect, even though the mitzvah of procreation is incumbent on him at all times. Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer, siman 118, seif 15, rules likewise. Rosh himself expressly ruled so in a responsum cited by Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 118, seif 36, concerning a wealthy widow past childbearing age whom Shimon married after swearing not to marry another woman. The Rosh ruled that the oath cannot simply be released on account of procreation, showing that the inclusive oath took effect despite the continuous mitzvah. The very source concerning matzah and sukkah proves the same: only if one says, "I will not eat matzah on the night of Pesach" or "I will not sit in the shade of a sukkah," meaning the mitzvah sukkah, does the oath fail to take effect and he is lashed and must eat or sit. If he says generally, "I will not eat matzah," even while standing on Pesach night, or generally, "I will not sit in shade," even during Sukkos, it is obvious that the oath can prohibit the act. The reason a direct oath against a mitzvah does not take effect necessarily has nothing to do with whether the mitzvah is presently incumbent or will become incumbent later. If one swears today not to put on tefillin tomorrow, or before Sukkos not to sit in a sukkah during the festival, the oath still does not take effect against the mitzvah. And the principle that an inclusive oath extends to otherwise prohibited matters is a general mechanism in the Torah. There is therefore no basis to distinguish between a mitzvah presently obligatory and one that becomes obligatory later. Shach's further emendation in seif katan 13 also does not appear correct. In my view, Rosh holds that the second oath, to pay Shimon, is itself inclusive of a permitted and a prohibited possibility: perhaps at the due date the debtor will have cash and can pay without selling, in which case performance is permitted; perhaps he will have no cash and must sell, which is prohibited by the first oath. Since an inclusive oath takes effect, the second is a valid oath of expression. This is what the Rosh means when he says it is preferable to violate the oath of expression passively. There are two valid oaths of expression, the first not to sell and the second to pay; nevertheless, he should violate the second passively rather than the first actively. Since circumstances now force him to violate the second by inaction, that oath also becomes a vain oath in the practical sense. After the Rosh cites the case of the loaf, one should add: "However, in our case the second oath takes effect inclusively, and nevertheless it is better to violate the oath of expression passively and not sell, in order to uphold the first oath; the second thus turns out to be a vain oath because it cannot be fulfilled." The remainder of the text is missing. There is another shorter way to emend the responsum. It was written with abbreviations: "it is better that he violate sh-b." The copyist thought this meant shevuas bitui, an oath of expression, but perhaps it meant shevuah beis, "the second oath." Likewise, later the abbreviation sh-sh may have been read as shevuas shav, a vain oath, whereas it may mean shtei shevuos, "two oaths." The correct reading would then be: "It is better that he violate the second oath, which is passive"; meaning, even though the second is fulfilled or violated through inaction, it is preferable to violate it by actively eating the loaf than to violate two oaths, for if he remains passive he violates both the oath of expression and the vain oath. This also fits the wording "rise and transgress" better than "rise and act," and could explain how a copyist altered the text. On this reading the Rosh's practical ruling follows by a kal vachomer: in the loaf case, even though eating is an active deed, we tell him to eat so as not to violate two oaths; all the more so here, where refraining from sale is passive, we do not require a sale merely to avoid the later violation. This reading is very sound. In a responsum I sent ten years ago to R' Yosef Almosnino of Belgrade concerning an inclusive oath, I discussed this at length. May Hashem grant us merit to publish it. End of the editor's words.
