The second oath is therefore an oath taken in vain. See the Perishah, where I wrote that even if he sells some of his own property in order to fulfill the second oath, nevertheless at the moment he swore to pay, that oath was in vain, since he had already sworn not to sell. He is therefore liable to lashes for it, as stated in Yoreh Deah siman 238, unless he regrets the first oath and finds an opening through which it can be annulled. If the first oath is annulled retroactively, it is as though he never swore it, and he is no longer lashed for the second oath, because it then becomes clear retroactively that the second oath was not in vain. This is why the Tur and Mechaber write that if, from fear of the lashes, he regrets the first oath, they annul the first oath for him. This is so even though the lashes are imposed because of the second oath, since annulment of the second oath would not save him from those lashes. This is clear. The proper reading of the Mechaber's ruling is therefore as follows: If the first oath preceded the debt owed to Shimon, Shimon has no means of collecting his debt until the borrower's first oath is annulled. His practical counsel is for the lender to go to beit din and ask that the borrower be lashed for having taken the second oath in vain. Out of fear of those lashes, the borrower may regret the first oath and seek its annulment. See the Perishah and Derishah for what I wrote further concerning this.
