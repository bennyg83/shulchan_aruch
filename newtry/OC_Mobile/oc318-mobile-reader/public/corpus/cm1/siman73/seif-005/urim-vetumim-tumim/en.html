And if the oath came first. Rosh compares this to one who first swears to eat a loaf and afterward swears not to eat it. He says it is preferable to violate the later oath passively than to perform an active violation of the earlier oath. The commentators object that Rosh himself cites the Yerushalmi saying the opposite: he should eat the loaf, because if he refrains he violates both the original oath to eat and the second oath, which was already uttered in vain because it contradicted the first. The cases can be distinguished. Where the second oath expressly says, "I will not eat the very loaf I already swore to eat," it is false from the moment it leaves his mouth. The shevuat shav violation already exists, and later nonperformance adds another violation. Here the second oath is broader and not facially contradictory. For example, he first swore not to sell his property, and later swore to repay a debt by its due date. When he made the later oath, he may have believed he would possess cash and would not need to sell anything. The second oath was therefore not necessarily vain when uttered. Only when the due date arrives and he has no cash does the conflict become actual. At that point there is one practical choice: sell and violate the first oath actively, or refrain and fail to fulfill the later payment oath passively. Rosh says the passive violation is preferable. This also explains why the punishment is discussed only when the due date arrives rather than immediately when the second oath is made. It further explains Rosh's statement that if fear of disciplinary lashes causes the debtor to regret the first oath and find an opening, the first oath can be annulled and he can then sell and fulfill the second. If the second oath had already been a shevuat shav from the moment of utterance, annulling the first would not obviously remove the punishment already incurred. But under this distinction, no false oath was uttered at the outset. The violation arises only upon nonperformance. The proposed opening is also no forbidden nolad. The debtor says, in effect: "Had I realized that maintaining this oath would place me in a position where I would be punished for violating it, I would never have sworn." Punishment for violating an oath is an ordinary foreseeable consequence, not an unforeseen newly created event. This also resolves the apparent contradiction with Yoreh Deah siman 238, where Rashba is uncertain whether annulling the first oath removes the status of a later oath that was already vain when uttered. There the second oath directly contradicted the first from the outset. Here, by contrast, the second oath was not false when made and becomes problematic only later. Once the first oath is annulled before the act, the debtor can lawfully perform the second. Thus the two rulings are consistent.
