The oath does not take effect. The Sema writes that there is no difficulty that he could simply give the creditor property equal to the debt instead of selling, because transferring property in payment is itself included in the prohibition against selling. One might ask why he cannot give the property as a gift to another, after which the creditor would collect from the recipient. The answer is that with an oral debt the creditor cannot collect from a gift recipient, and even with a documented debt the recipient can say, "I left you property with the donor from which to collect." The fact that the donor swore not to sell does not change the ordinary property rules. This is unlike the case where free property was destroyed, because here the property itself did not become legally impaired.
The oath takes effect. This means that he swore not to sell the property either to creditors already existing or to creditors from whom he might borrow later. See Tumim for how I answered the Shach's question why the oath should not take effect through an inclusive formulation. If he swore not to sell to creditors whether Jewish or non Jewish, I concluded in Tumim that the matter is disputed and therefore remains a doubt in prohibition. See there.
And we strike him, etc. This follows the rule that repayment of a debt is a mitzvah and one who refuses may be coerced by lashes. Sema and Shach. In addition, he is liable for lashes because he violated a vain oath, since one who swears to nullify a mitzvah violates the prohibition of a vain oath, as stated in the Gemara and authorities. In our time this results in disciplinary lashes. It requires explanation why the Rosh and Tur did not mention this.
And if the oath preceded, etc. It is not an oath to nullify a mitzvah because at the time he swore he was not yet obligated to that creditor and could avoid the issue entirely by refraining from borrowing. Although one who swears, "If I later know testimony for you, I will not testify," is considered to have sworn to nullify a mitzvah even though he does not yet know any testimony, testimony is not under his control: he may unexpectedly witness Reuven give money to Shimon. Here, however, it is entirely within his control not to borrow. So writes Behag.
The second oath is found to be a vain oath. He is already obligated to keep the first oath and not sell the property. The second oath, by which he undertakes to repay, is therefore a vain oath, because although he did not expressly swear to sell, repayment is understood to include sale if he lacks cash, and that would contradict the first oath. Shach, correctly.
A vain oath. The Sema holds that the second oath is immediately a vain oath at the instant it is uttered, even if the debtor later keeps the first oath and never sells. Once he swore to nullify the obligation created by his first oath, the vain oath was complete and he is liable for lashes. See Tumim. I wrote that according to the Rosh, because he did not swear absolutely to sell but only to repay, and repayment could still be made from cash without violating the first oath, the words were not false when uttered. Liability for a vain oath arises only later, when the payment date arrives, he has no cash, and keeping the second oath would necessarily require sale in violation of the first. The language of the Tur and Shulchan Aruch supports this. See there.
And we strike him with disciplinary lashes. We lack ordained judges and there was no formal warning, but even though the violation is one of passive noncompliance, lashes can apply to an oath, substitution of sanctified property, and cursing, where lashes are imposed even for prohibitions lacking a physical act.
And if because of fear of the lashes he regrets, etc. See Tumim. Maharshal appears to hold that regret cannot retroactively exempt him from lashes. I concluded first that here, where repayment of a debt is itself a mitzvah, all agree that annulment can also remove the lashes. More fundamentally, I concluded against Maharshal's view. See there.
And he finds an opening. See Tumim. In our practice a specific opening is not required and genuine regret suffices. The ruling teaches that even where he does not regret the original decision to make the vow and has no other opening, the present consequence of being subjected to lashes can itself serve as an opening and is not treated as an unforeseeable later development. See Tumim, seif katan 5 and 7, at length. The Bach writes that all this concerns compelling him to repay voluntarily, but beit din certainly may descend to his property and collect without his consent. The later authorities agree. See Tumim. I wrote that the discussion concerns movable property in circumstances where beit din would not otherwise directly seize it. The Sema writes that only the first oath may be annulled, not the second, because the second is already a vain oath and, independently, it was made for another person's benefit and cannot be annulled without that person's consent. See Tumim, seif katan 9.
