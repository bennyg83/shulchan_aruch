Demand. The Sema writes that at first glance this implies that only in an ordinary loan, where no time was expressly fixed, must the creditor wait thirty days after making his demand. But if at the outset of a loan secured by a pledge he fixed a date for repayment after thirty days, he would not need to wait another thirty days before selling the pledge. Yet according to the Tur's conclusion in siman 100, that even when a term was fixed for a loan and the creditor demands payment at the end of that term, the borrower is granted an additional thirty days, the same should seemingly apply here. There, in seif 2, the Mechaber presents as a dispute whether one who had a fixed repayment date is granted thirty days. See there.
The custom. The Sema writes: See below, siman 369, seif 8 in the gloss, where a similar rule appears, except that there it states that a creditor holding a pledge may sell it after one year because that is the law of the kingdom. This implies that without the law of the kingdom he would have to wait more than a year, whereas here the implication is the reverse, that without the custom he could sell it in less than a year. One must therefore say that there the case is a Jew who lent another Jew money against a gentile's pledge. The ruling teaches that the pledgor cannot say, 'Since you collect interest from me, you must wait until the gentile comes and redeems it, because I fear him.' Here, however, the case is a loan to another Jew without interest, and nevertheless the creditor must wait a year. This is unlike the Levush. The Shach challenges the Sema, since the rule in siman 369 is from Mordechai and the incident there concerned pledged books, which certainly were not a gentile's property. (The Taz likewise challenges him, and distinguishes between a creditor who wishes to sell the pledge and collect his debt, and one who demands that the debtor pay him from another source. See there.) Rather, it appears that here the creditor is demanding payment, and therefore, absent the custom, he could sell before the year. Below, however, the creditor has made no demand, and after one year he may sell immediately without first making a demand. The Sema's objection to the Levush's distinction between an interest-bearing and a non-interest-bearing loan is also not compelling, and the Levush's words appear correct. The Shach concludes: All this I have written according to the Rama's view, but in my opinion the basic ruling itself requires study. Since according to Torah law the creditor may sell after thirty days, how can we learn from gentile law so as to nullify Torah law? Heaven forbid that such a thing be in Israel. The principle that the law of the kingdom is law does not apply here at all. See there, where he discusses at length where that principle does and does not apply.
