Even though the term of the loan has passed, etc. The Tur likewise writes this in the name of the Rosh concerning an unspecified loan and concludes with the reason that had the lender brought the borrower to court, the court would have granted him thirty days. At first glance, this implies that the rule applies specifically to an unspecified loan. Although the lender need not wait longer than the original thirty day term, since no explicit term was fixed he must still wait thirty days after demanding payment. But if, when the loan was originally made upon the pledge, they expressly fixed repayment after thirty days, perhaps the lender need not wait another thirty days before selling the pledge. However, in light of the Tur's conclusion below in siman 100, that even where a term was fixed for the loan and the lender sues at the end of that term the court grants the borrower thirty days, it may be that the same applies here, since the Rosh links these two laws. The Mechaber in siman 100:2 presents as a dispute whether one whose repayment term was fixed is granted an additional thirty days. See there.
And even if he demanded payment only privately, etc. This is straightforward, because witnesses were created only to prevent falsehood. Rama writes this here in order to reconcile two responsa of the Rosh cited consecutively by the Tur, one in seif 22 and the other in seif 23. In the first, the Rosh writes that the lender should demand payment before witnesses, while in the second he writes that he may demand it privately. With this explanation, there is no need for the Rivash's reconciliation that the phrase "privately" is imprecise and means only that the demand need not be made before beit din. See the Perishah.
Where the custom is that one who lends to gentiles, etc. The same rule is applied to a Jew. See above in siman 369:8, where Rama writes a similar law: Therefore, one who lends upon a pledge may sell it after a year, since such is the law of the kingdom, etc. This is somewhat difficult. There it sounds as though absent the law of the kingdom he would have to wait more than a year, while here Rama writes that in a place where the custom is that one who lends to gentiles may not sell the pledge in less than a year, the same rule applies to a Jew. This implies that without such a custom he could sell it even before a year. It may be answered that in siman 369 the case concerns a Jew who lent another Jew money upon a pledge belonging to a gentile. The novelty there is that the pledgor cannot say: Since you are collecting interest from the gentile, you must wait longer until the gentile comes to redeem it, because I am afraid of the gentile. Here, by contrast, the case concerns an interest free loan from one Jew to another, and the novelty is that he must nevertheless wait a year. This is clear. See the Ir Shushan, who writes that one should not compare a loan to a gentile upon a pledge, where the lender receives interest and therefore has reason to wait a year, to an interest free loan between Jews. Regarding Rama's ruling, he writes that it applies only where the custom is that even a lender to a gentile without interest must wait a year, or alternatively where the Jewish lender also receives interest, for example where the pledge was given to him under the legal form of a gentile's pledge. He adds that it is strained to say that the reward for the mitzvah of lending to a Jew without charge substitutes for the actual interest received from a gentile's pledge, and that otherwise the ordinary Jewish loan upon a pledge should remain subject to the rule that an unspecified loan is for thirty days. This is the substance of the Ir Shushan's position. His ruling does not appear correct to me. If so, Rama should not have stated without qualification that Jewish pledges are governed by the practice concerning gentile pledges. The responsum of the Raavad and the Mordechai in chapter HaGozel, cited by Darkei Moshe, also speak without qualification and imply that the rule applies in every case. The same implication appears below in siman 369, as explained above. The reason is that when the borrower placed the pledge in the lender's hands, we say that he gave it as a pledge with the understanding that the lender would deal with it according to the same practice he follows with gentile pledges. It was incumbent upon the lender to stipulate that he would not follow that practice with this borrower. Since he made no such stipulation, we infer that this too was his intention at the time of the loan. This is clear.
