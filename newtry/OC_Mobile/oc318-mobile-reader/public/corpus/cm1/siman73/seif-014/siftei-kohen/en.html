Some say that where the custom is, etc. The Sema asks why here local custom is said to require waiting a year, whereas below the Rama invokes the law of the kingdom to permit sale after a year, implying that without the custom one would have to wait longer. His distinction between a pledge belonging to a gentile and an interest-free loan between Jews is not convincing, and the source case concerned Jewish books. The better distinction is that here the lender is presently demanding payment, so absent custom he could sell sooner; below he made no demand, and after a year he may sell without any prior demand. The Levush further limits the custom to loans comparable to gentile interest-bearing loans. This is substantially correct: if even a gentile borrower who pays no interest need not receive a year, why should a Jewish borrower fare worse? The custom should be applied only to the extent it actually governs the parallel gentile transaction. The Rama's principal ruling nevertheless requires study. Torah law expressly permits sale of a movable pledge after thirty days. How can gentile law override an explicit Torah rule? Some authorities apply the law of the kingdom only to royal taxes and matters benefiting the king. Even those who apply it more broadly do so only where it does not contradict an explicit Torah law. The Mordechai's case, where books were sold after a year without prior demand, is different because our law does not explicitly forbid such a sale, and an unspecified pledge may indeed be treated as lasting a year. The Rashba, Maharik, Ran, and Raavad all emphasize that civil law cannot displace the Torah's established rules between Jews. The Rama elsewhere says that royal law applies to matters benefiting the king or serving public order, but not so as to adjudicate by gentile law and thereby nullify Jewish law. The only defensible reading is that public custom may govern commercial matters where our law is not explicit, not where it contradicts a settled Torah rule. The Levush states this clearly. Accordingly, the Rama's ruling here remains difficult.
