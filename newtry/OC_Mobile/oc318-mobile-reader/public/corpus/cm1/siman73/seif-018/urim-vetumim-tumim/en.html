Many major authorities agree with Ramban, and Shach's question is justified. There is an even greater difficulty from Maggid Mishneh. Raavad holds that when a Jew pledges chametz to a gentile and says it will become the gentile's if not redeemed by a certain date, the arrangement can involve asmachta unless the gentile was given present acquisition. Raavad nevertheless says asmachta rules differ for gentiles. Maggid Mishneh explains that Raavad follows his view that a pledge can involve asmachta, whereas Rambam holds the opposite. Maggid Mishneh connects the issue to the broader rule of "I waive what is already mine." If a present waiver is not considered asmachta, then a pledge forfeiture should likewise not be asmachta. Ramban's own novelty is needed because he can hold that ordinary waiver can still be asmachta, yet a creditor's proprietary acquisition in collateral removes the concern. Rosh in Pesachim challenges Ramban from the law of earnest money, but under Rambam's system even that case is governed by the broader non-asmachta rule for waiver. Mechaber below in siman 207, seif 11, follows Rambam that a true "I waive what is mine" formulation is not asmachta. If so, it is very difficult for Mechaber to rule here without qualification that forfeiture of collateral is asmachta. Even if one argues that collateral is not technically a simple waiver, Maggid Mishneh and other authorities expressly connect the two categories. Therefore Mechaber's ruling here requires study, and Ramban's contrary position should certainly be regarded as substantial.
