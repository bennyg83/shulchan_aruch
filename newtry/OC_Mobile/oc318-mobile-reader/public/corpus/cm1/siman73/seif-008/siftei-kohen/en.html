Some say that even, etc., he must bring it after him. This ruling is not established. Maharik adduces several proofs, but all may be rejected. Most fundamentally, liability for an oath requires verbal articulation. He swore to bring the money to a particular place, not to some other place, even if the latter is no farther away. Since no obligation existed without the oath, liability cannot extend beyond what he uttered. Maharik distinguishes between personal prohibitory oaths, which are governed strictly by the words, and an oath made to another person to fulfill a condition, which follows the scope of the condition. I do not find that distinction persuasive. An oath voluntarily undertaken to pay another remains an oath of expression and is governed by the verse requiring articulation. Sources concerning vows made to another likewise follow the language, using evident intention only to narrow the oath, not to impose something never spoken. Maharik's proof from the Rosh, that one who swore to pay by a deadline remains bound to pay after the deadline, is also inconclusive. There the underlying debt already required payment, and the oath's essence was payment; the date merely urged prompt performance. Here the alternative place was never included. The practical ruling therefore requires study, especially because the debtor may readily explain that he expected to travel to the first place, had established means of transmitting money there, or trusted a regular traveler there, none of which applies to the second place.

The Sema explains that the Rosh's responsum concerned a deed stating that Reuven undertook under oath to pay a specified sum to whoever produced the deed. Even if the deed alone did not create a lien because no lender was named, the oath remained binding. The Sema suggests limiting this to a case where the original undertaking itself was made under oath; if the debt arose first and he swore afterward, perhaps the oath falls with the debt. I see no basis for this distinction. In either case, he later swore to pay and must fulfill his oath. Perhaps the Sema means only a case where the oath was expressly to pay whatever the prior lien required; if the lien was void, nothing was included in the oath. But that is not the plain meaning of his words, and in such a case there would be no difficulty with the Rama's general formulation.
