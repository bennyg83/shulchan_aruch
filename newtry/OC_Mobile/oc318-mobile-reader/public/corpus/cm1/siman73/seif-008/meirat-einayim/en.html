The lender is not in the city, etc. It was already explained in seif 6 that even if the lender is in the city, the borrower need not take an oath so long as the lender has not demanded payment. Rashba merely speaks in the ordinary manner, because normally, if the lender is present, he demands payment.Until the lender comes, etc. Rashba writes that he need not give the money to the lender's wife or children, because even if he does so the borrower is not discharged and remains responsible for it.
Nor must he pursue the lender to Media, for that was required only regarding one who robbed and then swore falsely; if he did not swear, it is not required.
He likewise need not convene a beit din and deposit the money with them.He may not spend it. Meaning, even after that day he may not spend it until the lender comes and he gives it to him.Or he left there. Meaning that he did not permanently relocate, but merely left on business.Some say even if he relocated, etc. This is also from Maharik and does not dispute the preceding ruling; it is phrased as 'some say' because it teaches the greater novelty that even in such a case the borrower must pursue him.One who swore to pay his fellow, etc., nevertheless must pay because of his oath. The Rosh's responsum concerned a document in which Reuven obligated himself under oath to pay a certain amount to whoever presented it against him.
The Rosh concludes that even if the document alone would not create liability because the lender was not named, the oath nevertheless binds him. In my opinion one should distinguish.
Where the original obligation itself was undertaken through an oath, he must fulfill the oath. But where the monetary obligation existed first and only afterward he swore to pay it, perhaps once the debt no longer exists, the oath made only on account of that debt also falls away.
Rama's unqualified formulation here therefore requires study.
