His agent. The Shach explains that the borrower need not give the money to the lender's wife or children, because even if he does so he is not thereby discharged and remains responsible for it. He likewise need not pursue the lender elsewhere; that obligation was stated only regarding one who stole and then swore falsely, as in siman 367. Nor must he convene a beit din and deposit the money with them, as the Rashba states. According to this, where the wife manages the household business, it appears that he must give it to her according to the view at the end of siman 120 that payment to her discharges him.
To remove them. Meaning that even after that day he may not take the money away until the lender comes so that he can give it to him. Sema.
From there. Meaning even if the lender did not permanently move away but merely left on business. See Responsa Maharshdam 386. Shach.
Distant. The Shach writes that this law is not compelling. Although Maharik supports it at length, his proofs can be rejected. In particular, regarding an oath one is liable only for what he actually articulated, so if he swore to bring the money to a particular place, why should he be obligated to bring it to another place merely because it is no farther? Maharik himself sensed this and distinguished between an oath or vow by which a person imposes a prohibition upon himself and an oath made to another person, but the Shach finds the distinction unpersuasive. He therefore leaves the matter requiring study, especially because the borrower can readily explain that he was already traveling to the other place or commonly went there, and similar explanations are possible.
To give. Where he is exempt from payment under the law, he is also exempt from the oath, because it is as though the debt was cancelled or waived. Maharik states this explicitly. This assumes that the person who swore is financially able to pay, or that the dowry will be placed with a neutral custodian. Otherwise it is obvious that the recipient can say that perhaps peace will not be restored and he will thereby lose the dowry. The same applies in comparable cases. Shach.
His oath. The Sema suggests a distinction. If the original obligation itself arose together with an oath, he must fulfill the oath. But if the debt existed first and only afterward he swore to pay it, then once the debt is gone perhaps the oath taken on its account is also gone. Therefore the Rama should not have stated the rule without qualification. The Shach rejects the distinction: once he later swore to pay, he must fulfill that oath regardless of when the underlying debt arose. He therefore approves the Rama's general formulation. See Responsa Maharshdam 57, 74, and 457. The Taz discusses one who swore to pay ten gold coins by a certain day and, if he failed, to pay ten gold coins to charity. He paid only five toward the debt. The Taz ruled that the penalty is due only proportionately to the unpaid balance, because the undertaking was not reasonably read as imposing the full penalty despite partial payment.
