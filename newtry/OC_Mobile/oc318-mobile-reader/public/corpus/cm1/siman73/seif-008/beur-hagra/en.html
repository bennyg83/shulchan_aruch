One who swears... is exempt until... Since the creditor did not demand payment, it is as though he told the debtor to keep the money for him, as in Bava Kamma 104a: once the owner knows, the money remains with the debtor as a deposit and the debtor has discharged his repayment obligation. Only a robber who swore falsely must pursue the owner because of atonement. Nor can he pay the creditor's wife or children, since this does not discharge his responsibility, as stated at the end of the first chapter of Gittin. Likewise merely appointing beit din is insufficient, because delivery without the creditor present is not treated as forgiveness. See Gittin 75a and the Sema.
But he must. Perhaps the creditor will later come and demand it. So writes the Rashba.
When does this apply... but. We follow the person's intent, as above in seif 7, and as Nedarim 55b says, "Rabbi Yehudah says everything depends on..." See also 63b concerning vows of wine and meat and circumstances where a person urges another, because he did not intend, etc.
Some say. Only where there is strong proof do we reinterpret an oath against its literal breadth. Thus Nedarim 25b concerning one who was dressed, etc., permits only the carrying contemplated by the context, while wearing remains prohibited in its ordinary forms even if not burdensome like the first case. See Rivash, siman 195, and certainly where one swore to another person, as in Yoreh Deah 218:1 in the gloss.
One who swore. We follow his intention, as above. See the Shach, seif katan 28.
One who swore to pay... See the Shach and Sema in seif 7 in the gloss. The Yerushalmi similarly distinguishes, regarding things technically permitted that others have accepted as prohibited under the rule of "he shall not profane his word," between one who knew the matter was permitted and adopted a stringency and one who acted because he mistakenly thought it was forbidden. The Rosh cites this in the fourth chapter of Pesachim.
