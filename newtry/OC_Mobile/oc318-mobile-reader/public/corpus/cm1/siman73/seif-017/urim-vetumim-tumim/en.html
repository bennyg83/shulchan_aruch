And if he does not know how much it was worth, and so forth. Sema proves that the case is where the creditor does not know whether the collateral was worth more than the amount he received for it. If he admitted that it was certainly worth more but did not know how much, he would be obligated to swear and, being unable to swear the amount, would have to pay. Sema therefore asks: if the creditor does not know whether he owes anything additional at all, why should the borrower swear and collect? Bach answers that Chazal applied the enactment for one who was robbed to a deposit case of this kind, because the creditor sold another person's property without a proper appraisal. Shach objects that if this were enough to classify him like a robber, then even where the creditor says definitely that the object was worth no more than the sale price, the borrower should always swear and collect. That objection is not decisive. Selling another person's property without formal appraisal does not automatically make a person a robber if he made a reasonable mistake, as Rivash explains. But where the creditor admits that the object may have been worth more and nevertheless sold it cheaply without carefully determining its value, his conduct is materially different. He exposed another person's property to loss through his own carelessness. That is close to direct destruction and justifies the special oath of the injured owner. Thus the borrower can swear and collect in this uncertain-value case. The statement that the creditor "does not want to swear" can simply mean he reverses the oath onto the borrower, who then swears and collects. There is another way to justify the result. In Perek HaMafkid Chazal transferred the oath from the borrower to the lender because of the danger that the borrower would swear about the collateral and the lender would later produce it, causing desecration of Hashem's Name. That concern is especially serious where the lender knows exactly to whom he sold the object and can readily retrieve it. If the lender directly denies the borrower's valuation and claims definite knowledge, beit din has reason to fear that one of the parties is mistaken and that the object can later be produced. In such a case Chazal would be reluctant to create a new owner-favoring oath contrary to the ordinary allocation. But where the lender himself says he does not know the value, there is no corresponding definite contradiction. The borrower's oath can be treated as truthful unless proven otherwise, and the lender has less incentive to retrieve the object merely to embarrass him. If the lender refuses to swear although he could, we can also infer that he likely lacks definite knowledge. Otherwise he would normally swear rather than permit the borrower to collect. Accordingly, in this specific uncertainty case the borrower may take the oath and recover the loss.
