And the excess shall be a gift from now, this is an asmachta. The Sema, seif katan 42, writes that this is unlike the ruling stated immediately afterward. There, since the entire pledge is acquired by the creditor from now, there is no asmachta, whereas here he said “from now” only regarding the excess. In my humble opinion, this distinction is not sound. Once a stipulation taking effect from now removes the defect of asmachta, what difference is there whether it concerns all of the property or only part of it? Rather, the correct explanation appears to me as follows. The reason “from now” is effective in the next ruling must be that this wording makes the arrangement appear as an actual sale, as Tosafot in Bava Metzia 66b, the Rosh, and the other authorities explain. Their words clearly imply that where the arrangement does not resemble a sale but merely a self-imposed penalty, it remains an asmachta. For this reason, the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter HaZahav cites authorities in the name of the Ramban and Ran who distinguish as follows: the rule that a pledge is not subject to asmachta applies when the borrower says, “You shall have no claim against me and I shall have no claim against you; take the pledge in satisfaction of your debt.” In that case one may say that the creditor acquired it from the outset, for the transaction is in the nature of a sale. But if he says, “If I do not repay you by such-and-such a day, my pledge shall be yours and I shall still repay your debt,” this is not a sale but security and a penalty, and is therefore an asmachta and does not effect acquisition. The same applies here. Although he did not expressly say that he would still repay the debt, he told the creditor to collect the amount of the debt from the pledge and that the excess would be a gift. This is plainly a penalty and does not resemble a sale at all. So it appears to me.
And if he stipulated with him, etc. This too is deemed an asmachta. See what I write below in seif katan 50, that the Mechaber’s ruling requires study, for many authorities disagree. In my humble opinion their view is primary: with a pledge of movable property there is no asmachta even if he did not say, “Acquire it from now.”
And if he said to him, “Acquire it from now,” etc. The Sema writes that the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 72, in Maslat, and the Darkei Moshe in this siman present this ruling as a dispute with the preceding ruling. This is incorrect. The Beit Yosef did not formulate it as a dispute; only the Darkei Moshe did so. The Darkei Moshe writes: “Regarding your question whether asmachta applies to a pledge, etc., the Mordechai likewise wrote in a responsum at the end of the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. Yet in Mishpetei HaYosher it appears that asmachta does not apply to a pledge.” It is difficult that the Darkei Moshe appears to understand Mishpetei HaYosher as disputing the Mordechai, since the Mordechai himself, in the chapter Eizehu Neshech, writes in accordance with Mishpetei HaYosher; see there. In any event, only the Darkei Moshe casts it as a dispute. The Beit Yosef’s intention is as stated here, that the cases do not conflict. This is necessarily so, because the following ruling is compelled by the Gemara, by the Rosh at the end of Eizehu Neshech, and by all the authorities, as I explain in the next seif katan. Otherwise, the Rosh’s responsum concerning the excess becoming a gift would contradict the Gemara and his own ruling. Rather, the cases are certainly different, and I already explained their proper distinction above in seif katan 47.
There is an authority who says, etc. I am astonished that the Mechaber attributes this ruling to “an authority,” for in my humble opinion it is an explicit Gemara in the chapter Eizehu Neshech and is accepted by all the authorities. The Mishna there teaches: one lent money against the borrower’s field and stipulated, “If you do not pay me within three years, it shall be mine,” and this was the practice of Beitus ben Zonin with the approval of the Sages. The Gemara explains that Rav Nachman said in the name of Minyomi that an asmachta does not acquire, and answers the Mishna either according to Rabbi Yose, who holds that asmachta acquires, or where the borrower said, “Acquire it from now.” Thus, when he says “from now,” the acquisition is effective according to all opinions. All the authorities rule accordingly, as do the Tur and Mechaber below, siman 207, seif 9. Tosafot, the Rosh, and the other authorities explain that although “from now” alone is generally insufficient, as stated below in siman 207, seif 14, here it is effective because the original mortgage resembles a sale: the borrower transferred the land to secure the money, and the lender benefited him by extending the loan. Therefore, when he stipulated that failure to pay by the fixed date would transfer the land, he intended to uphold the condition and did not merely exaggerate. Tosafot and the Rosh expressly indicate that this is certainly true of a pledge of movable property. Possibly the Mechaber used the phrase “there is an authority” because the Mordechai in Eizehu Neshech and the Hagahot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Mechira chapter 11, write concerning this ruling that Rashi’s responsum appears to disagree. Yet it is difficult to say that Rashi could dispute an explicit Gemara. Rashi’s responsum must therefore concern a case without the words “from now.” Indeed, it states only that the pledge would remain with the lender until a specified date and could no longer be redeemed, without saying “from now.” The Beit Yosef himself, below in siman 207, seif 19, and the Bach in siman 72, seif 4, explain Rashi in that manner. There is some room for hesitation. The Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot first prove from Nedarim 27b that ordinarily “from now” alone is insufficient, because the Gemara rules that an asmachta acquires only when a kinyan was made before an eminent beit din. A standard kinyan sudar necessarily operates from now, since otherwise the cloth has already returned to its owner, yet even so the Gemara requires an eminent beit din. Many authorities derive from this that in the Nedarim case “from now” does not suffice. Rashi likewise says in his responsum that an asmachta does not acquire unless possession was taken and a kinyan was made before an eminent beit din, as stated in Nedarim. Since every kinyan sudar is from now, and Rashi was discussing a pledge, his words might imply that even with a pledge “from now” is ineffective. This, in my humble opinion, is what the Mordechai and Hagahot Maimoniyot meant when they said that Rashi disagrees, and the Beit Yosef and Bach did not address this proof. Nevertheless, it appears clear to me that Rashi did not mean specifically that a kinyan before an eminent beit din is indispensable. The same result follows from an express “from now.” When Rashi mentioned a kinyan before a beit din, he meant an ordinary kinyan without express “from now,” in accordance with the Ran in Nedarim 27b. The Ran asks why an eminent beit din is needed if a kinyan was made, since every kinyan is from now. He answers that an unconditional transfer of a field with a kinyan operates immediately, but when one says, “If I do not come,” the words imply acquisition only after the stated date. At that time the sudar is no longer present, so no acquisition occurs. An ordinary kinyan before an eminent beit din suffices because the beit din has power to remove property and makes it equivalent to “from now.” This is also the correct explanation in my opinion. Even were one to say that Rashi truly disagrees, one would have to hold that Rashi understood the Nedarim passage as disputing the passage at the beginning of Eizehu Neshech and that no asmachta acquires except by a kinyan before an eminent beit din, following the explicit halachic ruling in Nedarim. Even then the Mechaber remains difficult. He himself rules without qualification in siman 207, seif 9, regarding a mortgage of land, that “from now” is effective, in accordance with Eizehu Neshech and all the authorities, without citing a dissenting view. If it is effective for land, it is certainly effective for movable property. Why, then, did he write the present rule merely as “there is an authority”? This requires study. Furthermore, the Mechaber’s unqualified ruling here that a pledge is not acquired without “from now” also requires study. Many authorities disagree, and their view appears primary. The Tur, seif 26, cites a responsum of the Rosh that one who lends against a pledge on condition that it become absolutely the creditor’s if it is not redeemed by a certain date has made an asmachta and the borrower may redeem it whenever he wishes. The responsum appears at the end of klal 108, siman 27. The questioner noted that the Ramban ruled that the creditor acquires, based on the Tosefta: one who lends against a pledge and is told, “If I do not pay you by such-and-such a day, I have nothing in your hand,” acquires the pledge when the date arrives, whether its value is less or greater. Maharam of Rothenburg, however, held that asmachta applies to a pledge, based on the laws of earnest money, the case of “collect from this wine,” and the case of one who deposits his document with a third party. The Baal HaItur cited all these sources without reconciling them. The Rosh answered that the Ramban’s reason, that a creditor acquires a movable pledge from now under Rabbi Yitzchak’s rule, is unconvincing because Rabbi Yitzchak spoke only of a pledge taken after the loan. Even were his rule to apply at the time of the loan, the creditor does not acquire the object as his own, but only becomes a paid custodian over it, as Rabbeinu Yitzchak explained. The condition that it become his is therefore an asmachta. The Tosefta may follow Rabbi Yose, who holds asmachta acquires, or its statement that “the pledge has reached him” may mean only that the lender cannot retract from having accepted it as payment, while as against the borrower it remains an asmachta. In truth, I do not understand the words of our master the Rosh. The Ramban’s proof from the Tosefta is clear. The suggestion that it follows Rabbi Yose is impossible for anyone who examines the first chapter of the Tosefta Bava Metzia. There the dispute of Rabbi Yehuda and Rabbi Yose is expressly brought regarding one who mortgages a house or field on such a condition: Rabbi Yose says the creditor acquires, while Rabbi Yehuda says, “How can this person acquire something that is not his?” Immediately afterward the Tosefta gives the rule of a loan against a movable pledge and Rabbi Yehuda does not disagree. Plainly, Rabbi Yehuda concedes in that case. This also refutes the Rosh’s second answer, for the Tosefta indicates that Rabbi Yehuda agrees that the creditor acquires what is already in his hand. The second answer is independently difficult. How can it refer to the lender’s inability to retract? The lender made no condition. The borrower said, “If I do not pay you by that day, I have nothing in your hand.” Why should that declaration cause the lender to lose the balance of his debt? Moreover, the Tosefta’s language, “the date arrived and he did not pay, the pledge has reached him, whether inferior or superior,” plainly means that the lender acquires it. The Tosefta is therefore a clear proof for the Ramban. Nor do the proofs cited in the name of Maharam establish otherwise. Earnest money is no proof, as the Nimukei Yosef in HaZahav explains and as is implied by the Maggid Mishneh to Hilchot Mechira chapter 11. The deposited document is also no proof, because the document is not in the lender’s own hand and is not itself a pledge whose substance he acquires; it is merely evidence. The case of “collect from this wine” is likewise no proof. The plain sense of Bava Metzia 66a is that the wine remained in the borrower’s possession. Rav bar Shava owed money to Rav Kahana and said, “If I do not repay you by a certain day, collect from this wine.” Rav Pappa initially thought that although asmachta does not acquire land, which ordinarily remains in its owner’s possession, wine intended for sale is like money. Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua answered in the name of Rava that every conditional “if” does not acquire. The wording indicates that the wine remained with Rav bar Shava, since it says merely that Rav Kahana was owed money and that he should “collect from this wine,” not that the money was lent against the wine or that the borrower renounced all claim to it. Rav Huna therefore ruled only that where the lender does not physically hold the movable property, the conditional transfer is ineffective. A movable pledge actually delivered into the creditor’s possession is acquired, as the Tosefta states. This also appears to be the Baal HaItur’s view. He cites the episode of Rav bar Shava and immediately afterward cites the Tosefta as practical law. Elsewhere he writes that because the pledge was given on that understanding it is as though the borrower said “from now,” citing the Tosefta concerning a loan against a pledge. The Semag likewise states explicitly that “acquire from now” removes asmachta, citing Eizehu Neshech, and then distinguishes an asmachta resembling a sale. He cites the Tosefta’s rule concerning movable pledges and notes that only a house or field is subject there to the dispute of Rabbi Yose and Rabbi Yehuda. His juxtaposition of the Rav bar Shava case shows that he understood it as I have explained. The Maggid Mishneh, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechira, also records that although the Ramban agreed that asmachta can apply even to property in the promisee’s possession, he ruled that one who lends against a movable pledge and is told, “If I do not pay by such-and-such a day, it shall be yours,” acquires it, as expressly stated in the Tosefta. The Nimukei Yosef in HaZahav similarly writes that land requires “from now,” but movable property is not subject to asmachta in this setting. He cites the Tosefta and notes that Rabbi Yehuda does not dispute it. He adds the Ramban’s distinction: this applies where the borrower says, “You shall have no claim against me and I shall have no claim against you; let your debt be discharged through the pledge,” so that the arrangement is a sale from the outset. If he says, “The pledge shall be yours and I shall still repay the debt,” it is only earnest money and a penalty, and is an asmachta. The Ran, students of the Rashba, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Hagahot Ashri likewise support this distinction. Hagahot Ashri further cites the view of Rabbeinu Yitzchak HaLavan that delivery of a movable pledge to the lender effects acquisition even without “from now,” though the gloss asks for proof and raises the Rav bar Shava case. The necessary proof is the Tosefta, and the Rav bar Shava case is no objection because the wine was not delivered. It was merely the particular event before the Gemara and therefore no distinction had to be stated expressly. The Beit Yosef in siman 207 writes that the uncertainty in the Hagahot concerns only Rabbeinu Tam’s approach, while according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak it is clear that the lender does not acquire. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Rabbeinu Yitzchak and Rabbeinu Tam dispute the reason that a mortgage of land is effective, but both speak only of land. Rabbeinu Yitzchak may well concede that actual possession of movable property is different. This is indicated by the Semag and by Tosafot in Bava Metzia 66a, who explain according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak that possession of land there was not equivalent to the possession of movable property in another case. Possession of movables is stronger. It is therefore reasonable that even according to Rabbeinu Yitzchak a loan against a movable pledge is not an asmachta, as the Tosefta proves. Finally, the Rosh’s objection that Rabbi Yitzchak’s rule applies only to a pledge taken after the loan does not refute the Ramban’s conclusion. Even if the creditor does not acquire the pledge in the technical sense, he has a lien upon it and it is physically in his possession. The conditional transfer is therefore not regarded as an asmachta, because the borrower resolves fully to transfer it. The students of the Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 207, state expressly that even according to the view limiting Rabbi Yitzchak to a pledge taken after the loan, the Tosefta may concern a pledge delivered at the time of the loan. Since the loan was made upon it, every condition made regarding it is equivalent to “from now,” though this is not true of land. This is the primary ruling.
