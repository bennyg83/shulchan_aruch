Mekor Chaim, Laws of Pesach, siman 441, seif katan 1, questions a case where the borrower stipulated, “From now, the collateral shall be yours if I do not pay by a certain date,” and by that date the collateral had fallen in value to less than half the debt. May the creditor nevertheless demand and compel payment, or does the collateral become absolutely his against his will, extinguishing the debt? Mekor Chaim concludes that the creditor may compel payment, because the borrower’s condition was intended as an obligation and penalty to induce payment, not to cancel the loan entirely. Responsa Bigdei Yesha, siman 5, disagrees, rejects his proofs, and concludes that the creditor cannot compel payment and the collateral becomes his absolutely. See there. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 124, discusses Reuven, who pledged an item to Shimon and stipulated in a valid, non-asmachta form that if it was not redeemed by a fixed date it would become Shimon’s retroactively. When the date arrived the creditor was away. Seeing that Shimon was absent, Reuven too traveled away. When both later returned, Reuven sought to redeem the collateral and Shimon argued that the date had passed and he had acquired it, since Reuven should have deposited the money with beit din. The responsum rules for Reuven, because when the creditor was not present at the due date the obligation tied to that date did not take effect. However, if the creditor left the collateral at home with his wife or children in a manner enabling the borrower to redeem it and informed him of this, then the borrower remained obligated to redeem it on time, and failure to do so causes the collateral to become the creditor’s. See there.
