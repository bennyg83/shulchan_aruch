One who holds a deed, etc. The Sema writes that the rule specifically concerns a deed, unlike an oral claim, because the borrower can take a heset oath and be exempt. It follows that even an unverified deed justifies restraining the money until the deed is authenticated, after which the creditor collects upon oath. This is also indicated by the Rosh. Because these laws depend upon local custom and judicial assessment, I have been brief.
Because the borrower is dissipating his assets, etc. The rule applies specifically where he is actively wasting them. If he is not dissipating assets, then even though his condition declines daily and he will be unable to pay at maturity, the creditor may not attach property before the due date, even movables. Maharshal so rules. His proof from a ten-year loan reaching the Sabbatical year is not decisive, because the lender knew in advance that Shemittah would intervene and accepted that risk. Nevertheless, the ruling appears correct. Several early authorities dispute the entire remedy of pre-maturity attachment, and their reading of the Gemara appears primary. Although later authorities and prevailing practice follow the Mechaber, the innovation should not be extended beyond its precise case. The many responsa cited discuss further rules of attachment, but because these matters depend upon local custom and judicial assessment I have not elaborated.
