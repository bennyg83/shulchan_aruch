The oath remains in force. Sema writes that if a person wrote in his own handwriting that he swore, he can later say, "I never swore." That is correct where he provides a plausible explanation for why he wrote it. Without such an explanation, he is not believed, even if the opposing party admits that the oath was not made in his presence. Perhaps he swore outside that person's presence and has rendered the matter prohibited upon himself. See Shach, Yoreh Deah 232. If the opposing party contradicts him and says he truly did swear, the Shach there is uncertain whether he is believed to retract. Rama in Yoreh Deah appears to imply that with a plausible explanation he is nevertheless believed. Sema further writes that if the document says explicitly that the oath was actually taken, he can no longer say, "I did not swear." Taz holds that with a plausible explanation he is still believed, and this appears correct, especially today when legal forms routinely contain standardized oath language. Certainly one can say that no actual oath occurred despite the boilerplate. The same may be true where witnesses merely wrote that he acknowledged having actually sworn. According to Taz, a plausible explanation may explain that acknowledgment as well. See what I wrote in Yoreh Deah 187, that where a person made an admission before others, perhaps a later explanation does not help. But if the witnesses themselves testify that he actually swore in their presence, everyone agrees he cannot say, "I did not swear." If he was coerced by governmental authority, see Yoreh Deah 232. He can be exempt even though financial coercion does not ordinarily excuse an oath, because here we effectively witness that he never intended the oath under such circumstances.
