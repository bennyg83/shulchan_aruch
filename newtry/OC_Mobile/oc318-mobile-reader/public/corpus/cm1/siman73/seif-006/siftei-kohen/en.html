One who swore to his fellow to repay him, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 18, and what I wrote concerning it in Yoreh Deah siman 232, seif katan 26, including my addenda there. See also the Rama’s responsum siman 108 and Mishpetei Shmuel siman 129.
The oath nevertheless remains in force. The date was imposed to urge prompt payment, while the essence of the oath concerns repayment itself. Although he did not violate the oath when no one demanded payment from him, he remains bound by the oath to pay immediately when demanded, just as a loan does not disappear merely because its due date passed. The Bach infers that one who swore to pay his fellow by a fixed date does not violate the oath merely because the date passed without demand, even if the lender was in town, but he remains obligated by the oath to pay whenever demanded. This is more directly supported by the Rosh’s own words, that he did not violate the oath because no one demanded payment. The Sema likewise understands the Rosh this way. See also Responsa Maharash Sasson, siman 230.
And see Yoreh Deah siman 228, etc. The Beit Yosef cites the Rivash in the name of the Rashba: one who swore to repay by a fixed date, where the lender himself owed money to a third party and the local ruler seized from the borrower the amount due until the lender paid that third party, is considered under compulsion because he may not violate the ruler’s command. This must mean that circumstances show he never swore with such a case in mind. Otherwise, monetary compulsion does not nullify an oath, as the Rivash states and as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 232.
