One who swears to repay his fellow. Maharam of Trani, part 1, responsum 51, writes that a debt instrument containing an oath to repay by a particular time does not create a binding oath of expression as to the underlying repayment, because whenever he repays he is merely fulfilling an existing mitzvah. Knesset HaGedolah in Yoreh Deah 237 reports that Maharam's son challenged this from the responsum printed at the end of Chazeh HaTenufah, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 97, which says that although ordinary debt collection leaves the debtor his basic necessities, if he swore to repay, he must fulfill the oath and no such arrangement protects him. If an oath to repay never takes effect because repayment is already a mitzvah, why must he honor it? The challenge is not decisive. Once the debtor later lacks the means to pay, the ordinary mitzvah of repayment requires only payment according to the debtor protection arrangement. Beyond that amount, his only obligation can arise from the oath. In such a changed circumstance, the oath can now operate, just as a second oath can become operative if the first oath that had blocked it is later removed. Thus the responsum can be reconciled even if the oath did not initially add anything to the basic duty. Knesset HaGedolah also says that Maharam of Trani merely follows the Rosh's responsum that an oath not to pay a debt is an oath to nullify a mitzvah. But these are not the same. An oath to nullify a mitzvah does not take effect. An oath to fulfill a mitzvah can have legal force in certain respects. Baal HaMaor in Perek Shevuot Shetayim explicitly writes that an oath to fulfill a positive commandment can generate lashes for violation even though it does not generate a sacrifice, because the verse concerning sacrifice excludes only matters equally capable of good or evil. Ramban disagrees and says it creates neither sacrifice nor lashes. Ran nevertheless appears to follow Baal HaMaor concerning lashes. Even according to Ramban, however, there must remain some prohibition in breaking such an oath. Scripture praises David's statement, "I have sworn and I will fulfill it," and the Gemara derives that one may swear to fulfill a mitzvah in order to strengthen oneself. If the oath added absolutely no prohibition, it would provide no strengthening at all. Rather, the oath carries a prohibition of false swearing or violating one's word, though according to Ramban it may be excluded from lashes and sacrifice because it is not equally framed as positive and negative voluntary conduct. The Tur in Yoreh Deah siman 236 makes this explicit. He lists matters where even if one violates the oath there is no sacrifice, including an oath to fulfill a mitzvah, and then says that nevertheless wrongdoing exists whenever one lies in his oath. Thus even an oath to fulfill a mitzvah creates a real prohibition. Therefore Chazeh HaTenufah is entirely understandable. One who swears to repay a debt incurs an additional oath prohibition if he fails to do so, even though the underlying repayment is already a mitzvah. The Rosh's responsum concerns the opposite case, an oath not to pay, which is an oath to nullify the mitzvah and is void. There is no contradiction. It is surprising that the great compiler did not cite Baal HaMaor and Ran on this point, especially since even the stricter Ramban agrees that some oath prohibition remains. The law is therefore clear.
Even if he did not demand payment. The Sema writes that the stringency of the oath does not obligate the debtor to pay on his own initiative until the creditor demands payment. The Shach agrees. Taz disagrees and holds that the oath itself obligates the debtor to pay without a demand. The Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah siman 228 supports Taz. It rules that one who swore to repay by a certain time can be released from the oath if the creditor says, "I consider myself as though I received payment," but the creditor cannot merely extend the time while leaving the original oath obligation in place. If an oath required payment only upon demand, an extension would be no more than the creditor refraining from demanding payment. He could then demand later and the original oath would revive. The fact that an actual release is needed shows that the oath itself obligates payment at the appointed time even without demand. Taz's view is therefore more persuasive.
