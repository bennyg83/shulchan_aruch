The claimants can compel, and so forth. This ruling originates in Rashba responsum 1119. There the defendant was about to leave town but had land in the city that was subject to the debt. The creditors argued that the case must be heard before he left, because afterward beit din might hesitate to descend to the land in his absence. Rashba answered that the defendant should either appoint an authorized representative or be summoned properly, and if he failed to appear, beit din could collect from his property in his absence. Chazal enacted such collection so a person could not take another's money and escape overseas. Rashba did not clearly say that a person with no property in town can always be detained and forced to litigate before travel. Rama extends the principle by analogy. Rashba himself elsewhere holds that even a debtor wasting assets is not necessarily subject to pre-maturity attachment. We do not rule so broadly. Once we accept that beit din can restrain dissipation of assets to prevent the creditor from being left without practical recourse, departure to a distant country can present the same danger. Nimukei Yosef indeed equates leaving overseas with wasting assets in this respect. Still, the extension must be limited. If the debtor is merely moving to another nearby, orderly place where he remains readily subject to beit din and the creditor can litigate without extraordinary hardship, there is no basis to restrain him. The rule should apply where the departure genuinely creates the practical equivalent of asset dissipation: a distant location, serious difficulty of suit, instability, or other substantial impairment of collection. We should not extend the restraint beyond that.
