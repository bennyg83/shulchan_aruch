He is obligated to pay on the first day of Rosh Chodesh. Taz in Yoreh Deah siman 228 compares this to Rambam's rule regarding Adar. If a person knows the year has two Adars, an unspecified reference to Adar can be interpreted differently from a case where he did not know. Taz therefore says that if a debtor knows the month has two days of Rosh Chodesh, perhaps he does not mean the first. The analogy is not exact. Human speech distinguishes "Adar I" and "Adar II" in an intercalated year. But when Rosh Chodesh has two days, ordinary speech still calls both days simply Rosh Chodesh. The Gemara's rules for vows concerning "this month" likewise do not create a knowledge-based distinction of the kind Taz proposes. Rambam's wording in Hilchot Nedarim requires explanation. He says one who vows from "this month" is permitted on Rosh Chodesh even if the month was deficient. I understand Rambam as follows. If the current month is full, the first day called Rosh Chodesh is technically day thirty of the outgoing month. In that ordinary case, one might have thought it remains included in "this month." Rambam teaches that the social calendrical designation of Rosh Chodesh can control. Even where the court makes the month deficient and the day thereby belongs fully to the next month, we do not say the person's hidden intent was to prohibit a full thirty-day span. Matters of undisclosed intent do not override ordinary language. Accordingly, in the present debt case the wording remains genuinely ambiguous enough that the stricter day should govern for an oath. Rashba is therefore correct that, because of the prohibition doubt, payment should be made on the first Rosh Chodesh day.
