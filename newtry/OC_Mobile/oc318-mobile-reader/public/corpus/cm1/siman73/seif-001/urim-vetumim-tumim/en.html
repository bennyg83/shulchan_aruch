He swears hesset, and so forth. This requires study. The difference between payment immediately and payment only after ten days is a real monetary difference. Otherwise the disagreement would not even justify hesset, and certainly one witness could not generate a Torah oath. The Mishnah at the beginning of Makkot proves this. False witnesses who testify that a debt is due in thirty days when it is really due only after ten years pay the measurable market value of that acceleration. Thus time has monetary value. Suppose the claimant demands one hundred zuz now, and the defendant admits the hundred but says payment is not due for five years. If the present market value of the delayed hundred is only ninety-eight, then economically the defendant appears to admit ninety-eight of a hundred and deny two. Why is this not an ordinary partial admission? Mahari T in Choshen Mishpat responsum 105 answers that the principal debt is established by witnesses and the dispute concerns only maturity. Then the principal admission does not count as the defendant's partial admission. But that is not implied by the general wording of the poskim. His other suggestion, that the admission is not of the same type as the claim, is also strained. A more plausible explanation is that the monetary value of the timing difference is not a sum defined by the parties' pleadings themselves. It requires a beit din valuation of how much present money is worth relative to deferred money. A partial-admission Torah oath requires a claim and admission stated in measurable, countable monetary terms. Here the denied amount emerges only from subsequent appraisal. That may be why only hesset applies. Even so, the matter is not entirely settled.
