Seif 2. One who lends to another and fixes a time for repayment. I was uncertain about a lender who claims that an entire maneh is due today, while the borrower admits that fifty is due today but says the remaining fifty is not due for another ten days. Is this a partial admission requiring a Torah oath, or is the borrower believed through a migo that he could have said the entire maneh is not due for another ten days? The issue initially appears to depend on the dispute between the Taz and the other judges in a related case. Where a defendant admits fifty is owed in ten days but completely denies the other fifty, those judges hold that this is not a partial admission because he has a migo that he could deny everything, and saying that the admitted fifty is not yet due is itself a form of brazenness. On their approach, our case would certainly be a partial admission, because the alternative claim that the entire maneh is not yet due is a more brazen denial and cannot serve as a migo. The Taz, however, holds in the related case that saying the admitted amount is not yet due is not such brazenness. That might suggest that here the borrower has a valid migo to say the entire maneh is due only in ten days. On careful analysis, however, even the Taz should agree that our case is a partial admission. His point is only that postponing the due date is not as brazen as denying the debt altogether, so a person who admits the debt but says it is not yet due cannot necessarily be believed through a migo that he could have denied everything. In our case, by contrast, once he admits that fifty is presently due, saying that the other fifty is not yet due is itself a denial of current monetary liability. The alternative claim that the entire sum is not yet due is therefore a migo involving greater brazenness. The ordinary rationale that a partial admitter is temporarily evading payment also applies. Thus the case should be treated as a partial admission according to all views. Seif 2: one who lends to another and fixes a term. The Sema, Knesset HaGedolah, and Shach cite Shiltei Giborim that those who lend money for shopkeeping arrangements cannot retract before the fixed term. This appears difficult in light of Yoreh Deah 177, which rules that one who receives an iska can retract during the term because he is comparable to a worker. Shiltei Giborim must also be discussing an iska, for otherwise taking profit would involve interest. The distinction is that a worker is paid for each unit of time, whereas a contractor accepts responsibility for a defined result and cannot retract merely because a time frame is known. Choshen Mishpat 333 makes this distinction, as does the Terumat HaDeshen cited by the Shach. Yoreh Deah concerns an ordinary iska in which the recipient undertakes to trade throughout the term and divide whatever profit is earned. Each day’s continued work produces that day’s share of profit, so his compensation is effectively time-based and he resembles a worker. Shiltei Giborim, however, speaks of an arrangement in which the recipient borrows capital to lend at interest to non-Jews and promises the investor a fixed amount of profit, retaining whatever excess he earns. His wording, that the borrower must pay the lender the profit “according to the condition between them,” indicates a fixed promised return rather than one half of whatever profit actually occurs. If during the term he has already earned the entire amount promised to the investor, he can of course pay it early. His undertaking is therefore comparable to a contractor who agreed to produce a fixed quantity of work by a deadline, not a worker hired day by day. A contractor cannot retract during the term. Under the common arrangement of our own time, the same rule should apply.
