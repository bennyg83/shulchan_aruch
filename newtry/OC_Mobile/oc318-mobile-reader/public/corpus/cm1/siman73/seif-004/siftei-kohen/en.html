Or today is the final day of the term fixed for him, etc. This refers to a time more than thirty days after the loan, or to a deed that does not state the date of the loan and the lender claims that thirty days have elapsed. An ordinary loan is for thirty days. Even if the lender claims that they expressly agreed upon earlier payment, it appears that the borrower may take a heset oath and pay only after thirty days from the date written in the deed.
The lender shall take a heset oath, etc. This follows the Bedek HaBayit and Kesef Mishneh, which explain the Rambam's reference to a heset oath on the ground that the parties dispute only the due date, not the amount. The Sema and Bach follow this approach. In my view it is strained. The reason a lender holding a pledge must take an oath while holding a sacred object is either because his claim is not directed to the body of the pledge or because a migo does not exempt him from an oath. Those reasons apply equally here. The Tur deliberately wrote merely that the creditor must swear, whereas the Rambam specifically wrote heset. The Rambam was discussing a loan evidenced by a deed, and there the lender takes only a heset oath because the borrower's allegation concerns a fixed term, not a claim for which the deed ordinarily stands, such as payment. Other defenses such as accommodation or interest likewise do not require the creditor to swear while holding a sacred object; he collects and afterward takes a heset oath. The Tur expanded the rule to include a loan upon a pledge and therefore omitted the word heset. Each case follows its own law: on a pledge the lender must swear while holding a sacred object, because the claim is not to the pledge itself; on a deed he takes only a heset oath, because the allegation is not one for which a deed ordinarily stands. This appears clear.
