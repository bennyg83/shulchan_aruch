Seif 17. One who lends another money against collateral, etc., and the arrangement constitutes asmachta. See the authorities cited by the Shach who hold that the law of asmachta does not apply to movable collateral. This appears difficult from Gittin 43b. The Gemara distinguishes cases based upon whether the appointed time has arrived. According to these authorities, however, one could answer differently. The Rosh writes in the first chapter of Bava Kamma that for rabbinic matters a slave is treated like movable property, and not only for maintaining possession but even where rabbinic law permits extracting money. This is reflected in Choshen Mishpat 202 and the Sema, where slaves can be acquired together with land by agav even though that mode of acquisition in that context is rabbinic. If so, the penalty against one who sells his slave to a non-Jew is itself rabbinic. For that rabbinic law the slave should be treated as movable property, and if asmachta does not apply to movable collateral the transfer should be effective as a sale. By contrast, land in the laws of maaser remains actual land and asmachta would apply, accounting for the different result. This Gemara therefore appears to support the Raavad, cited in siman 441, that asmachta does not apply in dealings with a non-Jew, whether a Jew undertakes toward the non-Jew or receives the undertaking from him. Those who hold that asmachta does apply in dealings with a non-Jew can answer by holding that asmachta also applies to movable collateral. But authorities who combine the positions that there is no asmachta in movable collateral yet there is asmachta with a non-Jew face a difficulty from this Gemara. The matter requires study.
