Shach asks why the oath does not take effect through kolel, just as one who swears generally not to eat matzah can become prohibited even on the first night of Pesach. Shach answers that in the matzah case the mitzvah obligation had not yet arrived when the oath was made, while here the debt already existed and the mitzvah to repay already rested upon the borrower. Chacham Tzvi notes that Tosafot and most authorities do not use that distinction in the parallel discussion of one who swears to eat both forbidden and permitted food. If a prior Torah obligation alone defeated kolel, the sugya would have been much simpler. Still, the distinction appears in earlier authorities and can represent Rashi's approach. The Nazir sugya is instructive. Rashi explains that the verse requiring a nazir to abstain even from mitzvah wine cannot simply mean kiddush or havdalah, because one is already sworn from Sinai to fulfill those mitzvot. The Gemara instead speaks of a person who first swore to drink wine and then accepted nezirut. Tosafot ask: why should nezirut override that oath? One way to understand Rashi is that nezirut contains a genuine kolel. The person's oath required only some quantity of wine, while nezirut prohibits all wine. The new prohibition has broader scope. But that only works where the first obligation has not yet become immediately operative. If kiddush time has already arrived, there may be no open field on which the kolel can first take hold. This can explain Shach's distinction. Yet many Tosafot reject such reasoning and define kolel differently. According to R. Yitzchak bar Shmuel, the central distinction is whether the oath explicitly names the mitzvah object. If one says "I will not eat matzah on Pesach night," the oath directly targets the mitzvah and does not take effect. If one says only "I will not eat matzah," the general formulation can include the mitzvah night. Rosh appears to follow this approach in his responsa. Accordingly, if a person swears generally not to pay creditors, including both existing and future creditors, Shach's kolel question is real: the oath can take effect regarding future debts and perhaps thereby include existing ones. Chacham Tzvi is therefore correct that one cannot dismiss the issue by saying the old debts already exist. Still, there is another distinction. Kolel works where the oath can immediately take effect on permitted matters and thereby extend to prohibited ones. Future creditors do not yet exist. At the moment of the oath there is no actual permitted debt-obligation on which the oath can take effect. Its supposed permitted field is still only potential. A later loan cannot retroactively create a kolel that was absent when the oath was uttered. Therefore the oath does not take effect against existing creditors merely because it might later apply to future debts. There is, however, a real kolel if the person swears not to repay either Jewish or gentile creditors, and under the relevant view withholding a gentile loan is not subject to the same mitzvah obligation. Then the oath has an immediate permitted field. Rosh's own approach may still reject even that if the wording expressly targets repayment of debts and thereby directly mentions the mitzvah category. The matter therefore depends on the underlying theory of kolel, but Rama's ruling can be sustained through the absence of an immediate permitted object at the time of the oath.
