With an ordinary deposit, even if the object consists of money, the language of forgiveness does not naturally apply because the specific deposited property remains the depositor's. Shach says unrestricted deposited money is different. Since the custodian may use it, and Tosafot say he need not necessarily return the identical coins even before actual use, it is effectively a loan and can be forgiven. Shach says similarly above in siman 47. Chacham Tzvi attacks this. Tosafot's statement appears in the dispute between Rav Nachman and Rav Huna concerning unrestricted money deposited with a moneychanger. Rav Huna makes the custodian liable even for unavoidable accident, while Rav Nachman does not. Tosafot explain Rav Huna by saying that unlike a borrowed utensil, the money need not be returned in the identical coins once the custodian has the right to use it. Chacham Tzvi argues that this reasoning belongs only to Rav Huna. According to Rav Nachman, whom we follow, the custodian is only a paid custodian until actual use and therefore must return the same coins if the depositor demands them before use. Maharam in Mordechai says this explicitly. This is a serious objection to Shach. At first one might say the issue is disputed because found money can make a custodian liable like a borrower even before use, and perhaps increased permission to use is enough to separate the owner's claim from the identical coins. But that does not prove unrestricted private deposit money works the same way under Rav Nachman. The better analysis is that liability and ownership must be separated. If the custodian expressly manifests that he wishes to treat the money as a loan and use it, then everyone should agree he becomes an actual borrower of the funds and owes equivalent money even before spending the identical coins. A loan becomes the borrower's monetary responsibility upon receipt. The dispute concerns a custodian who has permission to use but has not indicated any desire to exercise it. Rav Huna treats the use privilege as sufficiently valuable to impose borrower-like accident liability and therefore presumes a stronger appropriation. Rav Nachman does not. The custodian may prefer not to assume accident liability and may intend to continue holding the same coins as a deposit. Thus under Rav Nachman, until the custodian actually uses the money or otherwise manifests his adoption of it as a loan, the depositor can demand the identical coins. This also explains found money. There Chazal can deliberately structure the use privilege to benefit the lost owner by imposing greater responsibility, so the relationship can become more loan-like. An ordinary private deposit does not necessarily have that structure. Accordingly, absent an affirmative act or manifestation of borrowing, unrestricted deposit money should remain the depositor's property and ordinary language of forgiveness is not the natural mechanism. Maharam and Chacham Tzvi's position is therefore stronger in practical halachah.
