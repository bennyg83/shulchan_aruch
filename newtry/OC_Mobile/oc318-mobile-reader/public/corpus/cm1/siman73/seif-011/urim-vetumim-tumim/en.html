And some say that even if he moved his residence, and so forth. Shach attacks Maharik's ruling because an oath takes effect only according to what was expressed verbally. If the debtor swore to pay in one city, why should he be obligated after the creditor relocates elsewhere? Maharik's proof from Rosh is strong. Rosh says that if a debtor swears to pay by a particular date and the date passes, the oath does not simply disappear. The essential oath was to pay; the date was added to accelerate and clarify performance. If a person swears, "I will eat this loaf on Tuesday," failure to eat on Tuesday ordinarily ends the possibility of fulfilling that particular oath. Yet Rosh treats repayment differently because payment is the substantive object and time is secondary. The same can apply to place. If the debtor specifies a place only to identify where payment should conveniently occur and to prevent the creditor from forcing him on a distant journey, the essential oath remains payment. If the creditor later moves and there is no genuine insistence on the original location, the debtor cannot use the location phrase to escape the underlying oath. However, there is an important qualification. Suppose at the time of the oath the creditor lived in the debtor's city, but the oath named a different city to which the creditor later moved. If the creditor then abandons that city and relocates elsewhere, the debtor should not be required to chase him to a third location merely because the oath mentioned payment. Without the special place clause, ordinary law may not have required the debtor to transport payment at all. Thus when place supplies a new burden that would not otherwise exist, the oath cannot be stretched beyond the place actually named. Where the creditor originally lived at the named place, however, deleting the place phrase leaves the ordinary duty to deliver to the creditor, and relocation does not necessarily erase the oath.
And one should not object that if payment itself is the essence of the oath, repayment of a creditor is already a mitzvah and an oath to fulfill a mitzvah should add nothing. First, even an oath to fulfill a mitzvah can carry consequences of shevuat shav or reinforcement according to Rambam and Ran. Second, below in siman 97 there are assets that ordinary creditor collection would leave for the debtor under the laws of arrangement, while an oath may obligate him more broadly. The oath therefore has an immediate field of additional monetary effect. Through kolel it can reinforce the broader repayment duty. This also answers the difficulty raised by Mishneh LaMelech.
