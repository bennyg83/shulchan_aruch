A beit din of three ordinary persons. See Tumim. This suffices specifically for collateral, because a creditor acquires a possessory interest in collateral and it is therefore partly like his own property. Three ordinary people are sufficient merely to establish its value. For a deposit that must be sold, however, a beit din valid for monetary adjudication is required, meaning at least one judge who is learned and capable, because greater judicial authority is needed to remove another person's property rights.
And the sale is void, etc. Although regarding a widow who sold estate property without a beit din of ordinary persons the Rambam holds that her sale is valid after the fact, the Sages were more lenient with a widow because of the concern that women otherwise might be discouraged from marriage. Shach.
And if he does not know, etc. If the lender says that he knows the collateral was worth more but does not know how much more, he is obligated in an oath and, because he cannot swear to the amount, must pay. The borrower then collects without an oath. The present clause instead concerns a lender who does not know whether the collateral was worth any more than the sale price. Nevertheless he must pay according to the borrower's oath because the Sages applied here the enactment made for a robbed claimant. So writes the Bach. See Tumim for why the same enactment was not applied when the lender positively claims that the collateral was not worth more. I gave a reasoned explanation there.
Or he does not wish to swear. See Tumim. One can explain this through reversal of the oath. More likely, however, we presume that the lender truly does not know, for if he knew he would swear. He is merely acting strategically by saying that he knows but does not wish to swear. The enactment for a robbed claimant therefore remains in place, and the borrower swears and collects. All this is where it is known that the lender actually sold the collateral. If only the lender himself claims that he sold it, he must first swear that the item is not still in his possession, lest he have coveted it. Alternatively, the clause may concern an item readily replaceable in the market, where there is no concern that he coveted this specific item, as explained above in siman 72.
That he instructed him to sell it. Meaning to sell it for the very amount for which it was sold. In that case it makes no difference to the borrower whether the item was objectively sold cheaply or dearly, because the lender followed the borrower's instruction.
