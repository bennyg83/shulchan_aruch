Meaning, etc., and they shall appraise it, etc. The Rivash interprets the Rambam and Rosh to require sale under authorization of beit din and a formal appraisal. The Kesef Mishneh objects that an appraisal is pointless if no purchaser will pay the appraised amount. This objection is not sound. Beit din appraises according to the current market and the price at which an immediate buyer can be found. If only ninety can be obtained, they appraise it at ninety; if they appraise it at one hundred, it is because they know that a buyer at that price can be found. Any error is governed by the laws of mistaken judicial appraisal. Mere sale before witnesses cannot ensure that the creditor did not undersell, for witnesses cannot know whether a better buyer existed. A competent appraisal is therefore essential. Indeed, the Rambam himself requires knowledgeable appraisers for an widow's sale and for division among partners. There is, however, a further difficulty with the Rivash and later authorities, who allow three laymen. The Gemara's special leniency for a widow implies that an ordinary creditor requires an expert beit din. The Ran, Maggid Mishneh, Nimukei Yosef, Ritva, Tosafot, and Rashba all indicate that sale of a pledge or deposit requires a proper beit din, with at least one learned judge, together with appraisal. The Rosh's reference to three appraisers does not exclude a qualified beit din; it means that the beit din must arrange valuation by three appraisers, unless the judges themselves are competent appraisers. A sale without a qualified beit din is ineffective even after the fact and even if the price was fair. This is the principal practical ruling.

If he does not know how much it was worth, etc., the borrower shall swear, etc. The Sema explains that the lender does not admit that the pledge was worth more while merely lacking the amount, for then he would be one obligated to swear who cannot swear, and the borrower would recover without an oath. Rather, the lender is uncertain whether it was worth more at all. Although elsewhere such a lender takes a heset oath and is exempt even where the pledge was lost through negligence, here he actively and unauthorizedly sold another's pledge. I do not see why active negligence should differ. The Bach treats him like a robber and applies the enactment allowing the robbed party to swear and collect, comparing it to a bailee who says, "I do not know." This too is unpersuasive. Selling a pledge in one's lawful possession does not necessarily make one a robber, especially since a definite assertion of value would be accepted upon heset. Nor can one import the special enactment concerning deposits, which was made to promote careful custody. A lender already protects the pledge for the sake of his loan, and the enactment was not applied even when such a pledge was negligently lost. The better explanation is that the case is one where the lender does not know the value and declines to swear, either because he fears that uncertainty will make his oath false or because he refuses even a truthful oath. In that event the borrower swears and collects. Though the wording is somewhat strained, this appears the correct law.
The same applies if the lender says that the borrower instructed him to sell it for less than its value. This is explicit in Maharik.
