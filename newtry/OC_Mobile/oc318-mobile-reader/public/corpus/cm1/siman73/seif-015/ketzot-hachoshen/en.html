A beit din of three lay judges. The Shach argues at length against this ruling. His proof is from the widow who may sell estate property without an expert beit din, implying that ordinary debts require experts. Rivash's reason, however, is that the creditor is already in possession and acquires the collateral, as cited by Beit Yosef. For the same reason there is a dispute whether land collateral can be sold without expert judges, as the Tur discusses in siman 102. With land, Rabbi Yitzchak's acquisition does not apply. With movable collateral, however, the creditor acquires a real right under Rabbi Yitzchak, which greatly strengthens his position. The Shach also cites Tosafot in Bava Metzia and Pesachim for the need of an expert beit din in renting or disposing of collateral or deposits. But Tosafot there speaks of deposits, not collateral. A deposit is different because the holder has no acquired lien. Movable collateral is acquired under Rabbi Yitzchak. Likewise, the Rashba responsum cited by the Shach concerns a shop mortgage, which is land. Thus none of these sources contradict Rivash's ruling for movable collateral. Rivash's additional statement that even an ordinary deposit can be sold without expert judges remains difficult, because the widow source and Tosafot do imply greater formality for ordinary deposits. Perhaps Rivash distinguishes a deposit where the possessor has a stronger retained right, but this remains debatable. As to the Mechaber's practical ruling for movable collateral, however, I have found no clear opposing authority, because the creditor's acquired pledge status supplies the decisive difference.
