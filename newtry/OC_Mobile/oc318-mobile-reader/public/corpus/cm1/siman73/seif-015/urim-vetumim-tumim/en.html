Sema answers that the later case concerns collateral of a gentile in Jewish hands. Shach correctly rejects that limitation. Two simpler answers are possible. One is that without the custom the lender must first demand payment before sale, whereas the custom permits sale at the end of the year without an additional demand. Taz adds that ordinarily one might need to wait another thirty days after demand, while the established custom removes that waiting period. Both have merit. But Mordechai's source needs closer reading. R. Yitzchak bar Peretz ruled that a Jew who lends another Jew on collateral need not guard it beyond one year and may sell it under the king's law governing interest-bearing pledges. He applied this to books that were sold after a year and exempted the lender from liability. Shach is troubled because our own law already permits sale after thirty days. Why invoke gentile law to displace Torah law? He therefore says the special point was sale without prior demand. That interpretation leaves the heart of Mordechai unstated. A better reading is that R. Yitzchak bar Peretz follows Rabbenu Kalonymus, who understands the Mishnah as a dispute. Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel allows sale after thirty days through beit din, while the first Tanna does not allow forced sale at all, and halachah follows the first Tanna. Within that system, absent external custom the lender indeed cannot sell merely because time passed. R. Yitzchak bar Peretz then says the established law of the kingdom concerning pledged property creates a commercial custom under which even Jewish parties lend with the understanding that after a year the collateral may be sold. The borrower who sued over the sold books therefore lost because the original transaction was made on that commercial basis. Rama can derive a broader rule from this even though we do not follow Rabbenu Kalonymus on the underlying thirty-day law. If external commercial custom can add a one-year sale right where Jewish law otherwise grants none, then it can also limit an otherwise earlier sale right by showing that parties customarily contract on the understanding that collateral will remain for a year. The rule operates as implied agreement, not as gentile law simply overruling halachah. Therefore, where an established and lawful commercial custom fixes a one-year collateral period, the parties are presumed to have transacted on that basis unless they stipulated otherwise.
