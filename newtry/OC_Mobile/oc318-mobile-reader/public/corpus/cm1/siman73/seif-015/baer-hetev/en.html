Even according to Rambam and those authorities who rule regarding a widow who sold without beit din, or appraised property for herself, that the sale is valid after the fact, as stated in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 93 and the beginning of siman 103, one may say that this leniency applies specifically to a widow, because of the concern for her favor and remarriage, unlike the present case. Knows. The Sema writes that this does not mean that the creditor admits the pledge was worth more but does not know how much more, for then he would be one who is unable to swear and therefore must pay, as stated in siman 72, seif 12. Rather, he means: 'I do not know how much it was worth, and it may be that it was worth no more than the amount for which it was sold.' Although there it was explained that in such a case the creditor takes a hesset oath and is exempt even if the pledge was lost through negligence, because it resembles a claim of 'I do not know whether you ever lent me,' here it is different because he actively acted negligently by selling another person's pledge without authority. The Shach writes that he does not understand the distinction between active negligence and other negligence. The Bach distinguishes that only where the pledge was lost through negligence does the creditor take a hesset oath and become exempt, whereas here, since he sold it with his own hands, he is treated as a robber, and the enactment made for a robbed claimant applies. The Shach rejects this as well: why should he be deemed a robber merely because he sold a pledge already in his possession? Nor is this analogous to a deposit, regarding which the enactment for a robbed claimant was applied for the separate reason that people should be careful in guarding deposits. Rather, the proper explanation is that if he does not know how much it was worth and does not wish to swear that he does not know, because he fears that his oath may not be truthful given his uncertainty, or even if he knows with certainty that it was not worth more but nevertheless refuses to swear, even truthfully, then the borrower swears, and so forth. Although the wording is somewhat strained, this appears to me the correct practical ruling. The Taz writes: Here it is different because he is a full robber. Since he does not know the value of the pledge, how could he sell it? Clearly he was unconcerned with his fellow's money.
