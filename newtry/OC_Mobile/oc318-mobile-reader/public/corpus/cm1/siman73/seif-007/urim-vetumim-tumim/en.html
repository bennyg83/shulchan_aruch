And he shall find an opening, and so forth. I do not know why an opening is necessary, since we rule that a vow or oath can be released through genuine regret even without a separate opening. One cannot answer that the opening is generated from the regret, because Rosh himself says the case is not disqualified as nolad. If pure regret were enough, the whole nolad issue would be irrelevant. The correct explanation is that in this responsum Rosh follows his teacher Maharam, who held that release requires an actual opening and not regret alone. Therefore Rosh labored to identify an opening. According to our accepted ruling in Yoreh Deah that genuine regret itself can suffice, the opening is not strictly necessary. Mechaber cites it only to teach the stronger point: even where the person does not regret the original oath in itself but now identifies a valid circumstance showing he would not have sworn had he anticipated the consequences, the oath can be released. The fact that this circumstance became relevant later does not necessarily make it invalid nolad.
And Bach wrote that all this concerns voluntary repayment by the debtor. Beit din, however, may simply descend to his assets and collect for the creditor. I do not understand that. If beit din can always collect directly from the debtor's property, why all the concern about lashes, regret, and finding an opening? What mitzvah of repayment has the debtor nullified if the creditor can be fully paid by judicial seizure? The creditor does not care whether the money reaches him voluntarily or through beit din. The better explanation depends on the distinction between land and movables. Ramban observes from Ketubot 86 that the question whether repayment is a mitzvah matters especially according to the view that property is not automatically subject to a Torah lien. The personal obligation rests on the debtor, and if he refuses, beit din coerces him because of the mitzvah. Where the debtor owns only movables, their collection may depend primarily on that personal repayment duty rather than an automatic property lien. If the debtor's earlier oath prevents him from selling those movables and paying, beit din cannot simply bypass the oath by treating the movables as already liened land. The entire enforcement mechanism runs through his mitzvah obligation, and that is precisely where the conflicting oath matters. This also explains why the rule differs when the debt came first. Then the later oath is an oath against an already existing mitzvah and cannot defeat collection. It does not follow that a person can ordinarily defeat creditors merely by swearing before borrowing. Creditors commonly rely principally on land, while movables can in any event be hidden or dissipated. A konam on the assets is different because it can reach even land and therefore directly threaten the creditor's lien. Accordingly, Bach's broad statement that beit din always descends to the property should not be accepted without qualification.
