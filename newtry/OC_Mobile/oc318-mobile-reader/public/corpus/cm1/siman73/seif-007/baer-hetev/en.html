And they shall appraise. The Sema writes that this is permitted because it is a matter of mitzvah. It also appears that if an object given to him requires measuring, he may measure it on Shabbat, comparable to measuring a belt for medical treatment and measuring a mikvah, which were permitted on Shabbat, as explained in Orach Chaim at the end of siman 306. The Shach disagrees. This is unlike arranging a match, where the person performs the mitzvah himself. Here he merely prevents another person from committing a transgression, comparable to the case of bread stuck to an oven in the first chapter of Shabbat, where we do not tell one person to sin in order to save another. The comparison to measuring a belt is also incorrect, because there the measuring itself is incidental and the person's primary intent is medical treatment. In addition, a Jew may perform all needs of a sick person on Shabbat where life is at stake. Nor is a mikvah comparable, because that concerns communal needs and perhaps the measurer himself will require immersion. None of that applies here. The Taz also questions the Sema and understands that the Rosh permitted only giving a pledge, not appraising it. The Rosh concludes that had the pledge been given as actual payment it would have required appraisal, implying that appraisal would indeed be necessary but in fact should not be done. The Taz adds that he later saw in the Ramban that it is preferable, where possible, to seek annulment of the oath.

By law. This differs from the Rama's ruling in the following seif that one who swears to pay another remains obligated by the oath even if the underlying legal obligation is lacking. There he knew he was not legally obligated and voluntarily accepted the obligation through an oath. Even if he claims that he did not know he was exempt, we do not call the oath mistaken, because one who swears is presumed to formulate his oath carefully, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in siman 81, seif 28. Here, by contrast, he swore only to pay his debt, and the exemption arose afterward. This resembles a later waiver by the creditor, and we say that he did not swear with that eventuality in mind. So writes the Sema. The Shach notes that Mahari ibn Lev makes a similar distinction in responsum part 2, siman 38, and part 1, end of rule 12. The Taz below in seif 8 makes a somewhat different distinction. Mahari ibn Lev further writes that where leading authorities dispute the law, the person who swore may say, 'I rely on the authority who exempts me. When I swore, I thought everyone agreed that I was legally obligated.' He therefore ruled regarding one who guaranteed another's obligation by oath where the amount was not fixed that the guarantor may rely on the Rambam, who holds that an undertaking for an indefinite amount does not create a monetary obligation, and say that at the time of the oath he mistakenly thought one could become obligated in such an amount. The Shach finds this unconvincing. Once he has sworn, he can no longer invoke 'I rely on the authority who exempts,' because he already obligated himself by oath to perform. Responsum Maharashach likewise implies that even the Rambam agrees that an oath creates the obligation. Even without an oath, the Shach holds that one cannot rely on the Rambam in this particular law because all later authorities disagree, as he writes at the end of siman 132. The distinction could nevertheless matter in another case involving a genuine dispute among authorities. These are the words of the Shach.
