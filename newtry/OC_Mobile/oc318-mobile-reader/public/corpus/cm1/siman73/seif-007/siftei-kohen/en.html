He must give him collateral that day and have it appraised, etc. The Bach rules that no appraisal is required; rather, the debtor transfers ownership in the object for the amount owed. Accordingly, an appraisal should not be performed on Shabbos when it serves no sacred need. Even if he swore to pay in cash, he may give silver collateral and transfer the required value through it, but not other collateral when he expressly stipulated cash. It is possible that the Rosh also agrees that silver collateral is equivalent to money.
And they shall appraise it, etc. The Tur permits appraisal on Shabbos in order to enable fulfillment of the mitzvah, considering this a sacred need. The Rosh compares it to arranging a child’s match or hiring him for a craft. The Sema therefore learns that even others may conduct the appraisal to enable the debtor to fulfill his obligation, and that measurement would likewise be permitted where needed, by analogy to measurements allowed for healing and for a mikveh. In my view the analogies are not exact. Arranging a match is itself the actor’s mitzvah, whereas here one merely prevents another person’s transgression, and we do not ordinarily tell one person to sin so that another may gain. Nor is measuring for healing comparable, because care of a sick person has its own Shabbos rules, while measuring a mikveh is a communal need and may concern the measurer himself.
For whoever is exempt by law, etc. This differs from the next seif, where one swore to pay even though he had no underlying legal obligation, because there he knowingly accepted an independent obligation by oath. Even if he later says he did not know he was exempt, one generally assumes a person is precise before swearing. Here, however, he swore only to pay his debt and the exemption arose afterward, resembling a later waiver by the lender, so we say he did not swear with that eventuality in mind. Mahari ben Lev similarly distinguishes these cases and adds that where the underlying law is disputed, the defendant may claim that he relied on the authority who exempts him and that when he swore he thought all authorities held him liable. I am not persuaded. Once he has sworn, it appears that he can no longer invoke kim li, because he has independently bound himself by oath. Responsa Maharash Kohen likewise indicates that even the Rambam would recognize liability created by an oath. Indeed, even without an oath I have written below that one cannot invoke the Rambam alone regarding an obligation of an unquantified amount when all later authorities disagree.
