Seif 8. The oath nevertheless remains in force. See Sema and Shach, who conclude that even one who swore to pay is not required to pay until the creditor demands payment. The Taz disagrees here and in Yoreh Deah 239. In my sixth edition to Choshen Mishpat, siman 247, I rejected the Taz’s proofs, and the primary ruling follows the Sema and Shach. Without an oath all agree that payment, or fulfillment of a condition, is not required until demand, as I explained there and as Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer 143. The authorities also write that if a person swore to pay a debt and shemittah then intervened, he is exempt because his oath was understood only as obligating him so long as the debt remained legally payable. At first glance this seems contrary to Shevuot, where the Gemara discusses a nazir who swore not to eat a grape pit and suggests that when he swears he may have in mind only what is otherwise permitted. That implies a presumption that an oath addresses the legally permitted sphere, not merely what the person was already obligated to do. Why then do we assume here that he intended no more than his existing monetary obligation? One answer is that monetary undertakings are different. People do not ordinarily assume gratuitous monetary liabilities without indication, so an oath to pay is naturally understood as reinforcing only what the law requires. Another answer is contextual. A nazir swearing on his own initiative is speaking for himself, so it is plausible that he intends to add a restriction only in the permitted area. A debtor ordinarily swears in order to reassure the creditor that the debt will be paid. Such reassurance naturally follows the debt itself. Once the law extinguishes the debt, there is no longer any creditor expectation for the oath to reinforce. The difference between these explanations can matter where the debtor swore voluntarily, on his own initiative and not in the creditor’s presence. Under the first explanation, shemittah would still release him, because monetary oaths are presumed not to exceed legal liability. Under the second, such a self-initiated oath might remain binding even after shemittah. This requires further practical study.
Seif 8, in the gloss: one who swore to pay another even though he was not otherwise obligated. The Sema distinguishes between first formulating the oath and then mentioning the payment obligation, and first acknowledging the obligation and only afterward adding an oath. In the latter case, if the underlying obligation proves legally ineffective, the oath also falls away. I wrote in my Torah work on Parashat Vayigash, in 5619, that the same distinction can depend upon the order of speech. If he says first, “I swear that I will pay you,” the oath creates an independent obligation. If he says, “I owe you such and such, and I swear to pay,” the oath follows the stated debt; if the debt is not legally binding, the oath does not create it. In this context we do not apply the usual rule that words spoken within the brief interval of speech are treated as simultaneous. The Taz here was asked about one who undertook to pay a debt by a fixed day and agreed that if he failed he would pay a penalty of ten gold coins. He paid only part of the debt. The Taz disputed the judges about how much of the penalty is due. I found a passage in Midrash Rabbah, Parashat Vayeshev, where Antoninus asked Rabbi about a person sentenced to receive one hundred lashes with a corresponding payment arrangement. Rabbi’s illustration indicates that until the entire stipulated performance is completed, the entire counter-obligation can remain due; it is not proportionally reduced merely because part was performed. Although we ordinarily say that halacha is not derived from aggadic Midrash, here Rabbi cites an ordinary legal or commercial analogy from accepted human practice, which suggests that this is indeed the law. The passage therefore supports the position contrary to the Taz: where the condition required complete payment by the date, partial payment does not proportionally reduce the stipulated penalty.
