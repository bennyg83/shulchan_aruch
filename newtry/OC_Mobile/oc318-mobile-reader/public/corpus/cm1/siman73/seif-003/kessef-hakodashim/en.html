Seif 17. Go and sell it. Siman 73 treats the borrower's statement "go and sell the collateral" as appointing the lender an agent. That may be specifically because the imperative wording is decisive, just as "go, give it to him" differs from language merely stating that one is obligated to give. If the borrower says only, "you may sell the collateral," that is plainly not necessarily an agency appointment. He may simply be responding to the lender's demand for money by saying that the lender has the option of selling the pledge. Where the language is not clearly an agency appointment, it can be understood as permission to sell only through a beit din appraisal, or simply as assurance that the borrower will have no complaint if it is sold properly. This matters because the particular movable item may be difficult to replace, an appraisal may itself produce a lower sale and expenses, and the borrower's statement may merely acknowledge that he has no other immediate source of payment. The Rosh in the cited responsum was not specifically discussing a waiver of appraisal. Even though the actual sale may have taken place without one, his point was that the lender acted as an agent. The question whether appraisal was waived must therefore be determined by the language used. Since ambiguous language in a document is construed against its holder, appraisal should normally still be required. If the lender already holds the collateral and claims that the borrower intended to authorize sale without appraisal, perhaps the ordinary burden of proof favors the lender as possessor. This is especially plausible where the lender had threatened immediate collection because he feared the collateral was insufficient or did not wish to bear the burden of sale, and the borrower answered "sell the collateral" in order to stop that collection effort. In such a setting the language may somewhat imply broader authority. The matter requires further study.
