(Seif 2) “The borrower takes a heset oath.” This is where the parties agree on the amount of the loan and dispute only its maturity; the borrower denies the entire present claim and owes only heset. If they also dispute the amount, for example the lender says, “You owe me one hundred now,” and the borrower says, “I owe only fifty, payable next year,” he partially admits the principal amount. The denial of present maturity regarding the admitted portion is equivalent to denial of part of the debt, and he owes a Torah oath. Some judges have ruled otherwise, relying on Rabbah’s explanation that a partial admitter is merely delaying until he has funds. They reason that if the admitted portion is not currently payable, he could have admitted everything without present loss. This argument is unfounded. Tosafot explain that Rabbah asks why a partial admitter is not believed through a migo of complete denial, and answers that total denial is brazen whereas partial denial is temporary evasion. A debtor who extends the due date of the admitted portion may still be evading: he fears admitting the entire debt because he may lack funds at the later date too. He therefore denies part now, hoping that Hashem will later broaden his means and enable him to pay the balance. Moreover, every ordinary loan is initially for thirty days; on the contrary view, partial admission could scarcely exist during that period. The correct rule is that this remains a partial admission and requires a Torah oath.
