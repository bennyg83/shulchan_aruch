Seif 2: "The borrower takes a heset oath." This implies that specifically where the parties do not dispute the amount of the loan but only the time for repayment, the borrower is viewed as denying the entire presently due claim and therefore takes only a heset oath. If, however, they also dispute the amount, for example the lender says, "You owe me 100 to be paid now," while the borrower says, "I owe you only 50 and it is payable in a year," he has admitted part of the amount. Even though with respect to the due date he denies the present obligation entirely, that does not matter. Since he admits part of the principal claim and the verse's condition of "for this one" is fulfilled, his denial of the present due date on that portion is comparable to denying 50 of the principal, and he is obligated in a Torah oath. Where there is no dispute in the amount but only in when payment is due, however, we find no Torah oath at all. Although this appears simple, I write it because I heard of judges who ruled that if a defendant admits part of the amount claimed but says that even the admitted portion is not yet due, he is not considered one who admits part of the claim. They prove this from Rabbah's statement at the beginning of Bava Metzia: why did the Torah say that one who admits part of a claim must swear? There is a presumption that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor; he would really like to admit the entire claim, but temporarily evades because he says to himself, "When I obtain money I will pay." They reason that if the defendant admits part but postpones even that admitted part, then if his denial of the remainder were merely temporary evasion he could just as well have admitted all of it, since by his own formulation he would not have to pay anything immediately; therefore this is not the Torah's case of partial admission. I say there is not even a beginning of proof. Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia explain that Rabbah's question, "why did the Torah say that one who admits part must swear," is not asking why this claimant is different in essence. Rather, it asks why he is not believed through a migo that he could have denied everything, or alternatively whether we should learn from here that migo is never accepted. Rabbah answers that a person does not ordinarily brazenly deny his creditor. Tosafos then explain Rabbah's later phrase "he would like to admit the whole" as addressing a new problem: if his refusal to deny everything shows he is not brazen, perhaps we should assume his partial admission is entirely truthful and accept it without an oath. Rabbah answers that his partial denial is not brazen because it is merely temporary evasion. If a partial admission with delayed payment were not considered an admission because temporary evasion cannot apply, as those judges say, Rabbah's original question would immediately return: why must one who admits part swear? Let him be believed with a migo that he could admit part and merely extend the due date. According to their premise that such a plea involves no temporary evasion, there would be no answer to that migo.
The editor says: According to those judges there is no migo, because when one admits part payable immediately, his partial denial is not brazen because it is temporary evasion, as Tosafos explain. But if he admits part payable only later, and on their view temporary evasion cannot apply, then his denial of the remainder is complete brazenness; one cannot create a migo from a non-brazen plea to a brazen one. End of the editor's words. Nevertheless, the judges' position is null and void, and the Taz's ruling stands for another reason stated below. It is straightforward that temporary evasion can apply here as well. Even though the defendant says he will pay the admitted portion only after some time, he may still fear admitting the entire amount payable at that later date because he worries that even then he will not have enough money. He therefore thinks: for now I will deny part, and perhaps Hashem will broaden my means and I will later pay the remainder as well. There is further proof from an ordinary loan, which cannot be demanded in less than 30 days, as stated at the beginning of this siman. According to the judges' theory, the law of partial admission would almost never apply to a loan until 30 days had passed. No such limitation is heard or seen anywhere. Therefore their position has no basis, and this is properly considered a partial admission.
