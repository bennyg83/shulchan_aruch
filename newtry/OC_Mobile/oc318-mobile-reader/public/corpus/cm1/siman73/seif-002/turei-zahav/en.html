(Ibid.: If the borrower protests.) The Sema limits this to a loan made through a formal acquisition, because such a transaction ordinarily stands for writing. For an ordinary loan where no document was mentioned, he says the lender cannot retract and demand immediate return, nor compel a handwritten acknowledgment, because people commonly lend for thirty days on personal trust. The Levush rules that the lender may retract in every case. The latter appears correct. A lender ordinarily intends to secure proper proof of the loan. Ravina refused to lend Rav Ashi without a written acknowledgment. The fact that the Tur’s case included an acquisition merely records the facts; likewise the mention of a lien is unnecessary because liability for encumbrance is presumed. The Tur in siman 39 says that if the borrower refuses to write a document, the lender may demand return of the money, because he certainly lent only on condition of receiving a document. This applies to every case discussed there, whether there were witnesses without an acquisition or a formal acquisition. The glossator answers that money should not be extracted from a possessor based only on a general presumption. People often lend and repay without a document. A formal acquisition is a clear manifestation equivalent to a condition because an acquisition ordinarily stands for writing. Mere witnesses do not authorize writing without renewed instruction, and a private loan was plainly made on trust. The Tur’s wording and the Rama’s reference to the borrower’s “protest” support the Sema. Since the matter is doubtful, the borrower should not be compelled.
