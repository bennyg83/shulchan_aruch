And he fixed a time for him. See the Sema, who is uncertain whether extending the due date after the loan requires a kinyan, similar to a grant of special credibility. The Shach regards it as obvious that no kinyan is needed because it is a waiver. See siman 71, seif katan 3, where even if the creditor grants the borrower credibility in a written loan so that the borrower will be believed to say, 'I paid,' the creditor may retract before final judgment, meaning before the borrower actually claims payment, because in chapter Zeh Borer even one who says, 'It is waived to you,' may retract before final judgment if no kinyan was made. Here, however, the creditor voluntarily and independently extends the borrower's time. This is a true present waiver of his right to collect earlier and requires no kinyan. This is straightforward.
Today is the end of the term. Rambam in chapter 13 of Hilchot Malveh rules that if one witness testifies that today is the due date, the borrower must take a Torah oath. Maggid Mishneh says he did not find the source expressly but understands this to be Rambam's view. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, siman 105, writes that the matter is clear from the Mishnah in the first chapter of Makkot: witnesses who falsely testify that a person owes his fellow one thousand zuz are assessed according to how much a person would pay to have those one thousand zuz remain in his hand from now until thirty days rather than until ten years. This proves that delay in payment has monetary value and can create the monetary liability of false witnesses. Maharit then asks why, without any witness, the borrower should not already be liable for a partial-admission oath if the disputed period itself has the value of a perutah. He answers that Rambam did not intend to teach that aspect and could be speaking where the debtor had already admitted the debt before witnesses and the parties now dispute only the term. Alternatively, disagreement over the term may not count as partial admission because what is admitted is not of the same type as what is denied, similar to one who claims a gold dinar and the other admits only a silver dinar. Maharit thus understands the oath concerning the due date to rest on the monetary value of the delay. This is difficult. A Torah oath requires a claim involving an object whose very body is money. Even a document is excluded from certain oaths because the paper is not the money itself. All the more so a mere period of delay, though someone might pay something for that benefit, is not itself a monetary object. It is only indirect benefit. Furthermore, one who causes another's money to lie idle is exempt even according to those who impose liability for dina d'garmi, and is less direct than garmi. False witnesses are no proof. There the witnesses falsely impose an immediate monetary obligation and thereby cause the defendant to relinquish the use of his money before the real date, which the Torah measures as damage for the special law of false witnesses. Here, by contrast, the money remains in the borrower's hand and he wants merely to retain it longer. That is analogous to withholding another's capital, which is prohibited initially but does not create damages after the fact. Another difficulty is that the delay may not be worth a perutah. If the entire claim is only a perutah, and one witness says payment is due today while the borrower says it is due in ten days, the value of ten days' use of one perutah is certainly less than a perutah. Yet the law is stated without requiring appraisal of the delay. It therefore appears that even if the delay itself is worth less than a perutah, a Torah oath applies. The reason is that the creditor says, 'The debt is due today.' According to the claimant and witness, the whole principal is presently payable. The borrower says, 'I will pay you in ten days because you suffer no meaningful loss through the wait.' But if the creditor does not wish to wait, beit din may collect from the borrower's property immediately. The rule that one who causes lost use is exempt applies only after the fact. Initially, once the due date arrives, beit din certainly enforces payment if the creditor wants his money now, whether from available cash or after the period granted by beit din. The borrower cannot say, 'I will pay tomorrow or in two days and the delay is worth less than a perutah.' If the creditor wants immediate payment, his claim concerns the full principal right now. Although beit din does not attend to a claim under a perutah, here the principal itself is a full perutah. Similarly for oath: when the creditor refuses to extend the term, the present denial concerns the entire principal, not merely the economic value of the delay. This is like a dispute over a particular object. Suppose the claimant says, 'I bought this object from you through acquisition together with land or meshichah, and I will pay the price,' while the defendant says, 'I never sold it.' If one witness supports the sale, the defendant takes a Torah oath even though the immediate dispute concerns the object itself and there is no separate monetary damage being claimed. Tosafot in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, concerning 'let us see from whom he took the money,' implies this: the person against whom the seller's testimony stands would need to swear a Torah oath because of one witness, even though the dispute there concerns ownership of the article and the Gemara is speaking where the seller received money from both parties and not where the article increased in value. The reason is that when the claimant says, 'This object is mine,' he cannot be forced to sell it and accept money. Beit din must restore the very object. Wherever beit din will enforce delivery of the subject of the claim, a Torah oath can apply. Otherwise one could take another's utensil and offer its price, and beit din could not intervene merely because the difference between the utensil and its value is not itself a perutah. That is obviously wrong. So too here, when the creditor says, 'I want my money now,' and the borrower denies the present due date, the borrower is denying the creditor's present right to the full principal. His promise to pay later does not reduce the present denial merely to the value of the delay. The same analysis explains why the ordinary oath that a bailee does not presently possess the object is rabbinic when the bailee is ready to pay the item's value. Why is there not a Torah oath concerning the object itself? Because the Torah oath of bailees concerning possession arises ordinarily where the deposited item is not a specifically identified object that the depositor can point to and say, 'That very utensil is mine and must be returned.' The bailee says it was lost and offers its value. Where the depositor claims a particular visible object is his and the bailee says that object is not the deposit, this is a different ownership dispute, as explained in siman 234, not the ordinary bailee oath. In the standard bailee oath, once the bailee pays the value, there is no more property into which beit din can descend. The difference between a Torah and rabbinic oath includes whether beit din descends to property when the defendant refuses to swear. If he has already paid the full value, there is no additional property to collect. The Sages nevertheless required the oath and may place him under a ban for refusing. By contrast, where the specific object is identified, refusal to swear allows beit din to remove that very disputed object from his possession and give it to the claimant. The same applies to the due-date dispute. I believe Rambam learned the rule from the Gemara's explanation of partial-admission oaths: the debtor actually wishes to admit the entire debt but temporarily evades payment until he acquires money and can pay. If such an evasion of present payment could not itself support a Torah oath because he intends to pay later, why does the Torah impose the oath? Even if the disputed amount is merely a perutah, the economic value of a brief delay is less than a perutah. The answer is that when payment is due now and the creditor refuses to extend, delaying the entire principal is a present denial of that principal. Ritva explains the Gemara differently, that once the defendant leaves beit din he may persist in the denial and never pay. But Rambam can follow the straightforward explanation that the temporary withholding itself triggers the oath. According to this, Maharit's question about partial admission also falls away. The term itself is not a separate monetary object whose partial value is admitted or denied. The borrower who says, 'I will pay in ten days,' is simply denying the whole debt as presently payable. His later promised payment is not a partial present admission. False witnesses are therefore no proof, as explained. Examine the matter carefully.
