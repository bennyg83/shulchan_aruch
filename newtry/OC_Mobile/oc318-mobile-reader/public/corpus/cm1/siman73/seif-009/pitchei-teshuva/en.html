The first month. See Baer Heitev and the Taz concerning one who fixed Chanukah as the due date. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 172. Chukei Mishpat writes that this is the basis of the commercial custom at fairs: where a debt falls due “at the fair,” and the fair lasts several weeks, collection is not demanded until the end. See also Maamar Kaddishin. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 120, discusses Shimon, who borrowed one hundred from Reuven, promising to pay immediately upon arriving at a particular fair, with a penalty of ten gold coins to charity if he delayed, and swore to this. On arrival Reuven demanded payment. Shimon answered that he first had to unload and secure his merchandise. Two or three hours later Reuven demanded again, and Shimon said he was still occupied with paying workers, customs charges, and similar matters. Near evening he paid. The question was whether he incurred the charitable penalty because he had not paid immediately. Shimon argued that while engaged in matters integral to his arrival he was still, in effect, on the road, and paid as soon as his arrival was complete. Tzemach Tzedek rejects that argument: arrival occurs when one arrives, even if related tasks remain, all the more so where he expressly promised to pay immediately upon arrival. Nevertheless, he concludes that Shimon does not owe the penalty because payment on the same day is still called immediate, bringing proof from the Tosefta cited by the Rash in Taharot 9:4 and by Bartenura there. In my opinion this requires study, for Shimon presumably did not speak in Hebrew but used the vernacular word meaning “soon,” which may signify at once. See Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 28, seif katan 15.
