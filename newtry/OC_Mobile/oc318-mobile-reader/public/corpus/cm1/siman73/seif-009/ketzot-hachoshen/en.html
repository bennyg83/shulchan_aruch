On the first day of the new month. Rashba explains that because both days are called Rosh Chodesh, this is a Torah level doubt and we are stringent. Taz in Yoreh Deah siman 228 argues that according to Rambam's ruling concerning the two Adars, if the person knew when he vowed that Rosh Chodesh would have two days, the prohibition should continue until the second day. Rambam says that where one knows the year is intercalated and says "until Adar," he means the second Adar. Taz reasons a fortiori: in Adar there is a tannaitic dispute which is called plain Adar, yet we interpret the unqualified term as the second according to the halachic view. Here everyone agrees the second day is the principal Rosh Chodesh for many laws, so the person's intent should certainly extend through it. In my view the Mechaber's unqualified language here and in Yoreh Deah shows that no such distinction was intended. The cases differ. According to Rambam, Rabbi Yehudah's view is that the second month is the one called plain Adar, while the first is specifically called Adar I. Therefore, when the person knows the year is intercalated and says simply "Adar," he presumably means the second, because had he meant the first he would have said Adar I. If he did not know the year was intercalated, he naturally meant the month immediately after Shevat. With the two days of Rosh Chodesh, however, both days bear exactly the same name. Neither has a more specific designation in ordinary wording. Therefore even if the person knew there would be two days, the language remains genuinely ambiguous and does not leave the category of doubt. This is clear. The discussion in Perek Konam Yayin concerning "this month" and the status of Rosh Chodesh supports the same conclusion.
