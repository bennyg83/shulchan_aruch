Or a slave. See Urim, which follows the emendation of the Chacham Tzvi in siman 87. He understands the Yerushalmi’s statement that beit din does not administer an oath to a slave as based upon the ordinary presumption that a slave is disqualified and suspected regarding oaths. Accordingly, a slave known to be trustworthy would in principle be capable of taking an oath. I nevertheless do not understand the Yerushalmi in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, which says that the oath of testimony has a feature that the oath of deposit does not, namely that the oath of testimony applies to slaves, documents, and land, whereas the oath of deposit does not, and then objects that an oath of testimony is imposed by beit din and beit din does not administer an oath to one who is suspected. The oath of testimony is not itself an oath administered by beit din to compel testimony in the same manner. The witness transgresses by denying testimony under oath. Why then is the rule concerning beit din administering an oath to a suspected person relevant? Moreover, even on the Yerushalmi’s premise, there can surely be a trustworthy slave, so the category is not impossible. In any event, the Yerushalmi’s statement is independently difficult, because a slave is not legally qualified to give testimony at all, so there should be no oath of testimony with respect to him. If its intent is instead that witnesses testify concerning ownership of slaves, namely to whom the slaves belong, then the witnesses themselves are not slaves, and once again the question about beit din administering an oath to a suspected person is difficult. This requires study.
