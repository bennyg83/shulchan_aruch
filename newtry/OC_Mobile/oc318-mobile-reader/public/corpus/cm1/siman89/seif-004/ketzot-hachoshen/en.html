And that he worked for him. Pnei Yehoshua asks why witnesses are needed to prove the hiring. If the worker actually performed beneficial labor, the employer must pay even if there was no explicit hiring, under the law of one who improves another's property. The answer is that the special enactment allowing an employee to swear and collect applies specifically to one who was hired. A volunteer improver may have an ordinary restitution claim for benefit but does not receive the special employee credibility. Therefore, without witnesses to the hiring, the employer can say, "I paid you," and rely on the absence of the special enactment. There remains difficulty because if the employer says, "I never hired you," and the worker undeniably performed compensable labor, the denial does not necessarily exempt him. He may still owe the value of the benefit. The exact scope of the payment migo therefore requires further study, as I noted in siman 375.
