But not the heirs of the worker. The Sema discusses at length the Rashba and the Yerushalmi, from which it appears that even if the employer remains alive and the worker dies, the worker’s heirs do not collect. The Shach writes that this lengthy discussion is unnecessary because the rule is explicit below in siman 108: heirs do not collect where the deceased’s right depended upon an oath. This is correct, but the case there is the ordinary rule that a person cannot transmit an oath obligation to his children. The employer claims that he already paid the worker, and the worker then dies. The heirs cannot take the worker’s oath that he was not paid. They can say only that their father did not instruct them that he had been paid, and such a substitute oath does not suffice where the underlying law gave the deceased no right to collect without his own oath. There is nevertheless a different case that is not clearly resolved there. Suppose the worker died without payment, and only afterward the employer claims that he paid the heirs themselves. The heirs deny this and are prepared to swear that they were not paid. One might argue that there is no difference between the father and his heirs, because the very oath that the father would have taken can now be taken directly by the heirs concerning a payment allegedly made to them. This creates some uncertainty. The Yerushalmi, however, says that the enactment was made for the hired worker and not for his heirs. This indicates that the special enactment never applied to heirs at all. The Sages were concerned that the worker who toiled should see the fruit of his labor, not that his heirs should receive the same procedural preference. Therefore the Yerushalmi says that the enactment was not made for the son in any form. Perhaps this is why the Sema discussed the matter at length, because this conclusion adds something beyond the ordinary rule stated below in siman 108. Examine this carefully.
