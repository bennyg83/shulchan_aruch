One should not object from siman 294, seif 2, where one who is believed to say, 'I returned it,' only through a migo that he could have said, 'It was lost through unavoidable accident,' must swear regarding the return with an oath resembling a Torah oath, just as he would have sworn had he claimed unavoidable accident. Here too, since the employer is believed to say, 'I paid you,' only through a migo that he could have said, 'I never hired you,' why should he not swear as he would have sworn had he denied hiring, namely a Torah oath to contradict the witness? The cases differ. Even if there had been two witnesses here, Torah law would not require payment. The worker's oath and recovery are a rabbinic enactment, and the Sages did not enact it where there is only one witness. This is the Shach's conclusion. See Responsa Maharshdam, Choshen Mishpat, siman 194.
Believed. Although the reason for the worker's special oath is that an employer, being preoccupied with his workers, may forget and think that he paid them, why should a migo make him remember any better? Nevertheless, since he asserts with certainty that he did not forget, we do not presume him to be lying when he has a migo. Had he wished to lie he could have said, 'I never hired you.' Where he has no migo, however, we say that he is actually uncertain and falsely presents his claim as certain. This is the Sema's explanation. The Shach writes that although this is a migo involving brazenness, because if he denied the hiring outright the worker would know that he was deliberately lying, whereas in saying he paid he may merely be mistaken because he was preoccupied with his workers, this case is different because the worker's oath is only a rabbinic enactment. Even a weak migo therefore restores the matter to Torah law. The Rosh writes this, as does the Ran in the name of Rav Sherira Gaon, and they derive it from the Gemara's conclusion. In my view, this reasoning is unnecessary to exempt him from payment, because even a migo involving brazenness is effective to exempt from money, as I demonstrated at the end of siman 82 in the laws of migo, paragraph 6. It is needed only because the Gemara implies that by virtue of this migo the employer is entirely exempt, even from an oath taken while holding a sacred object. I later found this stated explicitly by the Ran. See there.
By Torah law. Since the basis is the migo that he could have said, 'I never hired you,' and in the presence of one witness he would then have had to take a Torah oath, when he instead says, 'I paid your wages,' he takes an oath resembling a Torah oath. So writes the Maggid Mishneh. See above, siman 121, seif 5, and Responsa Maharshal Kohen, part 3, siman 69. Shach.
Its time. The reason is that there is a presumption that a worker does not leave his wages unpaid beyond the due time unless he demanded them on time and was not paid. It is not as the Levush writes, that during the due period the employer is occupied with fulfilling the verse, 'On that day you shall give him his wage,' whereas after the due time he is no longer preoccupied. According to that explanation, if after the due time the employer claimed that he had paid during the due time, he should not be believed because at that point he had been preoccupied. This is contrary to the Gemara. Sema.
