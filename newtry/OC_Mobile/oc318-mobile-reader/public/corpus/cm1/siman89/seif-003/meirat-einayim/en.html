And that he performed work for him.
Since he could have said, 'I did not hire you,' etc. Although the reason the Sages say that the worker swears is that the employer is preoccupied with his workers and may have forgotten and thought that he paid him, not because we suspect him of deliberately lying, and if so a migo should seemingly not apply, since how would a migo make him remember better, nevertheless the employer claims with certainty, 'I did not forget; I know that I paid him.' We therefore do not presume that he is lying about the certainty of his own recollection, because he has a migo: if he wished to lie while making a definite claim, he could have lied by saying, 'I never hired him at all.' When he has no such migo, however, we say that he is actually uncertain and falsely presents his claim as certain. So wrote Tosafos. See the Prisha.
If witnesses do not also testify that the worker actually performed the work, testimony merely that he hired him does not help, because the employer has a migo that he could have said the worker never came to perform the job.Since he could have said, 'I never hired you,' etc.
Although the reason Chazal shifted the oath to the worker is that the employer is preoccupied with his workers and may have forgotten whether he paid, rather than because we suspect deliberate lying, the migo still has relevance to the legal structure of the claim.
The worker's special oath is a rabbinic enactment created in a defined setting.
Where the employer has an alternative claim that would have exempted him and the evidentiary conditions of the enactment are not established, the claimant cannot extend the enactment beyond its stated case.
Thus witnesses must establish both the hiring and the work, and then the special worker's oath applies concerning payment.
