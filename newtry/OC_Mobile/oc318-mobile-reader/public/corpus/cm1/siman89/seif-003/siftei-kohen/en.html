But if two witnesses were not present, etc. This implies that one witness is insufficient, and the employer takes only a hesset oath. Rambam and the Tur say the same. One cannot compare this to siman 296, seif 2, where a custodian who is believed to say “I returned it” only through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable accident must take an oath resembling the oath he would have taken on unavoidable accident. Here, even two witnesses would not create Torah liability. The worker's right to swear and collect is entirely rabbinic, and the Sages did not enact it where only one witness establishes the employment. The matter therefore returns to ordinary law, and the worker has no greater right than any claimant.
Through the fact that he could have said, “I never hired you,” etc. Although a depositary without witnesses must take the custodians' oath and is not believed through a migo that the deposit never occurred, because that is an audacious migo, the worker's oath is only rabbinic. Even a weak migo restores ordinary law. The Rosh and Ran explain this in the name of Rav Sherira Gaon. The migo is needed not merely to exempt the employer from payment, for an audacious migo may preserve possession, but to exempt him even from the Mishnah-level oath originally contemplated by the enactment. The Ran explains that a weak migo can preserve money but does not ordinarily remove an oath. Here it removes the special oath only because the workers' oath is a rabbinic enactment and most employment can be established through witnesses; the Sages did not extend their enactment to a case where the employer has such a migo.
[And] This is the meaning of Hashem’s promise that “I will give you a blessing from your hands, and that you will not be able to say that you will not be blessed when you are one of them, I swear that I will give you a blessing that I will swear to you that I will give you a blessing to you.”
A day worker has the entire night, etc. A worker hired by the hour during the day may collect throughout that day, and one hired by the hour at night throughout that night. A worker hired by the week, month, or year who finishes by day may collect throughout that day, and if he finishes by night, throughout that night, as stated below, siman 339, seifim 4 and 5. Although the authorities there follow Rav because the issue is one of prohibition, whereas here it concerns money and Shmuel ordinarily governs monetary law, since the primary dispute was ruled like Rav, the related monetary consequence follows Rav as well. The Rosh and Maharshal use the same principle elsewhere.
