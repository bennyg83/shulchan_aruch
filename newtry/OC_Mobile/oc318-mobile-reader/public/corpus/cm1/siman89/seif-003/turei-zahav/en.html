(See there, from the second day until evening) According to Sema, seif katan 13, it appears that if the worker continues to demand payment through the evening of the third day, he is then given two days and two nights. This is difficult. Why did the Sages say that he is given a period corresponding to the day of his demand? They should have said that the period follows the entire duration of his demands, so that if he continued demanding for eight days, he would have eight additional days in which to swear and collect. No such rule is heard or appears correct. Sema’s further statement, that he did not demand payment on the third day and that when he resumed demanding on the night of the fourth it became revealed, etc., is also exceedingly difficult. Certainly, if he ceased demanding for one full time-period, the advantage of acting within the prescribed time is entirely lost, and the employer swears and is exempt. It seems Sema derived support from the Tur’s wording, following the Rambam, “until the end of the fourth day,” which implies that no demand is required at the beginning of the fifth night, but that the fifth night is combined with the fifth day. It appears to me that the Tur’s wording should be read carefully. He presents, in the Rambam’s name, the rule where the demand continued until the end of the fourth day as though it were a new law, although at first glance it has no distinct reason. The novelty is that when the Gemara says that the worker is given a period corresponding to his demand, it means that he is given twelve hours after the time during which he could have demanded. Thus, if he could demand on the second night, he is granted another twelve hours, namely the third day, provided that he demanded at the end of the second night. One might have thought that because the Gemara used the word “day,” it grants only one day, through the third day and no further. The Tur therefore writes in the Rambam’s name that the same pattern continues afterward. If he demanded even at the end of the fourth day, without having ceased demanding, he receives the following period as well. If the text says, “all of the fifth day,” but he demanded at the end of the fifth night, it appears to me that the correct reading is “all of the fifth night.” I have likewise seen it emended in the name of a great authority. The Rambam’s essential intent is that one always receives twelve hours following the demand. If the demand was during the fifth day, he has the sixth night, and so on, provided he did not cease demanding for one entire time-period. Ran in the chapter Kol HaNishba’in explains similarly, except that he writes that the worker is always given one complete day. Thus, if he demanded on the second day for work performed on the third night, he receives one complete day in which to collect and need not repeat his demand on the third night. Examine his words carefully and this will be clear.
