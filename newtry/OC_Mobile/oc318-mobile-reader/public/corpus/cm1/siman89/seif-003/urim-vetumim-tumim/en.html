Even if the worker was claiming against a minor. This is the correct reading in the Rambam: even if the employer was a minor. The Mishneh LaMelekh asks what force the word “even” has. If, when the employer is an adult and makes a definite claim, the Sages nevertheless remove money from him by allowing the worker to swear and collect, then surely they may do so when the employer is a minor. This is not such a strong difficulty. According to what was explained, the Sages originally placed the oath upon the employer and then transferred it to the worker. Thus, where no oath could ever rest upon the employer in the first place, one might have thought that no oath would be transferred to the worker either. A minor is never subject to the oath. The Rambam therefore teaches that after the enactment established the worker’s oath, there is no distinction between an adult employer and a minor employer. A better explanation is that where the employer is a minor, one might have thought that even after the ordinary payment period the worker should continue to swear and collect. The Gemara explains that after the due time the worker no longer collects by oath because of the presumption that an employer does not violate the prohibition of delaying wages. That presumption does not apply to a minor. The law might therefore have reverted after the due time to the same status as during the due time. The Rambam’s “even” thus refers back to his preceding limitation that only during the due time does the worker swear and collect. Even where the employer is a minor, his law is the same as that of an adult and the worker does not continue indefinitely to collect by oath.
