Where there are witnesses that he hired him. Tumim is uncertain whether, if the witnesses have gone overseas, the employer can still use a migo because he fears that they may return and contradict him. In my view there is no uncertainty. We find migo in precisely such circumstances where one says, "I repaid you in the presence of so-and-so," and those persons went overseas. Likewise, an unverified document can be attacked through a migo that the defendant could have said it was forged, even though he may fear that witnesses will later come and contradict him.
If two witnesses were not present there. See Shach seif katan 5, who writes that if only one witness was present, the witness changes nothing and the employer merely takes a shevuat heset; see his objection there. It appears that this can be resolved as follows. Tumim writes that where a craftsman performed work without an express hiring and later claims he was not paid, he does not have the special status of a hired worker who swears and collects. Rather, the employer swears and is exempt. Therefore, even if one witness testifies that the employer hired him, the employer is believed to say, "I paid you," by a migo that he could have said, "I did not hire you. You performed the work without a hiring, and I paid you," in which case the worker would have no special enactment allowing him to swear and collect. There is no d'oraisa oath merely to contradict the single witness, because even if the employer admitted everything the witness says, the worker would still have to swear before collecting. Shach in siman 75 seif katan 47 states the rule that where two witnesses would not by themselves obligate payment without an oath by the claimant, one witness does not create a Torah oath. Therefore, when the employer says, "I hired you and paid you," he likewise is not one who is obligated to swear because of the witness. Ketzot HaChoshen objects to Tumim from siman 375 seif 8 regarding one who descended into another's field without permission and is not believed to say that he paid. This appears limited to a field not ordinarily planted, where the expenditure and enhancement require appraisal and, as explained there, the improver must also swear even where his hand is on top. But in a field ordinarily planted, where he receives the customary amount paid to local planters and the wage is known, he can be believed, just like a worker whose customary wage is known. The rule in seif 8 does not address that case. Otherwise every worker hired without a stipulated wage would be unable to claim repayment, which is untenable. There is proof from the source of that rule, the case of a wall above four cubits in Bava Batra. Up to four cubits the builder is presumed paid even though the claimant himself built and the amount may require appraisal, because the obligation and customary valuation are known to all. The same applies here. More generally, where the legal obligation is not obvious, such as an unplanted field or a case dependent upon an owner's particular manifestation of consent, formal adjudication is needed, for perhaps the Sages would not obligate him. A claim that judgment already occurred is not believed because a judicial proceeding creates publicity. But where the law is evident to all, such as a worker without an agreed wage or a field ordinarily planted where the work was needed, he must plainly pay. Only an appraisal by three laymen is necessary, and he can be believed to say, "It was already appraised and I paid." This also resolves the uncertainty of Mahara Sasson responsum 235 concerning adjudication and appraisal.
And if he admitted part, he shall swear an oath. See Sema seif katan 9, who cites Ri that where the employer admits part, the worker swears and collects, because the employer's proposed migo would be a migo of brazenness. Later authorities object that a migo of brazenness can be used to exempt from money. They also ask on the Mechaber, who holds that even in a partial-admission case we use a migo, from Bava Batra 48, where the baraita concerning a craftsman who says, "You fixed my wage at two," is established without witnesses. Tosafot ask why, within the wage period, the worker should swear and collect if the employer has a migo, and answer that a partial admitter does not receive that migo. The dispute can be explained from the structure of the wage enactment. Shmuel says that the worker's special rule applies only where the hiring was before witnesses. Tosafot ask that when the employer's denial may result from an approximate recollection because he is preoccupied with his workers, why should a migo help? Rabbi Yehudah holds that we do not make one enactment upon another. Ran explains that initially the Sages imposed an oath upon the employer because of the worker's livelihood, then because the employer is preoccupied with his workers they removed that oath from him and shifted it to the worker. Rabbi Yehudah's refusal to make an enactment upon an enactment means that where the employer cannot reliably take the special oath because he is preoccupied, the Sages do not create an additional structure beyond the original enactment. According to this, Ri holds that the original enactment imposed an oath on the employer. If there are no hiring witnesses, the employer has a migo. Had he not been preoccupied and asserted a clear bari claim, that migo would exempt him even from the special oath. Thus the first enactment itself did not impose an oath in a no-witness case. If no oath attached to the employer, there is nothing to reverse onto the worker through the secondary enactment. But in a modeh bemiktzat case the employer is independently liable to a d'oraisa oath. Since he is preoccupied and cannot swear reliably, the worker swears and collects. There is no operative migo because the employer's claim is effectively an approximate recollection rather than a reliable bari statement. This explains Ri. The Mechaber follows Tosafot that the primary enactment was directly for the worker to swear and collect. Nevertheless, where the employer has a migo, even though he is preoccupied and his recollection may be imperfect, Tosafot permit the migo because he actually asserts a definite claim. This applies even in a modeh bemiktzat case, because although in ordinary modeh bemiktzat we reject a migo that he could have denied everything as a migo of brazenness used to escape an oath, in the worker context the migo exempts from payment itself and is therefore stronger. One might object from a person disqualified from a d'oraisa oath, where the opposing party swears and collects and the disqualified defendant cannot use a migo that he could have denied everything, even though that migo too would exempt him from money. The distinction is that in the disqualified person's case the monetary consequence arises specifically through the d'oraisa oath. We do not use a migo to exempt him from money that comes as the result of that oath mechanism. In the worker case, by contrast, the money does not arise from a Torah oath. Under the Sages' enactment, even a total denial can require payment after the worker swears. A migo can therefore operate directly against the monetary enactment. The baraita concerning the craftsman is accordingly consistent. It follows Rabbi Yehudah, as is clear in Shevuot. According to Rabbi Yehudah, a total denial is exempt, and only a partial admission permits the worker to swear and collect, because the employer has a d'oraisa oath that he cannot reliably take while preoccupied with his workers. Since the monetary liability there arises from the oath, a migo is not used, just as in the case of one disqualified from swearing.
After the time for payment. See Sema seif katan 6, through his statement that a worker does not delay collecting his wage. The Gemara mentions two chazakot: an employer is presumed not to violate bal talin, and a worker is presumed not to delay his wage. Tumim asks according to Ramban, who establishes the baraita of a craftsman claiming a wage of two according to the view that a craftsman acquires an interest in the improvement of the utensil. On that view the employer does not violate bal talin, as the Gemara states. Why then does the baraita say that after the payment period the burden of proof is upon the claimant, when one of the two chazakot is absent? This also helps resolve Tosafot's question in Bava Batra 45 on the phrase "after his time." Tosafot ask that once the time has passed there can be no chazakah that the employer does not violate bal talin, because in fact he has delayed payment. Even if the worker permitted the delay, the contrary chazakah that a worker does not delay his wage is likewise absent, because the worker did delay it. In Bava Metzia 112, where the Gemara rules that if witnesses testify that the worker demanded payment within the proper time he swears and collects, the Gemara asks whether this should continue forever. Rashi explains: can a worker really delay his wage so long? Tosafot ask that the chazakah against violating bal talin is still absent. Rashi's view appears to be that "a worker does not delay his wage" is not an evidentiary chazakah proving that payment occurred within the time. If it were, then when the employer claims after the deadline, "I paid you just now," he should not be believed, because the worker has in fact delayed his wage and the chazakah cannot show prior payment. Nor can this be explained merely by a migo that the employer could have said, "I paid within the time," because Ri HaLevi, cited by the Rosh in Shevuot, holds regarding the migo of "I never hired you" that a migo does not exempt from the special oath enactment. Rather, Rashi interprets "a worker does not delay his wage" as Tosafot explain in Bava Metzia 103: extending the special enactment indefinitely would not be a sound enactment. The ordinary practice is that a worker may wait only through the legally defined payment period, such as the night following a day laborer's work. The Sages therefore created the special rule only during the period in which workers ordinarily wait. After that time, because prolonged delay is uncommon, they made no enactment. This is also Rashi's meaning when he asks in Bava Metzia 113 whether a worker delays his wage so long. Since such delay is uncommon, the enactment was not made for that case. Accordingly, the chazakah that a worker does not delay is relevant primarily to a total denial, where the employer would otherwise be liable only because of the special enactment. Since the enactment does not extend past the customary period, there is no secondary enactment after the deadline. But where the employer admits part, he is independently liable to a d'oraisa oath even after time has passed. Even if one treated the worker's prompt collection as an evidentiary chazakah suggesting payment, such a chazakah would not remove a d'oraisa oath. The poskim never state that after the deadline a modeh bemiktzat is exempt from a Torah oath. Thus he remains obligated. Because the employer is preoccupied with his workers and the Sages treat that as a real likelihood of forgetfulness, ordinarily the worker would swear and collect when the employer cannot take the required d'oraisa oath. Here the second chazakah becomes necessary: the employer is presumed not to violate bal talin. Its meaning is that when the payment deadline arrives, the employer focuses upon the obligation and remembers, as the Gemara says. His claim therefore becomes bari against bari and he can take his own oath and be exempt. The presumption against violating bal talin alone would not suffice, because even in disputes over the fixed wage, where both parties are bari, the special worker oath can apply. We therefore also need the principle that the worker normally does not delay his claim, meaning that the Sages did not extend their enactment beyond the proper time. This resolves Tumim's question regarding a craftsman who, according to the relevant view, does not create bal talin liability. That baraita follows Rabbi Yehudah. Rabbi Yehudah views the employer only as possibly forgetful, so absent a special enactment the employer would swear and be exempt and the worker would not collect. Once the time has passed, the Sages made no special worker enactment because they limited it to the period when workers ordinarily wait for payment. Even if this particular worker intentionally delayed, the enactment was not created for that uncommon circumstance. This likewise answers Tosafot's question in Bava Batra. According to this reasoning, there is room to say that if an employer admits part and also admits that he violated bal talin with respect to the admitted amount, the worker should swear and collect even after the ordinary time. However, the employer can ordinarily be believed by a migo that he could have said he did not violate bal talin because the worker authorized the delay as to the amount admitted. But if witnesses establish that the employer actually violated bal talin with respect to the admitted portion, then the worker should indeed swear and collect. The chazakah that the employer focuses and remembers at the deadline is demonstrably unavailable, and since he is preoccupied with his workers and cannot reliably take the d'oraisa oath of modeh bemiktzat, the worker swears and collects. This appears clear.
