Even if his wage was only a perutah. The Shach in seif katan 2 discusses at length an apparent contradiction in the Rambam. In chapter 11 of Hilkhot Sekhirut the Rambam rules that even if the worker’s wage was only a perutah, as the Shulchan Aruch rules here. Yet in chapter 3 of Hilkhot To’en VeNitan he cites in the name of his teachers the view that there must be a denial of two silver ma’ah, and his words there plainly imply that he accepts that requirement. The two rulings appear contradictory. Even if we strain to explain that the Rambam did not wish in practice to rule against his teachers and therefore wrote that even for a perutah the worker swears, or if we adopt the explanation of the Chacham Tzvi that the Rambam agrees regarding a hired worker that he may collect even a perutah with an oath, and disagrees with his teachers only regarding the other categories who swear and collect, who by Torah law would collect without an oath and concerning whom the Rambam holds that the Sages imposed their additional oath only when the denial is at least two silver ma’ah, what are we to say about the Tur? At the beginning of siman 87 the Tur rules like the Rambam that even regarding a hired worker there must be a denial of two silver ma’ah, contrary to the Chacham Tzvi’s approach, while at the end of that siman he writes in the very language cited here that even if the wage was only a perutah the worker swears and collects. The Shach attempts to reconcile this. The substance of his words is that in every case the defendant must initially deny two ma’ah. If he did not originally deny two ma’ah, beit din does not impose the oath. Once he has denied two ma’ah and beit din has ruled that an oath is required, the claimant then takes the rabbinically instituted oath and may collect even from a perutah upward. The Shach, with the breadth of his understanding, expressed himself briefly and relied upon readers of his own level. We, however, do not understand his precise intent. If the defendant denies two ma’ah, how can the claimant later swear and collect only a perutah? The denial itself was two ma’ah. Perhaps the Shach means that the claimant later admits that his original claim of two ma’ah was false and that in truth he has only a one perutah claim, but since beit din already ruled that he may swear, the ruling remains in force even after it becomes clear that the actual claim is only a perutah. This is exceedingly difficult to read into the Rambam and Tur. More fundamentally, it is remote from reason. If the root of the law is that one who claims only a perutah is not among those whom the Sages allowed to swear and collect, because the enactment was never made for such a small claim, how can the claimant benefit from having lied in beit din and exaggerated his claim until beit din imposed an oath? His deceit would then enable him to collect a perutah with an oath contrary to the enactment, so that the sinner who falsely inflated his claim profits while the innocent claimant who truthfully demanded only a perutah loses. That cannot be correct. Perhaps the Shach’s intent is different. The total denial and contradiction between the parties must indeed concern two silver ma’ah, analogous to the Torah oath of one who admits part of a claim, but the amount ultimately collected under the special oath may be only a perutah. For example, the worker claims, “You fixed my wage at two silver ma’ah and you owe them to me.” The employer says, “I fixed your wage at only one perutah, and I already paid that perutah.” Concerning the amount of the wage, the parties contradict one another regarding two silver ma’ah. The employer swears regarding the wage amount and is thereby exempt from the larger claim. The worker then has only the one perutah which even the employer admitted was the agreed wage, and he may swear and collect that perutah. Thus, although the amount collected through the worker’s oath is only a perutah, the dispute itself involved the requisite two silver ma’ah. The same pattern can be found in the other categories who swear and collect. In the case of an injured person, for example, witnesses may establish one injury by testifying that they saw him whole before the quarrel, so that with respect to that injury the injured party may swear and collect. Suppose that injury is worth only one perutah, but the injured party also claims a second injury worth enough that the two injuries together equal two silver ma’ah. As to the second injury there are no witnesses who saw him enter the other’s domain uninjured, so he is not believed under the special enactment. There is nevertheless a total denial of two silver ma’ah when both injuries are counted, while the amount he may actually recover by oath is only the single perutah injury. Similarly, in the case of one who was robbed, witnesses may have seen the defendant enter the claimant’s premises in circumstances supporting a claim worth only one perutah, while the claimant alleges another entry not seen by witnesses. Together the denials reach two silver ma’ah, yet the special oath permits recovery only for the one perutah transaction witnessed by the witnesses. There is room for such a distinction. It is nevertheless difficult to fit it into the Shach’s precise language. Perhaps instead he means that the employer initially denied two ma’ah and thereby caused beit din to impose the worker’s oath, but later the employer admitted all but one perutah. Since his initial denial generated the oath, his punishment is that even after narrowing the dispute to one perutah the worker may still swear and collect. Had he admitted the larger part from the beginning, he would have benefited by avoiding the special oath. Since he falsely denied more and beit din already imposed the oath, it remains in place. This, however, is also very strained, as explained above, and the wording of the Rambam and Tur does not indicate it. What appears to me to resolve the Rambam is as follows. The Raavad’s objection is powerful: what is a poor laborer to do if he hired himself out for only a perutah? Did the Sages not care for his livelihood merely because the wage was small? Certainly, if the entire wage was only a perutah and the dispute concerns whether that perutah was paid, the worker swears and collects, as the Raavad argues. But if the worker was hired for a larger amount, at least two silver ma’ah, and the parties agree about all but one perutah, so that their actual dispute concerns only that perutah, the Raavad’s concern no longer applies. This worker’s employment was substantial, like an ordinary monetary transaction, and the parties differ only over a very small residual amount. In such a case, just as with ordinary claimants and defendants, the Sages did not consider a denial below two silver ma’ah significant enough to support the special oath. The same should apply to all who swear and collect. Since the underlying transaction was larger, but their actual dispute concerns only one perutah, the enactment does not apply. This distinction is clear and sound. The Tur’s wording is especially precise. Earlier he accepted the Rambam’s rule requiring a denial of two silver ma’ah. At the end of the siman he writes, “Even if his wage was a perutah, he swears and collects his wage.” He carefully says “his wage was a perutah,” not “his claim was a perutah.” The legal test is therefore not merely the amount currently demanded. If his entire wage was two silver ma’ah but his present disputed claim is only one perutah, there is no oath, in accordance with the Rambam’s rule. If the original wage itself was only one perutah, however, the Sages did institute the worker’s oath even for that small amount. The Mechaber likewise preserves this golden precision in the Shulchan Aruch by writing, “Even if his wage was a perutah.” He too agrees that if the original wage was larger and only a perutah remains disputed, the Rambam’s minimum applies. This is therefore how one should rule, as the Bach and the Shach conclude, for the Rambam’s view is the more lenient one. Moreover, we are dealing only with an oath created by enactment. We should extend such an enactment no further than the points on which the early authorities agree. We should not add further cases that would displace the Torah rule, “its owner shall accept it and he shall not pay.” Afterward, I saw that this very resolution appears in the Taz, though expressed in somewhat unclear language.
