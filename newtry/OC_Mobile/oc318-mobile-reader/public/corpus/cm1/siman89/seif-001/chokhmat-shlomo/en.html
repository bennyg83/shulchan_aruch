Seif 1: All oaths of the Torah are taken by the defendant, who swears and does not pay, etc. Note: In the seventh chapter of Shevuot the Gemara relates that Rav Menashya sent a ruling that the employer should swear by the Torah and the craftsman should lose. This is difficult. Why would there ever have been an initial thought that the employer should be entirely exempt from an oath? He admits part of the claim, so why should he be less liable than any other partial admitter? At first glance one could answer that here the employer has a migo that he could have claimed to have paid everything. Tosafot explain the case as one in which he has not actually paid and therefore has such a migo. In ordinary litigation, denying everything may be more brazen than admitting part, but a hired worker did not do the employer a favor. On the contrary, the worker benefited from being hired. The employer may therefore be no more reluctant to deny the whole debt than to admit part, so the initial thought could have been that the migo exempts him even from an oath. The conclusion would then be that even here complete denial is more brazen and the migo does not operate. But if so, another difficulty arises. Why does the Gemara challenge this from the baraita that within the proper time the worker swears and collects? Perhaps Shmuel can still maintain the initial premise that an employer is willing to make a complete denial, yet nevertheless obligates him to swear here because the migo produces no practical advantage. If the employer had claimed, “I paid everything,” the worker would have sworn and collected, so the employer would gain nothing by complete denial. Therefore there is no migo, and the employer must take a Torah oath on his partial admission. The baraita could then mean: within the proper time, since a claim of full payment would leave the worker able to swear and collect, the employer has no migo and must swear; after the time has passed, if the employer had said “I paid,” the burden of proof would rest upon the worker, so now the employer would have a true migo and would be exempt even from an oath. Why does the Gemara not answer this way? This requires study. Further there: all oaths of the Torah, etc. Rashi at the beginning of the chapter Kol HaNishba’in explains, “The Torah did not obligate one to swear and collect,” etc. In my eyes the wording is imprecise. Why would one have thought that the Torah obligates a person to swear and collect? He can simply choose not to collect and not to swear. It would have been more precise to say that the Torah did not grant credibility to collect by means of an oath. One could perhaps explain Rashi as referring to cases such as “I do not know whether I repaid you,” or to one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear, where the claimant collects. Rashi would mean that the Torah did not require a claimant who is already entitled to collect to take an oath as a condition of collection. Either his oath would not enable collection at all, or if he collects, he does so without an oath. Likewise, when the Mishna says, “These swear and collect,” the point would be that although they are entitled to collect, they may not collect without first swearing. Even so, it remains difficult why Rashi did not explain more simply that Torah law does not believe a claimant to collect through his oath, while the Sages enacted that in the listed cases he can collect by swearing. This requires study.
