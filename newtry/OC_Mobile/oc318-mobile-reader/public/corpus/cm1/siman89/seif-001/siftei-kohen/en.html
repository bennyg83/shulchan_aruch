The hired worker, etc. The Mordechai records a dispute whether a worker who ordinarily swears and collects may reverse the oath onto the employer. Ri Migash says that he may, apparently because one may decline a rabbinic enactment made for his benefit. Rif and Rambam say that one may not reverse a Mishnah-level oath. Maharam agrees here because the enactment was not made solely for the worker's benefit, but also for the employer, who is preoccupied with his workers and may not remember. Maharam adds that if the worker says, “Swear that you do not know with certainty that you paid me and be exempt,” the employer must swear, like any defendant who answers, “I do not know.” The source attributed to Ri Migash is apparently Semag, which cites Ri Migash and his teachers that a claimant may say, “I do not want the rabbinic enactment allowing me to swear and collect. Treat me like any other claimant,” thereby requiring the defendant to take a hesset oath. Rif and Rambam do not disagree. Their rule that a Mishnah oath cannot be reversed means that it cannot be reversed into an equivalent oath, but the claimant may certainly abandon the special enactment and demand only an ordinary hesset oath. Their reason for not reversing the Mishnah oath is that the defendant may say, “You have nothing established against me. Swear and collect if you wish; I do not wish to swear.” Maharam appears to agree with Rif and Rambam here for a different reason. In general he follows Ri and Tosafot that one who swears and collects may reverse the oath, but he treats a worker differently because the enactment was not made solely for the worker. Maharam also follows his own view that even hesset requires holding a sacred object. According to Rambam, Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and those who rule that hesset does not require a sacred object, the worker's comparison to one who says, “I do not know,” supports only a hesset oath without an object, and cannot impose an oath with an object on the employer. This follows from Rif and Rambam's stated rationale and is clear. All this assumes that the employer claims with certainty that he paid. If he says, “I do not know whether I paid,” he must pay the worker without any oath, for the worker is no worse than any other claimant.
