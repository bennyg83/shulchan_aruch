Even if his wage was one perutah, etc. The Mechaber and later authorities did not explain this correctly. Rambam first writes that his teachers held that those who swear and collect do not require a claim of two silver ma'ot, while he holds that the defendant must deny two ma'ot before the claimant swears and collects. Yet elsewhere Rambam writes that a worker, and every person who swears and collects, must swear even for one perutah. The Maggid Mishneh suggested that Rambam either followed his teachers in practice or distinguished workers from other claimants. The latter is untenable, especially because Rambam expressly says, “and likewise every person who swears and collects.” The Beit Yosef's explanation based on rolled oaths, and the Bach's explanation based on reversal, are also unsatisfactory. The correct reading is evident from Rambam's own sequence. He first states that custodians and all who swear and collect swear concerning one perutah or more. He then records his teachers' view that they need no two-ma'ot claim, and says that in his opinion the defendant must nevertheless deny two ma'ot before beit din imposes the special oath. Once that threshold denial exists and the oath is imposed, the claimant may swear and collect even one perutah. Thus Rambam distinguishes the amount denied, which must be two ma'ot, from the amount actually collected, which may be one perutah. This also reconciles the Tur's two statements. Rabbeinu Yerucham expressly explains Rambam in this way, and Semag and Hagahot Maimoniyot agree. Since these authorities concur, and the rule is lenient for the defendant, this is the halachah.
The employer swears and is exempt. The language of the Mechaber and Levush implies only a hesset oath. But the Ran, the source of this ruling, indicates that when the worker is disqualified from swearing or is a slave, the original oath returns to the employer, who must take the same Mishnah-level oath with a sacred object that the worker would otherwise have taken. The Jerusalem Talmud likewise asks rhetorically whether beit din gives an oath to a slave or a suspected oath-taker, and then discusses the case where both are suspected. Ramban in Milchamot explains that the original enactment placed the oath upon the employer because of the worker's livelihood. Since the employer is preoccupied, the Sages transferred it to the worker. Therefore, when the worker cannot swear, the employer swears and does not pay. The same applies where the worker is a minor. The Ramah cited by the Tur in siman 92, seif 13, and Baal HaItur likewise hold that a suspected worker causes the employer to take an oath resembling a Torah oath. Rambam, however, appears to rule that with all who swear and collect, including a worker, if the claimant is suspected, the opposing party takes only hesset. The Maggid Mishneh, Baal HaMaor, Ritva, Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot support this approach, at least outside the case of a document. The Jerusalem Talmud is not decisive, because it may follow the initial assumption of the Babylonian Talmud that one rabbinic enactment may be added to another. Our Gemara concludes that one enactment is not built upon another. Moreover, the Jerusalem Talmud's language appears to address all who swear and collect, not workers alone. If accepted literally, it would require the defendant to take a Mishnah-level oath in every such case, contrary to the prevailing authorities. The fact that the Sages originally considered placing the oath upon the employer does not mean that it remains there once they definitively enacted it upon the worker. Tosafot explains that the enactment was established upon the worker from the outset, and if he is disqualified, no further enactment was made. The same reasoning applies to all who swear and collect. Therefore the view of Baal HaMaor and those who require only hesset from the defendant is primary.
