Even if his wage was one perutah. See Shach, seif katan 2, who writes that the employer must deny at least two maah before beit din can impose the oath, after which the worker swears by rabbinic enactment and collects even a single perutah. The meaning is where the employer disputes both the agreed wage in an amount of two maah and also denies payment of one perutah. Although the employer is not believed regarding the disputed wage, he can still swear regarding the separate one perutah payment issue because the total denial reaches two maah. See Tumim. The primary answer is Tumim's distinction: if the worker's entire wage was only one perutah, the worker swears and collects so that he not leave empty handed and embittered. If the wage was substantial and only one perutah remains disputed, however, he does not receive the special worker's oath unless the denied amount itself is at least two maah.
The employer takes a hesset oath. See Shach, seif katan 3, who concludes that the employer takes only hesset because the original enactment imposing an oath was made for the worker's benefit. Although in a dispute over the amount of the wage the Sages placed the oath on the employer, the special rule regarding payment rests on the fact that the employer is occupied with many workers, and the enactment therefore empowers the worker to swear and collect. It resembles other cases of those who swear and collect, where if the claimant is disqualified from swearing, the employer takes only hesset.
From the heirs of the employer. This applies specifically where the heirs make a definite claim, or even where they are uncertain but the worker died at the end of the workday. If the employer died during the day, however, the worker collects without an oath because the claim arose on the very day the debt became due, as explained in Bava Metzia, and we do not plead payment on behalf of heirs where the debt is still within its due period, as explained above in siman 78. Ketzot HaChoshen likewise writes this.
The heirs of the worker. See Shach, seif katan 4, who says that Sema's lengthy discussion was unnecessary. His comment is difficult because Sema needed to discuss a case where the heirs of the worker themselves make a definite claim, as Tumim also observes. It appears that according to Baal HaTerumot, the employer takes only a hesset oath, as in the law of one who is disqualified from swearing.
