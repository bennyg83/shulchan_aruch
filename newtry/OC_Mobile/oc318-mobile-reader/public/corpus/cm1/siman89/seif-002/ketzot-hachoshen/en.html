The heirs of the employee do not. The Jerusalem Talmud says that if the employer dies, the employee swears and collects from the heirs. If the employee dies, however, his heirs do not receive the special enactment, because it was made for the employee's livelihood and not for his heirs. A difficulty arises from the rule that on the day a debt becomes due, early payment is sufficiently uncommon that we do not ordinarily advance such a payment claim for heirs. If the employer dies during the employee's payment period, why should the employee need an oath at all? And if the employee dies, why should his heirs not collect from the employer's heirs when payment during that period is uncommon? The best explanation is that the Jerusalem Talmud speaks toward the end of the employee's due day, when payment is already common because the employer seeks to avoid the prohibition of delayed wages. The employee's special enactment is then necessary. If the employer dies earlier in the day, before payment has become common, the ordinary presumption against payment can be stronger. In that case the employee, and potentially even his heirs, can collect without relying on the special wage enactment, depending upon the certainty of their claim. This distinction reconciles the Jerusalem Talmud with the rules of fixed due dates and the principle that beit din does not advance an unusual payment claim for heirs.
