Urim VeTumim writes that if there are witnesses that the work was performed but no witnesses to the hiring, then when the dispute is only over a small amount there is no migo that the employer could have denied hiring him, because in any event he must pay for the benefit received, as explained in siman 264. But if the dispute exceeds the lowest customary wage in that city, the employer has a migo that he never hired him, for then the worker would recover only the minimum customary wage. Thus both elements are needed. Urim VeTumim then suggests that one who performed work without authorization, although entitled to compensation, may not receive the special enactment allowing a hired worker to swear and collect when the owner claims payment, because the Mishna speaks of a hired worker, not one who acted on his own. Netivot HaMishpat states this plainly. Teshuvot Chemdat Shlomo, siman 7, agrees and explains that with a hired worker the owner can protect himself by hiring trustworthy laborers who will not later sue him, whereas if the enactment applied to one who enters another's property without permission, an owner could be harmed by unscrupulous persons who act without authorization. Since the rule is only a rabbinic enactment, it should not be extended. He further observes that the prohibition of delaying wages apparently does not apply to one who acted on his own. In my view that point is clear, for such a case is no stronger than hiring through an agent, where siman 339 rules that the employer does not violate the prohibition.
