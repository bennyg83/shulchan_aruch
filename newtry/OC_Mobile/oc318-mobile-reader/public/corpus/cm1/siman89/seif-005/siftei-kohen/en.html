He swears while holding a sacred object and collects. Rambam, Tur, and Semag rule this way. At first glance this is difficult, because the baraita states that while the garment remains in the craftsman's possession, the employer must bring proof, implying that the craftsman collects without an oath. Baal HaMaor, Ramban, Ran, and the Jerusalem Talmud all appear to understand the baraita according to its plain meaning. Ramban distinguishes a craftsman because, according to the view that a craftsman acquires the improvement to the object, he claims against the object itself. Even according to the contrary view, the craftsman certainly has wages due and holds the object for those wages, and the owner entrusted him concerning the amount. Ran questions why this should be stronger than any creditor holding a pledge. Ramban's first distinction is sound only according to the view that the craftsman acquires the improvement, but we rule otherwise. His second distinction is also difficult, because a creditor with a pledge likewise has a certain debt, holds the object for it, and was entrusted by the debtor. Ran suggests that the Geonim require an oath only when the creditor seeks affirmative collection from the pledge, while one who merely retains it until paid takes only hesset. He also suggests that a craftsman is stronger because his claim was initially favored by the Sages. These explanations are strained. The correct distinction follows from the baraita itself. Its middle clause says that after the garment was returned within the payment period, the craftsman swears and collects. Whether that clause is initially read like the Sages or ultimately like Rabbi Yehudah, it shows that where the worker's claim is favored and the Sages allow him to remove money from the employer, his claim is stronger. Therefore, while he holds the garment and also has a migo, he may retain it without a sacred-object oath. But according to the final law of the Sages, where the craftsman does not swear and collect after returning the garment, his claim is not stronger than the employer's. If he holds the garment, he is like any creditor holding a pledge and must swear while holding a sacred object. This reconciles Rambam, Semag, and Tur. The Jerusalem Talmud's statement that a worker holding a pledge collects without an oath concerns a worker whose claim is otherwise favored, and indeed supports the distinction. It follows that whenever one of the categories who ordinarily swears and collects is already in possession and has a migo, he need take only hesset, not an oath with a sacred object. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites Ri as holding explicitly that all such claimants who have seized collect without the special oath. The Geonim do not truly disagree, for their rule concerns an ordinary creditor with a pledge, not a claimant whose position was independently strengthened by the Sages. This also answers Raavad's challenge from Rabbah bar Sharshom. He held land of the orphans and wished to consume future produce up to the amount of his debt. Since he had a document and a migo that the land was purchased, even if the land were in the heirs' hands they could not prevent collection without an oath. Once he was in possession, he needed no further oath. A hesset oath was inapplicable because the heirs' claim was uncertain, or because hesset had not yet been enacted.
The employer swears while holding a sacred object. The Beit Yosef's discussion here is not correct. As a matter of law, where the employer does not partially admit, he takes only a hesset oath, as I wrote above in seif 4, seif katan 9.
