For he cannot claim, "It is purchased in my hand." This appears limited to the first view cited in siman 78 seif 13 in Rama, that where witnesses establish that an item is held as a pledge but the creditor does not know how much was lent upon it, he is not believed without a migo. According to the second view there, which Shach in seif katan 44 considers primary, the creditor is believed in such a case even without a migo. The same should apply here. Once it is known that the item came into the craftsman's possession for his work, and that the owner owes him some amount but the amount is unknown, the craftsman should swear and collect even without a migo. He is no worse than a pledge, and Ramban writes that the craftsman's position is even stronger. Rashba, however, cited by Beit Yosef in siman 75 seif 1, holds that a craftsman is weaker because the item did not enter his hand initially as a pledge. This resolves the sugya in Bava Batra 45, where Rabbah and Abaye dispute a craftsman. Rabbah holds that a craftsman is not like property ordinarily lent out and requires witnesses, while Abaye treats it like property ordinarily lent and holds that merely seeing the item suffices. The Gemara challenges Abaye from the baraita: a craftsman says, "You fixed my wage at two," and the owner says one. So long as the garment is in the craftsman's hand, the owner must bring proof. If he gave it back within the proper time, the craftsman swears and collects. The Gemara asks: if there are witnesses, let us see what the witnesses say. It concludes that the case is without witnesses and without seeing the item. Several difficulties are well known. Tosafot ask why the Gemara did not pose the stronger question that if there are witnesses and the item was seen, the craftsman should not be believed at all while holding it. Tosafot also ask against Abaye's answer why the baraita says, "If he gave it to him within the time," rather than simply distinguishing whether the item was seen. Further, why does the Gemara say, "Let us see what the witnesses say," since perhaps the witnesses forgot the fixed wage? Finally, Rif rules that no migo helps a craftsman, and even without witnesses he cannot claim that he purchased the item once it was seen. How then can the baraita say the craftsman is believed in a no-witness case? The answer is that the Gemara's challenge to Abaye cannot simply be that the craftsman is believed while holding the garment, because perhaps his credibility is not based on migo at all. It may be because everyone knows the garment came to him for paid work and they do not know the amount, so he is like a creditor holding a pledge whose loan amount is uncertain, and is believed even without a migo. Indeed, Ramban says a craftsman is stronger than an ordinary pledge. The Gemara's actual inference is from the language that the owner must bring proof, implying that if he cannot, the craftsman takes without the structure of swearing and collecting. If his right were only that of a creditor holding a pledge, he should swear and collect. One cannot answer, as the Gemara does elsewhere regarding a disputed fixed wage, that the baraita is teaching alternatives, either the owner brings proof or the craftsman swears and collects, because the latter clause expressly says that once the item is returned, within the proper time the craftsman swears and collects. If the same rule applied while he still held the garment, the clauses should have been combined. Now, if the special worker enactment concerning a disputed fixed wage applied only where witnesses were present at the hiring, the first clause could be explained differently from the second. While the garment is in the craftsman's hand, he is believed because he possesses collateral, even without witnesses. Once he returned the garment, however, the owner is in possession and the craftsman could collect only by virtue of the special worker enactment, which on that assumption would require hiring witnesses. The baraita could therefore separate the cases. This is the force of the Gemara's question: if witnesses are needed for the worker enactment, "let us see what the witnesses say." In the payment case the Gemara asks why payment is not simply made before witnesses and answers that arranging witnesses at the moment of payment is burdensome. But if the worker's special right in a wage dispute existed only where witnesses were already present when he was hired, those witnesses could also hear the wage fixed at that time. No additional burden would be involved, and there would be no reason for a special enactment concerning the amount of the wage. The Gemara therefore concludes that the enactment applies even without hiring witnesses. Once that is so, in the latter clause the worker swears and collects even without witnesses after returning the item. Then the two clauses would have identical law if, while holding the garment, the craftsman merely swore and collected as a pledge holder. The fact that the clauses remain separate indicates that in the first clause, while holding the garment, he is believed even without an oath. The reason is the Shach's principle that one who ordinarily must swear and collect, but also has a migo from his possession, can collect without an oath. This proves that when there are no witnesses, even if the item was seen, he has sufficient basis to say that he purchased it, which challenges Abaye unless the case is established as one where the item was not seen. This also answers Tosafot's question against Abaye: the latter clause teaches a greater point. Not only while the garment remains in the craftsman's hand does he collect, where he may independently have the strength of a pledge holder, but even after he returned it he swears and collects by virtue of the worker enactment. It further teaches that this applies even without witnesses, notwithstanding the employer's migo that he never hired him, for the reason Tosafot explain. The Rif is likewise consistent. He rules that ordinarily a craftsman cannot use a migo to claim purchase. Here the circumstances are different: the parties dispute the fixed wage, and the craftsman already has the special right to swear and collect. Added to that, his migo that he returned the item removes the need for the oath, under the Shach's rule that one who is already entitled to swear and collect and also has a migo is believed without an oath. As to the Shach's principal question against the Mechaber, who requires the craftsman holding the garment to swear while holding a sacred object, and the related objections from the Geonim's rule concerning a pledge, those objections are compelling only under the Shach's own view in siman 72 seif katan 67 that even where the debtor makes only a shema claim, the creditor holding the pledge must swear. Tumim concludes that against a shema claim the creditor need not swear, and that view is persuasive. The Geonim's requirement of an oath is based upon treating the creditor as though he were extracting money. Yet all the poskim hold that where the opposing claim is shema, a migo can support extraction. Certainly, then, where without the migo the creditor would already be believed upon an oath, the added migo permits him to collect without an oath. The craftsman baraita follows Rabbi Yehudah, who holds that the employer can be preoccupied even concerning the fixed wage, so the dispute is effectively bari against shema and a migo can help. Although Tosafot write that Rabbi Yehudah treats the employer only as possibly forgetful, that possibility does not itself impose an oath. For our halachah, however, we do not follow Rabbi Yehudah on that point. We hold that a fixed wage is remembered, producing bari against bari, and the craftsman must swear like any other creditor holding a pledge.
