The employer swears, etc. See the Shach, who writes that the Rambam’s language implies that the employer takes only a heset oath, and that this is also the view of the Baal HaMaor. They were challenged from the Yerushalmi, which says that if both the worker and the employer are suspected regarding oaths, the law depends upon the dispute discussed below as to whether an oath returns to the party originally obligated in it. This implies that if only the worker is suspected, an oath rests upon the employer. Otherwise, what connection would the case where both are suspected have to that dispute, if there were no oath on the employer at all? The Shach answers that the Yerushalmi holds that even regarding a rabbinic oath, when the obligated party is suspected, the opposing party swears and collects. It therefore accepts one enactment on top of another. Our Gemara, by contrast, holds that we do not create one enactment upon another. Thus, if the worker is suspected, there is no corresponding oath imposed upon the employer. Although it is strained to create a dispute between the two Talmuds where no explicit disagreement appears, this explanation can fit the Baal HaMaor. He holds that in a dispute over the amount of the wage, where the employer certainly remembers and denies everything, the employer has no Mishnah level oath at all, only heset. In his view the special oath was never imposed upon the employer in such a case. Rather, it was imposed upon the worker only where the employer is distracted by his workers. Where the dispute concerns the wage amount, which the employer remembers, there is no basis for a special employer oath. Likewise, where the worker is suspected, there is no independent reason to impose a special oath upon the employer, and we do not make one enactment upon another. The Rambam, however, rules that in a dispute over the agreed wage the employer does take the Mishnah level oath, because of the worker’s livelihood. According to him, the original oath was indeed placed upon the employer, and only because the employer is distracted was it transferred to the worker. Where the employer does remember, the employer’s oath remains in its original place. If so, when the worker is suspected, why should the employer be in a worse position than in a dispute over the wage? The original employer oath should return. On this approach there would be no need to say that the Yerushalmi disagrees with our Gemara. A more plausible explanation of the Rambam is that the special concern for the worker’s livelihood does not apply where the worker himself is suspected. We are not responsible to create an enhanced enactment so that a wicked person, disqualified for testimony and for an oath in beit din, should not leave disappointed. It is fitting that such a person should suffer the consequence of his own status. This works if the rationale of the enactment is the worker’s livelihood. But if the reason is that the employer is distracted by his workers and an oath may cause him to focus and remember, just as he is expected to remember in order to avoid delaying wages, then even where the worker is suspected the employer should seemingly take the oath. We explained above, however, that the statement that an employer is distracted by his workers is only an incidental basis for accommodating a minority possibility, while the essential reason for the enactment is the worker’s livelihood. The Yerushalmi, by contrast, does not mention the worker’s livelihood at all. It gives only the reason that the employer’s affairs are numerous. According to that reason alone, even where the worker is suspected the employer should still swear so that he will focus and remember. Our Gemara, which grounds the rule in concern for the worker’s livelihood, can therefore exempt the employer where the worker is suspected. This reconciles the sources. With a minor claimant, in any event, the Mishnah level oath does not apply. Since one does not swear on a minor’s claim and his assertion is not treated as a full definite claim, the Sages did not institute the special Mishnah oath. It is enough that the employer take heset. This may resolve the Rambam, though the matter remains strained and requires study. In practice, there is substantial reason to follow those authorities who hold that just as in a dispute over the agreed wage the employer takes the Mishnah oath, so too here. A further explanation of the Rambam follows Tosafot. The Gemara says that when the due time passes the employer has a presumption that he does not violate the prohibition against delaying wages and therefore focuses and remembers. Tosafot ask why the parties should not simply wait until the next day, when the employer will focus and remember, and answer that this presumption alone does not suffice unless it is joined with the presumption that a worker does not delay demanding his wages. Once the worker demanded payment during the due time, the second presumption is no longer available. The Gemara requires both presumptions because it asks in the opposite direction whether the worker would violate the prohibition against theft. It therefore concludes that on the employer’s side there are two presumptions and on the worker’s side only one. Accordingly, where the worker is himself suspected, we may say that there is no reason to impose an oath immediately. If the employer truly owes him, then when the day passes the employer will focus and remember rather than violate the prohibition against delaying wages, and he will pay. If he owes nothing, he will remain exempt. One might object that after the worker has already demanded payment, the presumption that a worker does not delay collecting his wages is unavailable. But that second presumption is needed only to offset the competing presumption that a worker does not commit theft. A worker who is suspected regarding theft and false oaths has no such presumption of honesty. Thus the employer’s single presumption, that he will not violate the prohibitions against delaying wages and theft, is enough, and no special oath is needed. The Rambam may therefore have derived his ruling directly from the Gemara, in line with Tosafot’s question and answer. Indeed, I am surprised by the general ruling that a defendant must take heset in response to a claim made by a person known to be suspected. The Gemara explains that Rav Nachman instituted heset because there is a presumption that a person does not bring a false claim. It then addresses the competing presumption that one does not brazenly deny a true claim by saying that the defendant may merely be delaying payment. That reasoning applies to a claimant who retains a presumption of honesty. But if the claimant is a notorious robber and is known to swear falsely, how can we invoke the presumption that a person does not bring a false claim? The rationale for Rav Nachman’s enactment disappears, and the Torah law should return. One can answer only with some difficulty that the Sages made a uniform rule and did not distinguish among claimants. The practical conclusion from both explanations offered above concerning the Rambam, and likewise from the rule that when a worker dies his oath is not inherited by his children, is that if the worker’s heirs themselves make a definite claim that the employer did not pay, it is possible that the employer must take an oath while holding a sacred object, as the Bach writes. The Shach below in siman 108 agrees that the employer takes only heset, relying on the Baal HaMaor. But as I explained, the Baal HaMaor is no proof for the Rambam, because the Baal HaMaor holds that even in a dispute over the agreed wage there is no oath while holding a sacred object. According to the Rambam, the matter therefore still requires study.
