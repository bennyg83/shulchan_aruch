The depreciation caused by slaughter. Tur says that if the tortfeasor slaughtered the innocuous ox he must pay the depreciation, even today when courts do not ordinarily collect fines, because if the injured party seized the fine we would not remove it. Rambam similarly rules in the laws of false witnesses that witnesses who falsely testified that a master knocked out his slave's tooth and then blinded his eye can owe both values. Raavad objects that Rambam elsewhere says the master need pay for the eye only if the slave first seized. Why should false witnesses pay before seizure? Kesef Mishneh answers that because seizure would be effective and readily possible, the false witnesses have caused real loss. Migdal Oz adds that the special law of plotting witnesses depends on what they intended to cause, and they intended a liability the slave could have enforced by seizure. Even so, one could distinguish ordinary damages from plotting witnesses. In ordinary damages, before the injured party actually seizes the fine there is no certain collectible loss, and perhaps the slaughterer should not pay depreciation merely because seizure might have occurred. I therefore think Tur and Rama's rule should itself be read where the injured party has already seized. Today we do not order collection of the fine affirmatively. If the ox remains intact, the injured party must seize the body of the innocuous ox. Once the tortfeasor slaughtered and depreciated it, seizure of other property can suffice for that depreciation. But without any seizure, beit din should not affirmatively award it.
