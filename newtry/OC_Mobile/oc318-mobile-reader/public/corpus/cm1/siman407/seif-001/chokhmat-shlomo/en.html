Seif 1. "An innocuous ox that caused damage, if the tortfeasor sold it before standing in judgment, etc." I was asked by my relative Rabbi Nachum Ginsberg to resolve a difficulty raised by his learned correspondent. Bava Kamma 33 states that if the owner consecrated the ox the consecration takes effect even according to Rabbi Akiva, because of Rabbi Avahu's ordinance that people should not say that consecrated property leaves sacred status without redemption. His correspondent asked from Bava Kamma 13 and 44, where the Gemara requires that the animal be available both for standing in judgment and for valuation, and he likewise claimed that the Rambam in Hilchot Nizkei Mamon contradicts himself within a few lines. The questioner regarded this as a severe difficulty. I replied that I am surprised at both him and his correspondent, because they overlooked Tosafot on the spot. Tosafot explain that the discussion on page 33 concerns property consecrated for Temple maintenance, because consecration of bodily sanctity can indeed override a lien. The matter is therefore clear. On page 33 the case is consecration for Temple maintenance, which does not override the victim's lien. The consecration does not truly take effect against that lien, so the ox is regarded as available for judgment; only in order that people not say consecrated property leaves without redemption is it redeemed for a token amount. On pages 13 and 44, however, the discussion concerns consecration with bodily sanctity for the altar. Such sanctity does override a lien, so the consecration takes effect and the requirement that the goring ox be present for judgment is not satisfied. This is also how to read the Rambam. When he rules that an ox which gored and was then consecrated before judgment is exempt, he refers to bodily sanctity. When he later says it is redeemed for a token amount, he refers to consecration for Temple maintenance. Although he does not spell out the distinction, it is the Rambam's common practice not to add distinctions that are not explicit in the Gemara. There is therefore no beginning of a contradiction. The questioner later responded with objections, and I answered him at length, with the help of Heaven, in my work on Choshen Mishpat, fourth edition, page 106a. See there; this is not the place to elaborate.
