Yam Shel Shlomo objects that according to our ruling like Rabbi Akiva, an innocuous ox should not be sold even for temporary use before judgment, because the injured party ultimately owns the ox itself. In my view Rambam can read the baraita as following Rabbi Akiva. Even according to Rabbi Akiva, the injured party does not acquire the fine before standing in judgment, because an admission would still exempt the tortfeasor. The statement elsewhere that the injured party can consecrate the ox can be understood after judgment. Thus, before judgment, the owner can sell the ox for its use. Once the court establishes liability, the injured party can nevertheless collect the body of the ox from the purchaser, just as a creditor who acquires a lien only prospectively can still collect encumbered property sold earlier. Consecration likewise does not strengthen the owner's hand against the injured party beyond the ordinary rules governing consecrated encumbrances. The baraita's statement that sale after judgment is ineffective therefore can fit Rabbi Akiva. Before judgment the purchaser obtains temporary use, but the body remains subject to the injured party's later collection. Once judgment has occurred, the ox is already awarded to the injured party and cannot be sold by the tortfeasor. This appears to be Rambam's approach. Tosafot reads the baraita differently, but Rambam's structure is internally coherent.
