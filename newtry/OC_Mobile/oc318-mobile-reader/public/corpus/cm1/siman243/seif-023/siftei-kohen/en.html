A minor girl acquires through her courtyard, etc. The reason is stated in the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia 11a: a woman's courtyard was included by reason of her hand. Just as a minor girl has a hand, she has a courtyard. A man's courtyard, however, was included by reason of agency. Just as a minor boy lacks agency, he lacks a courtyard. This appears to imply that whenever the courtyard of an adult woman acquires, the courtyard of a minor girl acquires as well. Yet the discussion at the beginning of Bava Metzia 12a, where Rav Ashi says that a courtyard was included by reason of hand and is no weaker than agency, seems to prove otherwise. Regarding a bill of divorce, which is detrimental to the woman, one may not impose a detriment upon a person in her absence. Rashi explains that when she is not standing beside her courtyard, so that the courtyard cannot be compared to her hand because it is not adjacent to her, acquisition must derive from agency. This proves explicitly that only when she stands beside her courtyard was it included by reason of hand. When she does not stand beside it, even a protected courtyard operates only by reason of agency, and in such a case a minor girl has no courtyard. Tosafot there at the beginning of page 12 likewise write explicitly that a courtyard was included by reason of hand, and therefore when it resembles her hand, namely when she stands beside it, it acquires even against her will and even for a minor girl, like her hand. When she is not adjacent to the courtyard and it does not resemble her hand, it is nevertheless no weaker than agency, etc. The Rosh and Ritva there likewise state this explicitly, as does the Ran cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of seif 25 of this siman. In my humble opinion, however, the statement above on page 10 that a minor girl has a courtyard should be understood specifically when she stands beside it. The Gemara speaks generally only because this is the standard case. It also implies that her law resembles that of an adult woman, meaning that acquisition occurs either through a protected courtyard or when she stands beside it. This also appears explicit from the Rambam, Semag on page 161, column 4, and the Mechaber, who first write, 'When does this apply? In a protected courtyard,' etc., and immediately afterward write that a minor girl acquires through her courtyard. The implication is even stronger in the Tur and the Rama's gloss, because they hold that regarding a gift even an unprotected courtyard acquires although she is not standing beside it, and immediately afterward write that a minor girl acquires through her courtyard. According to this, the Tur would necessarily disagree with the Rosh. I therefore propose to explain the passage unlike Rashi, Tosafot, and those who follow them. Even apart from this, their interpretation has a difficulty in the Gemara's expression, 'and it is no weaker than agency.' If it is not hand, it is actual agency. What does it mean that it is 'no weaker than agency'? Rather, the explanation appears to be as follows. A courtyard was included by reason of hand and always has the legal status of hand. Nevertheless, this hand is no weaker than agency. Just as in ordinary agency one may not impose a detriment upon a person in her absence, so too regarding a bill of divorce, which is detrimental to her, one may not impose that detriment in her absence. Although the courtyard is her hand, because the matter is detrimental to her it is no weaker than agency, and we are certain that she does not wish her courtyard to serve as her hand to receive the divorce. When she stands beside her house, however, the detriment is imposed in her presence. I found this explanation explicitly in the Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia, on the passage 'he saw them running,' where he writes that if one stands beside his field, acquisition occurs because it is then guarded by him. A protected courtyard, which does not require him to stand beside the field, requires explanation. The Ran writes that any courtyard guarded for the person to whom acquisition is beneficial is regarded as his hand, because whatever comes into his hand he is pleased to acquire, and it was placed in the protected courtyard as an extension of his hand. This is unlike bills of divorce, where because acquisition is not beneficial to her, a protected courtyard is not regarded as her hand. When something is detrimental to a person while in his hand, he does not place it in a protected courtyard. On the contrary, he removes it from himself. Although this contradicts the Ran's own words cited by the Beit Yosef at the end of seif 25 of this siman, it appears that the Ran later retracted his earlier view. This is the principal ruling. Examine carefully.
Specifically, a minor girl who has no father, etc. This ruling requires study. The Nimukei Yosef indeed writes that a minor girl who has a father has no independent legal hand, as stated in the chapter HaTikabel, and that the Rif writes likewise. Yet the dispute among the poskim is well known: Rashi, Tosafot, Baal HaMaor, Baal HaItur, and the Rosh disagree with the Rif there in the chapter HaTikabel and hold that even a minor girl who has a father has an independent legal hand. The Mechaber himself cites the view of these dissenting authorities in Even HaEzer, siman 141, seif 4. Moreover, it appears to me that even the Nimukei Yosef refers only to a bill of divorce, not to a found object or a gift. Although the law of a found object is derived from that of a bill of divorce with respect to a courtyard being treated as her hand, this particular point cannot be derived from there. A minor girl who has a father cannot receive her bill of divorce, not because of any deficiency inherent in the girl, but because divorce was compared to betrothal. The Torah gave the father the right to betroth his minor daughter, as it is written, 'I gave my daughter to this man.' Her hand therefore has no legal significance for betrothal and divorce where her father's hand is present, for the Torah displaced her hand and assigned that authority to her father. Nevertheless, a minor girl does possess a legal hand, since when she has no father she may receive her own bill of divorce. Thus, only regarding divorce can it be said that when she has a father she has no hand. If another person gives her an object as a gift, however, there is no reason to say that she does not acquire the gift merely because she has a father. Since she has a hand, she likewise has a courtyard. Examine this carefully. See Tosafot Rid to Kiddushin 44a and the Ritva there.
But a minor boy, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 37, who writes that there was no need to derive the law for an adult woman, since it had already been learned from the verse, 'If the stolen article shall indeed be found,' etc. This is incorrect. What he writes afterward, that the verse 'and he shall send' comes to include a minor girl, and specifically a minor girl because that verse was written regarding a minor girl, is likewise incorrect. Rather, the reason is straightforward. Although the verse speaks of an adult woman, her courtyard was included by reason of her hand. Since a minor girl has a hand, she likewise has a courtyard, for it is impossible to distinguish between an adult woman and a minor girl when both possess a legal hand. A male's courtyard, however, was included by reason of agency. Since a minor boy cannot appoint an agent, he has no courtyard. This is the explanation in the Gemara, the Rif, and the Rosh. See Tosafot and Rashi there. As for the Sema's objection to the Ir Shushan, one who examines the Ir Shushan will see that his words can be reconciled in accordance with what I have written. See there and examine carefully.
