But a minor does not acquire until the gift reaches his hand. Bava Metzia disputes whether a male minor has courtyard acquisition. Regarding a girl and divorce, the courtyard is derived from her hand. Regarding finds, one opinion does not derive the boy from the girl. There is an apparent difficulty. Even if a man's courtyard works through agency rather than hand, acquisition for another in a beneficial matter is available to a minor according to many authorities, at least rabbinically and according to some by Torah law. A found object or gift is an obvious benefit. Why then should the minor's courtyard not acquire for him as an agent just as an adult can acquire on his behalf? According to the authorities who hold that acquisition for a minor is only rabbinic because acquisition itself is a form of agency, the answer is clear. The Sages instituted that an adult can acquire for the minor, but they did not extend that enactment to the minor's own courtyard. The Talmudic discussion therefore itself supports Tosafot's view that acquisition on behalf of a minor is rooted in agency. According to those who hold that such acquisition is Torah law, however, the lack of courtyard acquisition remains difficult.
Raavad provides a solution. Rambam says that if a minor buys land, pays, and makes hazakah, the land remains his because he is like one absent and one can acquire a benefit for a person in his absence. Raavad objects that, on Rambam's logic, if a minor rents the place of movables, the seller's intent should allow the place to acquire them for him. Raavad therefore says that the statement that a minor has no courtyard or four cubits applies only to ownerless finds. This fits the Gemara's wording. The dispute is framed specifically as one concerning a find. If the question were whether a minor has courtyard acquisition for all monetary law, the Gemara should have spoken of gifts, where his hand can acquire by Torah law. The choice of a find shows that where another person intentionally transfers property to him, his courtyard can indeed acquire through the beneficial acquisition principle. Only ownerless property lacks another person's transferring intent. Maggid Mishneh leaves this unresolved, but the wording strongly supports Raavad.
Even according to Raavad, the minor's own courtyard fails for a found object because there is no transferring person. But an adult can lift a found object for a minor. In that case the adult himself supplies the needed intent. The rule that a competent person can lift for a deaf person likewise demonstrates that a found object can be acquired for the disabled recipient when an adult performs the acquisition. This differs from the recipient's own courtyard, where no human transferring or acquiring intent exists. The distinction is exact.
