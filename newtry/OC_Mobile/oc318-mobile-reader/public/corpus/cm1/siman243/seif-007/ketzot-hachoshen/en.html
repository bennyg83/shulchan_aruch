And they said, on condition that the deeds be written. Ir Shushan disagrees with Sema. He reasons that the usual rule allowing the transaction to stand even if the condition cannot be forced rests on the fact that the recipient should have stipulated for his own benefit but did not, whereas the donor's statement can be treated as mere embellishment. Here the condition benefits both sides, because each wants a deed against the other. Therefore the witnesses should fulfill the condition by writing the deeds, and the underlying acquisition should stand. Sema objects that Rosh's case involved a condition that the witnesses give the recipient two hundred zuz. There the witnesses could theoretically fulfill the condition themselves by paying. Yet because the condition had been stated by the donor, Rosh treated it as mere embellishment. By contrast, where the condition is that deeds be written, the witnesses cannot write against a party's protest. Therefore Ir Shushan's forced performance theory cannot work. Taz simply agrees with Sema. In my view Ir Shushan's conclusion is correct, but for a different reason. Where a condition exists solely for the recipient's benefit and is not fulfilled, the recipient can say, "I regard it as though I received it," and waive the condition. This is codified in Even HaEzer. Why, then, when a seller says, "on condition that you write him a deed," does the Gemara say the seller can retract from both the deed and the field? Why can the recipient not simply say, "I regard the deed as though received"? Ramban and Ran explain that even though the deed benefits the recipient, the seller also inserted the condition in order to preserve for himself the power to retract until the deed is written. Therefore the recipient cannot unilaterally waive it. But where both parties say, "on condition that the deeds be written," each one is stipulating for his own benefit. If one party later prevents writing of the deed that was intended for the other, the other party may say, "I regard my deed as though received," because that part of the condition existed only for his own benefit. The witnesses still cannot write against protest. Ir Shushan's result is correct not because they can be forced to write, but because each beneficiary can waive the deed intended for himself.
Rosh's responsum concerning Rachel and Shimon appears at first to contradict this. Rachel was hiding because of taxes. A matchmaker arranged her engagement to Shimon, penalties were stipulated, and a kinyan was made. They instructed the witnesses to write debt instruments for five thousand against each party, to be held in escrow, with the understanding that if either delayed the marriage the two documents would be delivered to the other side. When Rachel's relatives shamed her for the engagement, she regretted it and protested to the witnesses not to write the documents. The matchmaker nevertheless pressured the witnesses to write and deposit them. Rosh ruled that because the kinyan had been made on condition that the documents be written and held in escrow, the matter resembled the Talmudic rule of a sale "on condition that you write the deed." Since Rachel had protested, the documents were ineffective and the engagement arrangement failed. According to what I wrote, Shimon should have been able to say, "I regard my deed as though received." The answer is that Shimon had already insisted on literal fulfillment of the condition. He did not waive the deed. He pressured the witnesses to write it, showing that he was particular about the condition. Once a party has insisted on a condition and the condition then fails, the underlying transaction is cancelled. Ran says similarly concerning physical defects in kiddushin: once a party learns of the defect and objects, the kiddushin are cancelled, and later waiver does not necessarily revive them. Beit Shmuel suggests that in monetary conditions a later waiver can perhaps still help even after initial insistence, because the waiver can be treated as though the condition was fulfilled. In my view that is not compelling. Once the beneficiary insists and thereby acquires a right to retract, the other party also gains a reciprocal right to treat the transaction as cancelled. Nimukei Yosef says similarly in overreaching: once the injured party elects to demand cancellation, the other party can also retract. Therefore later unilateral waiver need not revive the arrangement. Ir Shushan adds another point. Where the embarrassment caused by the failed engagement cannot be repaired, why should the withdrawing party escape the stipulated penalty merely because the document condition failed? This has force according to the Shach's view in simanim 207 and 333 that embarrassment damages can be payable even without a formal kinyan. Sema answers that by making the entire arrangement conditional on the writing of the deeds, each side effectively waived any separate embarrassment claim if the other side later prevented the writing. I do not find that necessary. The condition can be understood as governing the engagement transaction itself, while a distinct liability for embarrassment can remain, just as a borrower or purchaser whose conditional transaction fails can still recover the money he actually advanced. However, according to what I wrote in siman 207, embarrassment alone ordinarily does not create civil liability unless a kinyan was made upon the stipulated penalty. If the very kinyan containing the penalty becomes ineffective because its condition failed, then the embarrassment claim lacks the kinyan needed to support it. Ir Shushan may therefore depend on the Shach's broader view that embarrassment itself can create liability without such a kinyan.
