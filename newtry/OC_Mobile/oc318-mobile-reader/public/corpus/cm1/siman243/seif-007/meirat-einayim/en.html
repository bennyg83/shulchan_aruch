On condition that you write the document for him, etc. The reason for the ruling through the words "and the gift too is void" is that once he said "on condition," the gift was made dependent upon the writing. Without the writing, the gift does not take effect. Since his protest is effective to prevent the document from being written, the gift of the field, which depends on that writing, is automatically void. This differs from the preceding seif, which concerns a case where he did not say "on condition."
Or that they not give him two hundred zuz, and the gift is void, etc. The Rosh writes that we do not say that they should nevertheless fulfill the condition against his will by giving the recipient two hundred zuz from their own funds, which imposes no liability on the giver, so that the act would take effect retroactively. The reason is that this condition is merely conciliatory language for the purchaser's benefit. It was incumbent upon the purchaser to stipulate it, not upon the seller, and such a condition is not a true condition. One cannot argue that if it is not a true condition it should be treated as nonexistent so that the act should stand. It is treated as nonexistent only insofar as it benefits the purchaser, since he did not stipulate it himself. But insofar as the condition benefits the seller, it remains operative. Thus whenever he retracts the condition, the transaction is void, because he made the transaction depend upon it.
The same applies to two people who made a kinyan, etc. The Responsa Rosh there does not state this exactly. Rather, they stipulated penalties between themselves for whoever would breach the agreement, and they made a kinyan regarding those penalties on condition that documents be written. Only in such a case do we find that the kinyan takes effect, because it concerns money: he obligates his property for the stated sum if he fails to fulfill what he accepted. There is then a distinction between whether he said "on condition that a document be written" and whether he did not say "on condition." But a kinyan over a mere personal obligation that the two should marry one another, as Rama writes here, is not found, for that would be a kinyan devarim. It therefore appears that engagement documents customarily state named guarantors for the penalty, because guarantors for the mere obligation that the parties marry one another would not create a valid kinyan.
And they said, "on condition that the documents be written," etc. Specifically where he said "on condition," thereby making the kinyan of the engagement dependent upon writing the documents, if he then protests against the writing and has the right to do so, as explained above, the engagement kinyan is likewise void, just as with a gift of a field on condition that a document be written for the recipient. But if he did not say "on condition," the engagement kinyan is not void, and he is liable for the penalty for the embarrassment caused. This is explicit in the Responsa Rosh. Rama's gloss in Even HaEzer 50:6 must be understood this way as well. There he writes that engagement penalties are collected because of the embarrassment, and concludes, "specifically if the penalty documents were already written." Although his wording is compressed, he should have said, "specifically where he accepted a penalty and did not make it conditional on the documents being written," because if he did not make it conditional, even if the documents were never written and he later protested against writing them, the penalty is collected. Nevertheless, this is clearly his intent, because he concludes there, "but if they accepted a kinyan on condition that it be written, etc., and he protested, etc." Thus the matter depends on the phrase "on condition." In the first clause he means: specifically if the documents were written, then even if at the outset he said "on condition that they be written," the penalty is collected, since they were in fact written. If they were not yet written and he now protests, it is different. Even there, "written" is not precise. It means written and delivered, for so long as they have not been delivered he can retract, as the Tur and Mechaber write here in seif 7.
If he protests against writing the document, the kinyan is void. From what the Tur and Mechaber wrote immediately above, it follows that even if the engagement terms were already written but had not yet been delivered to the parties, if he protests against their delivery they are not delivered. The Responsa Rosh plainly indicates the same. See the Ir Shushan, who attributes this ruling to Rama and challenges it. He writes that even if the kinyan was made on condition, a protest against signing the terms should not void it, because the reason the transaction is void, rather than forcing fulfillment of the condition, is that where the condition benefits the recipient it was incumbent upon the recipient to stipulate it, and since only the giver stipulated it, it is mere conciliatory language. That does not apply to engagement conditions, where the parties made a kinyan that they would marry one another on condition that the documents be written. Writing the documents benefits each party, since each wants a document against the other to sue if the other breaches. Therefore the witnesses should fulfill the condition by writing the documents, and the engagement kinyan should take effect retroactively against his will. This is the substance of his words. It is surprising that he attributed the ruling to Rama without examining its source, for it comes from the Rosh himself, Responsa, klal 34, siman 3. There the Rosh explains the reason: since writing the document creates a liability against him, how can they write a document to his detriment without his permission? Where he appointed them as agents to write, he can also revoke their agency. In that case the parties had made a kinyan to marry one another on condition that a document for a specified sum be written against each and that the documents be placed with one of the witnesses, who would deliver both documents to the party who performed if the other breached. The Rosh writes that placing the document in the trustee's hand is a liability against him. He appointed them as agents to place it there, and before it reached the trustee he revoked their agency. Once that agency was revoked, delivery to the trustee was void, and consequently the writing itself was also void, because the two depend upon one another. The document was thus written unlawfully and is merely a shard. This is explicit against the Ir Shushan. It also shows that the Rosh does not base cancellation of the condition "on condition that the documents be written" on the theory of conciliatory words. He does not mention that reason in the responsum. In chapter HaSefinah, where he does use that reason, he says it only regarding Reuven who tells witnesses, "Make a kinyan from me that I will give this field to Shimon on condition that you give him two hundred zuz." Their giving two hundred zuz to the recipient creates no liability for the giver, and in that case the Rosh invokes conciliatory language. But where he says, "on condition that you write the document for him," writing it creates a liability for the giver, since documents will proliferate against him. As for the Ir Shushan's point that the bridegrooms intend the writing of engagement documents for their own benefit, so each will hold a document against the other if he breaches, one can answer that the Rosh understood each party's kinyan to require that documents be written for both sides, and writing both documents and depositing them together entails liability. Even if each initially intended the writing for his own benefit, once he later regrets the engagement and the writing creates a liability against him, he may revoke the agency insofar as it harms him. A kinyan sudar obligating the writing itself is merely a kinyan devarim. The claim that the witnesses should nevertheless fulfill the condition against his will, thereby preserving the engagement kinyan, is precisely what the Rosh rejects: we say that only where they were told to give the recipient two hundred zuz, not where they were told to write him a document that imposes liability. Since the matters were linked, cancellation of the writing voids the engagement kinyan retroactively. Nor is there force to the Ir Shushan's objection that there is no way to repair the embarrassment suffered by the other party, so why should the breaching party escape the penalty, unlike a borrower or purchaser who retracts from writing a loan or sale document, where the other can recover his money? Here each party waived the other's embarrassment if the other would protest against writing the document, for otherwise he should not have remained silent when the engagement kinyan was made conditional upon writing the document. This is all the more so if both expressly made the condition. The fact that both focused on the same matter is itself evidence of that understanding. The Rosh further concludes that there is another ground to void the arrangement as an asmachta, since Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam require a kinyan before a prominent beit din. This is not the primary reason, because the Tur and Rosh themselves write that the basic reason engagement penalties are collected without being treated as asmachta is the embarrassment to the other party, as the Tur cites them in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 50 and in Choshen Mishpat at the end of siman 207. We must therefore say that the Rosh meant that since strictly speaking the penalty would not be collectible without a kinyan before a prominent beit din, and it is only because of embarrassment that the Sages empowered collection, all the stated reasons are required. Without them, the penalty cannot be extracted from the breaching party despite the embarrassment. All this appears clear to me, unlike the Ir Shushan, who sharply questioned Rama's ruling. As I have explained, the Rosh himself, who elsewhere gives the reason of conciliatory language, also writes here that the two matters depend on one another and both are void when one retracts from writing the document. The reasoning is clear and sound; examine it carefully.
