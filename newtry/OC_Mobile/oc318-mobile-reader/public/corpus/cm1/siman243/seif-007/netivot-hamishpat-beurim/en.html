Ran in Kiddushin asks why, where a condition was imposed for the recipient's benefit, the recipient cannot simply say, "I regard myself as though I received it." Ran answers that the seller too may have imposed the condition so that he would retain an option to retract before the condition was fulfilled. Ketzot therefore says that where the parties conditioned that two deeds be written, each party imposed that writing only for his own benefit and not for the other's. If the other party later protests, the first can say, "As to my benefit, I regard myself as though I received the deed." He further argues that Rosh's responsum is no proof, because there the litigant had actually forced the witnesses to write the deeds and thereby demonstrated insistence on the condition. Once he insisted, later waiver should not help, similar to the law of conditions in Even HaEzer siman 38. Although Beit Shmuel says monetary conditions may be waived even after insistence, Ketzot argues this is not compelling and compares it to ona'ah. In my view this defense is not persuasive. In kiddushin, the relevant "insistence" means the person expressly says, "I do not want the kiddushin unless the condition is fulfilled," thereby making the transaction fail. Merely forcing performance of a condition that benefits him does not necessarily show that if the condition were not performed he intended the entire transaction to be void. Therefore, even after pressing for performance, he can sometimes later say, "I regard myself as though I received it." More fundamentally, if the condition said that two deeds be written, it is as though they conditioned that one deed be delivered to him and another to the opposite party. With respect to the other party's deed, that other party can revoke the witnesses' authority to write or deliver. Once that portion of the condition fails, the transaction itself can fail, just as in a condition "on condition that you write him the deed." Therefore the Ir Shushan's broad rule cannot be sustained merely by saying each condition was exclusively for that party's own benefit.
