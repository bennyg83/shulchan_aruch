Even a one day old child. The authorities dispute whether beit din may redeem a firstborn son whose father died before the thirtieth day. One view says beit din can act for the child through the law of acquisition on behalf of another. Taz objects that a child may prefer to perform his own mitzvah later, so the present act is not an unqualified benefit. Shach responds that if beit din first vests the redemption money in the child, or uses money already belonging to him, the mitzvah is in fact performed with the child's own money. He also cites guardians who separate tithes for orphans and the division of Eretz Yisrael through tribal leaders as proof that beit din may act for minors even where some detriment accompanies the benefit. These proofs are not decisive. Separating from the child's own produce is not ordinary acquisition on behalf of another. Agency requires the principal's knowledge. The question of terumah differs because priestly gifts are already considered separated in part and the guardian's act resembles division of jointly held property. Likewise, the tribal princes' division was explained by Ran as merely clarifying the minors' own preexisting shares. It does not prove a general Torah power to acquire on behalf of a minor. The fundamental dispute is whether acquisition on behalf of a minor is Torah law or only rabbinic. The version of Bava Metzia cited by Tosafot says that a minor has acquisition only rabbinically because acquisition is a branch of agency and a minor lacks agency. Several early authorities accept that version. If so, beit din cannot create a Torah redemption merely through rabbinic acquisition. Nor does it help to use the minor's existing money. There the transaction is not acquisition on his behalf at all. It is payment from his property and requires agency, which a minor lacks. If beit din uses its own money and seeks to transfer the mitzvah to him, the transfer itself is only rabbinic according to these authorities. The guardian's power to buy a sukkah, lulav, tzitzit, and other mitzvah objects for orphans is itself rabbinic, just as minors are not personally obligated in those mitzvot by Torah law. It therefore proves no Torah redemption power. There is also a more fundamental argument, stated by my grandfather in Maadaney Melech: a minor is not presently personally obligated in redemption at all. Even according to the view that a lien is Torah law, property serves as guarantor only where a personal debtor exists. A minor who is exempt from bodily monetary liability, even for his own theft, has no personal debt for his property to guarantee. Thus it remains difficult to say that beit din can redeem him before he reaches legal obligation.
A maidservant who sees dough about to spoil should not, in my view, separate challah from the owner's dough without authorization merely on the theory that one may acquire a benefit for another in his absence. Terumat HaDeshen permitted it, reasoning that saving the dough from spoilage is a benefit and that even without danger of spoilage separation today is uniform in amount. Rama and Taz follow him. Rashba and Tosafot in Nedarim distinguish sharply between agency and acquisition on behalf of another. Agency does not depend merely on whether the act benefits the principal. The Torah requires an agent comparable to the principal and appointed with his knowledge. That is why an unauthorized person who separates the owner's own terumah does not become an agent merely because the owner later approves. By contrast, one who separates his own produce for another person's obligation is giving the other person something from his own property, and the question is genuinely one of benefit and acquisition in absence. The maidservant is separating from the owner's dough itself. This is not the acquisition of a new object for the owner. It is an act that can be performed only by the owner or his agent. Therefore the owner's knowledge and appointment are required. The fact that the dough may spoil does not convert the act into ordinary acquisition on his behalf. The owner can still separate challah after baking if necessary. Tosafot in Nazir supports this. They discuss a woman's appointment of another woman to separate challah from dough that has not yet been kneaded and treat the issue as one of agency for something not yet in existence. If authorization were unnecessary because separation is always a benefit, the entire question would disappear. Therefore one should be stringent and not allow the servant to separate from the owner's dough without authority.
