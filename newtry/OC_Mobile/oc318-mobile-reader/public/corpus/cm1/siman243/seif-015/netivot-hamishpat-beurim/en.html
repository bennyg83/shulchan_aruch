A pebble, and he throws it away. Ketzot HaChoshen sought to resolve Tosafot's question in Eruvin 49. They ask that there the Gemara states that a minor has no capacity to acquire, whereas Gittin 64 indicates that a minor girl can acquire with respect to rabbinic shitufei mevo'ot. He answers that even for shitufei mevo'ot a minor acquires only through a mode of acquisition available to the minor personally, as Tosafot in Gittin write according to Rav Yehudah regarding Torah level acquisition. An eruv operates through acquisition, and Tosafot write that it has the legal character of monetary consideration. Since for the relevant acquisition only meshichah, not money, is effective, and that mode is not available to the minor personally, it is ineffective even for eruv. By contrast, the question in Gittin concerns a minor girl, for whom other acquisitions, including acquisition by money, can be effective and are available to her personally; therefore she can acquire for shitufei mevo'ot. See there. I am astonished, however, for in an eruv one must acquire rights in the houses, which are land, and even a minor girl cannot acquire land. Thus there is no mode available to her personally.
Another person's intent confers acquisition. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Peri Chadash that one may give the five selaim of pidyon haben to a minor kohen because the minor acquires them when another person's intent confers the property upon him. His proof is that priestly gifts may be sent to the homes of a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor, implying that a minor kohen acquires the gifts. Ketzot questions this, for Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia states that with priestly gifts, because the owner's benefit of choosing the recipient has no monetary status, it is not regarded as another person's intent conferring acquisition. The five selaim are different because there is no law of separation applying to them and they remain the owner's money, so the principle of another person's intent can apply. Moreover, it cannot be that the reason priestly gifts may be sent to their homes is that another person's intent confers acquisition, since they may also be sent to a mentally incompetent kohen, for whom that principle is ineffective. Ketzot therefore explains that although a minor cannot acquire gifts to the poor, as seen at the beginning of Bava Metzia regarding leket and pe'ah, that is because Scripture requires those gifts to be left. Priestly gifts, by contrast, are acquired by the kohanim in exchange for their service and resemble the wages of a minor, which Tosafot at the beginning of Ben Sorer state a minor acquires by Torah law. In my humble opinion this is unpersuasive, because minors are not fit for the service. Ketzot further writes that because payment against the creditor's will constitutes payment, giving is effective even to a minor, since the recipient's intent is unnecessary, just as a divorce can take effect against the wife's will by Torah law even where she is mentally incompetent. So too here the giver discharges his obligation by giving it to the kohen against his will, even though the kohen acquires against his will. Ketzot ultimately questions this from the requirement that the five selaim of pidyon haben be appraised, because without appraisal the kohen does not rely upon their value. This indicates that the kohen's cognizance is required and that one does not fulfill the obligation by giving against his will. See there. In my humble opinion, the reason priestly gifts may be sent to a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is different. When the Torah says "and he shall give," it does not impose an ordinary debt upon the owner. Rather, the Torah grants the owner the benefit of choosing the kohen to whom he gives. If the owner wishes to relinquish that benefit, he has no independent obligation of transfer. Proof is that one may betroth a woman with terumah by means of the benefit of choosing its recipient, showing that the owner does not bear a personal debt to a particular kohen. He can therefore give the gift to one who is deaf, mentally incompetent, or a minor, since he need not effect acquisition for the kohen. A kohen can take it himself, and such recipients can likewise take it. In the year of removal, however, where the owner must cause the produce to leave his possession, as shown at the beginning of Bava Metzia in the case of "the tithe is given to Yehoshua and its place is rented to him," giving it to a deaf mute, mentally incompetent person, or minor is ineffective unless they consume it immediately, because through their consumption it leaves the owner's possession. With the five selaim of pidyon haben, by contrast, the owner's benefit of choosing the recipient cannot be the operative concept, since pidyon haben creates both a personal lien and a lien upon property, as explained in Bekhorot. Still, what Ketzot cites in the name of Peri Chadash, that one may give the five selaim to the kohen against his will and thereby discharge the redemption because forced giving and forced payment are considered giving and payment, does not appear correct to me. Forced payment is considered payment where one owes a specific creditor. Here, however, pidyon haben resembles what Bava Batra 127 says concerning a firstborn's double inheritance share, that the firstborn may say, "I do not inherit and I do not pay." Rashbam there, s.v. eino notel, and siman 126 explain that Scripture calls it a gift, so if he wishes he may accept it, but if he protests one cannot confer a benefit upon a person against his will. All the more so for priestly gifts, which Scripture expressly calls gifts, as in "this shall be yours from their gift portion" and many similar verses. It is obvious that one cannot confer them upon a kohen against his will. There are many kohanim in the world, and if one refuses, the obligation remains toward the other kohanim. Ketzot's comparison of payment to a mentally incompetent creditor with divorce of a mentally incompetent woman also appears incorrect. Were it not necessary in divorce that she be capable of guarding the get, he could indeed divorce her because her consent is not required. But one certainly cannot repay a creditor who became mentally incompetent merely by placing the money before him. Although forced payment is payment, if he throws it before the creditor in a place where the creditor cannot guard it, he surely has not discharged the debt. Giving it to a mentally incompetent person is like placing it where it cannot be guarded. The same applies, in my opinion, to giving the five selaim to a minor kohen. If they are given to the minor and then lost from his possession, the giver has not fulfilled the requirement of giving, because the minor has not yet acquired them as his property. An adult kohen could seize them from him, as is indicated in Bava Metzia 6, Tosafot s.v. veha hacha dechi takfah. Tosafot ask why they are not taken from the kohen and answer that the owner can claim only the benefit of choosing the recipient. The Mishnah there also teaches concerning a firstborn human that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant, showing that if a kohen seized it one does not remove it from him. Since, before the giving, the owner retains only the benefit of choosing the recipient, giving to the minor resembles giving him something that is already his and is not regarded as another person's intent conferring acquisition such that the minor's possession should count as a valid giving. If, however, one feeds the five selaim to a minor kohen, so that the minor acquires through consumption, the giver has fulfilled the requirement even though one cannot otherwise confer acquisition upon the minor. The Torah did not require the owner personally to effect the kohen's acquisition, as is shown by the fact that even if a kohen seized the payment it is effective. Whenever a kohen, adult or minor, derives benefit from the five selaim, the son is redeemed.
