A minor to whom one gives a pebble and he throws it away. Gittin teaches that a child who throws away a pebble but takes a nut can acquire for himself, though not for others. Shmuel says that for rabbinic partnership of an alley a minor can acquire for others. Eruvin, however, records Shmuel's view that an eruv functions as a kinyan, and there a minor cannot effect the required acquisition. Tosafot asks how these statements fit together. The answer is that even for a rabbinic institution a minor can acquire for others only through a mode by which he himself can acquire. This is the principle Tosafot uses even according to the view that a minor can acquire for others by Torah law: the exclusion of a minor from agency concerns a matter he cannot do for himself, but where he himself can acquire he can also acquire for another. An eruv, according to Shmuel, transfers a right by a monetary or barter type acquisition. A male minor does not acquire through money or sudar kinyan, even though he can acquire movables by meshichah. Therefore he cannot acquire the eruv right for others either. A female minor is different. Where she has no father, she has courtyard acquisition and consequently can also acquire through barter and money under the relevant rules. This explains why the baraita can use a Hebrew maidservant who has not yet developed signs to acquire the alley partnership for others, while it cannot use the householder's own minor son or daughter. The daughter who remains under her father's authority lacks the independent courtyard right needed for these acquisitions. Thus Shmuel's statement in Eruvin about a minor is exact, while the maidservant case remains valid.
Specifically where another person's intent transfers it to him. Pri Chadash ruled that the five sela of redemption of a firstborn son may be given to a minor kohen, because a minor acquires by Torah law where another person's transferring intent is present. He also cited the rule that a deaf person, mentally incompetent person, or minor kohen is not given terumah publicly at the threshing floor because of the dignity of terumah, but gifts are sent to their homes. In my view ordinary priestly gifts cannot be explained as acquisition through another person's transferring intent. Since the owner's discretionary benefit in choosing the kohen is not monetary property, the owner has no ownership in the gift itself to transfer. The kohen acquires what already belongs to the kohanim, much like ownerless property rather than an ordinary gift. Responsa Maimoniyot makes this point concerning separated tithes: where the owner has only discretionary benefit, that is not another person's property being transferred. By contrast, before poor tithe has been separated from the owner's own money, the money remains his and a later transfer can indeed involve another person's intent. Redemption of the firstborn is stronger for Pri Chadash, because the father remains responsible for the five sela until they reach a kohen, and the particular coins he chooses remain his property until delivery. Yet the rule that even a mentally incompetent kohen receives priestly gifts cannot rest on ordinary acquisition, for such a person cannot acquire a gift even through another's transferring intent. The better explanation is that priestly gifts are already the property of the priesthood by Torah law. A minor kohen takes his own property, analogous to inheritance or wages that belong to a minor by Torah law. The obligation of redemption is likewise a debt owed to kohanim. Since liens for such obligations are Torah law, the minor can receive what is delivered toward his own debt. There is an additional route. Forced payment is valid payment. Rashba explains that where a debtor seeks to discharge his own obligation, the creditor cannot keep him indebted against his will. If so, priestly gifts and redemption may perhaps be delivered even without the kohen's consent. This would explain how delivery to a mentally incompetent kohen can discharge the giver. Yet the authorities say a kohen may not refuse a firstborn animal merely because its care is burdensome, calling such refusal disrespect for priestly gifts. This shows that forced delivery may discharge the giver without making the recipient acquire against his will. Tosafot in Kiddushin also requires that the kohen rely upon the valuation when an object worth five sela is used for redemption. That suggests priestly gifts require a genuine manner of giving rather than mere forced discharge. One can distinguish ordinary forced repayment, where the creditor's knowledge is unnecessary, from priestly gifts, where the Torah requires a form of giving to the kohen. The matter therefore still requires study.
But not with a found object. The decisors distinguish a minor's acquisition of a gift from his acquisition of a found object. Shach objects that if the child has the power to acquire for himself, why should gift and find differ, and he relies on the Jerusalem Talmud's gradations of childhood. In my view the decisors are correct. We are already forced to distinguish gift from ownerless property in the case of one who works another's land mistakenly believing it to be his own. In ownerless property he does not acquire without intent to acquire, whereas in a gift he can acquire because the transferor's intent supplies what is missing. So too, a minor who lacks mature acquisitive intent can nevertheless acquire a gift when another person intentionally transfers it to him. A found object has no transferor to supply that intent. A child below the stage of throwing away a pebble and taking a nut lacks even a legally meaningful hand, like a mentally incompetent person, and therefore cannot acquire even a gift. A deaf person or an older minor has a hand but lacks full intent. In a gift, another person's intent suffices. The Babylonian Talmud does not appear to follow the Jerusalem Talmud's additional stages for the rabbinic rule of finds. Rambam, Tur, and Shulchan Aruch state the law of a minor's find without those distinctions. The Jerusalem Talmud also limits liability for an oath differently from the Babylonian sugyot concerning theft from a minor. We therefore should not overturn the decisors' distinction between gift and found property on the basis of that passage.
