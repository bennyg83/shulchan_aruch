But he does not collect from encumbered property. See Sema, seif katan 16, who explains that once the obligor protests against writing the deed, purchasers will no longer hear a public report warning them, so collection is limited to free property. At first glance this is difficult. The witnesses must have created publicity on the first day, because otherwise why can collection ever be made from encumbered property from the date of the kinyan? If the debtor protests only the next day, why should witnesses be forbidden to write the deed, given that the original publicity already occurred? One could say the later protest itself also becomes public and alerts purchasers that no deed will exist. But there is a more fundamental difficulty from Bava Metzia 17. Shmuel says that one who finds a deed containing a kinyan should return it to its owner, because even if perhaps the borrower had not yet actually borrowed, he already obligated himself through the kinyan. The Gemara implies this even where the debtor does not admit. If lack of delivery of the deed is treated as protest and thereby destroys collection from purchasers, why return it? Perhaps the debtor protested and the deed fell from the witnesses' hands. As I noted in siman 65, the primary reason is not loss of publicity. Ramban in Bava Batra 77 writes that once the obligor protests against the deed, he becomes believed to claim payment. If he can claim, "I paid," he can no longer collect from purchasers. This applies even according to authorities who ordinarily do not allow a claim of payment against witnesses to a kinyan. That rule rests on the fact that in a normal kinyan the debtor should anticipate that the witnesses may later write a deed whenever the creditor asks, and therefore he should demand a receipt if he pays. Once he has expressly prohibited writing, he knows no deed should later be in the creditor's hands, and he can reasonably pay without a receipt. Ramban himself holds generally that one cannot claim payment against a kinyan, yet expressly writes that after protest one can. This proves the distinction. Shmuel's rule about returning the found kinyan deed is then understandable. Shmuel does not suspect payment in that sugya. Even if the debtor had protested against delivery, the underlying obligation is still true, and absent a real concern of improper collection from purchasers there is no reason to withhold the creditor's own document. Nor need we fear that perhaps the debtor protested and therefore the deed is forbidden to deliver. If the document states a true obligation and returning it will not cause unlawful monetary loss, we do not create that remote concern. One might object that Shmuel's reason for not fearing payment is, "Had he paid, he would have torn the document." Here perhaps the document fell before ever reaching the creditor because the debtor protested, so he had nothing to tear when he later paid. The answer is that he should then have taken a receipt. Otherwise, he had to fear that the creditor might later find the document. This same logic is implicit in the sugya itself. Even if the deed was originally written before the loan and fell before delivery, the Gemara still says the kinyan itself obligates the debtor. If he later paid that kinyan-obligation, there was likewise no delivered deed to tear. Necessarily he should have taken a receipt because of the possibility the document would later surface. Accordingly, Rama correctly rules that after protest there is no collection from purchasers because we must consider the possibility of payment. But if the debt remains within its fixed term, where payment is not ordinarily suspected, collection from encumbered property should still be possible. Sema says the rule concerns specifically a gift deed. That must mean a gift made through chazakah without kinyan sudar. If liability for warranty was created through kinyan sudar, the kinyan itself has publicity and can support collection like a sale. The case must therefore be one where the land gift was acquired only through chazakah and the warranty obligation is otherwise merely oral. Still, as I wrote, the primary rule follows Rama that even in sale, after protest, one does not collect from encumbered property because the debtor can claim payment in the absence of a deed.
