Even if he sold his field. This is written to exclude Rabbeinu Tam's view, cited by the Tur, that the rule applies specifically to a gift and not to a sale. Ri and the Rosh disagree with him. See the Perishah for the reasoning.
Nevertheless, he is liable for warranty, etc. This appears to refer to the case in seif 8 where he told others, "Acquire it for him and write," so that he may retract from the deed but not from the field. Even so, in a sale he remains liable for warranty if the field is later taken from the buyer. Once the buyer acquired the field, every ordinary purchaser is presumed to buy with warranty, since a person does not throw away his money for nothing. Yet the buyer cannot collect from encumbered property. Ordinarily, according to many authorities, witnesses to a kinyan create publicity sufficient for collection from later purchasers, because an ordinary kinyan stands for writing. Here, however, the seller expressly protested against writing the deed precisely so that no public document or publicity should emerge from the sale. The witnesses will respect that protest and will not spread the matter, so later purchasers cannot be expected to know and protect themselves. Therefore the buyer may collect only from unencumbered property. This is specifically in a sale. An ordinary donor of a gift has no warranty obligation unless he expressly states that he gives the gift with warranty. Rabbi Yonah writes that just as a person can obligate himself in warranty through a deed, he can do so through possession sufficient to collect from unencumbered property. In the Derishah I wrote that Rama's formulation is not compelled. It is possible that Rabbi Yonah's statement limiting collection to unencumbered property applies only to a gift expressly given with warranty where the donor protested against writing, whereas in an ordinary sale, even if he protested against writing, warranty may still be collectible from encumbered property. Some explain Rama's gloss as referring also to the immediately preceding case, holding that even though the seller cannot retract from writing, his protest reveals that he does not want publicity and therefore collection is only from unencumbered assets. That cannot be correct, because the Tur and Rabbi Yonah explicitly limit the rule to where no deed was written.
