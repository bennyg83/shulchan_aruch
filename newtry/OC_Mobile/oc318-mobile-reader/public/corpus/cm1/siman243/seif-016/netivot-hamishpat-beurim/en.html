And one who acquires for a mentally incompetent person. Machaneh Efrayim and Ketzot HaChoshen cite Rosh in Ketubot that zekhiyah does not work for a mentally incompetent person except during lucid intervals, because only then is he a person who can ultimately reach legal understanding. Machaneh Efrayim explains Rambam's contrary view, that future attainment of capacity is not required, from Gittin 85. R. Yochanan asks why a female minor can be divorced from kiddushin made by her father if the verse requires "she leaves and becomes another man's." Rashi explains that the woman must be of a class capable of kiddushin. At first glance this is difficult because Gittin 55 says a deaf woman married off by her father can be divorced from that d'oraisa kiddushin, even though she cannot presently perform kiddushin herself. Likewise, a mentally incompetent woman who can guard her get can be divorced d'oraisa, even though she cannot currently betroth herself. Necessarily, the possibility that she may later recover and then be capable of kiddushin counts as "capable of becoming." Even though a later act of recovery is needed, it is enough. Machaneh Efrayim says the same should count as future capacity for zekhiyah. Machaneh Efrayim further writes that if a minor attempts to transfer his own property to an adult through a third person, nothing happens. Ran on the Mishnah of one who sees people running writes that one can acquire for another only by becoming agent of the property's owner. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 71 say similarly. Since a minor owner cannot appoint an agent, the attempted zekhiyah fails. On that reasoning the same should be true if a non Jew tries to transfer property to a Jew through a Jewish intermediary, because the non Jew cannot appoint an ordinary Jewish agent. Machaneh Efrayim asks from Gittin, chapter Kol HaGet, regarding one who lends money to a kohen and acquires priestly gifts for him through another person. Mordechai says priestly gifts do not involve another transferring mind because the owner's only right is tovat hana'ah. How then can the intermediary acquire for the kohen if the person directing him is not the true owner? There is no difficulty according to what I wrote in siman 105. Where property has no present private owner, a person can make himself an acquirer for another without serving as agent of an owner. Agency from the owner is needed only to remove property from someone's ownership. Therefore with hefker and priestly gifts, which do not belong to the designating owner in the ordinary monetary sense, any qualified person can acquire for another. This differs from a found object that is still tied to its owner until despair in the manner I explain in siman 262, where one cannot simply appoint oneself as agent for another without agency from the owner. Machaneh Efrayim is correct that if the five sela'im of pidyon haben are merely acquired for a kohen through another person, the father is not yet discharged from responsibility. The intermediary's zekhiyah may prevent retraction but does not necessarily complete the mitzvah while the father remains responsible for delivery. So long as that responsibility remains, the mitzvah of giving is incomplete. Even giving to the kohen's own agent may not suffice, as the Mechilta derives, "and he shall give to the kohen," not merely to the kohen's agent. Machaneh Efrayim also questions a Yerushalmi, saying it implies one can acquire for another only if the acquirer himself could acquire the property. I did not find this in Yerushalmi Demai, but rather in Yerushalmi Peah concerning one who gathers and says, "This is for so-and-so poor person." In any event, that implication runs contrary to our Bavli. Priesthood gifts can certainly be acquired for a kohen through an ordinary Jew.
