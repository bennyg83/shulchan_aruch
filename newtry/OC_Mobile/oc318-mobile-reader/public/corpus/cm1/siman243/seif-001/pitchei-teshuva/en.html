Who gave a gift during her illness, etc. See above, siman 81 seif 2 in the gloss, and Even HaEzer siman 90 seif 10 in the gloss. Panim Meirot, part II, siman 134, discusses a woman who, while ill before her death, asked her husband to give her pillows and cushions to her poor daughter from her first husband. She told him: “As long as you live, use them, but after your death they shall belong to my daughter.” He promised her, and at her request gave her a formal pledge to keep his word. After the husband later died, the daughter sought the items on the strength of her mother's testament, to which her stepfather had agreed. Panim Meirot ruled that the heirs are not obligated to give them to her. As to the husband's pledge, once he died his heirs are exempt, as explained by Shach, siman 129 seif 5 seif katan 16, Sema, siman 207 seif katan 53, and Ramah, siman 209 seif 4. Nor does the rule concerning a gift to a poor person alter the result. Ramah here states that where Shimon's wife gave a gift and her husband consented, he cannot retract, but that means that the husband himself cannot retract, not that his heirs are bound. A gift promised to a poor person takes effect only by force of a vow, as Beit Yosef explains here. Sema, siman 125 seif katan 25, says that with charity the principle “a declaration to the Most High is like delivery to an ordinary person” may appropriately be invoked, but he says this only to explain the Tur's wording; as a matter of law he agrees that the obligation is only that of a vow. Once the promisor dies, his sons therefore need not fulfill it, especially here, where he agreed to give only after his death. Ramah's responsum, end of siman 48, states explicitly that if one says he will give after his death, the vow never takes effect and the heirs need not give; even if he said he would give immediately, the heirs need not fulfill the vow so long as the poor never acquired by a valid kinyan. Thus here too the heirs plainly need not fulfill his vow. See what I wrote above, siman 212 seif 7 seif katan 9, and below, siman 252 seif katan 2. Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 90 seif katan 40, writes that Ramah's statement that the husband consented must mean that he consented without her importuning him, as discussed in Yoreh Deah siman 232. In my view this is not compelled, in light of Shevut Yaakov, part II, siman 81, which I cited in Pitchei Teshuva there.
