Seif 10. Even if they performed a kinyan with him, etc. Note: The Sema cites the Tur that even if the parties performed a kinyan obligating the seller to write a sale document, that kinyan is merely a kinyan devarim, because the purchaser's lien upon the seller's assets already arose at the time of sale. According to this reasoning, a new practical rule might follow. If immediately after the sale the seller protested and instructed the witnesses not to write the document, and only afterward made a kinyan obligating himself to write it, perhaps that later kinyan would be effective. In that situation one cannot say that the lien already existed fully from the sale, because the Rama above, seif 8, rules that when the seller protests the writing of a document the purchaser cannot collect from encumbered property. The Sema explains that although an ordinary witnessed sale allows collection from encumbered property, a seller's protest prevents it. Thus, before the protest a kinyan to write the document adds nothing; after the protest, however, the document would create a new ability to collect from encumbered property, making the kinyan a substantive monetary acquisition rather than mere words. In truth this requires study. In a loan, an oral debt ordinarily cannot be collected from encumbered property, whereas a written debt can. If the above distinction were correct, a kinyan to provide a loan document should prevent the borrower from retracting. Yet siman 39, seif 5, cites as the first opinion that even after a kinyan the borrower may protest and prevent the writing of the document. That is the view of the Rosh and Tur. The Tur therefore seems inconsistent: here he explains that the kinyan is merely words because the assets are already encumbered from the sale, implying that if the lien were absent the kinyan would be substantive; there he says even in a loan the borrower may retract. The Mechaber likewise cites two opinions there but here rules simply that the party can retract. It seems the Mechaber regards the first opinion in siman 39 as primary and cites the second only out of respect, as he often does. To reconcile the Tur, the issue should not depend on whether a creditor can practically collect from later purchasers, but on whether a substantive lien exists at all. The Tur follows his father the Rosh, and this may show that the Rosh holds that the lien of a debt exists by Torah law. Even an oral loan therefore creates a Torah lien upon the debtor's assets, although the Sages prevented collection from purchasers because of public policy. Since the assets are already substantively encumbered, a kinyan to write a document does not transfer any new property right and is merely a kinyan devarim. The same applies after a seller protested. Although the purchaser cannot collect from encumbered assets, the underlying lien still exists, so the kinyan remains merely verbal. This may also explain Rashba's opposing opinion in siman 39 that the borrower cannot retract after a kinyan. Rashba may hold that a lien is not created by Torah law without a written instrument. A kinyan obligating the borrower to provide a document would then create a genuine monetary right, so he cannot retract. Thus the dispute may depend upon whether the law of liens is Torah law. According to the Mechaber's practical preference for the Rosh, the party can retract in every case. This appears correct.
