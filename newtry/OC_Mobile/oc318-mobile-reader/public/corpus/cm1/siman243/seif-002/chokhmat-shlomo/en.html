Seif 15. A minor to whom people give, etc. Note: Regarding whether a minor has the capacity to acquire by Torah law, see my novellae to Sukkah 46b, where I proved that he does, and derived this as well from Tosafot there. There, gloss: Specifically where another person's intent transfers it to him; but not with a found object. See the Shach, who extensively disagrees and maintains that the rule that a minor's found object belongs to him only because of peaceful relations applies to a child below the stage of recognizing and discarding a pebble. Once he has reached that developmental level, he can acquire even without another person's intention to transfer. I have written at length on this elsewhere, but I have now found strong support for the Rama in Rashi to Bava Metzia 92, in the sugya whether a worker eats from his own property right or from a privilege granted by Heaven. Rashi, s.v. "for the Merciful One granted it to him," explains that before the food reaches the worker's hand he has not yet acquired it so as to be able to give it to his wife and children. This implies that once it reaches his hand, an adult worker does acquire it and may transfer it to his family. The Tur likewise cites this opinion. Yet later on the same page, Rashi explains that if the worker eats by a heavenly privilege, an employer cannot make the relevant stipulation for his minor workers, because a minor worker has no property right until the food is actually put into his mouth. Rashi repeats that their master has no right in their eating because even the minors themselves do not acquire it until it is placed into their mouths. At first glance Rashi appears contradictory. Earlier he says that once food reaches the worker's hand it becomes his, while later he says that for a minor it is not his even after reaching his hand until it is put into his mouth. The necessary distinction is that the first passage concerns an adult, who can acquire ownerless property, whereas the later passage concerns a minor, who cannot acquire unless another person's intent transfers it to him. If the worker eats as part of his wages, the employer is transferring the right to him, and the minor can therefore acquire. But if the worker eats only by a privilege granted from Heaven, the food resembles ownerless property or a found object. The minor then acquires nothing until actual consumption. Rashi had to explain the passage this way. If even a minor can acquire ownerless property once it reaches his hand, then even according to the view that the worker eats from Heaven's grant, the employer should be able to make the same stipulation for minors as for adults, because once the food reaches the minor's hand it would be his. Rashi therefore necessarily holds that a minor cannot acquire in the absence of another person's transferring intent. This also proves that the law is not limited to a very young child below the stage of recognizing a pebble. If an older minor could acquire ownerless property, the baraita should have distinguished between minors themselves, allowing the stipulation for those mature enough to recognize and discard a pebble and disallowing it for younger children. Instead it distinguishes categorically between adults and minors. This is a clear proof that, in Rashi's view, every minor lacks independent acquisition where there is no other person's intent to transfer. Thus Rashi, the light of our eyes, supports the authorities followed by the Rama.
