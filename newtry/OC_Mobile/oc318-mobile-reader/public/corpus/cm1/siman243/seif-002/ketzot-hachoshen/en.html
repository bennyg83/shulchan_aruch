If the recipient is poor. Tur says the donor cannot retract because a verbal commitment to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person. Beit Yosef objects that the phrase "speech to Heaven is like delivery" applies to Temple consecration, while a poor person remains an ordinary person. He further asks why Tur speaks specifically of "Go and give," when the stronger case of a direct promise, "I will give a maneh to this poor person," would also be binding. The Gemara concerning one who had a claim against another and then said, "Give it to the poor," appears at first to contradict Tur. Rav Yosef says the poor had already acquired because "we are the hand of the poor." The early authorities ask why such a hand was needed if a charity vow is already irrevocable. Rif and Ramban answer that the money was not in the promisor's possession, so the rule of a direct charity vow alone did not effect transfer; the hand of the poor supplied the required transfer structure. Accordingly, that passage is not a clear proof for Tur's specific wording. Beit Yosef's second question can be answered by distinguishing the kind of retraction available. With a mere promise to give charity, the promisor can seek annulment of the vow. But where he says to an agent, "Go and give," we can treat "go" like "acquire" once the underlying obligation to the poor already exists. In an ordinary gift, "go" is not the same as "acquire." In a debt, however, "go" is treated like "acquire." Since a charity vow makes the promisor obligated to give, the poor person's claim resembles a debt for this purpose. Therefore the agent's receipt can become acquisition for the poor and the donor can no longer retract merely through annulment. This is the better explanation.
