Seif 1. 'And he shall collect it in addition to his debt.' Note: It appears to me that this applies specifically where there are enough assets to allow the lender to collect both his debt and his expenses. Since the lender suffers no loss, the Sages required that the borrower first be notified. But if there are not enough assets to cover even the lender's expenses, and if the lender pays the expenses there is concern that the borrower may refuse to reimburse him, causing the lender to lose his own money, we do not obligate the lender to give notice. This enactment was made only for the borrower's benefit, not to cause the lender a loss. A proof is that if giving notice would require more than thirty days, the lender is not obligated to notify him even where the inconvenience is small. All the more so where there is an actual monetary loss. Likewise, if the borrower is so distant that a messenger would need more than thirty days, but a postal letter could theoretically reach him and a reply return within thirty days, we do not wait thirty days, because a letter does not provide definite notification. With a messenger, the messenger will certainly return and tell us the borrower's position and whether he is present there. With a letter, if no reply arrives within thirty days, we do not know whether the letter reached him at all. Still, perhaps since clarification is possible, one should act favorably and wait thirty days. But where there is concern for actual monetary loss to the lender, we certainly do not require notice. See also my Choshen Mishpat, fourth edition, siman 93, where I wrote that when many people sue one individual, they collect in his absence even where notification is possible, because of the principle that a pot belonging to partners is neglected. If even depositing property in the hands of a third party is treated differently when one person is opposed by many, all the more so collection in his absence is permitted despite the possibility of notifying him.
