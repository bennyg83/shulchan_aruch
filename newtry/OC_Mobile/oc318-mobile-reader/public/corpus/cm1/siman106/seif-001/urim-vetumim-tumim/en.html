We collect his debt for him. The Giddulei Terumah, shaar 15, asks that the Rambam appears inconsistent. Here he follows the Rif that a creditor may collect in the debtor's absence. Yet in Hilkhot Shekhenim, chapter 4, regarding a house and upper story that collapsed, the law is that if the owner of the lower house refuses to rebuild he can ordinarily be compelled to do so. When that owner is absent, however, the Rambam rules that the owner of the upper story rebuilds and uses the lower structure until his expenses are repaid. The Maggid Mishneh explains that although the Rambam ordinarily permits coercing the lower owner, here he follows the Yerushalmi's principle that one does not collect from a person in his absence, contrary to the Rif. Nimukei Yosef and the Beit Yosef explain the Rambam similarly. The Giddulei Terumah answers that the house case concerns an oral obligation, where there is concern that the absent owner may already have paid. This is strained. The Rambam states the case without qualification, and if the issue were fear of prior payment it would not depend on the general dispute between the Ramban and Rashba, who never disagreed about collection where a genuine payment concern exists. The same difficulty applies to the Mechaber, who here follows the Rif without qualification but below in siman 164 copies the Rambam's ruling regarding the fallen house. It must be said that the reason we collect from an absent debtor is an enactment designed to prevent a person from taking another's money and then leaving for a distant country. That rationale is absent in the case of the collapsed house. A house does not ordinarily collapse, and there is no reason to suspect that the owner deliberately traveled abroad to avoid rebuilding while forfeiting the use and value of his own house. Moreover, the law itself protects the owner of the upper story by allowing him to rebuild and live there until his expenses are repaid. There is therefore no need to invoke the special enactment permitting collection in a debtor's absence. The case remains governed by the strict law, under which collection is not made in his absence. The apparent contradiction is thus resolved.
