The first proof is to authenticate the document in his possession, etc. If the document is not authenticated, collection may not be made in the debtor's absence. The same applies against purchasers and heirs, for beit din advances on their behalf the plea that the document may be forged. This is the view of the Maggid Mishneh, Tosafot, Rashba, Ran, Rabbenu Yerucham, and Nimukei Yosef, contrary to Rabbenu Yitzchak. That is the ruling.
Mabit rules that where two creditors appear with documents and the earlier document is not authenticated, but the debtor admits writing it and admits that it remains unpaid, the later creditor whose document is authenticated cannot claim forgery, trust, or collusion. In his view, the debtor's presence and admission are equivalent to authentication, and purchasers cannot assert forgery once the principal party admits the document. This does not appear correct. We remain concerned for collusion. Although the Rosh writes in one place that forgery is an uncommon plea when both seller and purchaser deny it, that discussion follows Rabbenu Yitzchak's view. We do not rule like Rabbenu Yitzchak, and elsewhere we regularly advance even uncommon pleas for heirs and purchasers. The incident of the orchard in Bava Metzia may appear to indicate that a debtor's admission permits collection from encumbered property. Tosafot indeed read the passage that way. In my view, however, the Gemara merely compares the cases because in both there is a defect in the document that does not permit the debtor himself to claim payment, yet the analogy fails where the instrument was not validly written. Rashi's explanation supports this reading. The case can also be understood as one in which the purchasers themselves admit the document and dispute only whether it was paid. Accordingly, a debtor's admission alone does not deprive purchasers of the plea of forgery.
I later found that Rivash states this rule explicitly. Even where the debtor admits signing the document, his admission is ineffective to the detriment of others, because the debtor and creditor may be colluding to defeat another claimant. Rivash compares this to the Rambam's rule that where a document makes the creditor's declaration of nonpayment conclusive, the creditor still cannot collect from purchasers merely because the debtor admits nonpayment, since collusion remains possible. Although the precise Rambam reference cited by Rivash is uncertain, the principle is sound. Baal HaMaor, Baal HaTerumot, Raavad, Ramban, and the authorities discussing a deathbed declaration all imply that only a deathly ill person enjoys the special presumption that he does not collude against consecrated property. In an ordinary case, even where the debtor admits that the document is genuine, the creditor may not collect from purchasers without formal authentication. This is the primary ruling.
