Regarding the first proof offered to validate the debt instrument in the creditor's hand, the Shach cites Mabit concerning two debt instruments. The earlier instrument was not formally validated, but the borrower admitted that he had written it and that it remained unpaid. Mabit ruled that the later creditor whose document was validated cannot claim the earlier document is forged merely because of possible collusion between borrower and creditor. Once the borrower admits writing it, Mabit treats it as validated even against third parties. Rashi at the beginning of Bava Metzia seems to support Mabit. In the case where lender and borrower both hold the same instrument and Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says they divide, Rabbi Elazar distinguishes whether each holds the essential text or only the lower portion. Rashi explains that the portion containing the date has greater value because it permits collection from encumbered property. He adds that, according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, once the borrower admits writing the document, no separate validation is needed. This sounds as though the borrower's admission can support collection even from encumbered property. Nevertheless, Tosafot there rejects Rashi's explanation, and our practical rule is that one who admits writing a document can still require its validation. Ramban's ruling, cited by the Tur in siman 88, strongly indicates that the borrower's admission does not create the status of a fully validated document against third parties. If there are no witnesses to the kinyan, even though the borrower admits there was a kinyan, the claim remains like an oral claim for purposes of partial admission oath. Likewise, if a document cannot be validated from signatures or witnesses, the borrower's admission that he wrote it does not prevent the accompanying denial from generating an ordinary partial admission oath. The Shulchan Aruch codifies this. If the borrower's admission created a document collectible from encumbered property, his admission would itself be an admission of a land lien. Under Ramban's approach that would remove the ordinary partial admission oath. The fact that Ramban still requires the oath proves that even after the borrower admits writing it, the paper has only the force of an oral admission until independently validated. Thus Mabit's broad rule cannot be accepted as stated. A remaining question is where the purchasers themselves recognize the signatures and know the document is genuine. Perhaps their own admission is enough to allow collection from them, because unlike the borrower they have no migo of claiming payment. Even if the borrower stands before us and says, "I wrote it but later paid," and has a migo that he could have denied the signatures, that migo may not help the purchasers, since they themselves know the signatures are authentic. Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim discusses a purchaser who knows the land originally belonged to the challenger. Rashba says we can still advance for the purchaser whatever claim the seller could have made, including a migo that the seller could have denied the challenger's ownership. Rabbeinu Yitzchak disagrees where the purchaser personally knows the alternative plea is false. Here, however, even Rabbeinu Yitzchak may agree that the borrower's migo can help the purchasers. If the borrower were to claim the document was forged, the purchasers' knowledge that the signatures are genuine would not itself create a debt against them, because the borrower's assets are only guarantors for his obligation. If the principal borrower is exempt, the guarantor property is likewise exempt. This requires further consideration.
