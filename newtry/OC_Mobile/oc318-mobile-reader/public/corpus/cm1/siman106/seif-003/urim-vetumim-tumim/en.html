To authenticate the document. The Mabit rules that if the debtor admits that the document is genuine and not forged, the creditor may collect even from liened property without separate authentication, and we do not suspect collusion. The Shach disagrees, and in principle his objection is supported by the Baal HaMaor, Raavad, and Ramban in the discussion in Bava Batra concerning a person on his deathbed who consecrated his property. They hold that a debtor's admission is not sufficient against purchasers to authenticate a document, because collusion must be suspected. One must say that although we ordinarily suspect collusion only where a document was found after having been lost, that limitation applies to an otherwise established document, whose force should not be weakened without reason. Here, however, the very question is whether the document is legally established at all, so the debtor's admission alone cannot necessarily establish it against purchasers. Yet there is an apparent difficulty from the Gemara in Bava Metzia, in the chapter Eizehu Neshekh, which states expressly that if a debtor admits that he wrote the document it need not be authenticated and the creditor may collect from liened property. This seems explicit like the Mabit. The Shach seeks to answer that the purchasers there themselves admit that the document is authentic, but this is very strained and has no real foundation in the halakhic discussion. It also conflicts with Tosafot there, as the Shach himself notes. See Shitah Mekubetzet, where some explain that the purchasers in that passage acquired only after the debtor's admission. That too is strained. Rashi at the beginning of Bava Metzia provides further evidence. In the case where one person grips the body of a document and another grips the text, the Gemara considers the difference in value between a document that bears a date and one that does not. Rashi explains that the signed witnesses on the body do not create an additional value because, according to Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, when the debtor admits that he wrote the document it need not be authenticated, and the debtor in that case admits it. If a debtor's admission were ineffective against purchasers, the signatures would still create substantial value with respect to collection from purchasers, just as the date creates value for collection from them even though it adds nothing against the debtor himself. Rashi therefore appears to hold that the debtor's admission suffices even against purchasers. The old reading in Bava Metzia is thereby well explained. It reads: "If he admits that he wrote the document, it need not be authenticated, and he collects from liened property." Tosafot deleted the latter phrase, but it is Rashi's reading and appears in old texts, as the Or Zarua records. The two clauses are logically connected. According to the authority who holds that the document need not be authenticated, the debtor's denial of authenticity is considered such a weak claim that it cannot support even a migo. If it is so weak when asserted by the debtor himself, we have no basis to plead it for purchasers. According to the authority who requires authentication, however, the debtor's forgery claim is treated as a substantial claim because it can operate through a migo. We then plead it for purchasers as well. Thus, the old text properly says that because the debtor's admission dispenses with authentication, collection from liened property follows. Tosafot deleted this wording because they follow their own view that even where the debtor does not admit the document, we do not plead forgery for purchasers. We do not rule like Tosafot on that point. Since our halakhah is that the document must be authenticated when the debtor contests it, we likewise plead forgery for purchasers, and the Mabit's broader ruling cannot be accepted without qualification.
