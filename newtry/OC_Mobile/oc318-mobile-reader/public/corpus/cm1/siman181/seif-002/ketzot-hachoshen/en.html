Mordechai is cited as saying that a partner who rescued partnership property can keep it for himself if he swears that he intended to rescue only for himself. Shach reads the rule as requiring that he actually say so at the time. Taz understands the cited language as allowing inward intention without speech. Bedek HaBayit objected to the inward intention reading because matters in the heart ordinarily have no legal force. It therefore interpreted the language as meaning that he said, "I rescue for myself," and if the other partner makes a certain claim he swears accordingly. In my view one must distinguish two cases. If the property could not be rescued even with difficulty, it is effectively ownerless. The other partners acquire only through this partner's act of lifting it for them. If in his heart he did not intend to lift for them, they do not acquire. In such a true ownerless property case, inward intent is sufficient. If the property could still be rescued with difficulty, it is not ownerless. The partner who wishes to divide for himself is altering the existing partnership because of the threatened loss. There, he must expressly say that he is rescuing for himself. An unexpressed intention does not dissolve the partnership. Rabbi Avi Ezri's ruling that he swears about his intention therefore concerns the first case, where the property was wholly unrecoverable and ownership depended on his act of acquisition. The fact that partners ordinarily rescue for the partnership creates a strong factual presumption, sufficient to require an oath even where the other party's claim is uncertain. This also explains the dispute about one who lifts a found object for another. Sema requires a statement at the time of lifting, while Shach says inward intent can suffice. In partnership, even if no words were spoken, the ordinary partnership expectation itself can make the act equivalent to an express acquisition for all, unless the rescuer proves he intended otherwise. See siman 269 and what I wrote in siman 279.
