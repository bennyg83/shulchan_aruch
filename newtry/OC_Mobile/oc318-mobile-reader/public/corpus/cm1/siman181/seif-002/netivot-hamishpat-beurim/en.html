That he said he was saving it for himself. It appears that when the partner is present, the declaration must be made specifically in his presence. This is also implied by the Shach in seif katan 5, who writes, "he said before them." The Bedek HaBayit proves that mere intention is insufficient from the ruling that the rescuer must swear. If intention alone were effective, why would he have to swear, since the other partner's claim would be uncertain? The same proof shows that the declaration must be made in the partner's presence. Otherwise the claim would still be uncertain, because the rescuer could say that he made the declaration out of the partner's hearing, or whispered it, similar to Tosafot in Gittin 34, s.v. Aval Nisan. The reason appears to be that although a partner may separate himself from the partnership without the other's knowledge in a situation of loss, that is only when the other partner is absent. When the other is present, he must separate in his presence so that the other knows to save his own share as well. Ketzot HaChoshen disagrees with the Darchei Moshe and writes that only where the property can be saved, so that it is not ownerless, is a declaration needed because the partner is severing his share. Where it cannot be saved and is therefore ownerless, the law of ownerless property is that one who lifts it for his fellow must say that he is lifting it for him, as the Sema writes in siman 269. Accordingly, if the rescuer intends to acquire for himself he needs no declaration. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. As long as the rescuer says nothing, the other partner does not despair at all. As the Shitah writes, when there are people capable of rescue the owner does not despair. This is also logical, for every person is obligated to save his fellow's property, and all the more so a partner, who is ordinarily presumed to act with his partner's consent. His partner therefore does not despair until the rescuer says, "I am saving it for myself," at which point he does despair.
He swears concerning this. See Shach, siman 186, seif katan 48, that he is believed only where he has a migo, such as where he received the property not in the presence of witnesses. Also see Shach here, seif katan 3, who writes that if the partner is absent no declaration is needed. At first glance this is difficult, because unexpressed thoughts are not legally effective. It must be that there is a compelling presumption that in a place of loss a person will surely save his own property first, and where there is such a compelling presumption an unexpressed intention is effective. As for the remainder, which he acquires because of the other's despair, despair can take effect without the other's knowledge where the situation is like property swept away by a river, as I wrote above.
