Heset. This also refers to a case where the claimant says, "I deposited one hundred with you, and these are only fifty." He need swear only a heset oath, because the amount he admits is "here it is," for by saying, "Whatever you gave me, you may take," he implies that those items remain intact in his possession for return, while he denies the rest. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes that in his view he is exempt from a Torah oath not because of "here it is," but because when he says, "Whatever you left, you may take," he has not admitted to a matter defined by measure or number, as explained above in siman 88, seif 23. The Beit Yosef says this explicitly. According to this, the case can be one in which "here it is" does not apply, such as where the admitted items rotted through his negligence. The Mechaber himself writes similarly in siman 88, seif 24. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 48.
He swears. It appears that he takes a heset oath that wheat was not deposited with him, and then he is exempt even from the barley that he admitted, as stated in simanim 88 and 400. According to the Rama's ruling in siman 88, seif 12, that even if the defendant knows he owes barley he is exempt because the claimant is deemed to have waived it, the oath can instead be explained as referring to the barley: even if he swears that barley was deposited with him, he is nevertheless exempt from returning the barley he admitted under oath, because we say the claimant waived the barley by claiming only wheat. Sema.
