He is believed with an oath while holding a sacred object. Shach says this oath is only rabbinic. Tumim asks that the migo of "I returned it" is weak, because the defendant may prefer the present claim in order to reverse the oath or because beit din can descend to his property. In my view the migo here differs from an ordinary migo. A custodian who could always have claimed "it was lost through accident" may reasonably fail to obtain a receipt when he actually returns the object, relying on the availability of that exempting claim. This is the rationale Sema gives. He can therefore swear that the object was lost through accident even though in truth he returned it, because once the depositor falsely denies the return, that denial itself places the custodian in a coercive situation comparable to accident or theft. Shach notes practical differences concerning reversal of the oath. There is no difficulty from the rule that even an ordinary document claimant can say, "swear to me," because a Torah scholar can be exempt from that oath and seizure can sometimes help where a party refuses to swear, while those distinctions differ under a reversed oath. Likewise, if the custodian is suspect, he can still rely on the migo that he could have claimed an exempting accident. He may fear that if he makes that claim, the depositor will falsely make a definite claim of shelichut yad or another definite accusation, in which case the law of a suspect custodian can shift the oath in the manner discussed in siman 92.
