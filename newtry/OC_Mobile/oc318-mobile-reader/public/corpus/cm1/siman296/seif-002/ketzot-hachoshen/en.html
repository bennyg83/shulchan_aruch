So too, when he claims that he returned it, he must swear. Sema, Maharshal, and Bach understand this as a full Torah oath, because the custodian is believed to say "I returned it" only through the migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable accident. Since the alternative plea requires a Torah custodial oath, the actual plea must be accompanied by the same Torah level oath. The Shach treats the oath as rabbinic and notes practical differences concerning reversal of the oath, a suspected swearer, and descent to assets. In my view the earlier authorities are correct. Shevuot asks how the Torah custodial oath can ever arise. If the deposit was witnessed, the custodian could say, "I returned it," and if the deposit was documented, the Gemara states that he must return with witnesses. Tosafot explains that this means only that he cannot be exempt without an oath. This clearly implies that where he says "I returned it" against a deposit document, the oath carrying his plea must have Torah force, otherwise the plea of unavoidable accident itself should be supported by the easier return plea and no Torah oath would remain. This does not necessarily mean every ancillary consequence of an ordinary Torah oath follows. Regarding reversal, I wrote in siman 87 that even where the custodian's oath is Torah in origin, he can sometimes tell the depositor, "Swear and take," just as a creditor holding a document can be required to swear when the debtor demands it. Regarding a suspected swearer, the custodian can still be believed through the migo of an unavoidable accident, because if he had pleaded that, he would be exempt under the special rule for a suspected custodian whose claimant has no definite claim that he consumed the object. There are therefore separate procedural questions, but the basic oath accompanying "I returned it" against a document is a Torah oath.
