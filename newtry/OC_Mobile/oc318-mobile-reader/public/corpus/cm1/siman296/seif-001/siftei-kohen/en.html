We do not say migo to exempt him from an oath, etc. The Sema rejects the Ir Shushan, but in my opinion the Ir Shushan is correct. Otherwise there is no principled reason to distinguish between money and an oath. The Sema's explanation, that the Sages were reluctant to remove money from its owner, is also unsatisfactory, for migo is a Torah principle and exempts from money by Torah law. One would then have to say that the Torah was lenient regarding money but strict regarding an oath, although an oath may itself lead to payment. Rather, the Ir Shushan explains correctly that when a person raises a factual defense to avoid payment, he mentally assumes responsibility for the truth of that defense. If his actual thought did not encompass exemption from the oath, migo does not create such an exemption. This differs from a monetary migo, which moves from one complete exemption to another. This provides a sound distinction. See Responsa Mabit, part II, siman 292.
Rather, this is not a good migo, etc. Because he would be brazen to deny entirely. This limitation applies here because the owner entrusted property to him, and likewise with a partial admission concerning a loan, where we do not believe him through a migo that he could have denied everything, since complete denial would be brazen toward one who lent him money. In other contexts, however, such a migo may be valid, as I proved above, siman 93, from Tosafot and other sources, and at length at the end of siman 82 in the laws of migo, rule 6. The Sema's additional explanation that the principal oath is rabbinic and the Sages were lenient is therefore unnecessary. In the case of partners, where there would be no brazen denial, the migo applies.
