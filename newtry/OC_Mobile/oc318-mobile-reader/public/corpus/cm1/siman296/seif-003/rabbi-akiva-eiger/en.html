[Shach, letter 3] And likewise regarding reversal of the oath. Note: It appears to me that the Shach intends the authorities who rule that certain claimants collect only after taking an oath. At least those who, by strict law, would collect even without an oath may reverse the oath onto the defendant. See above, siman 87, Shach, seif katan 30. This yields a practical difference here. If the bailee's claim, "I returned it," is accepted by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed that the deposit was lost through an unavoidable accident, the resulting oath is a Torah oath and he cannot reverse it. The claimant must instead advance the claim that, leaving aside the migo, the bailee must swear directly that he returned it. The depositor can then place the oath upon the bailee. But if the oath on the claim "I returned it" is only rabbinic, the bailee can reverse it onto the depositor, and the depositor cannot collect without taking that oath. However, regarding what the Shach writes in siman 87, seif katan 31, questioning Rambam's statement that there is no oath that can be reversed and so forth, there is a difficulty. According to Rambam, in the cases of those who collect, even where by strict law they could collect without an oath, they cannot reverse the oath. If so, even without the law of reversal, the defendant can say, "Swear to me that I did not return it." See what I wrote there in my gloss. As to the basic Talmudic rule that an oath can be reversed, there is room to question it: so long as the bailee has not sworn, he does not possess the migo and is not believed. It follows that his credibility exists only if he actually takes the oath, but not if he reverses it. Examine carefully.
