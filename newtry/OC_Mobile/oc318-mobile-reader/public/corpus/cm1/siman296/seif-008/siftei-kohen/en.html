But we do not know whether it was subject to a condition, etc. The Kesef Mishna and Bach explain that even if witnesses saw an apparently unconditional deposit, the parties may have made an earlier condition and then delivered the deposit silently in reliance upon it. The custodian is believed to assert that prior condition through a migo. This excludes a claim that the condition was made only after the deposit, once the witnesses left, for that claim is not believed through migo, as the Sema explains.
Therefore he must swear, etc. The Maggid Mishna questions why this oath appears more stringent than the heset oath required where the defendant says, 'I merely told you to leave it before me.' Since the Rambam relies on a migo that the custodian could have claimed proper guarding followed by unavoidable accident, the present oath appears to share the stringent form of the Torah custodial oath. The Bach answers that once he became a custodian he must swear by Torah law that he did not misappropriate the item, and the oath concerning the condition is included by extension. This does not fully address the Maggid Mishna. His point is that the Rambam appears to require the stringent oath on the condition even where no extension is available, for example where witnesses establish that the item is no longer in his possession and that he did not misuse it. If the oath were merely heset, the custodian could be believed through the ordinary migo of denial or return, and the reference to a migo of unavoidable accident would be unnecessary. The Rambam's conclusion that witnesses to negligence impose liability also implies a case where the only unresolved issue is the condition. The proper explanation is therefore that witnesses establish the deposit and continued nonreturn, leaving the custodian unable to claim denial or return, but do not know whether the condition was made. In that setting he takes a rabbinic oath with an article, patterned after Torah law, just as he would on a claim of return supported by the migo of unavoidable accident. The Semag's language must be understood similarly. The Bach further asks that if the custodian claims a condition, he does not swear that he guarded properly and therefore should not be required to swear against misuse or present possession. But he must certainly swear that the item is not presently in his possession, which is the principal Torah custodial oath, unless witnesses establish the loss. The Rambam's statement that an unpaid custodian who proves both loss and absence of negligence is exempt from oath does not apply here, where no proof establishes loss. Thus the Bach's difficulty and proposed answer are unnecessary. The precise classifications stated above govern.
