Two disadvantages. The Sema writes that accepting one judge in place of three is not considered two disadvantages, because there is only one disqualification, namely a single judge in place of a plurality. He writes that this is proven from the Tur in the following siman, which says that this applies with one judge where he was an expert or where the parties accepted him. However, in that following siman the Tur is discussing shuda deDayanei. Even if the parties accepted one person as though he were three, three ordinary judges themselves cannot decide a case by shuda, as the Tur writes in siman 240. It must therefore be that the acceptance was in place of a single expert judge. In that case there is certainly only one disadvantage: an ordinary layman in place of one expert. There is no need to describe it as one judge in place of many. The Shach disagrees and calls this two disadvantages. According to what I have written, since the acceptance is in place of one expert, there are certainly not two disadvantages. Even if at the time of acceptance the parties said that they accept him "in place of three," the matter depends upon the substantive legal deficiency, not upon the wording. The lack of multiple judges is not a separate disadvantage, because one expert is legally permitted to judge alone, and an ordinary person can take his place if the parties accept him. Tumim seeks to prove the Sema's view from the case where the parties accept three cattle herders. Although all three are disqualified, the acceptance is effective, supposedly because all share only one type of disqualification. Tumim therefore concludes that even three relatives can be accepted, because again there is only one type of disqualification. That proof is not valid. We rule that one learned judge is sufficient, and the usual requirement of three exists only to avoid a panel of complete ignoramuses, except according to Ramah, who requires all three to be learned. Thus three cattle herders can still fall under the acceptance of a single expert replacement, and there is only one deficiency. Even according to Ramah, if the acceptance stands in place of a single expert, the same follows. Nor can one say that because each of three relatives has the same disqualification, each can be separately accepted. Taken together they form several disqualifications. More fundamentally, the reason an acceptance works for one deficiency but not for two is that accepting two separate deficiencies is an exaggerated concession, as Ramban explains, and one does not fully resolve his mind to such an extreme acceptance. There is no greater exaggeration than accepting an entire beit din composed of three relatives. Even according to the Sema's formulation that one judge in place of three is only one type of deficiency, that refers only to a single judge in place of a plurality. A whole court of relatives contains a separate disqualification in each judge and is certainly more than one deficiency. This is clear.
