But if they accepted two defects. The Sema writes that accepting one qualified judge in place of three does not constitute two disadvantages. "Two disadvantages" means two distinct grounds of disqualification, such as a relative who is also sitting alone. One judge in place of three involves only one defect, the absence of the required three, and is therefore not regarded as two disadvantages. The Shach disagrees. See Beurim, seif katan 3, that if he expressly said, "I accept him as three judges," then because a panel of three differs from a single expert with respect to receiving testimony and admissions, this indeed constitutes two disadvantages. But if he accepted one ordinary judge with the status of a single expert, there is only one disadvantage. If he accepted a relative who is also otherwise disqualified as one judge, that is two disadvantages, because kinship disqualifies him only toward that relative whereas the other disqualification applies toward everyone. He shall swear. If the judge is a relative or otherwise disqualified, there is no Torah oath to contradict his testimony, because he is disqualified, and the litigant swears only a hesset oath. If the judge is qualified, the Sema holds that since there is no direct denial of money, only a hesset oath is required. The Shach objects that according to this reasoning even a hesset oath should not be required. Rather, the matter is monetary because according to this judge's ruling the litigant is obligated to pay. See Beurim, seif katan 4, that only where the judge has already imposed liability is this considered a denial of money. If the judge has not yet ruled and the dispute is merely whether the parties made a kinyan accepting him as judge, it is not a monetary denial.
