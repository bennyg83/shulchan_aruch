To serve as a witness against him. Rashi explains the Mishnah as referring to acceptance of a judge, and the Tur likewise writes that case. But the language is not exclusive. The same rule plainly applies to accepting a relative or disqualified person as a witness, as the phrase 'he is trusted by me' indicates. The Gemara, Rif, and Rosh support this.
But if he deposited money with him, etc. This concerns one who swore to pay his fellow by a certain date. When the date arrived, he deposited the money with the city judge to satisfy his oath, but told the judge not to deliver it because he had claims against the creditor. The judge happened to be the creditor's relative. See the responsum and Darkei Moshe for additional details.
Even if he accepted one person disqualified because of a transgression as equal to two witnesses. The case of a generally disqualified witness is stated for greater novelty. All the more so if he accepts a relative, such as his father, who is valid for others, as equal to two witnesses or three judges.
Some disagree and hold, etc. This gloss refers to the Mechaber's later statement, 'until the judgment is concluded.'
With one judge. The dispute depends on the interpretation of the Gemara, discussed in Hagahot Darkei Moshe. Rashba writes that if another beit din is later appointed, it may adjudicate on the basis of the claims and admissions made before the first.
Because there are two defects. If he accepted one qualified judge in place of three, and judgment was completed, it appears he cannot retract. That is not called two distinct defects, because the only deficiency is number. The problem of 'two defects' applies where he accepted one relative or disqualified person in place of two witnesses, combining disqualification of the person with reduction of the required number. This is supported by the Tur and Responsa Rosh.
In every circumstance he cannot retract. Even if he accepted one witness in place of two, according to Rabbeinu Yerucham in the name of the Rosh and other poskim, excluding a dissenting view cited in Darkei Moshe.
It was explained what counts as completion of judgment. Completion means when the judges say, 'So-and-so, you are entitled; so-and-so, you are liable,' and certainly when they say, 'Go and give him.' The latter is especially final. Merely saying, 'You are obligated to give him,' can sound less final, but a definitive declaration that one is entitled and the other liable is a completed ruling.
He cannot retract. Responsa Rosh writes that he cannot retract even if both parties later agree to do so. Beit Yosef explains this as respect for the judge. Hagahot Darkei Moshe suggests the issue is not 'retraction' at all: once the legal result exists, the parties can change it only by a genuine mutual waiver, which of course they can make.
Provided it did not become known that they erred. The source says that making a compromise when they were accepted to rule according to law counts as an error. Although the parties accepted them, they did not accept them to issue a mistaken ruling. Before final judgment, retraction can matter because a party may alter permissible pleadings or receive additional time from a second beit din after withdrawing from the first.
He cannot retract. Meaning even if the accepted judges mistakenly made a compromise or committed another error, according to the cited responsum and Darkei Moshe. This differs from Ir Shushan, which interprets the line merely to mean that any acceptance made before them becomes binding even before final judgment.
Acceptance before the heads of the city, etc. Any undertaking made before three cannot be retracted. Hagahot Mordechai says likewise, and communal acts also do not require a kinyan, such as exempting someone from taxes.
He shall swear that he did not accept him, etc. Meaning a hesset oath. The judge is not treated as one witness who would create a Torah oath. First, the present case includes a relative or otherwise disqualified judge. Second, denial of accepting a judge is not direct denial of money, and a Torah oath is not imposed on that.
The litigants cannot object. Rama writes similarly at the beginning of siman 8.
