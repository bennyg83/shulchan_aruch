One who accepted. See Shach, who rules from Hagahot Ashri that where one says, 'My father is trustworthy to me,' he must make that acceptance specifically before a beit din of three valid judges. The Jerusalem Talmud is his proof. The principal law, however, follows Urim VeTumim. Nimukei Yosef at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, citing Rashba and Ran, explains that the Jerusalem Talmud's statement that before two he may retract is an independent ruling concerning ordinary valid judges: where claims were presented before two, a party can move to another pair; before three, after presenting his claims, he cannot. It is not speaking of accepting a relative or disqualified person. Likewise the dispute there between Shmuel and Rabbi Yochanan concerns Rabbi Meir's position about retraction before and after final judgment, not the rule of Hagahot Ashri. Thus the Jerusalem Talmud does not prove Shach's rule. The matter is also consistent with siman 5 concerning nighttime adjudication: Sema says nighttime can work through the parties' acceptance, yet nighttime is not an ordinary beit din session. If Hagahot Ashri required a formal court setting in every case, parties could change their claims by day after litigating at night. The principal practical law therefore follows Urim VeTumim: there is no such universal distinction. Still, Hagahot Ashri and Nimukei Yosef can be partially reconciled. Hagahot Ashri speaks primarily of accepting a disqualified person as judge. If the acceptance occurred completely outside beit din and the disqualified judges later issue judgment in the party's absence in a circumstance where absent-party adjudication is otherwise possible, the outside acceptance alone may not bind. But if the parties actually present their claims before those accepted judges, that itself forms the accepted tribunal. Nimukei Yosef speaks of accepting a disqualified person as a witness. There, once he makes the acceptance before beit din and the other side accepts the arrangement, it can be treated as though beit din formally ordered payment contingent on that testimony. This explains why Nimukei Yosef uses the reason that the court is as though it commanded him. On the substantive law, however, the principal conclusion remains that Urim VeTumim is correct.
With one judge. See Sema. The case where one judge adjudicates on the basis of the parties' claims and admissions refers to one in which they made a kinyan accepting his authority, as stated in Responsa Rashba. Without such a kinyan, an admission before him is not treated as a binding judicial admission.
Because there are two defects. See Sema and Shach concerning one who accepted a single valid judge in place of three. Ketzot HaChoshen argues that since a single expert may judge alone, this is only one defect: accepting one ordinary judge in place of one expert. Urim VeTumim similarly writes in siman 25. In my view this is incorrect. A single expert differs from three ordinary judges in significant procedural respects, including whether an admission before him has the status of a judicial admission, reception of testimony, and the ability to prevent parties from changing claims. Therefore accepting one individual to have the force of three creates two defects, and the acceptance is void without a kinyan. Urim VeTumim also writes that accepting a related woman as judge is two defects. By the same logic, accepting a relative who is independently disqualified may likewise be two defects, though I hesitate to depart from his ruling without proof. Ketzot HaChoshen further discusses one who said, 'Your wife shall be believed to say that I did not pay her,' and argues this is not like final judgment because a litigant can repeat his own claim. This is strained. The principal law is as Bach states: the authority he cites disagrees and holds that testimony itself is not final judgment. In any event, accepting a relative in place of two witnesses is two defects and is ineffective without a kinyan.
