One who accepted, etc. See Tumim, where I cite Shach's conclusion following Hagahot HaRosh that the law concerns acceptance before beit din. In that case, after the judgment is completed he cannot retract. If the acceptance occurred outside beit din, Shach holds that even after the decision he may retract, unless he said, for example, "Administer an oath to me through this person," and the oath was actually taken on that basis. Since an act was performed in reliance upon him, he cannot retract even outside beit din. I do not agree. The simple implication of the poskim is that there is no distinction. Even according to Hagahot HaRosh, the distinction may apply specifically to accepted witnesses, but regarding accepted judges it appears that he would agree.
But if he already deposited money in his hand, etc. Meaning that the litigant owed money and deposited it with a judge, instructing him not to deliver it to the other party until the depositor complied properly with the law. That judge, who was a relative and therefore ordinarily disqualified, then adjudicated the dispute. Rivash rules that merely placing the money in his hand, without explicitly accepting him as judge, is not deemed acceptance of the disqualified judge, and his judgment is void.
He accepted one of those disqualified. This includes one disqualified because of a transgression, whose disqualification extends to everyone. All the more so does acceptance help for a relative, who is generally qualified and disqualified only toward his relatives. Sema. Moreover, one disqualified for wicked conduct is suspected of false testimony, while a relative may be entirely truthful and is disqualified only by Scriptural decree. Certainly explicit acceptance can validate his role.
With one judge, etc. Sema cites Rashba, responsum 1126, that if another beit din is later appointed, it adjudicates on the basis of the claims and admissions made before the first judge. His citation as applied here is difficult. If the first judge already completed the judgment, why would a second beit din reopen the case? The judgment is complete and there is no retraction. If the first judge had not completed it, perhaps because circumstances intervened, and another beit din must take over, then if the litigants remained free to retract before completion, the earlier claims and admissions should have no binding beit din status. Rashba's case is instead one where the judge was accepted through a kinyan, so retraction was impossible even before completion. Rashba then says that if this accepted judge did not manage to finish and another beit din was installed, the claims and admissions stated before the first remain binding because the parties could not retract and the admissions had the status of admissions before beit din. See the responsum, where this is explicit. Sema's citation therefore does not belong here.
But if he accepted two, etc. Sema writes that accepting one qualified judge in place of three is not considered "two defects." Two defects means two different grounds of disqualification, such as being related and being only one judge. One qualified judge in place of three has only one defect, namely insufficient number. Shach disagrees. See Tumim, where I show that Sema has support from another source. I also conclude that accepting three relatives as judges does not count as two defects, because all share the single category of relationship.
This is also the agreement of most poskim. Shach writes that major authorities, including Ramban, Rivash, Baal HaItur, and, according to him, Rabbeinu Ephraim, follow the first view. The matter is therefore a legal doubt. See below in my treatise on possession whether a litigant may say "I rely upon these authorities" when leading authorities support that view even though most poskim disagree. If one accepts a female relative as a single witness, it appears that this does constitute two defects. Disqualification as a woman is a different category from disqualification as a relative, since a relative is otherwise fully qualified while a woman is generally disqualified. This is obvious.
But if they made a kinyan from him, etc. Shach cites Nimukei Yosef that although such a kinyan appears to be a mere kinyan on words, we find in the laws of adjudication and mortgages that a kinyan can strengthen an undertaking. Ravyah, however, implies that the kinyan must obligate the litigant to carry out whatever the disqualified judge rules or whatever the disqualified witness establishes. I do not understand why Shach overlooks Mordechai in the first chapter of Sanhedrin concerning compromise, who states explicitly that the object itself must be transferred by kinyan, otherwise it is merely a kinyan on words. Rama cites this above in siman 12, seif 6. There is no distinction between compromise and accepting a disqualified judge or witness. Maharam Tiktin likewise writes in his glosses to the Krakow Rif that Mordechai disagrees with Nimukei Yosef, and this is the ruling we follow. Knesset HaGedolah writes that if a person made a kinyan accepting the judge without knowing that the judge was his relative, and only afterward discovered the relationship, the kinyan is plainly void as mistaken. The same is true even if he accepted with an oath, because the entire acceptance was mistaken.
And above, siman 12, etc. Sema explains that the conclusion of judgment is when the judges say, "So and so, you are exempt; so and so, you are liable," and all the more when they say, "Go and pay him." But merely saying, "You owe him," is not a final judgment because it sounds tentative. He understands Nimukei Yosef this way. Shach writes that Tosafos and other authorities disagree. See Tumim.
Regarding testimony, etc. See Tumim concerning a case where Reuven demands payment from Shimon and Shimon replies, "I paid your wife, and I accept your wife as credible if she says I did not pay her." She then says she was not paid. Ravyah holds that Shimon may retract. I wrote in Tumim that this law may actually be accepted according to all opinions and perhaps has no dissent. See there.
The judgment was completed, etc. Rosh writes in a responsum that a litigant cannot retract even if both parties now agree to do so. Beit Yosef asks: if both want to retract, who can prevent them? He explains that beit din may object out of respect for the judge. See Tumim, where I discuss Sema's and Shach's explanations and what appears correct to me.
Provided it is not known that they erred. One might ask what practical difference there is between retraction before and after the conclusion of judgment. If the judges erred, the case can always be reopened. If they did not err, why should a party retract even before judgment is final, since another beit din would reach the same Torah ruling? Mordechai answers that if the party retracts before final judgment, the first beit din is treated as though it never existed. The party may then alter or add a claim, because the first claim is treated as having been stated outside beit din. Likewise, he may receive a new period of time, because the original deadline was imposed by a proceeding that is now disregarded. Mordechai and Sema. Other consequences also exist. If no error is known and he cannot retract after final judgment, he may not insist on going to the Great Beit Din before paying; he must pay first. If he can retract before final judgment, he need not pay until another beit din confirms that the ruling was correct. The same applies where the original ruling depended upon judicial discretion, because another beit din may assess the matter differently. Mordechai states one practical difference among several. If the parties accepted judges for strict law and those judges instead imposed a compromise, this is treated as error and the parties may retract even after the decision. This must apply even where a kinyan was made. Without a kinyan, even a compromise expressly accepted can generally be retracted because compromise requires a kinyan. The point is therefore that although there was a kinyan to accept them as judges, there was no kinyan accepting a compromise.
Whatever a person accepts before the heads of the city, etc. All the town leaders are unnecessary. Three of them suffice. For this law, the phrase "seven good men of the city" is not literal. So states Hagahot Mordechai in the later additions to Bava Metzia. See Shach.
So that he cannot retract. No kinyan is required. The same applies even if they erred, and likewise if they imposed a compromise where they should have ruled according to Torah law. Sema and Shach.
He shall swear. If the accepted judge is related or otherwise disqualified, there is no Torah oath to contradict him as a witness, because he is not a valid witness. The litigant takes only a hesset oath. If the party accepted one qualified person as judge and later contradicts him about the acceptance, Sema still holds there is no Torah oath, because the dispute is not directly a denial of money but only whether he accepted him to judge. Shach objects that according to this reasoning there should not even be a hesset oath. Rather, it is a monetary denial because the judge says that he imposed a monetary liability that would disappear if the litigant successfully retracted, since the litigant could then retract earlier admissions and claims. Shach therefore rules that where one qualified judge says he was accepted, the litigant must take a Torah oath to contradict that single witness. If two judges say they were accepted, all agree they are believed and the litigants cannot retract. See what I wrote above in siman 12 concerning Mahari Sasson's view in Tumim.
Many who are not learned, etc. This appears to refer to a town lacking Torah scholars, where the residents appointed unlearned judges so they would not be forced to litigate in secular courts, as Rashba writes in a responsum cited above at the beginning of siman 8. But where the town contains knowledgeable Torah scholars fit to judge the people, Heaven forbid that unlearned judges be appointed. It is like planting an idolatrous tree beside Hashem's altar. One who does so deserves rebuke and excommunication, because he dishonors Torah and distorts justice. Certainly even a single resident may object. This is clear and obvious.
