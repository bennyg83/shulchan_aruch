One who accepted upon himself, etc. Hagahot Ashri to the chapter Zeh Borer writes: “The entire Mishnah, whether one accepted ‘my father or your father’ as judge, accepted an oath by the other's life, or accepted a relative or otherwise disqualified person, concerns acceptance before beit din. If it was outside beit din and no kinyan was made, he may retract even after final judgment, according to all opinions.” Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Yesh Nochalin, however, writes that the Mishnah concerns acceptance outside beit din. If he accepted a relative or disqualified person before beit din, he cannot retract even before final judgment. Likewise, where he said, “Swear to me by the life of your head,” outside beit din and the other party swore as instructed, he cannot retract; but if he said it before beit din, then as soon as the other party accepted the oath it is as though beit din ordered him to swear. Beit Yosef cites Hagahot Ashri and then writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham holds that even if the statement was made outside beit din, he cannot retract once the other party swore, and that Nimukei Yosef writes the same in the name of later authorities. Darkei Moshe cites this. In my opinion, the law follows Hagahot Ashri, as I shall explain. Moreover, Rabbeinu Yerucham agrees with Hagahot Ashri in principle. His statement concerns only an oath, because the other party acted in reliance on him and actually swore. This is precise in Rabbeinu Yerucham's language. But regarding acceptance of one's father or the other's father, Rabbeinu Yerucham himself writes explicitly that if the acceptance was not before beit din, he may retract even after final judgment so long as no kinyan was made. Hagahot Ashri's statement that one may retract outside beit din even regarding “swear to me by the life of your head” may be explained as referring to the stage at which the other party merely accepted the oath, which Hagahot Ashri regards as final judgment, or when beit din told him to swear as proposed. Rabbeinu Yerucham expressly agrees that if the original statement was outside beit din, the proposer may retract at that stage. Once the other party actually swore in reliance on him, however, Hagahot Ashri agrees that he may no longer retract. Beit Yosef likewise writes in the name of the later authorities that final judgment regarding an oath is acceptance of the oath: before beit din, he cannot retract once the other party accepts; outside beit din, he may retract until the oath is actually taken, but after the other party swears in reliance on him he may not retract. Thus Rabbeinu Yerucham and Hagahot Ashri do not disagree. Nimukei Yosef also appears to agree concerning an oath and to disagree only regarding acceptance of a relative or disqualified judge, contrary to Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe, who understand him to disagree in both matters. As a matter of law, then, there is no dispute concerning an oath stated outside beit din. The dispute is only where one accepted a relative or disqualified judge. Here the primary ruling follows Hagahot Ashri: acceptance is effective only before beit din. Outside beit din, he may retract even after final judgment. Rabbeinu Yerucham supports this, and it is also proven by the Jerusalem Talmud in Zeh Borer. There it states that if one said, “Your father is trusted by me,” and accepted him before two, he may retract; before three, he may not retract. Nimukei Yosef, at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, cites this briefly and explains in the name of the Rashba and Ran that once the parties begin presenting claims before them, acceptance has occurred. Shmuel says that before two one may retract only so long as they have not taken from one and given to the other; once they have transferred the money, he may not retract. Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish say that even after they have transferred it, he may retract. Thus, even according to Shmuel, where acceptance was before two, one may retract after final judgment so long as the money has not been transferred. We rule like Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish against Shmuel, and according to them he may retract even after money has been extracted, when the acceptance was before two. Certainly he may retract after judgment but before extraction. Three persons sitting outside beit din are equivalent to two sitting as beit din. A gathering outside the fixed place of judicial session is not called a beit din even if three are present, as stated below in siman 39, seif 7. Moreover, Shmuel holds that the judgment of two judges is valid, yet even he says here that acceptance before two is not irrevocable until money is transferred. All the more so is acceptance outside beit din revocable.
Because there are two defects, etc. The Sema writes that accepting one qualified judge in place of three is not considered two defects, and that this follows from the Tur in the next siman and from Responsa Rosh, rule 56, ruling 8, cited by Darkei Moshe here. I do not agree. They do not say in place of three, implying specifically two. The Levush also states explicitly that accepting a disqualified person in place of two, or one qualified person in place of three, constitutes two defects. And above it was explained what is considered the conclusion of judgment. The Sema writes that this is when the judges say, “So-and-so, you are entitled,” and, “So-and-so, you are liable,” and all the more so when they say, “Go and pay him,” which is an unequivocal ruling that they have obligated him. By contrast, when they say, “You are liable to pay him,” it appears that they are still uncertain. But this distinction is entirely unconvincing. There is no basis to distinguish between “You are liable to pay him” and “So-and-so is entitled and so-and-so is liable.” Moreover, in siman 12, “So-and-so is entitled and so-and-so is liable” does not require both formulations. Rather, if they say either, “So-and-so, you are entitled,” or, “So-and-so, you are liable,” that is called the conclusion of judgment. The Tur states this explicitly here, saying that “So-and-so, you are liable” is the conclusion of judgment, without mentioning the successful litigant. Tosafot and the Nimukei Yosef likewise prove that there is no distinction between these formulations. They ask why “You are liable to pay him” is not the conclusion of judgment until the court says, “Go and pay him,” since in the first chapter of Sanhedrin it is taught that once the judges say, “So-and-so is entitled and so-and-so is liable,” compromise is forbidden. They answer that compromise is different, because once one party’s right has become partially established, compromise is forbidden. The Semag and Darkei Moshe also clearly indicate that the Sema’s distinction is incorrect. As a matter of practical law, the Bach rules that judgment is not concluded until the court says, “Go and pay him,” as Tosafot and the Nimukei Yosef hold, and not as the Rama writes. This appears correct. The statement in Sanhedrin that “So-and-so is entitled and so-and-so is liable” constitutes the conclusion of judgment means only that an actual transfer is unnecessary once the court has definitively decided the matter. It may therefore refer specifically to a ruling expressed as “Go and pay him.” The Tur and Mechaber can be read in this manner. The Semag likewise writes that compromise is forbidden after the conclusion of judgment, defines that conclusion with the language “So-and-so is entitled and so-and-so is liable,” and then cites Rav Nachman that it is not final until the court says, “Go and pay him.” Rabbeinu Avan similarly writes that a monetary judgment is concluded when the beit din tells the liable party, “Go and pay him.” Furthermore, the Rambam and Semag hold that the conclusion of judgment occurs only after the money has actually been transferred. Therefore, the practical ruling is that the judgment is not concluded until the beit din says, “Go and pay him,” or the judges complete the ruling and the litigant leaves the beit din. This is the primary view.
It was explained what is considered the conclusion of judgment. This excludes the formulation cited by the Tur in the name of the Rambam, and similarly by the Semag, that the judgment was concluded and money was transferred. The Tur infers that, according to the Rambam, a litigant may retract until money has actually been transferred, whereas the Tur himself holds that once the court says, “So-and-so is liable,” judgment is concluded even before payment. The Nimukei Yosef, Beit Yosef, and Bach understand that the Rambam did not mean actual transfer specifically. The Lechem Mishneh, however, maintains that the Rambam did mean it specifically, based on the Yerushalmi: one who accepted adjudication before two may retract, but Shmuel says this is only before one party took from the other and gave to his opponent; after such transfer he may not retract. This implies that the conclusion of judgment there means actual transfer. Although Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute Shmuel in the Yerushalmi, the Rambam disregards their position because the Bavli attributes to them the opposite rule, namely that after the conclusion of judgment one may not retract. The Lechem Mishneh therefore says that the Bavli overrides the Yerushalmi regarding their position, while it may accept the Yerushalmi’s definition of final judgment as actual payment. The language of the Rambam and Semag indeed indicates that “transferred money” is deliberate, as understood by the Tur, Ramach, Lechem Mishneh, and responsa of the Mabit. It also appears that the Rambam derived this from the Yerushalmi. Yet it is unnecessary to say that the Bavli disputes the Yerushalmi. The Rambam may understand that Shmuel refers to acceptance before two, where transfer prevents retraction, whereas Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish hold that even after transfer one may retract when acceptance was before two. The Bavli’s statement that after final judgment one may not retract concerns acceptance before three. Thus the Bavli and Yerushalmi do not conflict. Nevertheless, practical law follows the majority of authorities cited above: once the ruling has been issued, even before payment, judgment is concluded. This is also indicated by the ordinary language of “conclusion of judgment” throughout the Talmud. The Yerushalmi can be reconciled by saying that Shmuel introduces a separate rule: actual transfer is stronger than ordinary final judgment and prevents retraction even after acceptance before two; Rabbi Yochanan holds that before two, even transfer does not prevent retraction. Everything depends on acceptance before three, in which case, after final judgment, retraction is impossible even without transfer. This is the primary ruling.
