One who accepted a relative or disqualified person upon himself, etc. Shach cites Hagahot HaRosh that the rule that one cannot retract after final judgment applies only where the disqualified judge was accepted in beit din. If the acceptance occurred outside beit din, then even after final judgment the party can retract unless a kinyan was made. Nimukei Yosef writes the opposite distinction. According to him, only acceptance outside beit din allows retraction until final judgment. But if the litigant accepted the relative or disqualified person before beit din, he cannot retract even before final judgment. Shach decides in favor of Hagahot HaRosh and says this follows from the Yerushalmi. The Yerushalmi, on the Mishnah "my father is acceptable to me," says: if he accepted before two, he may retract; if he accepted before three, he may not retract. Shmuel says this is where money has not yet been taken from one party and given to the other. But once actual transfer occurred, he cannot retract. R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish say that even after money was transferred he may retract. Shach understands "accepted before two" as acceptance outside beit din. According to R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish, even actual transfer does not prevent retraction. According to Shmuel, only actual transfer does. Thus, even after final judgment, mere acceptance outside beit din is insufficient. That is the core of Shach's proof. At first glance, however, the Yerushalmi seems to prove Nimukei Yosef's position. Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia cites Rashba and Ran, who infer from the Yerushalmi's phrase "accepted before three, he cannot retract" that once parties begin presenting their claims before a valid beit din, they can no longer withdraw. Their presentation itself constitutes acceptance. Nimukei Yosef at the end of Bava Batra makes the same inference: after claims are presented before beit din, the parties cannot retract even if no formal selection document was written. If so, the Yerushalmi's statement "accepted before three, he cannot retract" refers merely to the beginning of argument. How can our Bavli then say that before final judgment a party who accepted a disqualified judge can retract? We cannot simply say the Bavli disagrees with the Yerushalmi. If so, Rashba and Ran lose the very Yerushalmi from which they derive their rule. The better approach would be that our Bavli discusses acceptance of a disqualified judge outside a panel of three. In that setting Shmuel, R. Yochanan, and Reish Lakish dispute the strength of later acts. But acceptance before a full beit din would indeed become binding immediately. Accordingly, this would support Nimukei Yosef. Yet that conclusion is also difficult. Once we admit the Bavli differs from the Yerushalmi concerning whether final judgment is enough without actual transfer, perhaps the Bavli also differs concerning acceptance before three. And the broad consensus of the poskim does not say that acceptance of a disqualified judge before three valid judges becomes irrevocable before final judgment. Tosafot and Rosh, in the discussion of "swear to me by the life of your head," analyze whether the oath itself counts as final judgment. That acceptance was made in beit din. If acceptance before beit din were independently irrevocable, the entire inquiry would be unnecessary. Ramban in Bava Batra, in the case of "you stole my slave," likewise indicates explicitly that even acceptance in beit din remains retractable before final judgment. It is difficult to say Rashba and Ran disagree with all these authorities. I therefore think Rashba and Ran understand the Yerushalmi differently. The phrase "my father is acceptable to me" is merely a citation-heading from the Mishnah, as the Yerushalmi often quotes a fragment of a Mishnah and then states a related but distinct rule. The following statement, "accepted before two, he may retract; before three, he may not retract," does not refer to acceptance of a relative or disqualified judge at all. It concerns acceptance of valid judges. If a litigant accepted two valid judges to judge him, he may retract so long as no formal selection was written. If he accepted three valid judges and began presenting claims before them, that constitutes binding acceptance and he cannot retract. This is the Yerushalmi from which Rashba and Ran derive their rule. The subsequent dispute of Shmuel, R. Yochanan, and Reish Lakish need not refer back to that statement. It can return to the Mishnah itself, "my father is acceptable to me," and debate R. Meir's position concerning a relative or disqualified person. Shmuel says R. Meir permits retraction only before actual transfer, while R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish permit it even after transfer. For the Sages, however, final judgment can already make the acceptance binding. This explains why the later poskim do not record the Yerushalmi's dispute as a practical dispute. It is merely an analysis of R. Meir's view. And it parallels our Bavli, where statements about "I will give you" similarly analyze the scope of R. Meir's position even though the halachah follows the Sages. Thus the Yerushalmi supplies no proof for Hagahot HaRosh's distinction between acceptance inside and outside beit din. Indeed, once the Yerushalmi is read properly, the ordinary rule emerges: with a relative or otherwise disqualified judge, a party may retract before final judgment and may not retract after final judgment, regardless of where the acceptance occurred, unless some separate kinyan changes the result. This is also supported by Rosh, klal 56, ruling 1. There Rosh says that if Shimon admits he accepted Yaakov as judge, after final judgment he cannot retract. If he denies the acceptance, he must swear. The acceptance clearly did not need to occur before three judges, because otherwise it would be difficult to understand how Shimon could deny it against a full panel. One should not force the case into a scenario where two of the three later disappeared. Rosh would have had to state such a crucial circumstance explicitly. This suggests acceptance before even a single person can become binding after final judgment. Likewise, according to those who hold in the case "swear to me by the life of your head" that until the oath is taken there is no final judgment, once the oath is actually taken the party cannot retract even if the acceptance was outside beit din, because a concrete act has occurred. If Hagahot HaRosh's distinction were absolute, the sugya's comparison between different forms of acceptance would become difficult. One could perhaps limit Hagahot HaRosh to accepting a relative as a witness rather than as a judge. An out-of-court statement accepting a witness may have less legal substance. But where the parties appoint someone to judge, the very acceptance gives him a judicial role, especially once arguments were presented before him. Even that limitation is not fully necessary. Rivash, responsum 311, does support Hagahot HaRosh in a case where money was deposited with a person who was both judge and relative. Rivash writes, among other reasons, that the acceptance was not made before a proper beit din. But Rivash appears to offer that only as an additional supporting consideration, not the sole foundation of the ruling. Most poskim state the law without distinguishing between acceptance in court and outside it. Therefore the more convincing practical rule is that before final judgment the party can retract, and after final judgment he cannot, regardless of the place of acceptance, unless a specific kinyan or concrete act creates a different result.
Where there are two disadvantages, etc. Sema writes that if the parties accept one person to judge in place of three, this is not considered two disadvantages. His proof is from Rosh, klal 56, ruling 8, where Rosh writes that one who accepted a judge and the case reached final judgment cannot retract even if both parties later agree to do so. Here, apparently, one person was accepted in place of three, yet the acceptance remains effective after final judgment. Shach, seif katan 3, rejects this proof. Perhaps there was a kinyan. Or perhaps the individual was a recognized expert and the only defect was family relationship. Or perhaps another judge sat with him so that the panel contained two instead of three, creating only one deficiency. Shach develops these possibilities at length. In my view Sema has a stronger proof from Rosh in the same klal, ruling 1. There Rosh says that if Shimon admits he accepted Yaakov as judge, Shimon cannot retract after final judgment, because in the case of "I will give you" we rule like the Sages after final judgment. If he denies the acceptance, he must swear. That case cannot readily be one involving a kinyan, because then the acceptance would already be binding even before final judgment, especially where both parties did not later consent to undo it. Nor can it readily be a case where another judge sat with Yaakov, because then how could Shimon simply deny that Yaakov had been selected and swear against the fact? He would be contradicting two judges. Nor is it reasonable to say Yaakov was both a close relative and a recognized expert. Aside from the fact that Rosh should have mentioned such an unusual feature, Shimon's response was that they had never selected Yaakov as judge and that Yaakov had ruled "like a man not requested to do so." If Yaakov was a relative, the response should simply have been that his ruling had no validity absent acceptance. And if he was a recognized expert, calling him a person who ruled without being asked would not fit the legal point. A recognized expert can sometimes judge without prior selection. Anyone reading the responsum will see that the case was not about an expert relative. It therefore appears that the parties accepted a single ordinary person to judge their dispute, and Rosh rules that after final judgment the acceptance cannot be retracted. This proves Sema's position that appointing one judge instead of three is not itself "two disadvantages." Shach's inference from Tur and Rosh's phrase "a valid person in place of two" is also weak. They discuss both accepting a witness and accepting a judge. With witnesses, two are the ordinary number, since two witnesses are equivalent to one hundred. Their wording therefore uses "two" because it applies naturally to both witnesses and judges. It does not prove that one judge in place of three creates a double defect. Sema's position is also conceptually sound. There is support from Tosafot in Sanhedrin 5, who prove that an individual expert can judge even against the parties' will. Otherwise, if his authority depended upon acceptance, what novelty would there be in his expertise? Even a nonexpert could judge if accepted. Rosh makes the same argument. But if accepting one judge instead of three were automatically a double defect, the proof would fail. Perhaps an individual expert can judge only with acceptance. Because his expertise gives him the standing of three ordinary judges, there is only one remaining issue, namely lack of compulsory authority, and acceptance cures that. A nonexpert individual would have two defects: he is both only one judge and not qualified as an expert. Acceptance might then be insufficient according to the stricter view. The fact that Tosafot do not make this distinction indicates that one judge in place of three is not categorized as two independent defects. This also helps explain Tosafot's proof that three lay judges can exercise compulsory jurisdiction like one expert. Maharsha and Maharam Lublin ask why the Gemara's statement "civil cases are judged by three" proves compulsory power. Perhaps the rule merely says that if parties agree to be judged, three lay judges suffice. At first glance one might answer that the Mishnah needs to teach three because if there were fewer and one were a relative or otherwise disqualified, acceptance would not work due to two defects. But if so, the same answer could be given concerning the individual expert: the Gemara teaches that one expert judge suffices with acceptance because he is legally equivalent to three ordinary judges, whereas an ordinary individual would present two defects. Tosafot reject such an approach by implication. Their proof therefore supports Sema that numerical reduction to one is itself only one type of defect. The Mishnah's case of three cattle herders points in the same direction. All three are individually unqualified, yet acceptance validates them. We do not count the fact that there are three disqualified people as multiple independent defects. It is one category of defect repeated across the panel. Likewise, accepting one person in place of three is a single numerical defect, not two. Accordingly, if three relatives are accepted as judges, that too should be treated as one category of defect, just as three cattle herders constitute one type of disqualification rather than three separate disadvantages. Sema's ruling is therefore correct.
And above, siman 12, etc. Sema explains, as stated in Urim, that although above we say "So-and-so, you are liable" counts as final judgment for purposes of forbidding compromise, the phrase "you are obligated to pay" may still not count as final judgment in this context. It sounds somewhat tentative, as though the court has not yet reached complete certainty. Derishah attributes this distinction to Nimukei Yosef. But anyone examining Nimukei Yosef in Perek Merubah will see that he does not distinguish between "So-and-so, you are liable" and "you are obligated to pay." He distinguishes only between "go and pay him" and the earlier formulation "you are obligated to pay him." Indeed, Nimukei Yosef himself asks from Sanhedrin, where "So-and-so, you are liable" counts as final judgment and compromise is forbidden, while in Bava Metzia "you are obligated to pay him" is not yet treated as final judgment for certain purposes. He answers by distinguishing the consequence of finality for retraction from the consequence for permitting compromise. He does not answer that the wording itself differs. This proves that in his view "So-and-so, you are liable" and "you are obligated to pay him" are legally equivalent expressions. Tosafot likewise indicate this, as Shach writes. However, I found in Shitah Mekubetzet to Bava Metzia, citing Tosafot HaRosh, an explanation like Sema's. Tosafot HaRosh distinguishes between Sanhedrin's phrase "So-and-so, you are liable," which counts as final judgment, and Bava Metzia's phrase "you are obligated to pay him," which is not final judgment. Thus Sema does have an important authority supporting his distinction. Nevertheless, Shach is correct that most poskim do not distinguish between the two formulations. Shach concludes practically that even "So-and-so, you are liable" is not final judgment until the court says "go and pay him," relying heavily on Semag. But see what I wrote above in siman 12. Semag does not hold that way. Semag agrees that "So-and-so, you are liable" is enough to prohibit compromise and counts as final judgment for that law. Therefore we should not depart from Shulchan Aruch's ruling. There is another issue. Rambam holds that even after the court says "go and pay him," the litigant may still retract acceptance of a disqualified judge until money is actually transferred pursuant to the ruling. Shach agrees with Lechem Mishneh, who derives this from the Yerushalmi. The Yerushalmi says: acceptance before two can be retracted; acceptance before three cannot be retracted. Shmuel and R. Yochanan with Reish Lakish then dispute the case of actual transfer. According to Lechem Mishneh, this means that where acceptance was before two, R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish permit retraction even after money was transferred. But where acceptance was before three, all agree that once actual transfer occurred there is no retraction. Before actual transfer, however, even acceptance before three remains retractable. This reading is strained. According to Shmuel, acceptance before three must already be binding even without actual transfer. Otherwise there would be no meaningful distinction between acceptance before two and before three. If so, when R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish disagree with Shmuel concerning acceptance before two, from where do we invent an additional dispute concerning acceptance before three? The Yerushalmi records only one disagreement: whether actual transfer prevents retraction in the two-person case. It never states that R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish also disagree with Shmuel about the separate three-person case and require actual transfer there. That would be a new and unstated controversy. One cannot simply say: since they are one level more lenient in the two-person case, they must also be one level more lenient in the three-person case. That is exactly what requires proof. Perhaps in the three-person case all three Amoraim agree that acceptance is immediately binding. Therefore I do not find the Yerushalmi proof for Rambam convincing. What appears to me is that Rambam can instead be understood through Rif's position concerning zabla. Rif holds that where the parties selected valid judges, they can still retract until the written selection is executed or until final judgment, depending on the circumstances discussed above. Ramban in his Torah commentary raises an important difficulty from the Mishnah "my father is acceptable to me." If even selection of valid judges can be retracted before final judgment, what novelty is there in teaching that one may retract acceptance of a disqualified relative before final judgment? The same would be true of valid judges. According to Rif, the answer must be that with a disqualified judge the phrase "final judgment" in the Mishnah is not meant in the ordinary minimal sense. The acceptance remains retractable until the judgment is carried into practical effect. With valid judges, ordinary final judgment fixes the forum and ends the right of retraction. With a disqualified judge, a stronger level of completion is necessary. Rambam can therefore understand the Mishnah as requiring an actual act pursuant to the ruling, such as transferring money, before the acceptance becomes irrevocable. This is the distinctive law of accepting a disqualified judge and explains why the Mishnah needs to teach it separately from acceptance of valid judges. Rambam's position is then coherent without relying upon Lechem Mishneh's strained Yerushalmi inference. Shach concludes that most poskim disagree with Rambam. They maintain the simpler rule: once ordinary final judgment is reached, acceptance of the disqualified judge becomes binding even without actual transfer. How, then, do they answer the difficulty under Rif that valid judges too may sometimes be retracted before final judgment? The answer follows Rama above in siman 13. Rif's extended right of retraction for valid judges applies where the custom is to write formal selection documents. Where there is no such custom, once the parties have presented their claims before valid judges they cannot retract. A disqualified judge is different. Even in that setting, the party can retract until final judgment. Thus the Mishnah still teaches a genuine distinction between valid and disqualified judges. This preserves the majority view against Rambam. See what I wrote above in siman 13, seif 2, concerning these competing positions.
And regarding testimony, etc. Bach cites Raavan, as quoted in a Prague edition of Maharam's responsa, concerning a case where Shimon said to Reuven, "Your wife shall be believed against me if I have not repaid her," and she said that he had not repaid her. Raavan ruled that this was still before final judgment and Shimon could retract. Bach therefore concludes that Raavan disputes Maharam in Mordechai, who rules here that once the accepted witness has testified, the litigant can no longer retract because the testimony is treated as final judgment. In my view there is no dispute. The reason one cannot retract after an ordinary witness has testified is that the witness himself can no longer retract his testimony. Once he has testified, the rule "once he has testified, he cannot testify differently" applies. The testimony is therefore fixed and the matter has reached the equivalent of final judgment. But Reuven's wife in Raavan's case is different. If Reuven himself had said, "You did not repay me," and afterward remembered and said, "You did repay me," he would certainly be believed because he is a litigant, not a witness. Below, siman 120, we rule that if a debtor repays the creditor's wife, the debtor is discharged. Therefore, with respect to receipt of this payment, the wife has the legal standing of the creditor himself. If she first says, "He did not repay me," and afterward says, "He did repay me," she can retract and is believed just as the creditor himself could. Since her own statement remains retractable, the case has not reached fixed finality merely because she spoke. Accordingly, Shimon can retract his acceptance of her statement. This does not resemble an ordinary witness, whose testimony cannot be withdrawn once given. Thus Raavan and Maharam are fully consistent.
He cannot retract, etc. Rosh's responsum concludes that this remains true even if both litigants later agree to retract. Beit Yosef asks: if both parties agree, who can stop them? He answers that the concern is the honor of the judge. Sema explains that a complete waiver is required. Shach explains the case as one where retracting would harm third parties, and Mishneh LaMelech offers a similar explanation while admitting it is strained. I do not understand why any of these explanations is necessary. Certainly both parties can agree to take their dispute to another judge if they fear the first judge erred. But the first judge originally sat with valid judicial authority. Therefore any admission made before him is a judicial admission, not an out-of-court statement. Their later agreement to seek another forum does not retroactively erase his status as a judge. Thus an admission made before him remains an admission in beit din. Likewise, testimony that he received remains legally received testimony, and a witness who testified before him cannot retract merely because the parties later choose another judge. By contrast, if the parties retract before final judgment, the first person never reaches the status of a completed judicial forum for this purpose. His prior reception of testimony can then lose its binding judicial effect, and admissions made before him can be treated as outside beit din, as Rashba explains. But after final judgment, the judicial proceeding already took legal effect. The parties' later agreement cannot retroactively nullify the testimony, admissions, or judicial status that existed at the time. This is the straightforward meaning of Rosh, and I am surprised that the great commentators did not explain it this simply.
