At the beginning of Perek HaMafkid the Gemara asks: if a custodian says, "I will pay," and later says, "I will not pay," do we say that he retracted and therefore the owner does not transfer to him the right to double payment from the thief, or perhaps he still stands by his original commitment and is merely postponing payment? Tosafot writes that Rabbeinu Tam initially wished to prove from this that one who says, "I will pay," may retract. Rabbeinu Tam later retracted that proof: once the custodian accepts liability and says, "I will pay," that constitutes his final judgment and he can no longer retract. The Gemara asks only whether his later attempt to retract prevents the transfer of the right to double payment. Rosh similarly proves that once he says, "I will pay," he cannot retract, for if the statement itself remained freely retractable, it could not transfer the future double payment. With this I can explain the sugya in Perek HaKones concerning an armed robber. The Gemara seeks proof from a renter whose cow was stolen and who says, "I will pay rather than swear," after which the thief is found and pays double to the renter. It initially assumes Rabbi Yehudah's view that a renter is like a paid custodian. Since the Mishnah says, "I will pay and not swear," it implies that he could otherwise exempt himself by an oath. This would fit a claim of armed robbery, and since double payment later goes to the renter, it would prove that an armed robber has the law of a thief. The Gemara rejects this by saying perhaps the Mishnah follows Rabbi Meir, who treats a renter like an unpaid custodian, or alternatively follows the reversed version of the dispute. Tosafot is forced to explain why the reversed version helps and says perhaps it is brought only incidentally from the parallel sugya in Perek HaUmanin. In my view the point is direct. In Perek Zeh Borer Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute whether one who accepted an otherwise disqualified judge may retract. Reish Lakish limits the dispute to before final judgment, while Rabbi Yochanan says they disagree even after final judgment. The Sages opposing Rabbi Meir are Rabbi Yehudah, as the Tosefta indicates. We rule like Rabbi Yochanan that they disagree after final judgment and like the Sages that one cannot retract. Consequently, according to Rabbi Meir, even after final judgment one may retract. Then a custodian's statement, "I will pay," would not bind him, because its entire force is that it is his final judgment. If Rabbi Meir allows retraction after final judgment, such a statement cannot transfer the double payment, as Rosh proves. Therefore the Gemara initially assumes the Mishnah follows Rabbi Yehudah, under whom final judgment is binding. Since Rabbi Yehudah treats a renter as a paid custodian, armed robbery must count as theft. The Gemara answers that perhaps the dispute over final judgment follows Reish Lakish, so even Rabbi Meir would agree that after final judgment one cannot retract. Alternatively, according to the reversed version, Rabbi Yehudah is the one who treats a renter as an unpaid custodian, and the Mishnah can therefore follow Rabbi Yehudah without proving anything about armed robbery. This explains the sugya directly.
