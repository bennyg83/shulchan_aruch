And it is forbidden. The Sema writes that if one litigant has a legal advantage under non-Jewish law that he would not have under Jewish law, then a kinyan to accept that law is effective. The rule that a person cannot stipulate against what is written in the Torah applies only to an ordinary condition, but once a binding kinyan has been made he cannot retract. If the non-Jewish law gives neither party any particular advantage, however, the kinyan is merely a kinyan on words. See below, siman 26.
Already. The Shach disagrees and holds that no distinction should be made between initially and after the fact. He does, however, distinguish between a general kinyan to litigate according to non-Jewish law, which is ineffective even after the fact, and a kinyan accepting a specifically named non-Jewish adjudicator. In the latter case the parties have demonstrated that this person is trusted by them and they rely on him, so the acceptance is effective just as when they accept a relative or otherwise disqualified judge.
