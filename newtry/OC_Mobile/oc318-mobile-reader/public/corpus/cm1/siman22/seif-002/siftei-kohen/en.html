"But if he has been given to him, then he has made a call to him."
But if he has already adjudicated before him. The Rama’s distinction between the initial permissibility and the validity after the fact apparently derives from the Mordechai, which says that the kinyan must be upheld. To avoid conflict with the Baal HaTerumot and Tur, who rule that such a kinyan is ineffective, the Rama understood them as speaking initially and the Mordechai as speaking after the fact. This distinction requires study. The Mordechai records that one who accepted a relative or otherwise disqualified person as equivalent to two valid judges, and made a kinyan, cannot retract. It then relates a case before Maharam in which a litigant accepted a particular gentile adjudicator by kinyan and later sought to retract upon noticing that the adjudicator inclined his words and accepted a bribe. Maharam ruled that he could not retract. The Mordechai does not say that the gentile had already ruled; its language implies the opposite. Moreover, the Rif and Rambam, whom it cites as support, explicitly discuss retraction before final judgment. Nor do the Baal HaTerumot and Tur distinguish between the initial stage and after the fact. Their statement that the kinyan is nothing implies that it cannot bind even after the fact. Perhaps the Mordechai disagrees. To reconcile them, one may distinguish between a general kinyan to adjudicate under gentile law, which is invalid, and a kinyan accepting one specifically identified gentile adjudicator, indicating that the parties personally trust him, like acceptance of a relative or disqualified judge. This distinction is compelling. The Mechaber, however, appears to understand the Baal HaTerumot and Tur as invalidating even acceptance of a specifically named gentile. I do not know his source. His further distinction between adjudication and testimony also appears incorrect. The Mordechai’s language concerning one who “inclined his words and accepted a bribe” applies to a judge, not a witness. The Rama likewise plainly understands the Mordechai as discussing acceptance of a gentile judge, but distinguishes between the initial stage and after the fact. In my view that distinction is unproven; the distinction stated above is preferable, though the matter requires study.
