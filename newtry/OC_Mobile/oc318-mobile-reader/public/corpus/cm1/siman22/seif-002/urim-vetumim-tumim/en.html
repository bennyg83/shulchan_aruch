But if he already judged, etc. Rama writes this distinction to reconcile two sources. Mordechai says that a kinyan is effective. Below, siman 26, however, Baal HaTerumot and Tur rule that a kinyan to litigate before a non-Jewish court is not effective. Rama therefore distinguishes between a kinyan made before the non-Jewish judge has ruled and one whose judicial ruling has already been issued. There is then no contradiction. Shach objects from the very incident cited by Mordechai. A person accepted a certain non-Jew as authoritative, made a kinyan, and when he realized that the non-Jew was distorting the matter and had taken a bribe, he sought to retract. Maharam ruled that he could not. Shach says this proves Rif's rule that once there is a kinyan, even two defects do not permit retraction, and the case appears to be before final judgment. The phrase "he realized that he was distorting his words" sounds like the non-Jew had not yet issued the ruling. But that inference from the phrase "he realized" is not compelling. On the contrary, how would he know what was in the non-Jew's mind unless he had already heard the ruling? The source says he realized that the non-Jew "distorted the judgment," not merely that he intended to do so. This sounds as though the ruling had already been issued improperly. Nor is it difficult that Mordechai cites this as support for Rif, who speaks before final judgment. If a kinyan is capable of overcoming two defects, then once made it works even before final judgment, because nothing further remains after the kinyan. If it is incapable of overcoming them, then even after final judgment it should remain ineffective. Thus the incident can illuminate the basic strength of the kinyan without matching Rif's procedural stage exactly. However, the truth is as Bedek HaBayit writes: that incident concerned accepting the non-Jew as a witness, not as a judge. Shach objects that the phrase "distorted the judgment" sounds judicial and says Bedek HaBayit must be wrong. But Shach evidently saw only the abridged version in Mordechai. In the fuller Maharam of Rothenburg responsa, Kremenz edition, small volume, siman 245, it is explicit that the non-Jew was accepted as a witness, not as judge. That source too cites the incident in connection with Rif. Had Shach seen the full responsum, he would not have challenged Beit Yosef. The interpretation of Beit Yosef is therefore correct. Still, even though Maharam's case itself does not prove Rama's distinction, Rama's ruling may be correct in principle. The prohibition against litigating before a non-Jewish court is fundamentally that doing so gives prestige and authority to idolatrous legal institutions and treats non-Jews as judges. The core transgression lies in going before them and submitting the case to their judgment. Once that prohibited act has already occurred and the non-Jew has issued the decision, enforcing the result does not itself repeat the same act of honoring that court. Even where the non-Jew judged according to Torah law, the original submission to him was prohibited. But once the judgment has been rendered, the prohibition has already been transgressed. There is therefore room to say that a kinyan made for the resulting obligation need not now be void merely because the process that produced it involved a prior prohibition. This does not fit neatly within the general principle that where Torah says "do not do," an act done in violation is ineffective. If it did, the Gemara should have identified this as a practical dispute between Abaye and Rava. The matter therefore still requires study, but Rama's distinction has a substantial conceptual basis.
