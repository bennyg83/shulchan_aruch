If he says, "I will pay." But if he merely says, "I will not swear," his words may be understood to mean that he wishes to reverse the oath onto the other party, and he may do so. Likewise, if beit din imposed an oath upon him and he said, "I will swear," that is only as though he said, "I will comply with the law," and he may afterward reverse the oath. This is the Shach's view and the accepted custom, unlike the Sema. See also Netivot, seif katan 6, that the same applies to a borrower on a note who became obligated to swear and accepted the oath. Even after leaving beit din he may again reverse the oath onto the claimant.
And he left beit din. The same applies to all the preceding cases cited by the Mechaber. Once he has left beit din he may no longer retract. Even if he is still physically in beit din, if they are no longer occupied with that matter, he likewise may not retract. This is the halachah.
For this is a final judgment. Ramban in his novellae to Bava Batra asks: when one says, "Swear and collect," is this not like accepting a relative as though he were two witnesses, which contains two disadvantages and therefore requires a kinyan? Nimukei Yosef answers that since he trusted him only subject to an oath, it is not considered two disadvantages. From this we learn that if one accepts a relative as equivalent to two witnesses provided that the relative swears, this likewise is not considered two disadvantages, and he cannot retract after final judgment. Urim VeTumim. A grant of credibility made at the time of the loan is also not considered two disadvantages. First, the loan and transfer of money itself is like a kinyan. Second, it is equivalent to accepting that he will not repay without witnesses. Rosh.
He cannot retract. According to Rama's ruling that immediately upon leaving beit din one may no longer retract, this must refer to a case where they are still occupied with the same matter and the other party jumped and swore before beit din without holding a sacred object. Even so, he cannot retract. Although the reversal was made on the assumption that the oath would be with a sacred object, and the oath must therefore be repeated properly, the oath already taken is at least effective to prevent retraction from the reversal. He must then swear again with a sacred object. The Shach disagrees: since the first oath is ineffective, it also does not prevent retraction.
