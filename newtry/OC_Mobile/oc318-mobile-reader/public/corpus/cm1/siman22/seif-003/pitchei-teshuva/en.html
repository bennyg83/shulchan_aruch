A relative or one who is disqualified. Knesset HaGedolah, entry 2, writes: If the relative revealed his disposition and said that he is necessarily obligated to assist and support his relative, the litigant's acceptance of him is ineffective. See Re'em, part I, siman 24. End of quote. This is also cited by the Baer Heitev of Maharam of Pinsk, seif katan 1. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 168, concerning a woman who, before her death, instructed her sons that if a dispute arose between them, a certain relative should judge them. They gave a formal handshake undertaking to that person. One of the sons now refuses to litigate before him, claiming that he is an ignoramus who does not know how to judge according to Torah law, and that at minimum he should not judge alone but should join a beit din. He further claims that he learned that this relative favors one of the brothers more than the others. Shevut Yaakov responds that, apart from the formal undertaking, there is no binding rule of 'it is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased' here, because that rule was said regarding the deceased's property and inheritance, not other matters, and even concerning property it applies only where the property was entrusted to a third party for that purpose. See what I write below in siman 252, seif 2, seif katan 1. Nevertheless, as an act of kindness beyond the strict law, it is fitting to fulfill the deceased's words even in other matters, although one does not compel conduct beyond the strict law except through verbal persuasion. See what I wrote above in siman 12, seif 2, seif katan 8 and 6. However, because of the formal undertaking, he may be compelled to litigate before that person. His argument that the man is an ignoramus is no argument, since he knew this from the outset and accepted him on that basis, meaning that he would rule according to his best judgment. See Yoreh Deah, siman 132, seif 18. If both parties want a ruling according to Torah law, that person can ask a beit din and rule in accordance with their instructions, as explained in Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 230. His argument that the judge favors one brother more is likewise no argument. Although, absent a formal undertaking, such a claim can be effective, as Knesset HaGedolah writes in siman 2 in the name of Responsa Re'em, it is ineffective where there was an oath or formal handshake undertaking. First, Yoreh Deah siman 230 explains that a vow whose annulment involves some element of transgression may not be annulled, and here there is at least some element of transgression, because beyond the strict law they are obligated to fulfill their mother's instruction. How then could the oath be annulled? Furthermore, Yoreh Deah siman 228, seif 21, explains that where four people swear together to do one thing, the oath is deemed to have been made with the consent of the public and cannot be annulled without their consent. Accordingly, in this case, how could one brother's undertaking be annulled without the will and consent of all the other brothers and sisters? It is therefore clear that in this case there is no basis for annulment at all. This is the substance of his ruling. See there further. It will be cited below in siman 252, seif 2.
