Bach objects from the Gemara's rule: if he says, "I will not swear," we release him from the oath, even though he did not explicitly say he would pay. Bach therefore rules unlike Maharik. Shach, seif katan 17, responds that the Gemara concerns a Torah oath, which ordinarily cannot be reversed onto the opposing party. That answer is not decisive. The rule appears in a baraita without qualification: if he says, "I will not swear," we release him. Yet Mar bar Rav Ashi holds that even a Torah oath can be reversed. According to him, how can the baraita state categorically that the litigant is released merely because he says, "I will not swear"? Bach's question therefore has force. Nevertheless, for practical halachah Sema's ruling appears correct. There are already many authorities who disagree with Rashi and hold that even if a litigant explicitly says, "I will pay," he can still retract so long as the opposing party has not yet sworn. Although we rule like Rashi, where the litigant's words are themselves capable of an alternative explanation, the possessor can certainly invoke the contrary authorities and say kim li. He therefore need not pay until the opposing party swears. Sema, Maharshal, Bach, and Maharam also infer from Tur that if a litigant said, "I will swear," and then left beit din, he can no longer reverse the oath onto the other party. Shach writes that this inference from Tur is not clear and notes that the established practice in Jewish communities is to permit reversal afterward. He is correct that this is indeed the practice I too have seen in Jewish courts. Still, the earlier inference from Tur may be textually correct according to the position Tur was discussing. Tur there follows the authorities who treat a completed verbal acceptance as final judgment even though an act remains to be performed. But Shulchan Aruch, in the analogous case "swear to me by the life of your head," holds that final judgment is not reached until the oath is actually taken. According to Shulchan Aruch, a statement "I will swear" still depends upon future action. Until the oath is taken, the matter has not reached final judgment. He may therefore retract and reverse the oath. Bach states this explicitly. Thus the established practice of Jewish batei din is correct.
For this is final judgment. Sema asks, as noted in Urim, why Rama mentions the effect of leaving beit din only in this one case. If leaving the court after acceptance is itself final judgment, Rama should have stated the same rule for all the cases listed in this seif. The answer depends on how we understand Rosh's discussion in Sanhedrin. Rosh addresses the case where one party owes an oath and the other says, "Swear to me by the life of your head." He says that if the oath has already been taken, the accepting party cannot retract because the case has reached final judgment. Before the oath, he can retract. Rosh then says that according to this approach, in the Bava Batra case "you stole my slave" the correct text must be "and he swore, he cannot retract," rather than simply "he swore," depending on the textual version. Rosh also cites the law from Shevuat HaDayanim that if a litigant says, "I will not swear," the court releases him. Rosh explains that they hurry him toward payment, but even after saying "I will not swear" he can still retract before paying. Rashi's commentary there, however, implies that once he leaves beit din after making that acceptance, he cannot retract. What he accepted before beit din and then allowed to stand when departing is treated as final judgment. Rosh concludes: "This appears correct." There are two ways to understand his conclusion. One can say, as Bach and Maharam understand, that Rosh ultimately retracts everything he wrote earlier and adopts Rashi across the board. Accordingly, even in "swear to me by the life of your head" and in "swear and collect," once the party leaves beit din he can no longer retract, even before the oath is taken. On that reading, the Bava Batra text would be "he swore" rather than "and he swore," and Rosh's conclusion would align with his ruling in Shevuot and with his comments in Perek HaMafkid. This is why some later authorities question Beit Yosef's statement that Rosh agrees with Rambam. Shach tries to defend Beit Yosef by saying that Rosh may read "and he swore" like Rambam but still accept Rashi's legal principle. The case would then concern a litigant who remained before beit din, where actual swearing is needed. But this is difficult because Rosh writes explicitly in Shevuot that according to Rashi the correct reading is "he swore," not "and he swore." If the litigant had not left the court, the Bava Batra passage would teach a weaker rule than Rashi and would not support Rosh's stated textual conclusion. However, Tur's own presentation strongly suggests a different understanding of his father. In the short rulings to Sanhedrin, Tur first records the law of "swear to me by the life of your head" and writes simply that until the oath is taken the party may retract. He does not distinguish whether the party left beit din. Only afterward does he record Rashi's rule concerning one who says, "I will not swear; I will pay," and there he distinguishes: before leaving beit din he can retract, but after leaving he cannot. This indicates that Tur understood Rosh as limiting Rashi's rule to that particular category, not as applying it universally to every acceptance involving a future act. The short rulings to Bava Metzia omit the issue, and those to Shevuot record only Rashi's rule concerning "I will not swear" and departure from beit din. Beit Yosef likewise presents Rosh in a way consistent with the text "and he swore." Tur himself, after citing Rashi and saying Rosh agreed with him, later writes that when one party reverses an oath onto the other, if the opposing party has already sworn he cannot retract, but before the oath he can. He again does not make the result depend on leaving beit din. That is precisely the Bava Batra case of "swear and collect." If Rashi's departure rule applied to all such cases, the opposing party would be compelled to swear after the first litigant left beit din. Sema himself notes this implication. Therefore Tur clearly distinguishes the categories. It appears that Tur understood his father as follows. In Bava Metzia and Perek Shevuat HaDayanim, Rosh mostly quotes the approach of Tosafot. His own decisive reasoning appears in Sanhedrin, where he distinguishes between cases in which a further significant act remains and cases in which nothing meaningful remains. Where the acceptance still requires an oath, the matter is incomplete. Even leaving beit din does not necessarily make it final. This is the view also cited by Shach in seif katan 18 from Ramban and Raavan. Thus Rosh could accept Rashi concerning "I will pay," where the obligation is settled and only payment remains, while still allowing retraction in "swear to me" until the oath itself occurs. There is another way to understand Rosh's textual discussion. Rosh writes that according to Rashi the Bava Batra text is "he swore, he cannot retract," not "and he swore." That wording can bear two meanings. It could mean the court compels him to swear because he already left beit din. Or it could mean: if he in fact swore, then there is no retraction; if he did not swear, retraction remains possible. The wording itself is not decisive. If the text had "and he swore," however, it would clearly imply that actual swearing had already happened as part of the case's completion. The Gemara's question "what does this teach us, we already learned it" would then be more difficult, because the baraita could be teaching specifically that leaving beit din is insufficient and actual swearing is needed. Therefore Rosh may have preferred the shorter text "he swore" because it leaves the precise procedural stage unstated and fits the Gemara's question, while his own substantive halachah still distinguishes cases where a future act is indispensable. This distinction also fits Rosh's broader view concerning exaggerated acceptance. Rosh holds that where there are two defects, a kinyan is required even after final judgment, because the litigant's acceptance has the character of exaggeration, as Ramban explains. The phrase "swear to me by the life of your head" is itself an exaggerated form of acceptance, since this is not a true oath in the ordinary legal sense, as Nimukei Yosef and Ramban explain. All the more so in the Bava Batra case "you stole my slave," the acceptance is unusual and exaggerated. Therefore, only once the oath is actually taken does the element of exaggeration disappear through concrete performance. This strongly supports the view that Rosh distinguished between cases where a significant act remains and those where it does not. One can ask how far that logic extends to ordinary reversal of an oath, where there is no obvious exaggeration. Still, whatever the precise conceptual boundary, Tur's presentation is clear that Rosh did not apply Rashi's departure rule uniformly to every case. This explains Rama's drafting. Rama did not add a dissenting gloss to all the cases listed by Mechaber because, where an act remains to be performed, Rosh himself can agree that actual performance is needed. Only in the case Rama identifies does departure from beit din itself create sufficient finality. Shach's own comments also suggest this was Rama's understanding. For practical halachah, however, I am inclined toward Bach's stricter view. Where a litigant accepted the matter before beit din and then left without retracting, that should generally be treated as final judgment, as the great majority of authorities indicate. Nimukei Yosef says this is Rif's opinion. Maharam Lublin questions that attribution because in the printed Rif the text reads "and he swore." But he evidently did not consult Milchamot Hashem. Ramban testifies there that Rif originally wrote the text as printed, "and he swore," but later personally struck out the conjunction and changed it to "he swore." That handwritten correction by Rif himself is decisive evidence. Thus Nimukei Yosef correctly reports Rif's final view. The only prominent authorities clearly disagreeing are Rambam and Semag, and they follow their broader position that even after a formal judgment, so long as no concrete act has yet been carried out and no money transferred, retraction remains possible. We do not rule like them on that general question. Therefore, even in the oath context, once the party leaves beit din after accepting the result, the stronger practical view is that he cannot retract. The halachah should follow the majority of the poskim.
