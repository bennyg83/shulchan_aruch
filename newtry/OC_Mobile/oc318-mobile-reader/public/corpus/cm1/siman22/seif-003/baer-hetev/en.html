He pays. The Sema writes that it appears to him that even if a litigant said in beit din, "I will not swear," and then left, he may still reverse the oath onto his opponent. He may say that his intent was, "I will not swear, but will instead reverse the oath," so long as he did not say, "I will not swear and I will pay." He further writes that if a defendant was obligated to take a rabbinic hesset oath and said in beit din, "I will swear," and left, he may no longer reverse it afterward. The Shach challenges this. Only where he said, "I will not swear; rather, I will pay," can he not retract after leaving, because by saying "I will not swear" he admitted liability, and an admission is like one hundred witnesses. But if he said, "I will swear," that is merely saying, "I will stand by and fulfill the judgment," not that he specifically accepted an obligation to swear. He may therefore still reverse the oath. The Shach states that this is the practice in several holy communities.
To retract. The Shach distinguishes as follows: if the oath itself was taken before beit din, then even if the statement accepting it was made outside beit din it is effective. But if the oath was taken outside beit din, it appears clear that the law is with the defendant, especially since the claimant seeks to extract money and the burden of proof rests upon him. Beit Yosef and Levush further write that if the defendant denies the alleged acceptance and says, "I did not trust him," he must swear that he did not say so, and afterward take the Torah oath required above in seif 1, analogous to one who denies having accepted a judge. In my view this is incorrect, for since the claimant can prevail only through an oath, we do not impose another oath over that issue, since it is not yet a definite monetary denial. Who says that he will in fact take the oath? This is as the poskim write in siman 91 regarding a shopkeeper's ledger. It is unlike seif 1, where the judge immediately imposes monetary liability. Although the present rule is in any event inapplicable where the oath was taken outside beit din, as explained, it remains relevant where one party claims that the oath was validly reversed upon him in order to collect and the other denies it. The latter need not swear about the alleged reversal. The Shach adds that he later found clear proof for this in Baal HaItur. See there.
