An oath in beit din, etc. Meaning a stringent oath that the claimant was obligated to take in order to collect, or the defendant was obligated to take in order to be exempt, and the opposing litigant agreed to reduce it to a lighter oath, such as swearing by the life of his head, either to exempt himself or to receive payment.
Until the judgment is concluded. With an oath, completion of judgment means that the oath was actually taken, as the Mechaber concludes. According to Rashi and the Rosh, leaving beit din can also be treated as completion; Rama rules this below.
A hesset oath, and he reversed it. This excludes a Torah oath, which cannot ordinarily be reversed onto the other party, as explained in siman 87.
Rather, 'I will pay,' etc. The Tur writes that if one was obligated by beit din to take a rabbinic oath, remained silent and did not say he wished to reverse it onto his opponent, left beit din, and afterward says he wishes to reverse it, he may do so, because silence is not equivalent to an express undertaking. It appears that even if he said in beit din, 'I will not swear,' and left, he may later say he meant only, 'I will not swear, but will reverse the oath,' so long as he did not say, 'I will not swear and I will pay.' But if a defendant obligated in hesset said before beit din, 'I will swear,' and then left, he cannot later reverse it, as I wrote in the Perishah.
And he left beit din, he cannot, etc. In the Derishah I proved that according to Rashi and the Rosh this applies throughout the kinds of accepted judicial arrangements discussed here. Whatever a person accepts before beit din and then leaves is treated as a completed matter from which he cannot retract. Ran and Nimukei Yosef add that according to Rashi even if he has not physically left but the judges are no longer occupied with that matter, it counts as departure and completion. The reason is that anything accepted in beit din is binding once the immediate deliberation ends; while they are still occupied with the same matter, however, it is no stronger than a kinyan from which the parties may retract while engaged in the same transaction. Acceptance outside beit din has no comparable point of completion until the oath is actually taken.
Some disagree with this. This is Rabbeinu Tam and those who follow him, who hold that even after saying, 'I will not swear but will pay,' and leaving, he can retract. Tosafot later records that Rabbeinu Tam retracted and accepted that an undertaking before beit din is binding. The Mechaber's adoption of Rambam may also be consistent with the view that leaving beit din completes the matter; his references to the oath actually being taken can be understood as referring to acceptance outside beit din or while the judges are still occupied with the matter.
He cannot retract. The Beit Yosef rules this in practice. Even though the reversed oath may still need to be retaken with the formal requirements applicable to a serious oath, once he has already reversed it and thereby trusted his opponent's oath, that first oath is sufficient to create the legal finality that prevents retraction. The Mechaber's statement that he may retract until the judgment is concluded and the oath is taken comes to teach that mere acceptance is not always immediately final, not that retraction remains meaningful after the oath has already done its legal work.
