If they made a kinyan from him, etc. The Mechaber, in Beit Yosef, ruled like Rambam because the Rosh holds like him. Maharam Lublin, Responsa siman 113, writes that the Mechaber erred in this, and the Bach likewise challenges him. In my humble opinion, they wrote this without justification. Beit Yosef's intent is only with respect to the Rivash's view that the text reads "he swore," without the conjunction, meaning that once a person accepted upon himself in beit din to swear he can no longer retract, contrary to Rambam. On this point the Rosh agrees with Rambam, for the Rosh holds that only after he leaves beit din can he no longer retract, whereas while still in beit din he can retract in every case. The Rosh therefore reads "and he swore," with the conjunction, like Rambam. This is explicit from the Rosh in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. See there carefully. If Beit Yosef meant what those authorities attribute to him, he should have raised the difficulty from the Tur himself. Examine this carefully. As to practical law, the Mechaber's ruling in accordance with Rambam, based on the reading "and he swore," does not appear correct to me. The primary reading follows the other authorities, who read "he swore," without the conjunction, especially since they are numerous: Ramban, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Rivash, Mordechai in chapter Yesh Nochalin in the name of Rabbeinu Yoel, and Rav Hai Gaon. This is also the actual text in Mishpetei Shevuot of Rav Hai Gaon, gate 2, page 9a. See there. Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 6, part 3, rules likewise. It also appears to me that the primary practical ruling follows Ramban in chapter Yesh Nochalin, Rashba, Rivash, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef, that an acceptance made in beit din is binding. Thus, if he says, "I will pay," or transfers the oath to his opponent in beit din and accepts that arrangement, and similar cases, he cannot retract once they are no longer engaged in that same matter. Certainly, if he left beit din he can no longer retract, in accordance with the Rosh and all the authorities cited above, contrary to the dissenting view brought by the Rama. This also appears to me from the Tosefta at the beginning of chapter Zeh Borer, which states: Rabbi Yehudah likewise said that one who became obligated to swear to his fellow, and the latter said to him, "Vow to me by the life of your head," and he accepted this, cannot retract. The entire sugya at the beginning of chapter HaMafkid also supports this. There, the expression that the custodian "paid" does not mean that he literally paid. Rather, once he said, "I will pay," the owner transfers the double payment to him. If he could still retract, the Sages would not have enacted that the double payment becomes his. It is possible, with some difficulty, that Rambam also agrees, as the Sema writes, and as Maharam Lublin writes in siman 113. Certainly, the Bach's ruling that even after leaving beit din one may retract in those three types of cases does not appear correct. Even if Rambam were understood that way, he would be a lone opinion against all these great authorities, especially since their reasoning is primary, as I have explained. Maharam Lublin likewise rules in accordance with my position there. All of this applies, however, only when the statement was made in beit din. If it was made outside beit din, or before two people who were not sitting as beit din, it is ineffective even if he accepted it and later left, unless the other party actually swore in reliance on his statement, as I wrote above in seif katan 1. See there.
Rather, "I will pay," etc. Even according to this, the Tur writes that if one became obligated to take a rabbinic oath and remained silent, without saying that he wanted to transfer the oath to his opponent, and he left beit din, and afterward said that he wished to transfer it, he may do so. The Sema writes that even if he said in beit din, "I will not swear," and then left, he may still transfer the oath, because he can say that when he said, "I will not swear," he meant, "I will not swear, but I will transfer the oath," so long as he did not say, "I will not swear and I will pay." The Bach challenges this from the Gemara in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, which implies that when he says, "I will not swear," even without explicitly adding, "Rather, I will pay," it is treated as though he said so explicitly, and he cannot later claim that he meant to transfer the oath. His objection is incorrect, because the Gemara there concerns a Torah oath, for which transfer of the oath does not apply. Maharshal in his responsa, Maharam Lublin there, the Sema in seif katan 19, and the Bach infer from the Tur's language that only if he remained silent may he later transfer the oath, but if he said before beit din, "I will swear," and then left, he may not transfer it. Their inference is not compelling. It appears that when the Tur says "he remained silent," this refers to the fact that he did not say he wanted to transfer the oath. It is not comparable to a case where he said, "I will not swear, rather I will pay," and then left, where he cannot retract. In that case, the moment he said, "I will not swear," in a context in which he thereby accepted payment, he admitted the opposing litigant's claim, and a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. Here, however, when he says, "I will swear," it is as though he says, "I stand by my claim and will fulfill whatever the law requires." He has not accepted specifically that he will personally swear rather than transfer the oath. This is indeed the practice in many holy communities.
And he left beit din, etc.; and there are those who disagree, etc. The Sema writes that one may question the Rama: he should not have stated the rule about leaving beit din specifically regarding one who says, "I do not wish to swear, rather I will pay," and then leaves. He should have written it as a general rule that one who accepts any matter before beit din and then leaves has reached a final disposition, together with the opinion that disagrees. In my humble opinion there is no difficulty. The Rama apparently agrees with the Mechaber and Rambam in the other cases, where the text reads "and he swore," because an act of taking the oath remains to be done. But where he says, "I will pay," the Rama is uncertain, for one may say that since he accepted this and no further act remains, he becomes obligated to pay. Ramban, in his novellae to chapter Yesh Nochalin, explicitly makes this distinction. He writes that Rabbeinu Tam's words are not satisfactory, because once the person said, "I will pay," it is like a final judgment. Why should it differ from one who says, "My father is trusted upon me," and judgment was concluded and beit din told him to pay? Even according to one who reads here "and he swore," one can say that he may retract before swearing because an oath still remains to be performed and perhaps he will not swear. Raavan also appears to make this distinction. In chapter Yesh Nochalin, page 107a, he reads "and he swore" and writes that until the person actually swears he may retract. In chapter Zeh Borer, page 112a, he writes that the final judgment in a monetary obligation occurs when beit din tells him, "Go and pay him," whereas the final judgment of an oath occurs when he swears. He explains this still more clearly in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, page 115, third column. The same distinction is explicit in Piskei Maharam Recanati, siman 264, in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak Or Zarua. As to practical law, I already concluded above, in seif katan 16, that in every form of acceptance he can no longer retract. See there.
One who became obligated to his fellow, etc. Beit Yosef indeed rules this way, but in my humble opinion his reasoning is not compelling. He writes that since with a Torah oath we do not transfer the oath, the defendant certainly did not intend a transfer. That is not decisive. Although beit din does not transfer a Torah oath, the defendant may still have agreed to pay only if the claimant swore to him with the form of oath that was imposed. Beit Yosef also writes that holding a sacred object is merely intended to instill fear. Even so, the Gemara states that a judge who administered an oath without the required sacred object is considered to have erred in an explicit Mishnah and the matter must be redone, and this is our ruling below, siman 87, seif 13, without dispute. Such an oath is therefore not an oath at all for this purpose. Nor is there proof from Nimukei Yosef, whom Beit Yosef cites for the reason that the defendant is treated as though he said, "I believe that the law is with you if you swear, and once I have trusted you I cannot retract." Even if that is the reason, he trusted him only on condition that he swear in the manner imposed upon him. All the more so, Beit Yosef's ruling in the incident that came before him is not compelling. There, one admitted part of the claim and, outside beit din, said to his fellow, "If you swear, I will pay you." The claimant immediately swore by the God of Israel, without holding a sacred object. The defendant then said, "I meant only if you swear in beit din, not that you should swear outside beit din. It is therefore as though you have not sworn, and I retract." Beit Yosef ruled that although the undertaking was made outside beit din, the defendant could not retract once the claimant had sworn, relying on Rabbeinu Yerucham and Nimukei Yosef, and he added that this is so even though the claimant did not hold a sacred object. In my humble opinion, the law is with the defendant, both because the claimant did not swear while holding the required sacred object, as explained above, and because the defendant claims, "I meant only if you swear in beit din." The words of Rabbeinu Yerucham and Nimukei Yosef have no relevance to this case. They discuss a person who admits that he intended the other party to swear outside beit din, but wishes to retract merely because his acceptance was not made before beit din. They therefore rule that he cannot retract once the other party swore in reliance upon him. Here, however, the defendant says, "I meant only if you swear in beit din, and since you did not swear in beit din I retract." Moreover, it is possible that Rabbeinu Yerucham and Nimukei Yosef discuss a statement made outside beit din followed by an oath administered in beit din. In that case, although the statement was made outside beit din, it becomes effective once the other party swore in reliance on it with the legally required oath. That differs from the present case, where the claimant swore outside beit din. It therefore appears that the defendant prevails, especially since the claimant seeks to extract money and the burden of proof rests upon him. This is clear in my view. See Shiltei Giborim, chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, page 320a.
He cannot retract. He must then swear again while holding the required sacred object. So appears in Beit Yosef and Ir Shushan. Beit Yosef and Ir Shushan further write that if the defendant denies the matter and says, "I never trusted him," he must swear that he made no such statement and afterward take the Torah oath, just as at the end of seif 1 above, where a person denies having accepted a particular judge. In my humble opinion this is incorrect. Since the claimant will recover only if he swears, we do not administer an oath regarding the defendant's denial of the transfer arrangement, because this is not presently a denial of money. Who says that the claimant will in fact swear? This follows the Ran in the name of the Raah in chapter Kol HaNishba'in regarding a shopkeeper relying on his ledger, cited by Beit Yosef below in siman 91. See what I write there. This is unlike the case at the end of seif 1 above, where the judge's ruling immediately obligates him to pay money. This distinction is straightforward. Although I wrote immediately above that, in my humble opinion, Beit Yosef's underlying ruling in that incident is itself incorrect, this principle has many practical applications elsewhere. If one claims that the oath was validly transferred to him so that he may collect upon swearing, and the other party denies that transfer, the latter need not swear about the transfer itself. I subsequently found a clear proof for this in Baal HaIttur, Dinei Chov Al Peh, page 62b. He records a responsum concerning Reuven, who produced a document against Shimon recording a kinyan by which Shimon agreed to a transfer of the oath. The witnesses died, one witness's signature was authenticated, and Shimon said, "I never agreed." Shimon takes a heset oath that Reuven has no claim against him and is exempt. There is no oath based on the single witness, because that witness did not testify to money itself, but only to the transfer of the oath, and something that merely causes a monetary consequence is not treated as money. This principle appears in the Gemara, chapter Shevuat HaEdut, 32b. It is possible that Beit Yosef ruled otherwise only in his particular incident because the claimant had already sworn, so the concern of "who says he will swear" no longer applied, and because the oath was to be repeated while holding a sacred object. Even so, the transfer is still only something that causes monetary liability and not money itself. In an ordinary case, however, it is possible that Beit Yosef would agree with the rule I have stated.
