A Jew who became an apostate inherits, etc. This is learned from the verse, "For I have given Mount Seir to Esav as an inheritance."
If beit din sees fit to deprive him, etc. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 349, learns from here that the Sages did not invariably penalize an apostate and remove his inheritance. Rather, beit din does so where it appears certain that he will not return to Jewish observance. For this reason Terumat HaDeshen concludes that when the apostate is still a minor, his relatives on his father's side continue to inherit him, as Rama writes at the end of this gloss.
If he has sons. The same applies if he has a father: the inheritance is given to the father. Mordechai in Kiddushin cites Ravyah to this effect. This is what Rama means by "the heir of his apostate relative, etc."
Some say the inheritance is left with beit din, etc. This law and the preceding law that the property falls before his heirs are both cited by the Tur in the Rosh's name. Rama presents this as "some say" because Terumat HaDeshen, siman 349, challenges the apparent contradiction in the Rosh and explains that the first law applies where beit din sees no realistic prospect that the apostate will return. In the Derishah I wrote that one can distinguish differently: ordinarily the inheritance is indeed kept with beit din in case he returns, at which time it will be given to him. The first case was one where the apostate wanted to give it to his mother, and there the Rosh says it is preferable to give it to his daughter instead. See there.
Even if he deposited the money, etc. Meaning, we do not say that the bailee holding the money acquires it on the theory that the apostate's assets are ownerless, unless the bailee had already misappropriated it during the apostate's lifetime, as the ruling later explains.
His heirs as well, etc. An adult apostate is treated like a deceased person in relation to inheritance rights, so his son does not inherit his mother "from the grave" in order to transmit her property to his paternal relatives. A minor is different. The Sages did not penalize him by removing his inheritance rights. Therefore, even though the inheritance is not physically delivered into his hands while he remains an apostate, he is not treated as dead. He inherits his mother and thereby transfers the inheritance to his paternal relatives.
A husband inherits in place of his apostate wife. The novelty is that this applies even where she is an adult, despite the rule that an adult apostate's heirs do not inherit rights that would have come to her from her mother, as just explained. A husband is different. According to Abaye his hand is like hers, and according to Rava the husband's hand is stronger than the wife's, as stated in chapter HaCholetz. Therefore, as soon as the relative of the apostate wife dies, the inheritance falls to the husband more directly than to her, because according to Rava, whose view we follow, his hand is stronger than hers. Other heirs, by contrast, have no present right in a relative's property during that relative's lifetime. Terumat HaDeshen writes this at the end of siman 349. Rama concludes simply that "his hand is like hers," using abbreviated language, because even according to Abaye, who holds only that his hand is like hers, the husband is still stronger than ordinary heirs of an apostate, who have no rights in the inheritance during the apostate's lifetime.
