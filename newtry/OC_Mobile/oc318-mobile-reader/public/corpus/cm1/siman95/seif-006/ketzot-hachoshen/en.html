Rambam's view is that monetary compensation for damage to land remains treated like land so long as no separate judicial monetary liability has yet been fixed. Raavad disagrees where the claimant asks to be paid the diminution in value rather than to have the holes filled, comparing it to an ordinary damage claim. Raavad's position is connected to the broader question whether every free person is compared to land. Rashi tends to treat a person as land for some purposes, while Tosafot and Ran limit the Torah comparison to Canaanite slaves. Even if one treats a person like land, the enactment allowing an injured person to swear and collect can still operate, because Rabbi Yehudah does not require a strict Torah partial admission but only something patterned after it. The more difficult cases are claims for bodily depreciation, humiliation, and damage. Since a human being is often appraised as a slave, one might think the resulting claim is like the value of land. In my view the appraisal method does not convert the free person's body into land for oath purposes. Even if damages are measured by asking how much the person would be worth as a slave, the injured person himself remains movable property in this context unless the Torah expressly compares him to land. This explains Rambam's ruling regarding a slave who says his master knocked out his tooth or blinded his eye. Rambam exempts not because the subject is a slave and one never swears on slaves, but because release for tooth or eye is a fine, and one who admits a fine is exempt. At the moment of the claim the dispute concerns whether the slave has already acquired freedom, not simply ownership of a slave as property. This supports the distinction above.
