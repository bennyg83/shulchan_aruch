When he demanded that he fill the excavations. This is the view of those cited above who hold that if he demanded payment for grapes that had been harvested, the claim is likewise regarded as money, even though it derives from something attached to the ground. The Shach extensively argues for this position, that although the liability derives from land, it is nevertheless called money and movable property. His words, however, are not clear. He writes that when the Gemara speaks of one who dug pits and caves in the land, it does not mean, as the Raavad explained, that the claimant demanded that he refill the excavations. Rather, it means as Rashi explains, that the land which the defendant admits to having damaged has depreciated, and he admits that he must pay for that depreciation. Thus there is no heilech. Even if the depreciation is deemed movable property, what difference does that make? It is a case in which he admitted liability for utensils and land and denied liability for land, and he is nevertheless exempt. It is true that the Ran writes this in the name of the Rashba, but the Maggid Mishneh already challenged this, for if so it is identical with the Gemara's second answer, namely that he admitted liability for utensils and denied liability for land. I would further explain the Maggid Mishneh's point. The Gemara gives two answers. According to one possibility, the first answer regarded it as difficult to say that the denial concerns utensils, because the verse "for this is it" is written regarding silver and utensils, from which land is excluded, while "for this is it" refers to the admission. See the Asifat Zekenim, who cites this in the name of the commentators. Alternatively, the first answer is necessary according to the authority who holds that if one claimed wheat and barley and the defendant denied one of them, he is exempt. Tosafot state explicitly in the passage beginning "Alternatively" that the second answer cannot work according to that view, and therefore another answer is required, namely that he dug pits, trenches, and caves in the land. According to the Shach's explanation, however, even the answer involving excavation is a case where he admitted liability for utensils and denied liability for land, because that which he admitted with respect to the land is treated as heilech. If so, the difficulty remains. The verse "for this is it," which is stated with respect to the admission, still does not fit, and the answer also cannot work according to the authority who holds that where one claimed wheat and barley and the defendant denied one item, he is exempt. Rashi indeed explains the case of excavation to mean that the land which the defendant admitted has depreciated. It may be said, however, that Rashi follows the Rambam and those who agree with him, who hold that even an admission concerning damage to land retains the status of land. Thus, in the first answer, both the admission and the denial concern land. According to the Shach, however, who holds that the depreciation is movable property, this is difficult. The Rashba's position also requires explanation, for if he understands the matter as stated, this is simply the Gemara's second answer. Although the Maggid Mishneh writes that under the first answer it can be said that the claimant did not demand payment for the excavations at all, but only demanded his land, I do not understand what would generate an oath. The land itself is present, which is heilech, while the depreciation that he admits is monetary liability, which again is an admission concerning utensils and a denial concerning land, exactly the second answer. It might be possible, with difficulty, to reconcile the Rashba. Tosafot there, and likewise the Ran at the beginning of the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, as explained above in siman 75, write that according to the view that liens are of Torah law, a case of partial admission governed by Torah law can be found only where the debtor has no land or where the creditor waived the lien. If so, why does the Gemara ask why a verse is required to exclude land, since an admission involving land is heilech? The verse is needed for a case where he admits fifty that he borrowed, but because he owns land he would otherwise be exempt from the oath. One must say that when Torah law permits collection from encumbered property, that itself is called heilech, as Rashi writes above regarding an admitted debt evidenced by a document, that it is called heilech because it may be collected from encumbered property. Thus, on the Torah level, the same applies even to an ordinary partial admission. Accordingly, one could say that the Gemara's second answer, that he admitted utensils and denied land, is directed to the authority who holds that where one claimed wheat and barley and the defendant admitted one of them he is exempt. The first answer, involving pits and trenches where the admission creates a monetary obligation, cannot apply to that authority, because it would involve two kinds of claims. The Gemara therefore answers that he admitted utensils and denied land, yet the claim is still of one kind. For example, the claimant says that he lent him one hundred, and with respect to fifty he waived the lien and with respect to fifty he did not. The defendant admits the fifty for which the lien was waived and denies the rest. His admission is therefore treated as involving utensils, while his denial is treated as involving land, because the unwaived portion is deemed land. Nevertheless, the underlying claim is of one kind, namely money. A claim of one maneh as a loan and another maneh as a deposit is one kind of claim, and the same would be true here. This, however, is strained. Furthermore, according to this approach there would be no place for Tosafot's difficulty there. Tosafot ask that even apart from the rule of heilech, if the defendant denies liability concerning land, how can the rationale of temporary evasion apply? He would still display the brazenness of one who denies everything. Yet according to the suggested approach, why is that difficult? The case could involve the lien on land. Although the lien is called land, temporary evasion is certainly possible with respect to the debt itself. This shows that Tosafot regarded such an explanation as strained, because it would make the entire Gemara's difficulty depend upon the authority who holds that a lien is of Torah law. The Gemara should then have said that the difficulty is resolved according to the authority who holds that a lien is not of Torah law. The Rashba's explanation is therefore difficult. This is also indicated by Tosafot's discussion where the Gemara asks why one who admitted land and denied utensils is exempt, for utensils analogous to land would constitute heilech and yet would obligate an oath. Tosafot write that the Gemara could have answered that the claimant alleged that the defendant dug pits in the land. According to the Shach's explanation, this cannot be said in a case where he admitted land. If the excavation liability is treated as an admission concerning utensils, then he admitted both land and utensils and denied utensils, which obligates an oath. It must therefore be that Tosafot hold that an admission concerning excavation is not called an admission concerning utensils, either because they follow the Maggid Mishneh or because they follow the Raavad that the demand is to refill the excavations. In any event, they do not agree with the Shach. One might answer in the opposite direction and argue that precisely from here the Shach derived support. On his view, Tosafot's question, that the Gemara could have answered with a case of excavations, is resolved, for such a case would not work. The fact that Tosafot were forced to give their strained answer concerning the latter clause might then support the Shach. This cannot be maintained, however, because Tosafot's words are compelled by the Gemara in Shevuot 38a. There the Gemara challenges the authority who holds that if one claimed wheat and barley and the defendant admitted one of them he is exempt, from the Mishnah. The Gemara infers that the exemption applies specifically to utensils and land, but that two kinds of utensils analogous to land would obligate an oath. It answers that the latter clause was needed. The Gemara then asks that the rule that movable property becomes subject to an oath through land was already taught elsewhere. This is difficult. Why did the Gemara not say, even according to its own assumption that the defendant would be obligated in a case of two kinds, that there is still a problem because the admitted item is heilech? This is the same difficulty raised in Bava Metzia in the discussion of an exchange. We rule that heilech exempts, and the anonymous Gemara there asks this very question. It must therefore be that the Gemara relies on its answer that the formulation is needed incidentally for the latter clause and to teach the rule that movable property is drawn into an oath by land. If so, these strained answers are also required according to the authority who holds that where two kinds were claimed and one was admitted, the defendant is exempt. One must therefore say that all of that reasoning belongs only to the Gemara's initial assumption, before it considered the case of one who dug pits, trenches, and caves, where the resulting liability is regarded as full monetary liability according to the Shach. Once the Gemara gives the answer involving excavation, then according to the authority who holds that heilech exempts, there is no difficulty from the Mishnah at all. The admission concerning land is the monetary obligation for the excavation, and the strained answers are unnecessary. The Gemara can then properly challenge the authority who holds that where two kinds were claimed and one was admitted the defendant is liable, because even if what he admitted regarding the land is monetary liability, what he admitted and what he denied remain two kinds, land and utensils, or money and utensils. See above, siman 88, seif 13. The Gemara's question therefore stands, and it cannot be answered by saying "according to your reasoning," because the prior heilech difficulty has already been removed. Tosafot's words are thus compelling. At first I thought to bring proof for the Shach from what is cited in the name of Tosafot Yeshanim, also found in the Asifat Zekenim in the name of the gloss to Tosafot. They ask why one who admitted part of the claim concerning utensils should be liable, since he could instead have admitted an equivalent amount of land. They answer that perhaps he prefers the utensils. One must say that they hold that there is no migo from partial admission to total denial, as Tosafot write in the passage beginning "Always, in a case of heilech," that if heilech exempts it would amount to a migo from partial admission to total denial. With heilech, the amount is treated as though already paid, so the defendant is effectively denying everything. With land, however, the exemption is merely a decree of Scripture. He still admits part of the claim, and the principle that a person does not brazenly deny everything remains applicable. If so, why does the Gemara ask, under the answer that the Mishnah needed the case where he admitted some utensils, why the rule that movable property is drawn into an oath by land was necessary because it had already been taught? Perhaps the Mishnah teaches that we do not accept the migo suggested by Tosafot Yeshanim and the gloss, namely that he could have admitted part of the land instead of part of the utensils. The proposition that perhaps he prefers the utensils is only a logical possibility, and such a matter might well require explicit teaching. One must answer that Tosafot Yeshanim's question applies according to the authority who holds that heilech is liable. According to the authority who holds that heilech exempts, there is no question at all, because if he were to admit part of the land, that admission would be heilech, and everyone would then agree that this is a migo from partial admission to total denial, as Tosafot Yeshanim themselves explain. The Gemara's discussion is according to the authority who holds that heilech exempts. According to the Maggid Mishneh, however, who holds that anything deriving from land retains the status of land even where the present liability is monetary, one could still raise the Tosafot Yeshanim question. The defendant could admit part of the land in a case where it had been lost or damaged, so that he is obligated to pay its value, yet he would still be exempt from an oath. If so, perhaps the Mishnah needed to teach that this migo is not accepted. Indeed, this would be an even greater novelty, because even though the liability is monetary, since it derives from land, perhaps he prefers the utensils over the value of land, and people's preferences vary. This might at first appear to prove against the Maggid Mishneh. It seems, however, that there is no proof. There is an independent difficulty in explaining the case of one who admitted land as meaning that he admitted the value of land damaged by excavation. Tosafot in Shevuot 40a, in the passage beginning "He admitted part of the land and is exempt," write that this clause is needed lest we think that in the first clause, involving utensils and land, the exemption is only because the claim contains two kinds. If the admission concerning land means an admission of the monetary value of the land, why was the Tanna compelled to state "utensils"? There would be no difference between utensils and money. Had the Mishnah stated money instead of utensils, the entire claim would be one kind, an admission of money and a denial of money, and yet the Mishnah would still state that he is exempt. It would then be evident that the exemption is because the money derives from land, and the later clause dealing with an admission of land would be redundant. One must therefore say that Tosafot wrote as they did according to the accepted ruling that if two kinds were claimed and one was admitted, the defendant is liable. The Mishnah specifically says land and utensils rather than money in order to teach incidentally that if the claim were utensils and utensils analogous to land, he would be liable despite their being two kinds. This is what the Gemara infers according to that authority. Had the Mishnah said money, there would have been only one kind. Thus the word "utensils" is purposeful, and the later clause is still needed. Accordingly, the earlier proof falls away. When the Gemara asks why the rule of attachment is needed, it cannot be answered that the Mishnah rejects the migo, because under the present discussion we are dealing with the authority who holds that two kinds, one of which is admitted, is exempt. If we were to say that the admission concerning land means its monetary value, the later clause would be redundant, because the Mishnah could simply have stated money instead of utensils. One must therefore understand that the case is not an ordinary case of monetary damages. In any event, the matter is properly resolved. Having clarified that the Shach's position is exceedingly strained and is almost rejected by the sugya, we return to the Raavad's explanation that the claimant demands that the defendant refill the excavations. The Maggid Mishneh, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef all agree that the claimant cannot compel him to refill the holes and is entitled only to money. The Shach disputes them without proof. In a matter such as this, the ruling of those authorities should be followed. The Mishneh LaMelech suggested that if the soil removed from the land is still extant, the defendant must replace it, but this too is without explicit proof or explanation. Still, it seems that even if the claimant cannot compel the defendant to refill the holes, if the defendant himself wishes to restore the soil, the claimant cannot compel him to pay money instead. Thus, when the owner makes his claim, he is in substance demanding: either refill my pits with soil or pay the depreciation. There is therefore no definite monetary claim, but a claim concerning the land itself, because the defendant may elect to restore it and no money will then be due. This differs from bodily injury and similar cases, where the only claim is for money because the injury is a distortion that cannot be repaired. There is no need for the Shach's strained statement that although the defendant pays money, the money will ultimately be used to bring soil and refill the land. Who is a prophet to know that the owner will do so? Perhaps he will consume the money and leave the land diminished. Rather, the matter is simple as explained, and there is no difficulty in the Raavad. At first glance it might seem that Tosafot agree with the Raavad, for they write that the Gemara's answer that he admitted utensils and denied land does not work according to the authority who holds that if two kinds were claimed and one was admitted the defendant is exempt. Why not say that the claimant demanded money together with the monetary value of depreciation to land, the defendant admitted the money and denied the depreciation, and he is exempt because the denial concerns land? As to the classification of the claim itself, it would still be one kind, because he claimed money and money. If one claimed fifty as the value of wheat and fifty as the value of barley, that is certainly one kind of claim. See Nimukei Yosef at the end of HaManiach, where a claim for payment from one's assets because of damage done by an ox that was forewarned against the larger animal, followed by an admission concerning the smaller animal, is treated as one kind, even though the money derives from two different subjects. Thus the claim here would be one kind, and the exemption would arise only from the Scriptural exclusion of land. This might appear to prove the Raavad's view that if the claimant demands money, even though the liability derives from land, it is regarded as money. In truth, however, this is not a proof. Tosafot there, in the passage beginning "According to the authority," ask that even according to the authority who holds that heilech is liable, the rationale of temporary evasion does not apply to land. To whom is their question directed? If to the Gemara's original question, the Gemara may hold that "for this is it" refers to the admission, so that the defendant admitted land but denied utensils, and temporary evasion is possible regarding the utensils. If the question is on the Gemara's answer that he admitted utensils and denied land, one may still answer that the denied land was land in which he had dug pits and damaged it. If he admitted, he would be obligated to restore the pits, and temporary evasion could apply. It must therefore be that Tosafot hold that if the second answer also required a case of excavation, there would have been no reason for the Gemara to depart from its first, straightforward answer. Hence the second answer must avoid the excavation scenario entirely. Their statement that the second answer cannot work according to the authority who exempts when two kinds are claimed is therefore no proof about the status of monetary damages derived from land. See what I wrote in seif katan 5, where Tosafot in the sugya of exchange appear to hold that because the obligation derives from land, it retains the status of land. It is difficult to distinguish that case from ours. According to Tosafot in the passage beginning "Why," the Gemara's question is not why the Torah needed a verse to exclude documents, but why the Mishnah needed to derive the rule through a generalization and specification. If so, there is a similar difficulty regarding slaves. Why is a verse needed to exclude them? A slave standing before us is heilech, as the Gemara later asks on 100a. The second answer, that he admitted utensils and denied land, can be adapted to slaves as well, by saying that he admitted utensils and denied slaves. But according to the answer involving pits and trenches, what is the parallel case of a slave? One must say, as the Gemara later does, that he cut off the slave's hand. Yet that produces a monetary claim that cannot be repaired, as the Raavad himself recognizes. The second answer would therefore still be necessary for slaves, and if so, what was gained by the first answer? Moreover, according to the authority who holds that if two kinds were claimed and one was admitted the defendant is exempt, how can the first answer resolve the slave case according to the Raavad? It cannot. The second answer also cannot resolve it, because utensils and slaves are two kinds, and the defendant would be exempt regardless of the Scriptural exclusion of slaves. One must conclude that even in such a case the liability retains the legal status of the slave itself. This is, according to Tosafot, a clear proof. The Raavad may answer that he does not agree with Tosafot's premise and holds that the Gemara's question is directed to the verse itself, perhaps specifically according to Rabbi Akiva, as the Maharsha explains. Tosafot in the passage beginning "One does not swear" write that Rabbi Akiva has a separate verse excluding land. But according to Tosafot's own approach, the matter is as stated. They hold that at the stage of the Gemara's question, it did not consider the possibility of admitting utensils and denying land because "for this is it" is attached to "silver or utensils," and therefore indicates an admission concerning land, as cited by the Asifat Zekenim. This logic applies only to the authority who derives the exclusion of land from the generalization and specification of "silver or utensils." One who derives the exclusion from a different verse has no basis to connect "for this is it" specifically to those categories. The Maharsha's difficulty is thereby resolved. There is an additional difficulty according to the authority who holds that if two kinds were claimed and one was admitted the defendant is exempt. Suppose the claimant asserted that ten clusters of grapes standing ready to be harvested were owed to him, and the defendant admitted five. What happened to those five? If they are still extant, this is heilech. If the five admitted clusters were consumed, while the five denied clusters remain extant, then the claim contains two kinds, money and grapes, so the defendant is exempt. Why then does Rabbi Meir obligate an oath? It must be that the denied grapes were also consumed and the defendant claims that he consumed his own property. The claim is then money against money. This supports the view that even after the grapes were consumed the resulting obligation retains the status of land. It is difficult according to the Raavad. The Raavad, however, can say that the grapes which he admitted are still extant in the defendant's house, so the claim contains only one kind, yet it is not heilech because he holds that a deposited item is not heilech. According to our ruling that a deposited item is heilech, the admitted grapes cannot be extant. Their value must be claimed, which restores the difficulty according to the authority who exempts in a claim of two kinds. This is nearly an unanswerable proof. The Raavad further argues from Rabbi Yehudah's rule that there must be an admission of part of the claim, and from the case of one who says, "You injured me." The Maggid Mishneh responds that a free person is not legally treated as land. The Shach discusses at length the dispute among Tosafot, Rashi, and the other authorities whether a human being is treated as land. Before addressing that point, it should be explained that even on the Raavad's own premises his argument is not compelling. As explained above regarding a hired worker and a victim of robbery, Rabbi Yehudah's requirement of an admission need not involve a full Torah category of partial admission. An admission analogous to partial admission is enough, even where there is heilech. The Ran says this explicitly, because the Sages enacted these oaths in a form analogous to Torah law. Indeed, above in siman 90, Tumim seif katan 1, I established that according to Rabbi Yehudah no distinct enactment is needed for an injured claimant, because the defendant is suspect and the opposing party swears and collects. Accordingly, there is no Torah oath here at all, only an enactment, so the Raavad's proof actually points in the opposite direction. There is another reason. Depreciation of land, or depreciation of a slave through cutting off his hand, reduces the market value of the land or slave, and that diminution can plausibly retain the legal status of the underlying property. But the additional sums imposed for bodily injury beyond the victim's reduction in market value have no relation to the victim's body in the sense required to classify them as land. The Rambam rules in Hilchot Chovel, chapter 5, law 6, that if the tortfeasor admits the injury, he pays all five categories of damages when witnesses establish the event. Certainly there can then be a Torah oath regarding the other categories besides actual damage, because they are not part of the victim's body. Once an oath applies to one component, it extends to the rest through gilgul shevuah. The matter is therefore resolved without difficulty. Perhaps the Raavad follows his own position in his gloss there, that all five categories are monetary obligations payable upon one's own admission. Even so, that does not suffice, because although they are monetary obligations, they are not all related to the victim's body as compensation for diminution of the body itself. Only actual damage has that character, and this is clear. As to the underlying question whether a human being is treated as land, the Shach cites the dispute among Tosafot, Rashi, and the other authorities. It seems proper to distinguish between a Hebrew slave and an ordinary free person. A Hebrew slave has the status of land, but a free person does not. This is also the Mishneh LaMelech's understanding of the Maggid Mishneh. The Rambam rules in Hilchot Mechirah, chapter 13, that a worker who is comparable to a Hebrew slave is not subject to the laws of overreaching because he is treated as land. The Shulchan Aruch rules likewise below, siman 227, seif 33; see the Sema and Shach there. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 318, likewise indicates that specifically a Hebrew slave is treated as land because he is compared by Scripture to a Canaanite slave, not an ordinary free person. This can also be supported from Kiddushin, where the claimant says, "Swear to me that you are not my Hebrew slave." The Gemara asks that this is an ordinary monetary claim, and Rashi explains that it concerns full movable property. The Gemara answers that the body of a Hebrew slave is acquired. This is difficult. Even if his body were not acquired, the master would at least possess a lien on his labor. If a human being were inherently treated as land, that lien would be a lien on land and there would be no oath just as there is no oath concerning land itself. It is therefore clear that an ordinary person is not treated as land. Only a Hebrew slave, whose body is acquired and who is Scripturally compared to a Canaanite slave, receives the status of land. If his body were not acquired, he would not be so compared. This distinction is correct and removes all the Shach's proofs.
