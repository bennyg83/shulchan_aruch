Some say. This is the view of Raavad in his glosses to chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en. The Ran, Nimukei Yosef, and Maggid Mishneh understood Rambam to disagree and wrote that Raavad's distinction was unpersuasive. In my view, Raavad's ruling is primary, and Rambam does not disagree, as I shall explain. At the beginning of Bava Metzia 5a, the Gemara asks according to the opinion that an immediate tender exempts from an oath why Scripture needed to exclude land from the oath of partial admission, since every admission regarding land is an immediate tender. The Gemara answers that the verse is needed where the defendant dug pits, ditches, and caves in the land, so that the land he admits is damaged and is not an immediate tender. Alternatively, it is needed where the claim included utensils and land, the defendant admitted the utensils and denied the land. Rashi explains that excavation prevents the admission from being an immediate tender because the property was damaged. The Rif cites this passage both there and in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. The Ran infers that every claim arising from damage to land is itself treated as a claim to land and does not generate an oath. He attributes this view to Rambam. Raavad, however, writes that the case is treated as land only when the claimant demands that the defendant fill and level the excavations. If he demands monetary compensation for the reduction in value, the defendant must swear. The Ran objects that even where the claimant formally demands restoration, the defendant can discharge the obligation with money, so the claim is ultimately monetary. Nimukei Yosef and Ritva similarly understand the passage to mean that although the defendant owes only the monetary value of the damage, a claim directed at the body of the land retains the law of land. In my view the passage proves no such rule. Those authorities appear to have understood that the claimant alleged two excavations and the defendant denied one, so that the denial too concerned the excavations. The plain meaning is instead that the defendant dug some pits, ditches, or caves in the portion of land he admits, enough to reduce its value by at least a perutah. He therefore admits part of the land in damaged condition, and his admission is not an immediate tender. This also follows Rashi's wording. What he denies remains intact land, which he claims belongs to him, while in the portion he admits he dug some excavations. The same structure appears in Bava Metzia 100b. The Gemara asks that the admission is still an immediate tender, and Rava answers that the admitted slave had a severed hand or the admitted field had pits, ditches, and caves dug in it. The excavation is thus in the item admitted, while the item denied remains intact, just as the small slave admitted has the severed hand while the large slave denied remains intact. Raavad likely understands the Gemara in this manner. His gloss stating that the claimant demands restoration of the excavations is needed only to explain Rambam's formulation, which expressly discusses a claim that the defendant dug two excavations, or a case supported by one witness. It does not follow that Raavad reads the Gemara itself as a direct claim for restoration. The Ran also appears to have reconsidered his inference. After saying that he would return to the subject, he later cites Rashba's conclusion in the dispute concerning attached produce, and Rashba's position accords with Raavad. There the Ran plainly interprets the Gemara as I have done: the excavations were made in the portion admitted, while the denied portion remains intact. Accordingly, the passage does not prove that every monetary claim arising from land is treated as land. The substantive law also follows Raavad. I wrote above in seif 3, seif katan 15, that a claim for the unpaid price of land generates a Torah oath. The same should apply to monetary compensation for damage to land. Once the land was damaged, the obligation is monetary, like a debt arising from the sale of land. There is no persuasive basis to distinguish them. The wording of the Gemara further supports this. It says that the defendant “dug in it” pits, ditches, and caves, meaning in the very land claimed and admitted. Had the Gemara meant that money owed because of damage to land retains the law of land, it could simply have answered that the verse is needed where the claimant demands the monetary value of land. Instead it specifies excavation in the admitted land, because a pure claim for money is movable property and cannot explain the verse. The claimant is therefore demanding the entire tract, the defendant admits part, and in the admitted part he caused some damage. The Maggid Mishneh quotes Raavad: If the claimant demands that the defendant fill and level the excavations, the claim concerns land. If he demands payment for the reduction in value, it is an ordinary monetary claim, comparable to one who says, “You wounded me twice,” and the defendant says, “I wounded you only once.” The Maggid Mishneh objects that the claimant cannot compel actual restoration, since the tortfeasor may pay the loss. Therefore both claims are ultimately claims to money arising from land. He also understands Raavad's comparison to bodily injury to rest upon the premise that although a person is compared to land, a claim for bodily-injury damages is movable property. The Maggid Mishneh rejects that premise, asserting that only slaves, regarding whom Scripture expressly says, “You shall bequeath them to your children after you,” are compared to land, but other people are not. I do not know the basis for the first objection. The rule below in siman 386 that damage is appraised for the tortfeasor concerns damage that cannot be repaired. When repair is possible, it is reasonable that the tortfeasor must restore the property. Even if only money can be compelled, Raavad's distinction remains sound. Where the excavations can be filled, the money paid will be used to restore the land, so the claim is essentially for restoration of land. Where restoration is impossible, such as where a river washed the land away, or where a slave was burned, killed, cast into the sea, or lost a hand, the money cannot restore the damaged property. The claim is then an ordinary monetary demand. Raavad's comparison to “You wounded me twice” refers to the Mishna at the beginning of the chapter Kol HaNishba'in. Rabbi Yehudah requires a partial admission, and gives the example where one says, “You wounded me twice,” and the defendant says, “I wounded you only once.” The Mishna plainly imposes a Torah oath. The Maggid Mishneh's claim that only slaves are compared to land is also unpersuasive. Sanhedrin 15a and Megillah 23b show that even a free Jewish person is treated as land for valuation purposes. The Mishna states that land is appraised by nine judges and a kohen, and that a person is treated similarly. Rabbi Abbahu explains that this means one who vows his own monetary value, who is appraised like a slave sold in the market, and a slave is compared to land by the verse concerning inheritance. Sanhedrin there also asks whether hair ready to be cut is regarded as already cut and appraised by three judges, or as still attached and appraised by ten. The entire discussion assumes that the body of a free person is compared to land. Kiddushin 7a similarly assumes that a woman would be property bearing responsibility, and Rashi explains that a person is compared to land. Tosafot object that the verse addresses a slave and not a free person, and suggest that the comparison applies only when one vows his own value because he is then appraised like a slave. In my view, the plain meaning of Sanhedrin and Kiddushin follows Rashi. Once Scripture teaches that a slave's body has the law of land, there is no basis to distinguish the body of a slave from the body of any other person. The verse speaks of a slave because it is addressing inheritance, but it reveals the legal character of the human body generally. Kiddushin 28a proves this further. The Gemara asks how far an oath may be extended through the principle of a derivative oath and gives the example, “Swear to me that you were not sold to me as a Hebrew servant.” It initially calls this a substantial monetary claim, then answers that Rava holds a Hebrew servant's body is acquired. The Gemara asks that if so the claim is one concerning land, already covered by the Mishna. The passage expressly treats even a Hebrew servant as land. The Rif and Rosh cite it, and Rambam and the Tur rule above at the beginning of siman 94 that a derivative oath may extend even to a claim that the defendant was sold as a Hebrew servant, because the servant's body is acquired. The Ritva and Ran give strained alternative readings because they found no explicit verse comparing a Hebrew servant to land. Careful analysis shows their explanations to be unsatisfactory. Scripture's comparison regarding a slave reveals that the human body is land. Accordingly, the Mishna's example of bodily injury proves Raavad's rule that when the claimant seeks compensation for an irreversible reduction in value, a Torah oath applies. In my view Rambam does not disagree. Raavad's phrase, “It appears that the case is where he demands restoration of the excavations,” indicates that he interprets Rambam in this way. The Tur likewise cites Rambam and then states Raavad's qualification as an explanation. Rashba also agrees with Raavad, Rabbenu Yerucham cites Rashba as his conclusion, and the Ran appears to have returned to that view. Since Raavad, Rashba, Ran, the Tur, and Rabbenu Yerucham hold that a demand for monetary diminution creates a Torah oath, at least where physical restoration is impossible, and Rambam need not disagree, and there are sound proofs for the ruling, this is the accepted law.
