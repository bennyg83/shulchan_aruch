And the other says, “This never occurred.” The Mechaber's words here are indeed difficult. The Sema discusses them at length and initially suggests a textual error. He ultimately explains that when the defendant says, “This never occurred,” we interpret him to mean that he never accepted responsibility as a custodian. This is incorrect. The Sema himself concludes that if so, it requires study why the Mechaber stated the rule without explanation, especially since he thereby departed from Rambam and neither Beit Yosef nor Kesef Mishneh mentioned such an interpretation. Responsa Masat Binyamin, at the end of the work, likewise writes that the Mechaber only glanced at the issue through a narrow opening, or else the text contains an error. In my view there is no textual error. The Mechaber wrote this deliberately, on the basis of Kesef Mishneh's explanation of Rambam. Kesef Mishneh asks why, when the defendant says, “This never occurred,” he must take a rabbinic oath of denial. Since the claimant did not entrust him with proof of the document's contents, even if the defendant destroyed the document with his own hands he would not owe money. Kesef Mishneh answers that here the claimant is not demanding money but the document that constitutes his proof and has monetary value. The defendant must therefore swear, or the claimant may swear if the oath is reversed. Kesef Mishneh then asks what the oath accomplishes, for perhaps the document was lost without negligence, in which case a custodian of documents is exempt. He answers either that we do not assume it was lost at all, but rather that it remains in the defendant's possession and he is withholding his fellow's document, or that by denying the entire event the defendant is deemed maximally negligent. Kesef Mishneh's final answer makes liability depend upon negligence. Accordingly, it applies only according to Rambam, who holds a custodian liable for negligent loss of documents. The Mechaber, however, ruled above in seif 1 that negligence regarding documents does not create liability. Thus, even if a total denial is considered negligence, the defendant remains exempt. This appears to be the Mechaber's intent. Cited cross-references in the source: 100.
The primary law, however, does not follow this final explanation. Rather, Kesef Mishneh's first answer is correct. Since the defendant says, “This never occurred,” the claimant's account necessarily portrays him as presently withholding the claimant's document and directly causing him loss. He must therefore take a rabbinic oath of denial, as Rambam rules. This also appears to be the view of the Tur and of the Mechaber himself above in siman 66, seif 39, and below in siman 301, seif 3. The Bach likewise writes that a total denial is equivalent to burning the document. He nevertheless explains that the defendant is not believed through a migo that he could have claimed that the document was lost, because Rambam holds that a migo does not exempt from an oath. Lechem Mishneh, in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en, similarly asks why he is not believed through the migo that he could have claimed loss, and answers that Rambam does not recognize a migo to avoid an oath. These explanations are unnecessarily strained, as will be explained below. The Bach further writes that as a matter of law we follow the Rosh, who recognizes a migo to avoid an oath. Certainly here, where a total denial is brazen, the defendant should be believed through the migo that he could have claimed loss, which would exempt him even from a rabbinic oath. The Mechaber, he says, therefore erred in seif 4. The Mechaber's ruling at the end of siman 66 that one swears a rabbinic oath regarding documents applies only to a partial admission or the testimony of one witness. Yet at the beginning of this siman and in siman 301, seif 3, the Mechaber implies that such an oath applies even to a total denial, and the Bach leaves the matter unresolved. The Bach's analysis is incorrect. The Mechaber plainly did not rely on a migo, for he rules above in siman 93, seif 2, and below at the beginning of siman 296, that a migo does not exempt from an oath. Nor is it persuasive to distinguish the end of siman 66 as concerning only partial admission or one witness, since a migo would still be present. The Bach's substantive ruling is also uncertain. Even according to the opinion that a migo can exempt from a stringent oath, the Sages did not create distinctions within the enactment of a rabbinic oath of denial. The Ritva states this expressly in the responsum cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 66. One who deposits documents with another and is told that they were lost is believed without an oath, even if the loss occurred through negligence, and according to Rambam he is exempt whenever he was not negligent. If the custodian claims that he returned the documents, one might say that he is believed through a migo that he could have claimed an unavoidable accident, a claim that requires no oath concerning documents. It was possible to argue that he should not even take a rabbinic oath of denial. Nevertheless, there is no migo against such an oath. A rabbinic oath of denial is itself an enactment imposed where the defendant is exempt by Torah law. The Sages required it even where the defendant has a migo, since the migo gives no greater force than the claim of return itself. Ritva adds that this was the view of his teachers, although some authorities exempt even from this oath in cases involving documents. Ritva appears to agree that the oath is required, and this is the primary ruling. Cited cross-references in the source: 66, 76, 93, 301.
Rambam writes in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en, law 6: “I delivered a document to you, and it contained proof that ten dinars were owed to me.” If the defendant says, “This never occurred,” he takes a rabbinic oath of denial. If the oath is reversed, the claimant swears that the document contained proof for ten dinars, that the proof was lost through the loss of the document, and he collects. If the defendant says, “It is true that you delivered it to me, but it was lost,” he is exempt even from a rabbinic oath of denial, because even if he was negligent and it was lost, he is exempt, as Rambam explained in Hilchot Chovel. Rabbenu Yerucham, at the end of netiv 14, cites Rambam's ruling at the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut that one who is negligent with slaves, documents, or land is liable. He then notes that Rambam writes in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en that the custodian is exempt, quoting this law through the words “as we explained in Hilchot Chovel.” In chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel U'Mazik, Rambam rules that one who burns another person's documents is liable if the owner trusted him regarding their contents, but if the owner did not trust him he pays only the value of the paper, as stated below in siman 386, seif 2. Rabbenu Yerucham concludes that he could not understand Rambam unless the texts were corrupt, although authorities report in Rambam's name that negligence creates liability. The Maggid Mishneh resolves the issue. He notes that some manuscripts read that if the defendant admits receiving the document and says it was lost, he is exempt from a rabbinic oath of denial because even negligence would not create liability, as explained in Hilchot Chovel. The Maggid Mishneh explains that this concerns a case where the claimant does not trust the custodian's assertion that the loss of the document caused the loss of his proof. If the claimant does trust him regarding the document's contents and the loss occurred through negligence, Rambam holds him liable, consistently with chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut. The essential reason for exemption from the oath in the present law is that the claimant cannot assert with certainty that the document remains in the custodian's possession. A rabbinic oath of denial is not imposed on an uncertain claim. Raavad objects that perhaps the document is still in the custodian's possession. The Maggid Mishneh's explanation of Rambam is correct and is also the primary law. The Bach, in seif katan 9, distinguishes between two forms of negligence. He claims that chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut concerns negligence that causes complete destruction, while the present law concerns negligence followed by ordinary loss. He interprets “as we explained in Hilchot Chovel” to mean that Rambam there imposed liability upon one who burns a document, implying liability only where negligence results in complete destruction, but exemption where negligence merely leads to loss. He then rejects both the Tur's and the Maggid Mishneh's understanding. May his Master forgive him. The Tur did not even state an interpretation of Rambam on this point. More importantly, the Bach's distinction between one form of negligence and another is entirely unsupported by Rambam. Chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut plainly states that every negligence that creates liability regarding movable property creates liability regarding documents as well. Nor can Rambam's reference to Hilchot Chovel reasonably rest upon an inference from his use of the specific case of burning. If such an inference were valid, one could equally infer that even negligence resulting in total loss is exempt, since Rambam mentioned only active burning, which is direct damage. That would contradict chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut. In truth, Rambam discussed burning there because that chapter concerns indirect causation. The Gemara says that even one who burns a document is exempt according to the opinion that does not impose liability for indirect damage, whereas the accepted law imposes liability upon the burner. The same applies to a custodian whose negligence is equivalent to causing damage, as Rambam rules. Kesef Mishneh asks why the defendant is liable in the first clause. Even if he admitted the facts, he would not be liable to pay money, because the claimant does not trust him concerning the document's contents. Kesef Mishneh answers that the document constituting the claimant's proof has monetary value to him. This does not fully resolve the issue, since the claimant does not trust the defendant regarding any fact. The original difficulty, however, does not arise. If the defendant admitted that the document remained in his possession and contained the proof, he would have to return it. If he refused, he would owe money for the resulting loss. When he says, “This never occurred,” the claimant's certain account portrays him as presently causing damage by withholding the document. His denial itself shows that he intends to retain the document and knows that it contains proof, for otherwise there would be no reason to deny receiving it. Thus, according to the claimant's certain assertion, the defendant knowingly possesses the proof and is directly causing loss. This also answers Kesef Mishneh's further question, as well as the similar question of Lechem Mishneh, as I explained above at the beginning of the preceding seif katan. Kesef Mishneh also questions the Maggid Mishneh's statement that the defendant is exempt even from a rabbinic oath because the claimant cannot make a certain claim. The claimant certainly asserts that he delivered the document. In my view, the Maggid Mishneh's meaning is clear. The defendant is exempt from an oath that perhaps the document remains in his possession. Although a custodian is exempt if the document was actually lost, he must return it if it remains in his possession. This is why a total denial requires an oath, for according to the claimant's certain assertion the document is still held by the defendant and the defendant is causing damage. When the defendant admits receipt but says it was lost, however, the claimant cannot assert with certainty that it was not lost and still remains in his possession. No rabbinic oath is imposed on an uncertain claim. This is precisely why Raavad objected that perhaps it remains in the defendant's possession. Gidulei Terumah, gate 29, part 2, folio 134b, also questions Kesef Mishneh and explains the Maggid Mishneh to mean that the defendant would be asked to swear that he did not know the document contained proof, but the claimant cannot say with certainty that the defendant knew this. Although the legal conclusion is correct that the case does not involve a certain claim of the defendant's knowledge, that is not the Maggid Mishneh's meaning. His meaning is as I have explained. Lechem Mishneh likewise explains that if the claimant said, “I saw the document in your possession and you did not lose it,” that would be a certain claim requiring a rabbinic oath, but here the claimant cannot say so with certainty. This is correct. Lechem Mishneh nevertheless asks why the Maggid Mishneh needed this reason when Rambam expressly gave another reason, namely, that even negligence followed by loss is exempt. He answers that Rambam's reason applies when the claimant does not trust the defendant, while the Maggid Mishneh discusses a case where he does. This is incorrect. The Maggid Mishneh also addresses the case where the claimant does not trust him. Even then, if the document were still in the defendant's possession he would have to return it and would have to swear if its continued possession were asserted with certainty. The exemption rests upon the absence of such a certain claim. As a matter of practical law, however, I ruled above in siman 66, seif katan 124, that even regarding documents a custodian must take a rabbinic oath that the item is not in his possession.
