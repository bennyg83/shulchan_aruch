I delivered a document to you, etc., and even if he was negligent with it and it was lost, he is exempt, as explained, etc. The Mechaber’s words are exceedingly difficult. At first sight he appears to be copying the Rambam in chapter 2 of Hilchot To'en, as cited by the Tur, for he begins and ends this ruling in the Rambam’s language. Yet in the middle he departs from the Rambam by equating the defendant’s answer, “It never happened,” with the answer, “It was lost from my possession,” and ruling that in both cases he is exempt even from an oath of hesset. The Rambam distinguishes between them. If the defendant says, “It never happened,” the Rambam requires an oath of hesset; if he admits receiving it but says it was lost, the Rambam exempts him from hesset. At the very least the Mechaber should have recorded the Rambam’s opinion, especially since both the Rambam and Tur state it without dissent, and then cited any dissenting view. Nor is it easy simply to emend the Mechaber’s language to match the Rambam, because the Mechaber wrote “or he claims that it was lost,” and the word “or” plainly equates the two responses. Correcting the text would require deleting and adding substantial language, including the Rambam’s further ruling that if he says, “It never happened,” he takes an oath of hesset, and if he reverses the oath to the claimant, the claimant swears that the document contained proof of ten dinarim and that he lost that sum through the loss of the document, and then collects. It is also surprising that Rama, who generally scrutinizes the Mechaber’s language and supplies necessary qualifications, says nothing here, either in his glosses or in Darkei Moshe. There is a further difficulty. The Rambam concludes, “as has been explained in the laws of one who causes injury,” and the Maggid Mishneh explains that he refers to the Rambam’s ruling that one who burns another’s documents must pay, provided the tortfeasor admits that the document was validated and that because he burned it the owner can no longer collect the debt; if he does not admit this, he pays only the value of the paper. Thus, when the Rambam says here that even if he was negligent and it was lost he is exempt, he means where the defendant does not admit that the document had been validated and collectible. But the Mechaber says “as has been explained,” even though he had not yet explained this anywhere; the law concerning one who burns another’s document appears only later in siman 386, so he should have written “as will be explained.” The Ir Shushan simply copied the Mechaber’s words without explanation. In my view, once we address the latter difficulty, the earlier ones are also resolved. The Rambam’s ruling that one who answers “It never happened” must take an oath of hesset is consistent with his own position that, although Scripture exempts documents and the other excluded categories from a Torah oath, if the custodian negligently loses or damages them he must nevertheless pay when he admits the negligence. Therefore, where the claimant certainly asserts that he delivered the document and the defendant denies receipt altogether, the denial concerns a potential monetary liability, and an oath of hesset applies. The Mechaber, however, follows the Rosh and those who agree with him, who hold that even when such property is lost through negligence the custodian is exempt from payment, as the Tur records at the beginning of this siman and as the Mechaber rules there. For that reason, the Mechaber here exempts the defendant even when he answers, “It never happened.” This is why he concludes, “for even if he was negligent with it and it was lost he is exempt, as has been explained,” meaning as explained at the beginning of this siman, where the Mechaber explicitly writes that even if the custodian was negligent and the property was lost, he is exempt from payment. One might object that at the beginning of the siman the Mechaber nevertheless requires an oath of hesset on a definite claim, and here the claimant definitely says that he delivered the document, so why should the denial not require hesset? To understand this, consider the basis of Rav Nachman’s institution of hesset for one who denies the entire claim. Although there is a presumption that a person does not brazenly deny everything in the claimant’s presence, there is also a presumption that a person does not bring a claim against another unless he has a genuine basis. The Sages reconciled these presumptions by suspecting that the claimant is truthful and that the defendant is not intending permanent denial, but merely to delay payment until he has funds. They therefore imposed an oath so that he will admit and pay now, or as beit din sees fit. Accordingly, when a person says concerning a deposited document, “It never happened,” we interpret his response, where possible, as meaning, “I never accepted responsibility to guard it,” because this minimizes his brazenness. At the same time, we suspect that he did accept responsibility but that the document was lost through negligence, and he denies the custodianship only to delay the monetary liability that he believes follows from negligence. That rationale supports the Rambam’s requirement of hesset, because according to the Rambam negligence in guarding documents creates liability. According to the authorities who exempt a custodian even for definite negligence concerning documents, however, there is no monetary liability to which the hesset could attach. Even if we suspect that the document was negligently lost and that the defendant wrongly thinks he would have to pay, according to the actual law he owes nothing for that negligence. Nor should we suspect, merely to create an oath obligation, that he intentionally burned or tore the document, because we do not presume people to be wicked destroyers of another’s property. We likewise do not ordinarily suspect that he damaged it directly by accident, because the rule that a person is always responsible for direct damage itself assumes that a rational person must guard himself from causing such damage. Thus, absent definite proof of direct damage, we do not create such a suspicion. It also follows why the Tur could cite the Rambam’s language requiring hesset even though the Tur holds that one is exempt for negligent loss of documents. The Tur relied on what he had already ruled at the beginning of the siman and cited the Rambam mainly in order to bring the Raavad’s objection that the defendant must swear that the document is not in his possession, an objection the Tur rejects. With this, the Mechaber’s language is coherent. The Maggid Mishneh’s explanation of the Rambam’s concluding reference to the laws of damages likewise concerns the Rambam’s own position, whereas according to the Mechaber there is no difficulty.
