(Seif 9, and Raavad objected to him, etc.) “Although they exempted him from the oath of negligence, they did not exempt him from the oath that the item is no longer in his possession.” According to this, I am puzzled by the Gemara’s question in HaZahav from the case of townspeople who sent their shekels, etc. Perhaps that case concerns only the oath that the item is no longer in the bailee’s possession. My father-in-law, the great Rabbi Yoel, answered that Raavad follows Rav Hai, whom the Tur cites in seif 14, that no oath enactment at all applies to consecrated property. This answer is insufficient. Rav Hai wrote only according to the Gemara’s conclusion that no oath is imposed concerning consecrated property, and he holds that even hesset is unnecessary. But when the questioner in the sugya challenges one case of consecrated property from another by citing a case where an oath is imposed concerning consecrated property, the question remains: perhaps that oath was only that the item was no longer in his possession. [The author’s gloss:] It appears to me that in that case the Gemara plainly understood that the oath could not be the oath that the item was no longer in his possession, but only the oath that the bailee was not negligent. Rashi therefore explains at the beginning of 58a, “They swear to the treasurers that they were not negligent.” The Mishnah says, “If they were stolen or lost,” rather than, “If the bailee says they were stolen or lost.” This implies that even where witnesses establish with certainty that the items were stolen or lost, the bailees nevertheless swear. It therefore cannot be an oath that the items are not in their possession. This is why Rashi was exact in saying “that they were not negligent,” rather than using the general phrase “the oath of bailees,” which might include the oath that the item was stolen. Examine this carefully.
