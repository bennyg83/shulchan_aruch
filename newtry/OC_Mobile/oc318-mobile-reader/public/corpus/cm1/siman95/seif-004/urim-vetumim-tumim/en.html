And this one says, "Nothing ever happened". Rambam says concerning this clause that the defendant takes a hesset oath, and the Tur copies the ruling. The Sema and Shach discuss it at length, and the root of the Shach's position is obscured by the length of the discussion. The matter can be stated simply. If the amount of evidentiary value contained in the lost document is known, it is obvious that the defendant must take hesset. But even if that amount is not independently known, one who says, "Nothing ever happened," necessarily asserts that the document was never lost from him because he never received it. This is analogous to the principle that one who says, "I never borrowed," is treated as saying, "I did not repay." If he refuses to swear and instead admits the claimant's allegation that the document was delivered to him, he has thereby admitted that the document did not merely disappear under circumstances unknown to him. He must then pay the amount claimed unless he can produce the document and show what it actually established. Consider an obvious case: one person takes another's document and hides it. He cannot say, "I do not believe your assertion about how much the document proved." He must either produce the instrument so the creditor can establish its contents or answer for the loss according to the claimant's proven demand. The same is true here. The claimant says definitely that the document is in the defendant's hands. If the defendant admits receipt after first denying any transaction, he has no remaining neutral explanation for its absence. This is essentially what the Shach means, despite his lengthy formulation. The Beit Yosef, in Kesef Mishneh, gives a different reason for the hesset oath: the defendant would be liable if he was negligent. His words are difficult. What does negligence have to do with a plea of "nothing ever happened"? The Sema strains to explain that "nothing ever happened" really means that the document was lost, but that is unnatural. Perhaps the Kesef Mishneh reasons as follows. If this person was never subject to any custodial liability, and would be exempt even for negligence, then the matter of whether he received and lost the paper is something he had no legal reason to keep carefully in mind. It can be considered a fact not imposed upon him to remember. In that respect his denial differs from an ordinary debtor who says, "I never borrowed," because a borrower must know whether he borrowed and repaid. Here, a person with no custodial obligation might casually forget whether a worthless or legally irrelevant paper came into his hands and was later lost. His statement "nothing ever happened" would not necessarily amount to an admission that no loss occurred. But if he is liable for negligence, he did accept a legally significant custodial responsibility. Whether he received the document and what happened to it is then a matter he was obligated to remember. A plea of "nothing ever happened" is therefore equivalent to saying it was not lost. If we instead excuse his failure to remember on the ground that the matter was not important enough to attend to, that very indifference is itself the clearest negligence. The Kesef Mishneh can therefore tie Rambam's hesset ruling to Rambam's view that negligence creates liability. The later authorities reject this limitation, and as practical law Rambam and the Tur should be followed: hesset is required. The Tur itself holds that in the relevant custodial setting negligence may not create full liability, yet still rules that hesset is imposed. This shows that he does not depend on the Kesef Mishneh's rationale. One might ask that according to the Tur the defendant has a migo: instead of saying "nothing ever happened," he could have admitted receipt and claimed an unavoidable accident, which would exempt him, and the Rosh generally recognizes a migo to avoid an oath. The Shach cites the Ritva that regarding hesset all agree that a migo does not exempt from the oath. There is a proof from the later version of Rav Nachman's enactment, which imposes hesset where the defendant says, "I borrowed and repaid," even though he could have said, "Nothing ever happened." The Gemara nevertheless requires hesset, showing that the migo does not remove it. It is strained to assume the two versions of the enactment dispute this entire principle. Alternatively, the Tur's case may concern a document whose paper itself is worth a perutah. For the paper, negligence certainly creates liability or at least an oath regarding its loss, so the defendant cannot freely switch to a claim of accident without consequences. The Perishah also suggests this. But the Tur's unqualified wording supports the Shach's broader rule: even without relying on the value of the paper, a migo does not remove hesset in this case.
