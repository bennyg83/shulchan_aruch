I delivered a promissory note to you. See Sema and Shach, who question this ruling because Rambam requires an oath where the custodian denies that the note was ever entrusted to him, and they offer strained resolutions. In my opinion the matter can be explained simply. If the custodian produces the note in beit din and says, “You never entrusted this note to me. Rather, the debtor deposited it with me, or I found it,” or says, “I do not remember whether you deposited it with me,” he is certainly exempt from an oath to the alleged depositor. This is evident because if a definite claim by the depositor required an oath, then where the custodian says, “I do not know whether you deposited the note with me,” he should also have to swear that he does not know, just as one who says, “I do not know whether I became obligated to you,” must swear to his uncertainty. Yet siman 65, based on an explicit Mishnah, rules that where a note is found with a third party and he does not know its status, meaning whether it was deposited by the creditor or debtor, it remains in safekeeping, and the third party need not swear. The reason is that the note is written in the alleged depositor's name, and the custodian cannot collect it from the debtor without writing and delivery. There is therefore no concern that he is withholding it for himself. His statement functions only as testimony between creditor and debtor, and there is a chazakah that a person does not sin without personal benefit. Accordingly, he need not swear, like any third party. Therefore, if he does not produce the note in beit din and says, “You did not deposit it with me,” he likewise need not swear. Why should there be greater suspicion merely because he does not show the note than if he shows it and gives the same account? Moreover, an oath is imposed only where an admission would create monetary liability. Here, once he has already told beit din that the alleged depositor did not give him the note, why should he swear? Even if, at the time of the oath, he admitted that the depositor had given him the note and then returned the note in beit din, the creditor could no longer collect from the debtor with it, because the note was found in another person's possession. A custodian is ordinarily believed to say that the note came to him from the creditor, as explained at the end of siman 41, only because he has a migo that he could have returned it to the creditor, as the Sema writes there. Here, after he has already stated in beit din that it did not come to him from the creditor, if he later returns it and says that it did come from the creditor, he no longer has that migo, because it would be a retroactive migo. The Mechaber therefore properly rules that no oath is required. Rambam follows his own position in siman 66 seif 11 that the holder is believed with respect to his acquisition of the note and can collect from the debtor where the debtor does not say, “You are not my litigant.” Thus, when the custodian denies ever receiving the note, we suspect that he wants to keep it and collect it himself, because from the debtor's standpoint it makes no difference whether he pays him or the original creditor. Since, according to Rambam, the holder is believed regarding acquisition, the debtor would indeed pay him, and an oath is therefore required. According to our ruling, however, the holder is not believed regarding acquisition, and the debtor may not pay him. There is therefore no concern that he is retaining it for himself, since the note has no independent monetary value to him. This differs from a custodian who falsely denies receiving an ordinary object that has monetary value to him. When he denies receiving a note, we treat him only as one giving testimony between creditor and debtor, and no oath is required. One might still argue that the creditor can say: “It is true that I deposited it with you. By saying that I did not, you have impaired my note through your statement.” Perhaps this is like the law in siman 66 concerning one who sells a promissory note and then says it was paid, where he must pay the note's full value because he is treated as a damager. But that comparison is incorrect. There, absent the seller's statement, the note is presumed valid because he sold it as valid. When he then says that it was paid, or refuses to swear where an oath is necessary, we know that his act has impaired an otherwise valid note. In addition, his liability arises from the seller's warranty, as Ritva explains in Ketubot, because the sale was certainly made with the understanding that the seller would take the necessary oath. By contrast, where a note is in another person's possession, the creditor cannot collect until the custodian states and testifies that it came to him from the creditor. If the custodian refuses to testify that it came from the creditor, he is merely withholding testimony. This is no worse than negligence concerning documents, for which there is no liability. Even according to Rambam, who imposes liability for negligence regarding documents, here the status of the note is not certain. Perhaps the custodian truly forgot and does not know whether the creditor deposited it. He is certainly exempt for such negligence, as shown by the explicit Mishnah that where a note is found and the third party does not know its status, it remains in safekeeping and the Mishnah does not say that the custodian must pay. A further reason is that the custodian is believed to say that the note is not in his possession by a migo: he could have produced the note but remained silent and declined to testify that the creditor deposited it with him, in which case the law would be that the note remains with him, as in the Mishnah. This resembles the Tumim's explanation in siman 82 seif 2 concerning a migo of forgery, namely that the alternative claim could have been, “I do not know.” In that event he would likewise not have to return the note to the creditor and would be exempt from payment for negligence of this kind. Therefore, when he says that the note is not in his possession at all, he is likewise believed. This seems so clear to me that even the Sema and Shach, in my opinion, require an oath only regarding a bearer note payable to whoever produces it, where there is concern that the custodian is retaining it for himself in order to collect. But where the note names a particular creditor, all agree that no oath is required.
