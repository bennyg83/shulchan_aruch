See Shach, seif katan 19, who writes that the seller cannot place the slaves and fields into the buyer's hands because they were taken away due to the seller, for example where the seller's creditor seized them, or the seller himself gave them to others, burned them, or damaged them. At first glance this is difficult. If he burned or damaged them, it resembles the Mechaber's case in seif 6, where one says, “You dug two pits,” and the other says, “I dug only one,” and the defendant is exempt from a d'oraisa oath because the claim arises from land. Here too, the claim would appear to be for damage to ten cubits of land while the defendant admits damage to only five. It therefore appears that if Reuven sold an article to Shimon for one hundred, Shimon drew the object into his domain and benefited from it, and afterward Reuven stole or damaged it so that it is worth only fifty, Reuven pays only fifty, because all thieves pay according to the value at the time of theft. But if Reuven sold the object for one hundred by a kinyan sudar, received payment, and was obligated to deliver it, and afterward refuses to deliver the object, then even if it is worth only fifty he is not treated merely as a thief liable for fifty. Rather, he must return the entire one hundred he received, because he took the buyer's money and now refuses to deliver the purchased object. If he gives the object to others or destroys it with his own hands, the buyer may void the sale. Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel write a similar rule in the laws of gittin, Even HaEzer siman 120, and the same is evident from siman 312 seif 2 and resembles the case in siman 226 seif 5. Accordingly, the Shach correctly writes that a d'oraisa oath is required, because the buyer can void the sale and his claim is effectively for the movable object itself. The Mishnah therefore specifically uses a dispute about a large slave and a small slave rather than a dispute concerning one large slave, where the buyer says, “I paid such and such for him,” and the seller says that he received less. The latter formulation would not produce partial admission, because the buyer has the option to affirm the sale, as in siman 226 seif 5, and claim compensation according to the object's present value, since he acquired it by an effective kinyan. If the slave is worth the full sum that the buyer claims, then even if the defendant says he received less, he is still obligated according to his own account to pay the slave's full value, so there is no partial admission. Therefore, the Mishnah had to formulate the dispute as one over a large slave versus a small slave.
