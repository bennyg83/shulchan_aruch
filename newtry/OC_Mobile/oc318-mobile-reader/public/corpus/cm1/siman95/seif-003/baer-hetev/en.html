That you rented. The Maggid Mishneh writes that if the dispute is that one says, "I still have the right to live there," and the other denies it, this is a claim concerning land and only a shevuat heset applies. That is, the tenant says he has two months remaining and the landlord says only one. Although the landlord admits part, only a shevuat heset is imposed upon him. Accordingly, if the landlord says, "You rented from me from today for two months. Live there and at the end of two months pay me for both months," while the tenant says, "I rented only for one month. I will live there one month and then pay for one month," this too is not treated as a partial admission requiring a Torah oath, since the payment date has not yet arrived. The tenant takes only a shevuat heset. See siman 312, seif 16. Shach.
That he sold. The law specifically says "sold," because the defendant's obligation was never anything except the purchase money. If, however, one deposited grapevines with attached grapes and later claimed their value, the original obligation was to return the vines together with the grapes themselves, which are attached to the ground. Similarly, where he claims the value of the paper of promissory notes, the case is where he admits that the evidentiary value written in the notes is not owed to him, such as where he could not have collected them in any event, and his only claim was always the paper value. Sema.
How much. The Shach writes that this wording implies that if the parties dispute how much land was sold, for example the seller says, "I sold you the entire field and you owe me its price," while the buyer says, "Half the field was mine and you sold me only the other half, so I owe only half the price," the claim would be treated as one concerning land. In the Shach's view, even there the claim has the law of movable property, because according to both parties the present claim is not for the land but for money. He cites proof from the Mishnah in the chapter HaShoel regarding two slaves, one large and one small. He suggests that the phrase "since their dispute is not how much land there was" may merely exclude a case where the buyer claims, "You sold me two fields," while the seller says, "I sold you only one," because there they dispute the fields themselves. Although the wording is not exact, he prefers to say that the authorities were imprecise rather than attribute to them the view that a monetary price dispute of this kind is treated as a land claim.
