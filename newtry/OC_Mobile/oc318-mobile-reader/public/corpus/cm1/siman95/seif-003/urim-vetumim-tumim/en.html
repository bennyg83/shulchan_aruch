The Shach, seif katan 12, answers the difficulty from the rule that a daughter's tenth share is collected from income of houses, which shows that rental income can have the status of land. He distinguishes that there the rental period has not yet ended and rent is ordinarily payable only at the end. The uncompleted rental is therefore analogous to produce still needing the land. Here, by both parties' accounts, the payment date for the disputed rental period has already passed, so the rent has become ordinary movable money. This needs explanation. Even if the payment date has not arrived, the accepted rule is that rental accrues continuously from beginning to end. Each day that has passed creates an existing debt, merely one not yet due until the end. A loan within its term is still movable property. Why then can a daughter's tenth share be collected from accrued rent as though from land? The answer is that before the rental term ends, the prior accrued rent remains functionally bound to the continuing land obligation. If an armed force or other external cause deprives the tenant of the house during the term, the landlord may have to provide another house, as ruled later in siman 312, seif 2. If the future rent alone is insufficient for that substitute obligation, the economic value of rent already accrued may also be absorbed in satisfying the continuing rental arrangement. Thus the accrued rent remains tied to the land until the term concludes, resembling fruit still connected to the ground. This is what the Shach means by comparing it to produce that still needs the land. It follows that if one says, "I rented you my courtyard for one dinar per month for two months, the two months have passed, pay me," and the tenant answers, "The rental was indeed one dinar per month, but only one month has passed," there is no Torah oath of partial admission. According to the claimant, both claim and denial concern accrued money. But the admitted dinar, according to the tenant's own position, concerns a rental term that has not yet ended. That admitted amount is therefore still treated as land. The admission is in land while the denied amount is money, so the Torah partial admission oath does not arise. However, this creates a question on the Gemara. According to the opinion that heilech exempts from the partial admission oath, the Gemara asks why a verse is needed to exclude land from oaths and answers with the strained case of one who dug pits in another's land. Why not use the rental case just described? The admitted rent is classified as land, but it is not heilech, since the dinar for the elapsed month is not physically before the claimant and still requires collection. Perhaps even apart from the exclusion of land there is no Torah oath here because the admission is not yet an admission of an absolute, fully matured obligation. Until the rental term ends, the accrued rent can still be affected by the landlord's continuing duty if the tenant loses use of the house and replacement costs arise. A partial admission oath requires an admission of a presently definite liability. The tenant's admitted rent has not yet reached that character. This may explain why the Gemara did not use the case, though the matter still requires study.
Since they do not disagree, etc. The wording implies that if the claimant says, "I sold you half a field worth a maneh," and the defendant answers, "I bought only one quarter, because the other quarter was already mine," there is no Torah oath. The essential dispute concerns what portion of land was sold, and oaths are not imposed upon land. The Shach disagrees. He says that once the present practical dispute is whether money must be repaid, the litigation is monetary. He interprets the Gemara's case of one claiming the price of a large slave accordingly: the buyer says, "I paid you money for a large slave, but your creditor seized the slave, so return my payment," while the seller says, "I received payment only for a small slave." The present demand is therefore repayment of money rather than title to the slave. This interpretation accords precisely with the great Ritva in Bava Metzia, who explains the sugya explicitly in this way. Nevertheless, Rashi and Tosafot do not explain it so. Their silence implies that where the oath ultimately depends upon whether the sale concerned a large or small parcel, the core dispute remains a land dispute even though money is now the remedy. This is also the meaning of the Gemara's answer elsewhere involving one who dug pits in land: because the root of the liability lies in the land, the resulting monetary claim retains the land character for the exclusion from oaths. The Maggid Mishneh writes regarding the pit case that the damage was done without authorization. His wording may imply that if the owner expressly authorized the defendant to dig and agreed that he would pay the value, the later claim would be a pure monetary obligation and would no longer be treated as land. If so, that would support the Shach all the more in a sale case structured as an independent monetary repayment. But where the entire disagreement remains what land was sold and what price belonged to that sale, Rashi and Tosafot indicate that the land character persists.
