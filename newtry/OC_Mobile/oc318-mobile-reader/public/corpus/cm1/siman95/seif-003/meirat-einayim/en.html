You lived in my courtyard, etc. The courtyard is land, and no Torah oath is taken concerning land. Nevertheless he must swear here because the claim is not about the land itself. All the more so if the claim is, "You lived in my house," because the principal rental is for use of the constructed house. According to the view that something detached and later attached, such as a house, is considered detached, there is certainly an oath. In the Rambam editions before us the wording is "you lived in my courtyard," while the Tur quotes Rambam as saying "in my house," because ordinarily people do not live in a courtyard without a house.
Worth two silver ma'ot, etc. The wording follows the verse, "money or utensils." It has already been explained in siman 88 that a Torah oath applies only where the amount denied is at least two silver ma'ot.
The value of land that he sold, etc. The ruling specifically says "sold," because from the outset his obligation was only the money price of the sale. But if he deposited vines with attached grapes and later claims their value, originally the bailee was obligated to return the vines and grapes themselves, which were attached. Likewise, where he claims merely the paper value of documents, the case is one where he admits he has no obligation for the evidentiary value of the documents, such as where the documents were in any event uncollectible, so from the outset only the paper value was owed.
