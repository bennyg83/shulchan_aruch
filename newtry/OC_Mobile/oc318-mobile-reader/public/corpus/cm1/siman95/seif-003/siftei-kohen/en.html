He claimed rent for two months. This entire seif is the wording of Rambam in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en. The Semag, positive commandment 95, folio 182a, reproduces it verbatim, and the Mordechai cites it in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. The Mordechai and Hagahot Mordechai at the end of the chapter HaMokher Et HaBayit discuss the rule that daughters collect one tenth of the estate from the revenues of houses, meaning rental income. They ask that this contradicts the Semag's statement in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim that although one does not swear a Torah oath concerning land, one does swear concerning house rent, because rent is treated as movable property. Here, however, house rent is treated as land. In my view there is no contradiction. Tosafot, the Rosh, the Ran, and the Ritva in the chapter Metziat HaIshah, as well as the Maggid Mishneh and Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 20 of Hilchot Ishut, prove that the case in which the daughters collect from house revenues concerns rent whose payment date has not yet arrived. Since rent is payable only at the end of the rental term, the accrued rent is then comparable to produce still attached to land and requiring the land, and is therefore treated as land. It follows that once the payment date arrives, the debt is treated as movable property. The Mordechai himself implies this in the chapter Metziat HaIshah. He explains that the house was rented for a year and the tenant had occupied it for only half a year. Although rent had already accrued, it was treated as land because rent is not payable until the end. This implies that once the entire rental term has elapsed, the rent is treated as movable property even if it has not yet been collected, because its payment date has arrived. This is contrary to the implication of the Rama's gloss in Even HaEzer, siman 113, seif 3, which I challenged there. Accordingly, there is no difficulty, because the Semag, Rambam, and the Mechaber expressly discuss a case in which the due date for rent has passed. The Maggid Mishneh likewise explains Rambam's words here: A claim for rent after the term is a complete monetary debt. If the dispute instead concerns the right to remain in the property, such as where the tenant says, “I am entitled to live there for another two months,” and the landlord says only one month remains, the claim concerns land, and only a rabbinic oath of denial applies. This means that if the tenant claims the right to live there for two further months and the landlord admits only one month, the landlord does not take a Torah oath of partial admission, but only a rabbinic oath. The same follows where the landlord says, “You rented from me from today for two months. Live there and pay the two months' rent at the end,” while the tenant says, “I rented for only one month. I will live there one month and then pay for that month.” Since the payment date has not yet arrived, this is not a partial admission of a presently payable debt, and the tenant takes only a rabbinic oath. Consider this carefully. Cited cross-references in the source: 110, 310, 488.
You resided in my courtyard. The Tur quotes Rambam as saying, “in my house.” This supports what I wrote above in seif 1, seif katan 7. The Sema appears to follow the Rama's ruling above in seif 1 and therefore writes that in the case of a house the defendant would in any event be obligated to swear according to the opinion that an item originally detached and subsequently affixed remains detached even when it forms a house. In my view, however, all authorities agree that a house is treated as land for purposes of oaths, as I wrote above in seif katan 7.

Since they do not dispute how much land he sold him. This implies that if they did dispute the quantity of land sold, the claim would be governed by the law of land. The Maggid Mishneh states this even more clearly. He writes that even if their claim concerns the price of land, such as where the seller says, “I sold you the field, the sale remains valid, and you owe me one hundred of the price,” while the buyer admits fifty, the buyer pays fifty and takes a Torah oath concerning the remainder, because there is no dispute regarding the sale itself or how much land was sold. Beit Yosef cites this in seif 8. This implies that if the seller says, “I sold you the field and you owe its full price,” while the buyer says, “Half the field was already mine; you sold me only the other half, and I owe only the price of that half,” the claim is treated as one concerning land. In my view, even in that case the claim is governed by the law of movable property, because according to both parties the present demand is not for the land but for money. I find proof in the Mishna in the chapter HaShoel, Bava Metzia 100a. If a seller had two slaves, one large and one small, and the buyer says that he bought the large one while the seller says that he does not know, the buyer acquires the large one. If one says large and the other says small, the seller swears that he sold the small one. The Gemara asks why he should swear: what the claimant demanded he did not admit, what he admitted the claimant did not demand, his admission is an immediate tender, and one does not swear concerning slaves. Rav answers that the claim concerns the value of a large slave and the admission the value of a small slave, or the value of a large field and the value of a small field. Rashi explains that the claimant says, “I delivered to you money to purchase a large slave for me, and you did not purchase it,” and now seeks return of the funds. The plain sense of the Mishna does not support this. Tosafot likewise object that according to Rashi the Mishna should have spoken of an agent and principal, not a buyer and seller. Rabbenu Yitzchak therefore explains that the seller sold him a slave and the buyer paid, but no deed had yet been written, in a place where a deed is customary, or they stipulated that either party could retract until delivery of the deed. That explanation also appears strained. If they dispute how much money the buyer paid, the size of the slave is irrelevant. The Mishna should simply have said that one claims to have paid a particular amount and the other admits a lesser amount. The correct explanation appears to me to be as follows. The buyer purchased a slave or field for a dinar, but the seller can no longer deliver it, because the slave or field was taken away as a result of the seller, such as where the seller's creditor seized it, the seller transferred it to another person, burned it, damaged it, or the like. The buyer says, “I purchased the large one for the dinar. The sale was valid, so pay me the value of the large one.” The seller says, “I sold the small one for the dinar and owe you only the value of the small one,” because he can no longer deliver the slave or field itself. It appears that Rashi and Tosafot would also agree with this ruling, although they did not think to explain the Mishna this way. The Ramah cited by Nimukei Yosef at the end of the chapter HaManiach also appears to explain it in this manner. I cited his words above in siman 88, seif 12, seif katan 16, and siman 400, seif 4, seif katan 10. One might object that the Gemara later answers in the name of Rav Sheshet that the Mishna follows Rabbi Meir, who holds that one may swear concerning slaves. The Gemara then again asks that the admission is an immediate tender, and Rava answers that the admitted slave had a severed hand and the admitted field had pits, ditches, and caves dug in it. This seems to imply that originally the slave and field were intact. There is no difficulty. Under Rav's first answer, all the slaves and fields had been ruined or become unavailable and the claim concerned their value, so the Mishna followed all opinions. Rav Sheshet, however, did not accept that the Mishna concerned their value. He understood the claimant to demand the slave or field itself and therefore answered that the Mishna follows Rabbi Meir. Under that answer, the item demanded was intact, but the item admitted was not an immediate tender because the admitted slave had lost a hand or the admitted field had been partially excavated. It follows that where the buyer says, “You sold me the large field,” and the seller says, “I sold you the small field,” if the seller can no longer deliver the field because he damaged it or it was taken as a result of his conduct, so that he must pay its value, the seller is liable to take a Torah oath. The same should apply in the reverse situation where the seller says, “I sold you the large field for its value,” and the buyer says, “You sold me only half, because the other half was mine.” Since according to both sides the remaining dispute concerns only money, a Torah oath applies. Although one might distinguish the cases, careful analysis shows that there is no sound distinction, and indeed liability is even more persuasive in the latter case. The words of the Maggid Mishneh and the Rama, who condition the ruling upon there being no dispute regarding how much land was sold, therefore require study. Perhaps they intended only to exclude a case where the buyer demands that the seller convey two fields and the seller says that he sold only one, so that they dispute the fields themselves. Their wording does not readily imply this, and if that were their intent the Maggid Mishneh did not need to begin by describing a claim for one hundred of the purchase price. Nevertheless, it is preferable to say that their wording was imprecise rather than to attribute to them the view that the monetary claim described above is governed as a claim to land. Cited cross-references in the source: 80, 400.
The value of the paper. The Maggid Mishneh cites Rav Hai Gaon in Shaarei Shevuot, gate 2, at the end of folio 6: If the claim concerns the value of the parchment, one must determine whether the claim contains two silver ma'ah and a perutah, according to the measure previously explained. If so, a Torah oath applies. This remains true even if the document is useful only for wrapping something over the opening of a small flask, as the Sages said that one who sells his documents to a perfumer is subject to the laws of overreaching. I discussed this above in siman 66, seif 39, seif katan 115. If the claim does not amount to two silver ma'ah and a perutah, the defendant is liable to take a rabbinic oath of denial. Rav Hai follows his own view that such an oath applies to every claim of at least one perutah, as I wrote above in siman 88, seif 6. Cited cross-references in the source: 60, 80.
