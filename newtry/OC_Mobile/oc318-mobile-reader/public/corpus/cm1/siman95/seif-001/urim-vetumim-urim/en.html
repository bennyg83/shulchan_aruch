For one does not swear, etc. This is derived by the rule of general and specific terms from the verse, 'If a man gives his fellow silver or utensils.' Silver or utensils excludes land, which is not movable. Slaves are compared by Scripture to land, and promissory notes are excluded because their physical substance is not the money itself.
Even land outside Eretz Yisrael. This excludes the view of the Geonim who held that because such land does not have the status of land regarding consecrated fields, the same should apply to oaths and ona'ah. The ruling teaches otherwise, because such property is still not movable.
And promissory notes. This means that one claims, 'I deposited two notes with you,' whether the notes contain a lien upon property or not, such as a handwritten note or a note without property liability, and the other denies part of the claim and says that only one note was deposited. Since the notes are merely evidence of an obligation, the Torah oath does not apply to them.
And likewise the property of a non-Jew. Scripture says 'his fellow,' excluding a non-Jew. See Tumim, where I asked why a verse is needed, since withholding repayment of a non-Jewish loan may otherwise be permitted. I wrote there that the main exclusion concerns a resident alien, whose loan may not be withheld, yet with respect to oaths and custodial liability he is nevertheless excluded by 'his fellow.'
And one witness contradicts him. For this purpose the oath generated by one witness is compared to the oath of one who admits part of a claim. The general principle that wherever two witnesses obligate payment one witness obligates an oath applies only where the subject is ordinarily subject to an oath in a partial admission case. See what I wrote at the beginning of siman 88.
The custodial oath does not apply. Even if the promissory note has now been lost and the owner therefore sues for its monetary value, since the custodian's obligation took effect initially when he accepted the item for safekeeping, and at that time the subject was excluded property, no Torah oath arises. Shach writes that all agree to this.
Even if they were negligent, etc. See above, siman 66, where Rambam holds that one is liable for negligence because it is treated like direct damage. See what I wrote there at length.
And if he stipulated, etc. See above, siman 66, whether such a condition requires a kinyan.
But one swears hesset. Hesset applies to all property, even land, because the concern of temporary evasion does not govern this rabbinic oath. This is provided that the claimant makes a definite claim. The same applies to a Mishnah oath.
But consecrated property, etc. This refers to consecrated property with a fixed monetary amount. With property consecrated for the poor where the amount is not fixed, the laws of custodians do not apply. Nowadays there is no consecration for Temple maintenance, and ordinary consecrated property is for the poor.
Therefore, if he borrowed a house. Shach discusses this at length and writes that everyone agrees a house has the status of land. The dispute concerns detached building materials alone. A house itself is attached property according to all opinions, and this is correct in practice, for we say that the support allotment from an estate may be collected from the value of houses. Accordingly, either we rule that something detached and later attached is considered attached, or a house is exceptional. See Tumim. It may be that although such an item is not generally considered attached, with respect to oaths it has the status of land. This also resolves why Rama here cites two views whether detached property later attached is treated as attached, whereas in Yoreh Deah siman 145 seif 3 he rules simply that it is treated as detached. According to this distinction there is no contradiction. See Tumim.
And it burned. Although a borrower is ordinarily liable even for unavoidable accidents, since a house has the status of land he is exempt from accidents and from all custodial liabilities.
