See Responsa Chatam Sofer, Yoreh Deah, siman 134, who discusses at length and establishes that banknotes do not have the law of documents, but rather the law of currency. The defining feature of currency is that the sovereign decrees that it circulate and that one who refuses to accept it is subject to punishment. Such an instrument is monetary property in itself, whether made of gold, silver, or paper. Therefore one may impose an oath concerning a claim for banknotes, they are subject to the laws of custodians, and they are full money even for kiddushin, redemption of consecrated property, and redemption of second tithe. This applies specifically to banknotes that people are legally compelled to accept in payment by virtue of the law of the kingdom. By contrast, instruments called bamashka and copper exchange notes, which no one is compelled to accept as payment, are merely documents. According to this, a lottery note likewise has the law of a document. At the end of that responsum he mentions the words of She'elat Yaavetz, part 1, end of siman 85, regarding one who negligently lost lottery notes, who writes that these are even superior to ordinary documents, because ordinary documents are not equally marketable to everyone, whereas these notes have fixed prices, a market quotation, and sell readily. It is possible that She'elat Yaavetz wrote this only as an additional consideration, because in any event the primary law follows the Shach, who rules that one is liable for negligence regarding documents.

In Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 5, he was further asked about an incident in which Reuven asked Shimon to exchange for him a banknote of one hundred gold coins into smaller notes. Shimon did so and gave him ten banknotes of ten each. Several hours later Shimon demanded the large banknote from Reuven, and Reuven answered that he had given it to him at the time of the exchange. They both counted their bundles of money and found that Shimon was short one hundred and Reuven had no surplus. Both trusted one another that neither wished to steal from the other, but the note that Reuven had placed on the table had apparently been stolen by the non Jews who were there. Shimon claimed: You did not place it in my hand, and I did not ask for it immediately because I assumed it remained among your things and planned to ask you for it later. Reuven claimed: I handed it over to you immediately, though he did not know whether he put it literally into Shimon's hand. He did know that he said at once, "Place the one hundred note here," and therefore Shimon should have taken it, and the loss was Shimon's. Chatam Sofer answered that the defendant's claim did not clarify whether he delivered the large banknote before receiving the smaller ones or afterward. Currency and documents are not acquired through chalipin, even in an equal exchange. Therefore, by receiving the smaller notes from the claimant, the claimant did not acquire the large banknote unless it was actually delivered into his hand and he performed meshichah. If it was merely placed before him on the table, he did not acquire it. See what I wrote below, siman 207, seif 3, seif katan 18. Nor did he become a custodian over it. Even according to those who hold a custodian of documents liable for negligence, and even according to She'elat Yaavetz that lottery notes are superior to ordinary documents because they are highly marketable, and all the more so these banknotes, here he never became a custodian, because the owner did not say, "Place it before me." Moreover, the Shach in siman 291, seif katan 13, concludes that custodians require meshichah and incur no liability without it. Thus, if it was not actually delivered into his hand, the loss falls upon the defendant. The defendant, however, is uncertain and says that he delivered it into Shimon's hand and that the loss occurred from Shimon. The outcome therefore depends on the sequence. If he is certain that before Shimon gave him the smaller notes he had already delivered the large banknote, and his only doubt is whether he placed it into Shimon's hand or on the table, this is a case of "I do not know whether I ever became liable to you." He takes a heset oath that he does not know and is exempt. If, however, he delivered it only after he had already received the smaller notes and had thereby become indebted, this is a case of "I do not know whether I repaid you," and he must pay. If he also does not know whether he delivered the large banknote before receiving the smaller ones, in my view he is liable, because at least the debt itself is certain while repayment is doubtful.
