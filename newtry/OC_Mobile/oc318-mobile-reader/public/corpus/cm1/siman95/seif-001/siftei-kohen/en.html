There is no distinction whether he denies everything and one witness contradicts him. So write the Rambam and Tur. It appears that their intent is to prevent one from arguing that a single witness should obligate him in a Torah oath, because wherever two witnesses would obligate a person in money, one witness obligates him in an oath, and two witnesses would obligate payment even regarding these categories. This is also why we say above in siman 88, seif 4, that one witness can obligate an oath even over a perutah, although a partial admission oath requires denial of two silver ma'ah, and according to many poskim a custodian's oath also requires two silver ma'ah, as discussed there in seif 5. Nevertheless, once these matters were excluded from all Torah oaths, they were excluded from the oath of one witness as well. The Ran writes this in Perek HaDayanim in his discussion beginning, these matters independently obligate and draw others with them, and also that no oath is administered concerning liens on land, etc. He explains that since the legal strength of land and the other excluded categories is weakened even with respect to two witnesses, for example where two witnesses testify that an unpaid custodian or borrower suffered an unavoidable accident and he is exempt, their status is likewise weakened regarding a single witness. Therefore we do not apply the general principle that wherever two witnesses obligate money one witness obligates an oath. The Ran further writes that Perek HaKosev proves that these matters were excluded from the oath generated by one witness, and the Beit Yosef cites him briefly. This is indeed clear there in Kesubos 87b. Rabbeinu Yeruham, Nesiv 3, chelek 2, writes that the Yerushalmi states the same, as cited by the Beit Yosef. It is indeed explicit in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of Perek Shevuas HaDayanim. The Mishna states that the claim must be two silver ma'ah, etc., and the Yerushalmi says: just as silver means two ma'ah, so utensils means two ma'ah. Rav Yehuda said in the name of Shmuel: wherever two witnesses obligate a person in money, one witness subjects him to an oath. But two witnesses can obligate him regarding land. The Yerushalmi answers that land is different because one does not swear regarding land. The Rosh cites this passage in Perek HaDayanim when discussing what he wrote in the name of the Rif, that an oath can be imposed on an uncertain claim through a witness. Tosafos in Perek HaDayanim, 40a, and at the beginning of Bava Metzia 5a, likewise state plainly, as do many other poskim in many places, that one does not take an oath concerning slaves, documents, or land, even an oath generated by one witness.
The custodians' oath, etc. It appears that even according to the Raavad and those who agree with him, whom I cite below at the end of this siman and whose view I adopt, that where the claim concerns only the monetary value a Torah oath is required, nevertheless here, regarding a custodian's oath, he is exempt in every case. It is strained to say that the case here concerns property that was lost but still exists and can be recovered, such as where it was taken by an armed robber and could be repurchased from the robber. Rather, the implication is that it was entirely lost from the world, like something broken or captured. The difference is that here the custodian did nothing at the moment of loss that would create liability. His liability exists only because he accepted a duty of safekeeping. Had he not accepted that duty, he would owe nothing. Thus his fundamental obligation arose at the time he accepted the duty of safekeeping, and at that time the subject was land. The Torah excluded land from the laws of custodians, so it is as though he never accepted custodial responsibility at all. This differs from the case discussed there, where no obligation existed until he damaged the property. There, the present act of damage is what obligates him, and the monetary claim is the cost of filling the pits. This appears correct to me.
And even if they were negligent, etc. I proved above in siman 66, seif 40, seif katan 126, that the primary ruling follows the Rambam that negligence creates liability, and I also cited there several great authorities who agree. See there.
And if he stipulated through a kinyan. This appears in the Gemara, Perek HaZahav, Bava Metzia 58a, and in the Rambam at the beginning of chapter 8 of Hilchos Sechirus, and was explained above at the end of siman 66, seif katan 129. See also what I wrote there in seif katan 128. The Ran at the beginning of Perek HaNose in Kesubos writes that according to the Rambam's view above at the beginning of siman 40, the same law applies if he says, you are my witnesses, or obligates himself by a document, etc. In my humble opinion, however, the Rambam agrees here, as I wrote above in siman 40, seif katan 4. See there.
But with a heset oath one does swear. The same applies to every rabbinic oath, such as a Mishnaic oath and the like, as is clear from the Gemara in Perek HaKosev, 87b. Indeed, the Maggid Mishneh writes that the law of heset regarding these matters is learned from that passage in Perek HaKosev. The Bach writes in siman 3 that the rule that one takes heset was not learned from Perek HaKosev, but from the discussion at the beginning of Bava Metzia, as the Semag writes in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, and not as the Maggid Mishneh understood at the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchos To'en. This is not precise. Nothing in the Gemara there proves the rule. The Semag merely records it in the name of Rav Hai Gaon and then reconciles the passage at the beginning of Bava Metzia. See also Tosafos at the beginning of Bava Metzia 6a, who write the same in the name of Rav Hai Gaon. The Maggid Mishneh's words are therefore correct. Examine carefully.
And consecrated property, etc. Nowadays every form of communal consecrated property has the law of ordinary property in this respect. The Tur writes the same below in siman 212, seif 8. See the responsa of Rabbi Eliyahu ibn Chaim, simanim 30, 103, and 108, and the work Agudas Ezov, page 84, fourth column.
And some say, etc. The Tur in seif 7 cites this in the name of Baal HaItur. It is clear there that Baal HaItur disagrees with Rabbeinu Chananel, who ruled like Rabbi Eliezer. I cite his words in the following seif katan. I am puzzled by the Tur's statement in seif 5 regarding produce growing from the ground, where he writes that Rabbeinu Chananel likewise ruled like Rabbi Eliezer. Rabbeinu Chananel said this only about something that was detached and afterward attached, as appears from the Rashbam in Perek HaMocher Es HaBayis, Bava Batra 67a, and from Baal HaItur there. Regarding produce growing from the ground, however, since it is destined to be cut, one may say that it is considered already cut. Tosafos explicitly write this in several places in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel, who ruled like Rabbi Meir that produce destined to be cut is treated as cut. The Semag, Hagahos Maimoniyos, and Mordechai likewise write this in his name, and I cite their words below in seif 2, seif katan 9. There is a further difficulty. Why does the Tur here in seif 7 state Baal HaItur's view without qualification after having cited Rabbeinu Chananel in seif 5? He should have written here that Rabbeinu Chananel disagrees. It therefore appears that an error entered the Tur, and Rabbeinu Chananel's words in seif 5 belong in seif 7. The correct reading would be: Baal HaItur wrote that this applies specifically to produce growing from the ground, but regarding something detached and later attached we follow the Sages, while Rabbeinu Chananel ruled like Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that anything attached to land has the status of land. The Beit Yosef writes that he does not know where Rabbi Eliezer said this. It appears that he overlooked the Gemara in Perek HaMocher Es HaBayis and several other places.
Something detached and afterward attached, etc. The Rama's words appear to mean that according to this second opinion even a house is considered something detached and afterward attached for purposes of custodial oaths. The Rama apparently inferred this from the Tur's citation of Baal HaItur, that regarding a built wall, a pipe that was affixed, and a beehive, we follow the Sages. Darchei Moshe likewise explicitly writes, for example a built wall, etc. But the Mordechai in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim writes that a house has the status of attached property, and one who borrows a house and it burns is exempt. So records Darchei Moshe. In my humble opinion, however, Baal HaItur referred only to a built wall, whereas a house itself has the status of land. This is why the Tur immediately afterward writes in the name of the Rambam, you resided in my house, etc., implying that a house is land. In particular, the Rambam's original wording is "my courtyard," while the Tur substitutes "my house," showing that the Tur plainly regarded a house as land. Although the Gemara at the beginning of Chullin 77a implies that a house is like a built wall, that discussion concerns ritual law. In monetary law a house is land. The proof is that everywhere a house is acquired by money, document, and chazakah like land. It is also called property bearing responsibility with respect to liens, priority, the tenth share of an estate, and other monetary laws. The same therefore applies to custodians and oaths. The Rambam and Tur likewise write below in siman 227, regarding ona'ah, that even if one sold a palace for one dinar there is no ona'ah. This is also why Baal HaItur wrote that the law follows the Sages, since the Sages did not discuss a house itself. Baal HaItur further writes that Rabbeinu Chananel said, from the fact that Rabbi Nechemiah sent instructions to collect a tenth of the estate from mill structures that are movable items fixed to land, it follows that the course of the discussion follows Rabbi Eliezer, who says that anything attached to land is treated as land, but Baal HaItur adds that this is not sufficiently clear to him. Rashbam in Perek HaMocher Es HaBayis, Bava Batra 67a, also cites these words of Rabbeinu Chananel. Baal HaItur's difficulty appears to be that in Bava Batra 65a even the Sages agree that those mill structures are sold with the house, and their dispute concerns other movable items. In any event, the primary practical ruling appears to me to be that a house is land for purposes of custodians and oaths. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 5 of Hilchos To'en, halachah 5, likewise indicates this when he writes that chapter 7 of Hilchos Sechirus establishes that rental of land has the status of land regarding oaths, and the Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchos Sechirus discusses a house, as I write below in siman 312, seif 7. The Tur here in seif 7 likewise writes in the name of the Rambam, you resided in my house, etc., as noted above. The Mordechai and Hagahos Mordechai in Perek HaMocher Es HaBayis, which I cite below in seif katan 12, likewise state this explicitly. Tosafos in Perek HaDayanim 42b, Tosafos Yom Tov there, and the Semag likewise write plainly that one who borrows a house and it burns is exempt because the laws of custodians do not apply to land, as does the Mordechai in Perek HaDayanim and the Agudah there. This is the ruling we should follow.
