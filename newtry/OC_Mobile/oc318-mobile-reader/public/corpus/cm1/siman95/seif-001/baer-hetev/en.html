And documents. The Sema explains that the claimant says, "I deposited two documents with you, each worth a stated amount. Return the documents." The defendant admits one and denies the other. Therefore even if the claim concerns handwritten documents, he is exempt from a Torah oath. If, however, he claims the monetary value of the documents, it is as though he claimed, "You owe me a maneh," and the defendant admitted part, in which case a Torah oath applies where the claim is based upon handwritten obligations. But where the claim is fifty by oral obligation and fifty by a witnessed document, and the defendant admits the fifty in the document and denies the oral fifty, just as no oath is imposed for denial of land, so too an admission concerning a lien upon land does not trigger an oath, as stated in siman 69 and siman 88, seif 28. Sema.

Stipulated. This means by a kinyan, which is why the Mechaber refers to siman 66, where the condition is said to involve a kinyan. The same appears in siman 301, seif 4. There the Sema explains why this does not contradict siman 251, where a condition with bailees is effective without a kinyan. Sema.
Shevuat heset. The same applies to a Mishnah oath, such as the partners' oath or the oath of one who swears and collects. Each oath applies according to its own law, as is clear from the Gemara. Sema.
For the poor. This means funds in which the poor of the city have an established, defined share. Such charitable property has the law of ordinary private property, as the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state at the end of siman 212. The Tur writes there that nowadays every form of hekdesh has the law of ordinary property because there is no present consecration for Temple upkeep, but rather charitable designation. Where the poor have no defined share, however, siman 301, seif 7, explains that a bailee is exempt because the money has no specific claimant. Sema. See Responsa Raanach 30, 103, and 107.
Attached. The Shach writes that the Rama appears to understand that according to this opinion even a house is treated as detached property that was later attached, for purposes of the oaths of bailees, and that this follows the Ittur and Darkei Moshe. In the Shach's view, however, the Ittur refers only to a constructed wall. A house itself has the law of land. He cites several authorities who state simply that one who borrowed a house and it burned is exempt because the laws of bailees do not apply to land. That is the ruling we follow.
