The exclusion of property of a gentile from the laws of custodians and their oaths follows from Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, and the Mekhilta's reading of "his fellow." For the same reason the reverse case also does not create ordinary Torah custodial liability of a Jew toward a gentile. See what I wrote in siman 72, seif katan 43.
If the custodian expressly stipulated that he would pay despite the ordinary exemption, the undertaking requires a valid kinyan. See siman 301.
The Tur cites Baal HaIttur that a built wall, a fixed pipe, and a beehive can still be treated as movables under the view of the Sages, and Rama therefore records an opinion that something detached and later attached does not necessarily acquire full land status. The Shach distinguishes a house, which he regards as land for monetary law even if a wall may be treated differently in some prohibitory contexts. He proves this from the fact that a house is acquired by money, deed, and possession like land, is classed among assets with responsibility for liens and priority, and is treated as land for many monetary laws. In my view the distinction is persuasive. A completed house is not merely a movable object attached to the ground; its legal identity in monetary law is land. This explains why ordinary rules of custodial oath that exclude land also exclude a borrowed house, even if individual building components might be discussed differently.
