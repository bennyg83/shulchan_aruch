And likewise the property of a gentile . The Mekhilta derives the exclusion from the word "his fellow," and a similar exclusion is taught regarding bailees. One must understand why a verse is needed. The law generally permits withholding a gentile's monetary claim in circumstances of a mere debt, so why would there be an oath even if the claim were true? I discussed above, simanim 72 and 86, whether this principle applies equally to a deposit, where the deposited object remains the gentile's property wherever it is. Even HaEzer siman 28 appears to indicate that the same issue can arise even with a deposit. Regarding bailees, the Scriptural exclusion has clear applications. If the gentile already seized property from the Jewish bailee, the ordinary rule permitting nonpayment of the gentile's debt is irrelevant. Yet if the Torah excluded the gentile's property from the laws of bailees, the seizure cannot create a bailee liability that Torah law never imposed. Likewise, if the Jew swore personally to pay the gentile or return the deposit, his oath may obligate him, but if the deposit was lost through negligence, or a paid bailee suffered theft or loss, the independent Torah laws of bailees still do not apply because the verse excluded this relationship. Another application is where the gentile owes money to a Jew and has deposited property with another Jew. The first Jew may have rights against assets of the gentile, so one cannot simply invoke the permissibility of withholding a gentile's debt. Even if the gentile gave the deposited item away, the creditor may be able to reach it. Nevertheless, the second Jew accepted the item as the gentile's bailee and the Torah excluded that custodial relationship from the laws of bailees. The creditor's separate claim does not transform it into a Jewish bailment. For the oath of partial admission, however, these examples are less satisfactory. If the gentile has seized property, the admitted portion can amount to heilech or the procedural structure differs. If the obligation exists only because the Jew swore to the gentile, then the relevant issue is enforcement of that personal oath, not the Torah oath of partial admission. And if the gentile owes money to another Jew while this Jew owes the gentile, Rabbi Natan's rule of direct collection can apply. The first Jew then sues the second Jew directly for money, and if he asserts one hundred while the second admits fifty, the litigation is between Jews. If the first Jew knows of the debt only through the gentile's word, there is no Torah oath in any event because a claim based solely on a gentile's report is not the definite claim required for a Torah oath. I therefore do not see an ordinary necessity for the verse excluding a full gentile from the oath of partial admission. It seems that the verse is needed principally for a ger toshav. Withholding his debt is prohibited, since the Torah commands us to sustain him, and certainly one may not wrongfully retain his money. Yet the Torah's oath of partial admission and the laws of bailees still do not apply to him because the verse says "his fellow," and a ger toshav is not included in that term for this law. This helps explain Tosafot in Bava Metzia 70b, s.v. "You shall lend upon interest." According to the opinion that lending to a gentile with interest is permitted, Tosafot ask why the person in Yechezkel who lent to a gentile with interest was not revived with the dead. The Maharsha asks why this is difficult, since according to the opinion prohibiting such interest the prohibition is only rabbinic, lest one learn from the gentile's conduct. Tosafot then suggest that a descendant of Noach may itself be prohibited from taking interest, and ask that the verse permitting interest with a gentile is therefore needed to prevent the Jew from violating the prohibition of placing a stumbling block before another. One could have answered that when borrowing from a full gentile there is no classic fixed interest obligation at all on the Jewish side, because the underlying gentile debt can be withheld. When the Jew later pays principal and interest, the additional payment can be characterized as a gift rather than the consideration for waiting. But that answer fails for a ger toshav, whose debt may not be withheld. If the Noahide prohibition exists, borrowing from a ger toshav on interest could therefore create a problem of placing a stumbling block. The verse might be needed to permit precisely that case. The Mishnah says that one may borrow from and lend to gentiles, and mentions a ger toshav as well. If the ger toshav clause referred only to lending money to him, one could have stated the more significant case of the ger toshav alone, for if one may take interest from a person whom one is commanded to sustain, all the more from an ordinary gentile. The need to mention a full gentile could be because of the separate rabbinic concern that close financial dealings lead one to learn from his conduct, with exceptions for livelihood or a Torah scholar. According to the opinion that there is no such concern when lending to a gentile, however, the Mishnah's structure strongly suggests that the ger toshav clause also permits borrowing from him. That reopens the concern of placing a stumbling block if a Noahide is prohibited in interest. Tosafot's discussion can therefore be read coherently. Their first suggestion is that interest is included in the Noahide prohibition of theft. They then ask why the Jew is permitted to participate, and the question is especially sharp for a ger toshav, where the debt cannot simply be withheld. One might answer that the Mishnah's mention of a ger toshav refers only to lending to him, but under the opinion that lending to an ordinary gentile carries no concern of learning from his conduct there is no reason to mention the ordinary gentile separately if the ger toshav alone were intended for that clause. Hence Tosafot's question stands, and they ultimately derive the permission from the verse concerning interest between brothers. The entire passage is thereby well explained.
But a hesset oath, etc. The Bach writes that this is learned from the first chapter of Bava Metzia. He refers to Tosafot's question on the Gemara's challenge: regarding Rav Nachman's enactment of hesset, why do we not say that one who is suspected of lying about money should be suspected of taking a false oath? One might answer that a debtor merely delays payment temporarily. Tosafot therefore explain that Rav Nachman instituted hesset even concerning land, where ordinary temporary delay does not explain the denial. The Maggid Mishneh, however, writes that the source is HaKotev, where an oath was instituted concerning a ketubah. Tosafot in Bava Batra 33 likewise say explicitly that just as we find a Mishnah oath concerning land, we may assume hesset was also instituted there. The Bach apparently did not consider that Tosafot and therefore dismissed HaKotev as a source. One Tosafot answer in Bava Metzia says that ordinary temporary delay is not relevant to land, and the Gemara in Shevuot gives temporary delay as the reason for hesset. That answer would seem to imply that Rav Nachman's enactment did not automatically extend to every land case, and that we do not simply say the Sages made no distinctions. We can explain that this answer follows the view that does not rely on Abaye's additional concern of an old outstanding loan.
Once Abaye introduces the possibility that the defendant believes he has some old counterclaim, even land disputes can generate a rationalized denial. The Gemara in Shevuot still mentions temporary delay because Rav Nachman's enactment applies even where the claimant previously waived other demands, as I wrote above in siman 92 in the name of the Nimukei Yosef. The Sages then did not divide their enactment into endless subcategories. However , Tosafot in Bava Metzia 5, s.v. "According to the one who says heilech," ask why, even according to the view that heilech still creates an oath, a verse is needed to exclude land from the Torah oath when the rationale of temporary delay does not apply to land. Why did they not answer that Abaye's concern about an old loan applies even there? This suggests that, according to that Tosafot approach, an uncertain old loan may be enough to answer the argument that a person suspected of money is suspected of an oath, but it is not enough to overcome the principle that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor. A person may rationalize his conduct through an old claim and still hesitate to make a full brazen denial. Thus the question from a migo of total denial remains. That is one Tosafot answer. Under their second answer, which relies on Rabbi Chiya's question, one can maintain the distinction stated above: once Abaye introduced the old loan concern, Rav Nachman's hesset enactment can extend to land as well. The apparent contradiction within Tosafot can also be resolved factually. Some land claims can involve temporary delay, for example where the defendant says the land was mortgaged to him. Other land is openly possessed and clearly not mortgaged, so temporary delay is implausible. The Torah nevertheless needed a verse to exclude land because without the verse one might impose an oath in those land cases where temporary delay is possible. But Rav Nachman's enactment applies broadly even to land cases where that particular explanation is absent. The passages are therefore consistent.
