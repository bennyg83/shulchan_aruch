One does not take a Torah oath concerning them, etc. The Gemara derives this from the passage of bailees: "If a man gives to his fellow," which is a generalization; "money or utensils," a specification; "to guard," another generalization. From the rule of a generalization, specification, and generalization, we include only matters similar to the specification. Just as the specified items are movable property whose body has monetary value, and an oath applies to them, as it states, "the oath of Hashem shall be between them," so too all comparable items. Land is excluded because it is immovable. Slaves are equated to land. Documents are excluded because their bodies are not monetary value; they stand only as evidence. Hekdesh and property of non-Jews are excluded because they are not included in "his fellow." What the Mechaber writes about property of non-Jews is not stated in that Gemara; he copied it from Rambam, who holds that it too is excluded. Maggid Mishneh writes that this is from the Mechilta, by analogy to the exclusion of the property of a non-Jew from "his fellow" in the law of an ox that gores. Accordingly, when Jewish courts have authority, a Jew who is a bailee for a non-Jew is exempt even if the item was lost through negligence, and even if he used it without permission, so long as property of the non-Jew did not remain in his hands.
Even land outside Eretz Yisrael. This excludes the view of certain Geonim who held that land outside Eretz Yisrael, since it stands to be sold, is like movable property. They brought proof from Arakhin, but Raavad distinguishes that this was said only regarding valuations, while for oaths and ona'ah it retains the law of land because it is not movable.
And documents. Meaning, the claimant says, "I deposited with you two documents, each for such and such an amount; return them," and the defendant admits one and denies the other. Therefore even handwritten documents are excluded from the Torah oath. But if he claims the monetary value of the documents, this exclusion does not apply, for it is as though he claimed one hundred in money and the defendant admitted part and denied part. This is specifically where the claim is based on the defendant's own handwriting. If he claims fifty orally and fifty by a witnessed document, and the defendant admits the fifty in the document but denies the oral fifty, just as denial involving land does not generate an oath, so an admission of an obligation secured by land does not generate an oath, as explained above in simanim 69 and 81.
Whether he admits part, etc. By contrast, with a deposit of movable property the bailee must swear. Although we say that the sections of the Torah are intermingled, we do not uproot the primary meaning of the sections, as explained in simanim 75, 87, and 88.
Even if they were negligent and the items were lost. Here the ruling rejects Rambam, cited by the Tur, who equates negligence to direct damage and imposes liability. Other authorities distinguish: only direct destruction creates liability, because that is ordinary tort liability. So rule the Tur and Mechaber above at the end of siman 66. The Tur further writes there that if a paid bailee was negligent with them and they were stolen or lost, he forfeits his wage until he swears that he guarded properly.
If he stipulated, etc. This means through a kinyan, as in Rambam and as written in Darkei Moshe. Rama's reference to siman 66, seif 40, is to the ruling there that the condition is through a kinyan. The same is written below, siman 301, seif 4. I explained there how this does not contradict siman 251, where a condition with bailees can be effective without a kinyan.
But a hesset oath, etc. This applies only where the claimant asserts with certainty, because hesset was instituted only for a definite claim. And not only hesset: the same applies to rabbinic oaths such as the oath of partners or an oath by one who swears and collects, each according to its law.
But property consecrated for the poor, etc. Meaning funds in which the poor of the city have a fixed entitlement. Such consecrated property has the law of ordinary private property. The Tur writes similarly at the end of this siman and, together with the Mechaber, below at the end of siman 212. But where the poor have no fixed share, siman 301 explains that the bailee is exempt because it is property without a specific claimant.
Therefore, if one borrowed a house and it burned, etc. Although a borrower is ordinarily liable even for unavoidable accidents, here he is exempt.
