See Shach, seif katan 9, who cites the Rambam's Commentary on the Mishnah and explains that his view follows Rabbi Yosef HaLevi in the Tur: only with respect to the guardian's oath and a guardian's liability are produce that no longer requires the ground nevertheless treated as land. With respect to the oath of partial admission and other laws, they are treated as movable property. The reason appears to be that a guardian's liability stems from the original acceptance of custody. At the time custody was accepted, the produce had not been given to him for severance, so the principle that something standing to be detached is regarded as detached does not apply, and it remains treated as land. By contrast, the oath of partial admission is triggered by the present denial and admission. At that moment the produce stands to be detached, and therefore it is treated as movable property. Tumim challenges this from the Mishnah, which states that where one claims ten vines and the other admits only five, the Sages exempt him. That case necessarily concerns the oath of partial admission, because for a guardian's oath partial admission is unnecessary. Thus the Sages apparently exempt from the oath of partial admission as well. A recently printed work, Mishnat Chachamim on the Mishnah, attempted to answer this question, but plainly erred, as anyone who examines his words can see. It appears to me that the distinction between the guardian's oath and the oath of partial admission is indeed correct as explained. The reason the Sages exempt from the oath of partial admission is based on Rabbah's principle that every person who partially admits a claim should theoretically be exempt by a migo that he could have denied everything. The only reason this migo does not ordinarily exempt him is the presumption that a person does not brazenly deny his creditor, so it is a migo involving brazenness. Here, however, since the guardian is exempt from a guardian's oath if he claims unavoidable loss, he is likewise exempt from the oath of partial admission by a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss as to the item he admitted. Such a claim involves no brazenness because the other party cannot know that it is false. Although he could not claim unavoidable loss as to the portion he denied, because that denied portion is present before us, regarding the admitted portion the case is one where, according to the Rama in the name of the Ran, he has already consumed it. He could have claimed unavoidable loss regarding that admitted portion while admitting the portion he presently denies, and under that version he would be exempt from an oath according to the Rambam because, for guardianship liability, the produce is treated as land. This is analogous to Tosafot's question in Bava Kamma 67 concerning Rami bar Chama: why is the defendant not believed by a migo that he could have admitted the portion he denied and denied the portion he admitted? Tosafot answer there that had he admitted the portion currently denied, it would have been treated as immediate tender, making him one who denies the entire claim, and one does not brazenly deny everything. Here that answer does not apply, because if he had claimed unavoidable loss regarding the admitted portion, he would be exempt from the oath since for guardianship the property is land, and the claim of unavoidable loss is not brazen. One cannot simply formulate the migo as, “he could have claimed unavoidable loss regarding the admitted portion and thus denied everything,” because he would still be brazen regarding the portion he currently denies if he admitted nothing at all. The migo must be formulated as I have described. The Sages hold that a migo that exempts from an oath is accepted where it is not a migo of brazenness, as Maharshal explains in Ketubot 18 regarding the dispute between Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov and the Sages concerning occasions when a person swears based on his own claim. Rambam and Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, however, rule like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov that a migo does not exempt from an oath, as explained above in siman 93 seif 2. Maggid Mishneh likewise writes that Rambam rules like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, and see Lechem Mishneh. Even according to those who understand Rambam as ruling like the Sages there, Rambam must have another proof from the Gemara that a migo does not exempt from an oath, as he rules in several places. In the case of Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov, his reason for following the Sages would be that the status of one returning a lost object is stronger than an ordinary migo, as Tumim writes. He therefore properly rules that one must take the oath of partial admission even in the case of a guardian holding produce that does not require the ground. A second resolution is that Rambam may follow the Ran's interpretation that the vines were already harvested and consumed, as the Rama writes. Although the Sages treat even detached produce in that case as land, Rambam can rely upon the ruling in Bava Kamma cited by the Shach, where the law follows Rabbi Shimon that one who consumed fully ripened produce pays as detached produce, precisely because it had already been detached. Thus, in our case too, where the produce had already been detached, Rambam rules like Rabbi Shimon that it is movable property rather than like the Sages. This also answers the Sema's question in siman 193 against the Rama: why did the Rama note there that some disagree and treat such produce as land, yet not make the same gloss in siman 95? According to this explanation, siman 95 concerns produce already detached, and the Rama agrees that it is movable property. See the Shach, who concludes that generally such produce is treated as land, except regarding a creditor, where it is treated as movable property because the creditor does not rely on it. Concerning siman 257, the Shach explains in siman 193, seif katan 11, that the donor's intent did not include it. His meaning appears to be that where the law distinguishes between land and movables, attached produce is not called movable because it cannot actually be moved. In siman 257, however, where a donor reserves produce for himself, we follow ordinary speech: fully ripened produce is called “produce,” whereas produce not yet fully ripened is not ordinarily so called. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that, regarding guardians, grapes that no longer require the ground are still land because Rambam holds that meshichah was instituted for guardians. Since their law for acquisition is that of movables, chazakah does not work, only meshichah or acquisition together with land. Meshichah is impossible while they remain attached, and acquisition together with land likewise does not work because the guardian bears no liability for the land itself. He compares this to Bava Kamma 117 regarding theft together with land. In my opinion he erred on two points. First, one can readily find a case where the land and vines belong to the guardian, but the produce belongs to the depositor, for example where Reuven entrusted the fruit of his trees to Shimon and afterward Shimon entrusted his own fruit to Reuven for safekeeping. Reuven then becomes liable as guardian because the produce is in his courtyard, and one's courtyard acquires for him just as meshichah does, as explained in the laws of deposits, siman 291. Alternatively, one can effect acquisition together with land by granting the land itself to the guardian by loan or rental, which suffices for acquisition together with land as explained in siman 202. Second, Rambam holds a guardian liable for negligence regarding land, and the land itself can be acquired by chazakah for purposes of guardianship liability for negligence. This is unlike the case in Bava Kamma, because land cannot be stolen at all, and therefore the thief acquires no status of theft in the land with which to acquire the movables together with it.
