Seif 4. (I entrusted a promissory note to you.) A person sent a promissory note with an agent to collect from the debtor. The agent claims that he returned the note to the sender's son, a youth of about fifteen who was not known to engage in business and ordinarily remained in school. The son denies receiving it and says that the agent gave him only an unsigned written paper. That paper still exists and is plainly not the promissory note in question. According to the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 130, only where a wife regularly conducts business in the household does a custodian who returns entrusted property to her become exempt, even if he was a borrower. This is also connected to siman 97, where the Shach says that nowadays women in these countries are generally business-active. If she does not conduct the household business, even an unpaid custodian remains liable after returning property to her. I therefore initially told the parties, without issuing a formal ruling, that returning the note to this son did not constitute a valid return at all. I later saw that the Urim VeTumim there disagrees with the Shach, holding that an unpaid custodian is exempt while a paid custodian remains liable. Yet the Urim adds that where the son or recipient denies having received the object, the custodian is liable. I did not at that moment find this point discussed in the Tumim itself. At first glance one could argue that if the son had admitted receiving the note the unpaid custodian would be exempt. Why, then, should the custodian not be allowed to swear that his account is true and thereby be exempt, while the son takes a shevuat heset that he received nothing, analogous to the shopkeeper and workers? Perhaps there is a distinction because that case rests on a specific Mishnaic enactment. At least, however, the son should take a shevuat heset, and the unpaid custodian should then be exempt through the oath of custodians in a case to which custodial law applies. Why should he not be believed through a migo that he could have claimed unavoidable loss? Perhaps the answer is that handing property to a person who later denies receipt is itself negligence, first because the custodian did not deliver it before witnesses, and second because the recipient's denial retroactively reveals, according to the custodian's own account, that he delivered it to an unreliable person. If so, with a promissory note, which ordinarily lies outside the standard monetary liabilities of custodians, the custodian might still be exempt according to those who hold that even negligence concerning such documents does not create liability. Yet perhaps delivery without witnesses should be treated as direct destruction. Even if the item had originally been entrusted to him without witnesses, the owner can say, "I trusted you to support your account by the custodian's oath, which is more stringent than a shevuat heset. Why should I be forced to rely on the denial of some third person?" Logic also supports this, for otherwise every custodian could simply claim, "I gave it to someone else." One might suggest a migo that a liar would distance his testimony and say that he returned the item through someone who has since died or gone overseas, a claim involving less direct brazenness. But perhaps even that is considered brazen, like the poskim's rule that there is no migo to say "I do not know," because people would infer that he is deceitful. For public policy, then, delivery without witnesses should perhaps be treated like direct loss. Accordingly, even with a promissory note the custodian would be liable when he returned it to this son and the son denies receipt. If the son did not deny receiving it, there would be room to say that even according to the Shach the custodian is exempt regarding a note, at least according to the view that negligence does not create liability for documents, because he did not directly destroy it but gave it to a son of understanding. Even a paid custodian or borrower might then be exempt according to all opinions if negligence alone does not create liability. If, however, the parties expressly stipulated that the ordinary laws of custodians would apply to this promissory note, the debtor was unquestionably solvent and certain to pay in cash, and the agent was a shopkeeper in whose hands the collected money would have remained for some time, it still appears that before actual collection the agent is not a borrower of the money. Perhaps, though, these circumstances make him a paid custodian, analogous to the rule that a borrower whose period of borrowing has ended becomes a paid custodian. All of this requires careful study.
