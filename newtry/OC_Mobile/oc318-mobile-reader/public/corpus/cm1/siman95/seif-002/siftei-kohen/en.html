He must swear concerning them. In my view, the primary ruling is that he does not swear concerning them, because produce attached to the ground has the law of land. Since many opinions are involved and these rules affect numerous areas of law, I must discuss the matter at some length. Raavad, the Maggid Mishneh, and Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en understood Rambam to rule like Rabbi Meir in the Mishna in Shevuot 42b, and the Tur apparently understood him the same way. They did not correctly interpret Rambam. The Maggid Mishneh's alternative suggestion, that Rambam rules like the Sages but understands their dispute to concern produce that still requires the ground, is also untenable. The Ran attributes that explanation to Rambam, and it also appears to be the view of the Ritva in the chapter Na'arah and of Shiltei Gibborim, but Rambam's own words plainly show that grapes ready to be harvested no longer require the ground. His ruling in chapter 1 of Hilchot Mechirah, law 17, is explicit, and the Gemara itself says that Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute grapes ready to be harvested. Sanhedrin 15a and Gittin 39a say that Rabbi Meir spoke only of grapes whose continued attachment causes deterioration, implying that they do not require the ground at all. The Ran writes that they presently require the ground and deteriorate only later, but this is strained and does not accord with the plain Gemara or Rashi. Tosafot at the beginning of Ketubot 51a and Ramban in Sefer HaMilchamot at the beginning of Bava Metzia, cited below in siman 115, seif katan 18, likewise disagree with the Ran and Ritva on this point. The authorities overlooked Rambam's Commentary to the Mishna in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim. Rambam writes there that Rabbi Meir and the Sages dispute grapes ready to be harvested, and that the law follows the Sages. He then adds that when such produce is delivered for safekeeping, the law of custodians treats it as land, but for sale, overreaching, and partial admission where the principal claim does not arise from custody, it is treated as movable property, because anything ready to be harvested is deemed harvested. Rambam cautions the reader to preserve this distinction. Bartenura states the same, following the approach of his teacher, Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, cited by the Tur here. Baal HaIttur, folio 67a, and Rabbenu Yerucham, netiv 30, end of part 2, also cite Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's distinction that produce is treated as land specifically when entrusted for safekeeping. Rambam's wording in chapter 1 of Hilchot Mechirah, copied by the Mechaber below at the beginning of siman 193, and in chapter 2 of Hilchot To'en, copied here, is precise according to this interpretation. It is especially clear in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut, where Rambam says that one who delivers an attached item for safekeeping, even grapes ready to be harvested, has delivered land for purposes of custodians. The Mechaber rules accordingly below in siman 301, seif 5. This refutes the Sema at the beginning of siman 193, who viewed Rambam's and the Mechaber's rulings as contradictory and did not grasp their intended distinction. Beit Yosef here and Kesef Mishneh in chapter 1 of Hilchot Mechirah likewise understand Rambam to follow Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, although the opening of Kesef Mishneh's discussion, suggesting that the dispute concerns produce requiring the ground, is incorrect. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi was apparently led to distinguish custody from other laws by the passage at the beginning of Ketubot 51a. There, after an instruction to give the woman dates fit for matting, the Gemara says that anything ready to be cut is deemed cut. The Bach instead writes that Rabbi Yosef HaLevi was troubled by a beehive and a baker's board, but that is not persuasive, because the rule that an item ready to be harvested is deemed harvested does not apply to those objects. Tosafot in many places, the Mordechai in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, the Semag on folio 182a, and Hagahot Maimoniyot in chapter 5 of Hilchot To'en infer from the chapter Na'arah that Rabbenu Chananel rules like Rabbi Meir. The Tur's citation of Rabbenu Chananel is difficult, as I wrote above in seif katan 7. In truth, Rabbenu Chananel's ruling is not primary. The Gemara does not rule for Rabbi Meir in the very passage where the dispute appears. Rather, it explains the dispute without deciding it, which implies that the ordinary rule applies that the law follows the majority, particularly where the Mishna explicitly contrasts Rabbi Meir with the Sages. Tosafot and their colleagues further cite Bava Kamma 59b, where Rav rules like Rabbi Shimon that one who consumes fully mature produce pays the full value of mature produce, and infer that this follows Rabbi Meir. That is no proof. There the produce had already been detached by the damage, and valuation together with the land follows different rules, as will be explained below from Baal HaMaor and Rashba. Indeed, the passage supports the opposite conclusion. It says that Rav adjudicated like Rabbi Meir but issued a general ruling like Rabbi Shimon. Rashi explains that “adjudicated” means that Rav acted that way in a particular case, whereas “issued a ruling” means that he taught his students that the law follows Rabbi Shimon. Had Rav accepted Rabbi Meir's general principle that anything ready to be harvested is deemed harvested for the law of oaths, he should likewise have taught it as law. The implication is that Rabbi Shimon's rule in Bava Kamma is accepted, but Rabbi Meir's view in Shevuot is not, because the Mishna states that the Sages disagree. The primary position is therefore that of Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, Rambam, and Bartenura that the law follows the Sages. The Ran expressly writes that the law follows the Sages because the law follows the majority. The Rif and Rosh likewise clearly follow the Sages. Tosafot Yom Tov in the chapter Yesh Nochalin and Maadanei Yom Tov in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim understand the Rosh to follow Rabbi Meir and question the Tur, but their interpretation is incorrect. The Rosh's plain words in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim concern a Torah oath arising from partial admission outside the law of custodians. His statements elsewhere can be reconciled without difficulty. Raavad writes that the Rif and he himself follow the Sages. Rav Hai Gaon states in Mishpetei Shevuot, folio 6b, that fruit ready to be cut or harvested is nevertheless land and does not generate a Torah oath, citing the Sages' rule that everything attached to land is land. Raavan likewise writes in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim that everything attached to land is land and no oath is administered concerning it. Baal HaIttur also appears to hold this way. Nevertheless, the distinction drawn by Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and Rambam between custodians and other laws does not appear correct. It is intrinsically difficult, the Mishna gives no indication of it, and if that distinction were intended the Mishna did not need to frame the dispute specifically through partial admission. The plain Mishna, the Rif, the Rosh, and the other authorities imply that according to the Sages attached produce is land for every Torah oath. Sanhedrin 15a and Gittin 39a, concerning one who consecrated the hair of his slave, also undermine the distinction. The Gemara compares that dispute to Rabbi Meir and the Sages. If the Sages agreed that hair ready to be cut is detached whenever the matter does not concern custody, the comparison to consecration would fail. Although strained distinctions are possible, the plain Gemara indicates that according to the Sages everything attached to land is land for all such laws. Rav Hai Gaon's formulation likewise indicates that attached fruit never generates an oath. The passage in Ketubot should instead be understood as follows. Dates fit for mats are ready to be cut immediately. Since the heirs can cut them whenever they wish, a creditor or widow does not rely upon them remaining available for collection. The Gemara answers that the case concerns dates that still require the tree, which the heirs will not cut, so the creditor and widow do rely upon them. This distinction applies specifically to creditor collection, maintenance, and similar claims where movable property is excluded because the claimant did not rely upon it. The claimant initially had no reliance upon fruit that the heirs could cut before collection. In other Torah laws, however, attached produce remains land. Tosafot at the end of Bava Metzia 100a and at the beginning of Shevuot 43a similarly write that even the Sages agree that for creditor collection produce ready to be harvested is treated as harvested if it no longer requires the tree. Hagahot Ashri states that one does not swear concerning slaves or land, and even grapes ready to be harvested are not deemed harvested for an oath, whereas for a creditor all agree that such grapes are deemed harvested when they no longer require the vine. Raavad explains similarly that one may not infer the law of oaths from creditor collection, just as slaves belonging to orphans are treated as movable property for creditor collection while no oath is administered concerning slaves. He did not explain the underlying reason. In my view, the reason is that a creditor does not rely upon fruit in the heirs' control that is ready to be cut, since they may cut it whenever they wish. The same lack of reliance explains why slaves of orphans are treated as movable property for creditor collection, since a slave can flee. This understanding removes the need for Tosafot and the Rosh to say that liens and acquisition through movable property together with land are only rabbinic, and it avoids treating the verse concerning fortified cities as a mere support. Rashbam in Bava Batra 128a expressly explains that a creditor does not collect such property from orphans because he did not rely upon it. Nimukei Yosef in the chapter HaGozel Kama cites the Raah stating this explicitly and attributes the same reasoning to Rashbam. Raavad's phrase that one does not learn “oaths” from other matters is not limited to oaths. The same rule applies to overreaching, acquisition, theft, and all similar Torah classifications where the reason of reliance is irrelevant. His contrast is with creditor collection, maintenance, a widow's claim, and similar matters. This is why he invokes slaves of orphans. Slaves are land for overreaching, acquisition, and theft, yet are movable property for creditor collection due to the absence of reliance. Baal HaMaor at the beginning of Bava Metzia goes further and rules that a creditor does not collect fruit at all, even attached fruit that still requires the ground. The Maggid Mishneh cites him in chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh and writes that Rashba agrees. They apparently interpret the passage in Ketubot as concerning valuation together with the land or detached fruit. Their position cannot be reconciled with Rabbenu Chananel or Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, because even Rabbi Meir agrees that produce requiring the ground is land for all laws. The necessary conclusion is that creditor collection is different because the creditor did not rely upon fruit the purchaser can detach. The same distinction therefore applies according to the authorities who limit it to produce not requiring the ground. The Tur concludes that Rabbi Yosef HaLevi limits the land classification to custodians, whereas the Ramah holds that it applies not only to oaths but to every area in which the Torah excluded land, including theft, overreaching, and the oath concerning a deposit. The Tur's presentation implies agreement with the Ramah. At the end of siman 192, the Tur likewise writes that produce ready to be harvested appears to remain attached for acquisition and overreaching, because the Sages treat it as attached for an oath. The practical conclusion is therefore that fruit attached to the ground, even when it no longer requires the ground, is treated as land for oaths, overreaching, acquisition, theft, and similar Torah laws. This is the position of Rav Hai Gaon, the Rif, the Rosh, Raavan, Baal HaIttur, Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri in the name of Mahari Chayut, Raavad, Baal HaMaor, Rashba, the Ramah, and the Tur. This is the ruling we follow, contrary to Rambam and the Mechaber. Lechem Mishneh in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sechirut also discusses this issue at length, including the positions of Rambam, Raavad, the Maggid Mishneh, and the Tur. He raises the same objections from Sanhedrin and Gittin against Rabbi Yosef HaLevi and Rambam, and his attempted reconciliations appear strained. His interpretation of Raavad is unnecessarily forced. According to Raavad, attached produce is land for oaths and likewise for consecration according to the Sages. Only creditor collection, maintenance, a widow's claim, and similar matters are excluded for the reason explained above. The Gemara can therefore properly compare the disputes. The Bach cites Baal HaIttur as distinguishing grapes and all produce requiring human action to detach from dates that fall by themselves. According to that citation, grapes ready to be harvested are still land, but dates that fall by themselves are deemed detached. The Bach wonders why the Tur omitted this. There is no difficulty, for the Tur is not required to reproduce every statement of every authority. More importantly, the distinction is not accepted law. Baal HaIttur apparently offered it only to reconcile the date case in Ketubot with grapes ready for harvest in Shevuot. All the major authorities reject a distinction between dates and grapes. Tosafot in many places ask from the date case against the grape case and answer that creditor collection is different. In the name of Rabbenu Chananel they instead say that the law follows Rabbi Meir. The Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Mordechai state the same. Raavad, the Maggid Mishneh, the Ran, Ramban in Sefer HaMilchamot, the Rosh, and the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 21 of Hilchot Malveh all plainly treat dates and grapes alike. There is also no logical basis to distinguish them once the principle is that anything ready to be cut is deemed cut. Baal HaIttur himself writes elsewhere, in Maamar Achrayut, that Shmuel collects even appreciation ready to be carried on one's shoulders, meaning fruit ready to be harvested. He explains from the date case in Ketubot that a creditor does not collect fruit that no longer requires the tree, and cites Rashi's definition of such appreciation as grapes ready to be harvested. The Maggid Mishneh likewise states that Baal HaIttur agrees with Rashi. Thus Baal HaIttur himself equates grapes and dates. His statements would otherwise contradict each other. The Bach evidently overlooked this passage and the other authorities cited above, and his presentation of the distinction as practical law is not precise. Rather, the case is where he claimed the grapes, the defendant admitted some of them, and said that he had harvested and eaten them. They dispute the remaining grapes that are still standing. The claimant says that those too are his, while the defendant denies it. According to Rambam and the Mechaber, who regard such grapes as movable property, the defendant is liable to take a Torah oath. I have already written, however, that the accepted ruling is that they are treated as land, and he is exempt because the denial concerns land. Examine the Ran's wording, which Beit Yosef also cites in seif 5. One must say that the Ran holds that the vines themselves are land according to all opinions. Thus, when the claimant alleges ownership of ten vines, the vines are necessarily land. According to Rabbi Meir, the grapes growing on them are treated as movable property, but the vines themselves remain land. The Ran therefore had to explain at length that the claimant is demanding both movable property and land, and that the defendant is denying both movable property and land. See there and consider it carefully. This is also implied by the Gemara in the chapter Shevuat HaDayanim, Shevuot 43a, where it suggests that the dispute should have been framed regarding bare vines. This implies that vines are land according to all opinions and that the disagreement concerns only the grapes growing upon them.
