Provided that they no longer require the ground. In the Derishah I wrote that the Rambam deliberately added this phrase and did not suffice with saying that they are standing ready to be harvested. He thereby teaches that only when the produce requires the ground no longer at all does he regard it as though it were already harvested. Even in that case, Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rosh disagree with him. See what I wrote in the Perishah to siman 193.
With respect to denial and admission, etc. See the Derishah, where I wrote that it appears evident that the ruling is not limited specifically to these two matters.
That the case is where he claimed the money, etc. See Nimukei Yosef, who presents both opinions. He first explains that the case is one in which all the grapes had already been harvested. The claimant says, “Pay me their value,” and the defendant admits part and is willing to pay that portion while denying the balance. Nevertheless, if the claimant says that he had deposited with the defendant grapes that at the time still required the ground, the claim is treated as a claim concerning real property, and the defendant need not take a Torah oath. Although at the time of litigation the grapes had already been harvested and the demand concerns their monetary value, so that superficially the present claim is for movable property, nevertheless, since the underlying deposit consisted of produce attached to the ground, it is treated as a claim concerning land. Nimukei Yosef brings proof from the discussion that, according to the opinion that one who says “here it is” is exempt from an oath, Scripture’s exclusion of real property is needed for a case in which a person dug pits, ditches, and caves in another’s field. Even though his liability is only to pay the monetary value of the damage, the claim is considered a claim concerning land because it arises from damage to land. He then concludes that the Rashba disagrees with this reasoning and explains the case as one in which the grapes themselves are being claimed, as Rama states here in the second opinion. The Raavad likewise agrees with the Rashba, as the Tur and Rama write at the end of the siman concerning one who dug pits, where they explain that the claimant demands that the pits themselves be filled, which is literally a claim concerning real property. See what I write there further.
In any case of those who were twisted in the mouth, they needed the ground:
Rather, the case is where he claimed the grapes, etc., and he says that he ate them, etc. This means that ten vines stand before us, five still bearing grapes and five already harvested. The claimant says, “All ten vines are mine, and I deposited them with you when all of them bore grapes.” The defendant denies the claimant’s ownership of the five vines that are still bearing fruit and says, “Those were never yours. The other five you did deposit with me, and I harvested their grapes and ate them, and I will pay you the value of those grapes.” The ruling is that if the five vines he denies still require the ground, he is exempt from a Torah oath. Although his admission concerns movable property, his denial concerns something that requires the ground and therefore has the status of real property. One does not take a Torah oath upon a denial concerning real property. See the Perishah.
