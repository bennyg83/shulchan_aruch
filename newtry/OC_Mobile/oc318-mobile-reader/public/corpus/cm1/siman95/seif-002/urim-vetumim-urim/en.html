Provided that they do not need the ground. Sema writes in Derishah that Rabbi Meir holds that even if the produce still needs the ground somewhat, it is treated as though it does not need the ground. The implications of the Gemara in Sanhedrin and Gittin, however, are that the case is where leaving the produce attached would weaken it. If it would improve by remaining attached, it is not treated as harvested according to anyone. It is difficult to say, as Ran suggests, that because ultimately, when fully ripe, it will deteriorate if left attached, it is already considered harvested now even though presently it improves. What difference does the later stage make when it is presently improving? It is therefore clear that Rabbi Meir speaks where it no longer needs the ground at all. See Tumim.
With respect to denial and admission. See Tumim. I clarified that this means a claim such as, 'I lent you ten vines,' and the defendant says, 'Only five,' or 'I sold you ten,' and he says, 'Only five.' That is a true case of partial denial and admission. If, however, the claimant says, 'I delivered ten vines to you for safekeeping,' and the custodian says, 'Only five,' Rambam, whose view is adopted by the Mechaber, holds that they retain the status of attached property and no oath applies. See there at length. I also concluded there that Rosh disagrees and holds the opposite concerning oaths and double payment: since they do not resemble silver or utensils, they are treated like land and no oath applies at all. A possessor may therefore say that he relies upon Rosh and Raavad.
That he claims the money. This means that he says, 'I delivered ten clusters of grapes to you, you harvested and consumed them all,' while the defendant says that only five were delivered and harvested. Although the present claim in beit din concerns money alone, because the obligation arose from land and when it was originally delivered it was attached, it retains the status of land. This is the same principle stated below by the Mechaber in seif 6 regarding a claim for the monetary value of damage to land.
And some disagree, etc. They hold that because the present claim is monetary, it has the status of movable property. The case is therefore where ten vines stand before us, five harvested and five still bearing produce. The claimant demands the remaining produce and payment for what was harvested. The defendant admits that he must pay for the harvested produce but says the produce still attached belongs to him. He has thus admitted movable property and denied land, and is exempt. The same is the view of the dissenting opinion below in seif 6, which requires that the claimant demand that the pits themselves be refilled, because it holds that money arising from land is treated as movable property. See what I wrote in Tumim.
