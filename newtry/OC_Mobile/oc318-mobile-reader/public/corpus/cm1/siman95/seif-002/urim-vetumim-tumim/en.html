Regarding denial and admission. This is Rambam's view, distinguishing between property delivered for safekeeping and other claims. The Beit Yosef, Tosafot Yom Tov, and Shach explain that Rambam follows his teacher Rabbi Yosef HaLevi. In general he accepts the principle that produce attached to the ground but standing ready to be harvested is treated as already harvested. The dispute of Rabbi Meir and the Sages whether such produce is regarded as detached or as land applies, in his view, specifically in the law of bailees. The Sages treat it as land because the produce was delivered while attached and was given for safekeeping in that state, not in order that the bailee detach it. The Tosafot Yom Tov and Shach understand this to mean only that a bailee's special oath concerning negligence or loss treats the produce as land. They therefore struggle with the Mishnah's case in which the claimant says, "I delivered ten vines to you," and the other says, "Only five." That sounds like an ordinary partial admission oath rather than a bailee's loss oath. If so, even the Sages should regard the harvest ready vines as movable property. They leave the Rambam difficult. I do not think this is Rambam's meaning. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's reason is that the vines were not delivered to be detached but to be guarded and returned as they were. The same reason applies when the dispute is about how many vines were entrusted. If the claimant says, "I gave you ten attached vines to guard until their proper time and then return them," and the bailee says he received only five, the disagreement is technically denial and admission, but it still arises from a custodial transfer whose object was to keep the produce attached. According to the Sages it is therefore land. Only where the transaction is not one of safekeeping at all, for example one says, "I lent or sold you ten attached vines for your own acquisition," and the other admits five, were the vines transferred in order ultimately to be detached and consumed by the recipient. There Rambam holds that all agree they are treated as movable property because whatever stands ready to be harvested is considered harvested. This is what Rambam means when he distinguishes "denial and admission": he means an ordinary claim whose essence does not derive from the laws of bailees. His Commentary to the Mishnah says expressly that where the principal claim was not that they were delivered for safekeeping, the attached produce is treated as movable property. The language is somewhat compressed, but the substance is clear. The Mishnah can therefore speak specifically of ten vines delivered for safekeeping, with an admission of five. It teaches a novelty: even though the form is partial admission, because the object was never delivered to be detached, the Sages still treat it as land. There is no need to interpret the Mishnah as a separate negligence oath. This distinction was also necessary to Rambam because elsewhere the halachah plainly treats produce standing ready to be cut as detached, including the law of a daughter's tenth share from an estate. Moreover, according to Rambam's reading of Bava Metzia concerning one who undertakes to guard produce, the Gemara's difficulty is from ordinary planted produce regarded as land, not from sacred sheaves. If the simple rule were always that produce ready to harvest is detached, the Gemara could have established the case as a ripe crop rather than resorting to a kinyan. Rambam therefore understood that for a custodial transfer the Sages' rule applies and the produce remains land. The Lechem Mishneh and Shach challenge Rambam from Sanhedrin and Gittin, where the Gemara discusses hair of a slave standing ready to be cut and connects whether one commits meilah with the dispute of Rabbi Meir and the Sages concerning grapes standing ready to be harvested. If the Sages agree that ripe attached produce is movable except in bailments, what does a slave's hair have to do with their bailment rule? The Gemara can be understood more precisely. It asks how much hair standing to be cut is worth for purposes of classifying the object, and cites the Tannaitic dispute whether meilah applies to a slave's hair. Rashi understands that the inquiry depends on that dispute. This seems odd if the underlying dispute is not accepted as halachah, since Rabbi Meir and the Sages disagree about attached produce and the halachah ordinarily follows the Sages. The point is that Rabbi Meir's position in bailment reveals a broader conception: although at the present moment the owner intends the grapes to remain attached for safekeeping, their ultimate destiny to be detached is enough to classify them as detached. The Sages require that the present intended state also be one of detachment. Slave hair is similar. At this moment it may be advantageous to leave it growing, yet eventually it will certainly be cut. Rabbi Meir's principle would tend to treat it as detached; the Sages' principle would tend to treat it as attached so long as present intention favors leaving it. The Gemara then answers that even Rabbi Meir can distinguish. Grapes that remain attached deteriorate, so an intention to leave them indefinitely is contrary to ordinary practice and is disregarded. Hair improves while it grows, so the owner's present intention to leave it is entirely reasonable, and even Rabbi Meir can treat it as attached. Thus the discussion uses the conceptual difference exposed by the bailment dispute without saying that the Sages regard ripe produce as land in every area of law. This also explains why Rambam in Laws of Meilah rules that there is no meilah in the slave's hair while it remains attached and gives the reason that it improves as it grows. There is another indication from Bava Metzia's case of one claiming a large slave and a large field. The Gemara establishes the movable component as the slave's clothing and the field's sheaves. Why not use the slave's hair standing to be cut, which would be physically attached yet arguably movable and would fit the structure much better? The answer is that the sugya seeks a case that works according to the accepted halachah. Hair that improves while attached is treated as attached even under the final view. The phrase "sheaves of the field" can itself refer to crop standing ready to be harvested, where the relevant dispute concerning oaths follows the Sages because such produce is not sufficiently analogous to silver and vessels for the special Torah oath. This avoids the strained assumption that already harvested sheaves somehow become part of the land. We must next examine the Rosh. The Tur cites him as holding like the Sages that produce attached to the ground is treated as land. Yet in Bava Batra, concerning one who gives his property to his son from today but retains the produce until death, the Rosh rules that ripe attached produce goes to the father's heirs because it is treated as detached. He proves this from the law in Bava Kamma that when an animal eats fully ripe attached fruit, Rabbi Shimon requires payment as for completed fruit, as though detached. The Tur himself rules this way in siman 254. This seems to contradict both the Tur's citation of the Rosh here and the Rosh's own ruling. And the gloss. The Perishah notices the difficulty and says that a creditor is different, as Tosafot distinguish in several places because a creditor does not rely on produce that the debtor can cut at will. The Shach appears to have the same idea in mind. That distinction is correct for creditors, but it cannot explain the case of a father giving property to his son. There is no issue of reliance there: whatever the gift legally includes is transferred, and whatever it does not include is not. Nor can reliance explain damage by an animal eating another's crop. That law depends on the intrinsic classification of the produce, and Rabbi Shimon derives his rule from a verse. Indeed, Tosafot in Sanhedrin and Shevuot say that Rabbenu Chananel proved from the Bava Kamma rule that the halachah does not follow the Sages' attached classification generally. Thus reliance cannot resolve the contradiction. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 59b say that the halachah follows Rabbi Shimon and that this proves whatever stands ready to be cut is considered cut, even though for purposes of oaths there is a dispute in Shevuot. This itself suggests that the oath context is special. The explanation is not necessarily Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's exact bailment rationale. Rather, the Torah's exclusions for oaths, double payment, onaah, and certain acquisitions are formulated not merely as "land is excluded" but by positive comparisons to movable objects such as silver and vessels, or to property that passes from hand to hand. Ripe produce attached to the ground may no longer have the full legal status of land for ordinary property classification, yet it is still physically not movable from hand to hand and is not comparable to silver and vessels. The Sages can therefore exclude it from these laws while agreeing in other contexts that it is treated as detached. This explains the Rosh. Where the legal question directly asks whether the property is land or movable property, such as the father's gift or the animal's damage, ripe produce is treated as detached in accordance with Rabbi Shimon. But regarding oaths, double payment, onaah, and acquisitions whose Scriptural categories demand property resembling ordinary movables, the attached crop remains outside the required class because it is not actually movable at the relevant moment. Thus the Rosh can agree with the Sages here without contradicting his rulings elsewhere. The same distinction explains the Tur's citation of the Baal HaIttur that an item once detached and later affixed is treated like land for these laws, while Rabbenu Chananel treats it as detached. One might ask why the Tur omits the Rosh, who rules in the laws of idolatry that a house, being constructed from detached materials later affixed, has the law of detached property for the prohibition of idolatrous objects. The Rama here cites both views, whereas in Yoreh Deah siman 145 the Mechaber treats such an item as detached. According to what has been explained, there is no contradiction. In the laws of idolatry the Torah directly distinguishes attached objects from movable ones through the verse concerning mountains. A house built from detached materials can therefore retain its detached classification. But for an oath derived through the general and specific formulation comparing the subject to silver and vessels, an object presently fixed to the ground is not comparable to movable silver and vessels, regardless of its prior history. The Rosh can therefore agree that there is no oath upon it. A house, as the Shach notes, is certainly treated as attached for this purpose. Rambam similarly rules in Laws of Impurity of Foods 12 that an item detached and later attached can retain its detached status for the law of susceptibility to impurity. Again, that law asks a different classificatory question and does not undermine the special comparison required by the oath verses. One remaining difficulty concerns meilah, where the Gemara links slave hair to the dispute of Rabbi Meir and the Sages. According to the distinction just given, the Sages agree that ripe attached produce is intrinsically movable for ordinary property law but exclude it from oaths because it does not resemble silver and vessels. Why should that affect meilah? The answer may lie in the source for excluding attached property from meilah. The law is derived by analogy of "sin" from terumah. Tosafot ask why that analogy would not require produce after full processing just as terumah does, and answer that an additional expression of meilah broadens the category. This shows that without the additional inclusion even fully detached produce might have been excluded. Once we know that attached produce is not treated as fully movable in several Torah categories such as oaths, double payment, and onaah, there is no reason to use the broad inclusion to reach produce still attached to the ground. We apply the principle that where an inclusion can be read narrowly or broadly, the narrower reading is preferred. Rabbi Meir, who treats ripe attached produce as fully movable even for the oath comparison to silver and vessels, can extend the meilah inclusion to it. The Sages, who do not, can require complete physical detachment. Thus the Gemara's linkage remains intelligible. For practical law, the Shach is correct that there is a genuine dispute among major authorities. The party in possession can certainly plead kim li in accordance with the majority who follow the Rosh and Raavad rather than Rabbi Yosef HaLevi's special approach. Even if the Shach's arguments do not conclusively defeat Rambam, the matter is unquestionably a substantive doubt in halachah.
