I was uncertain where a claimant alleges that another has occupied his land for years as a robber, demands both the land and the fruits consumed, and produces one witness supporting him. The occupant denies the claim. Does the witness impose a Torah oath regarding the fruits, allowing the land claim then to enter through oath extension? Or is the central dispute ownership of the land, as to which one witness does not impose a Torah oath, so there is no principal oath at all? After consideration, it appears there is no Torah oath. Even according to Raavad, who says monetary compensation for physical damage to land can be treated as ordinary money, that is where the parties dispute the damage itself, such as whether one dug one pit or two. Here the fruits are claimed only because the land allegedly belongs to the claimant. The dispute over fruits is entirely derivative of ownership of the land and is therefore treated as a land claim. Tosafot in Perek Chazakat HaBatim supports this. If the occupant's seller had lived there one day, a purchaser can sometimes be protected by the seller's claim. Even though one witness could testify about occupation, Tosafot says no oath arises because one does not swear on land. Likewise, a protest before one witness is ineffective even though the land necessarily produces fruits. Were the fruit claim independently movable, the land could routinely be attached by oath extension, which the sugyot do not say. The same applies where Reuven challenges Shimon's house and also claims rent that Shimon collected from others. Since the rental income is demanded only because the house allegedly belongs to Reuven, the claim remains a land claim. Likewise if Shimon sold the land and took money from the buyer, and Reuven now says the land was his and therefore the sale proceeds belong to him, the essential dispute is still land ownership. By contrast, if both agree whose land it is and dispute only how many years of fruit were consumed, that is a genuine movable claim. This also answers the Shach's proposed explanation of a claim for the value of a large or small slave or field when the seller can no longer deliver it. If the only dispute is whether the land or slave belonged to the buyer, payment of its value remains derivative of that underlying land or slave right and retains the excluded character, even if the remedy is money. The first interpretation of Maggid Mishneh can be understood similarly. This analysis also resolves the sugya in Perek HaKotev where a clever husband seeks to transform the wife's oath into a Torah oath by paying before witnesses. Until he actually pays the later money, a witness that the ketubah was paid testifies only concerning the land secured document. The movable property she may have seized is merely derivative of that document. Therefore the witness does not directly impose a Torah oath over those movables. He must first restructure the transaction so that the later payment itself becomes an independent oral loan claim. Ramban's discussion of a protest before one witness can be reconciled as well. When the occupant admits he has no ownership in the land and says only, "I entered for the fruits," then the litigation concerns fruits alone and the witness can create the ordinary monetary oath. But where he claims ownership of the land, the fruits remain derivative and the dispute is land.
