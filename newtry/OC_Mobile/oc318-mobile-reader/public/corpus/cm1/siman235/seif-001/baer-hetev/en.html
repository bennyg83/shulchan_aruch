A large amount. If a minor sold more than necessary for his livelihood, the Tur writes that Rav Hai Gaon and Ramban dispute the matter. The Mordechai in the chapter HaNizakin follows the Ramban that once the Sages enacted the allowance they did not distinguish, and even more than needed for livelihood may be sold. He concludes that the same applies to other matters permitted because of a person's livelihood, such as bread baked by non-Jews, as discussed in Yoreh Deah siman 112, or lending to a non-Jew with interest, there in siman 159, which are permitted even beyond the minimum needed for livelihood. Thus far the Sema. See these laws in Responsa R. Moshe Alshich, siman 101; Responsa Mabit, part 1, simanim 191 and 192, and part 2, siman 122; Responsa Rashach, part 3, siman 71; and Be'er Sheva, folio 106a.
By enactment. The Sages also enacted that a gift made by the minor should be valid, because if the recipient had not done something pleasing for him he would not have given the gift. It is therefore included in what is necessary for his livelihood. Sema.
The daughters. As stated in Even HaEzer siman 112. There it is explained that from the enactment of the Geonim onward, even if the estate contains only movable property the same rule applies. This explains the wording here, 'for example, if they sold in the case of a small estate.' One should not object that their sale is valid only with movable property while the Gemara's law of a small estate originally concerned land, because after the enactment the rule applies to movable property too. Ibid.
That he acquired. This is a novelty. One might have thought that the concern that a minor's mind is drawn to money and he may cheapen land for cash applies only to land he inherited from his father or other relatives, for which he did not labor and paid nothing. With land given to him as a gift, people ordinarily give gifts only after the recipient has exerted himself to please them in many ways. All the more so where he purchased it himself, or his guardian purchased it for him with money, since we see that he preferred the land to the money. Nevertheless, the Sages ruled that his sale is invalid because dealings in land require greater sharpness and expertise. Ibid.
The guardian. It appears that the text specifies acquisition through a guardian because then the purchase is complete and even the minor cannot retract. If the minor bought it himself, although the Rambam holds that it falls under the principle that one may acquire a benefit for a person without his presence, as the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 7, the minor may nevertheless retract if he wishes. In such a case perhaps the acquisition lacks the full status of land for this purpose and resembles movable property, since the seller must return the money if the minor chooses to rescind. Ibid.
A person dangerously ill. One might have thought that because the Sages enacted that the words of a person dangerously ill are treated as written and delivered, so that his mind not become disturbed if his wishes are not fulfilled, the same rationale applies to a minor and his gift of land should be effective. The ruling teaches otherwise. It concludes that the principle of fulfilling the words of the deceased does not apply to a minor. See Responsa Rosh, rule 1, seif 8. Ibid.
Like movable property. This teaches that one should not say that because a debt instrument carries a lien on land and is treated like land for oaths, onaah, and several other laws, it should also be treated like land here. The ruling excludes the Ritva's view that a minor cannot sell a debt instrument because it consists of intangible words, and the rabbinic mechanism of writing and delivery was instituted only for an adult. Thus far the Sema. The Shach writes that a leasehold right in a shop has the law of land. So writes Be'er Sheva, folio 106a. See Responsa R. Moshe Alshich, end of siman 38.
