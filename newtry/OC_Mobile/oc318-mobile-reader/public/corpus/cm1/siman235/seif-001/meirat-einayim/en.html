A minor until six years old, etc. A minor in all areas of law is like a deaf-mute or incompetent person whose acts have no legal effect, as will be explained in this siman in the Tur and Mechaber. A male is called a minor until thirteen years and one day, and a female until twelve years and one day. Therefore, by Torah law their transactions are ineffective even regarding movable property until adulthood. Chazal enacted, however, that once a child reaches the age of pe'utot, his purchases and sales of movable property are valid for his subsistence. Otherwise no one would conduct any business with him, since he could retract whenever he wished and his relatives could likewise void his acts. Because the enactment was only for his subsistence, if he has a guardian and that rationale does not apply, the child's transactions apart from the guardian are ineffective, as will be explained below. The age of pe'utot begins from six years and continues until adulthood, depending on the child's sharpness. From six through ten he must be examined to determine whether he understands commerce. From then until thirteen there is a dispute between Rambam and the Rosh, as I will explain below. If he sells more than needed for his subsistence, the Tur writes that Rav Hai and Ramban disagree. Mordechai in chapter HaNizakin follows Ramban that once the Sages enacted legal capacity they did not distinguish, and the child can sell even beyond his subsistence. Mordechai concludes that the same applies to other leniencies established because of human subsistence, such as bread baked by a non-Jew, as discussed in Yoreh Deah siman 112, or lending to a non-Jew with interest, as discussed in Yoreh Deah siman 159; these are permitted even beyond bare subsistence. Darkei Moshe, seif 3.
His transfer to others is ineffective. The Mechaber carefully writes "his transfer to others," following Rambam. Acquiring land for the minor himself is discussed by Rambam and cited by the Tur and Mechaber below in seif 7, and is included in the rule that one may confer a benefit upon a person outside his presence. This means only that the seller cannot retract. The minor can retract, because one may not impose a liability on a person outside his presence. See there.
From six years until adulthood, if he understands commercial dealings, etc. Rambam's wording implies that even during his thirteenth year, so long as he has not become an adult, he must understand commerce. This is also clear from Rambam's words cited by the Mechaber below in seif 8: "A minor who became an adult and produced two hairs, even if he does not understand commercial dealings..." This proves that before adulthood, commercial understanding is required. Rama therefore should not have inserted here the Rosh's view that after age ten, so long as he is not incompetent, his transaction is valid, as a gloss upon the Mechaber's quotation of Rambam. The Ramah, cited by the Tur, appears to agree with Rambam: he writes that from age ten upward a child is presumed sharp unless shown otherwise, implying that if it is known he is not sharp, even though he is not incompetent, his transaction is invalid. "Sharp" means that he understands commerce, which is Rambam's view.
In the nature of commercial dealings. Aruch under the root "tov" explains that the Jerusalem Targum of "the evil report of the land" uses a term meaning the bad character of the matter, and from the character of relatives one can infer the character of distant persons. Thus "tiv" means the nature or character of a matter. Parenthetical gloss: Rashba writes in a responsum that where a six-year-old minor received a gift through an adult, and the adult did not specify when he should hand it to the child but gave it to him immediately, if the child is not sharp and knowledgeable, the adult was negligent in giving it to him. See Responsa Ran, siman 49, cited in Darkei Moshe, seif 4.
And his gift is valid. The Sages also enacted that his gifts be valid, because if he could not confer a benefit upon someone who pleased him, people would not give him gifts in return. Thus this too is included in the needs of his subsistence. See what I write further below.
Whether a large amount. The amount of the gift can correspond to the magnitude of the benefit the recipient rendered him.
Whether a gift of a dying person. The law of a dying person's gift is explained in siman 250: mere speech can transfer because his words are treated as though written and delivered. The distinctions will be explained there.
In a case of a small estate. This means where the father did not leave enough property to support both daughters and sons until the daughters mature. Such property does not remain subject to ordinary inheritance in full. Beit din sets aside from the estate enough for the daughters' support until maturity, with the remainder going to the sons. If the estate suffices only for the daughters' support, it all goes to the daughters and the sons must seek charity. If the sons nevertheless preceded them and sold the estate, the sale is valid, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 112. There it is also explained that from the enactment of the Geonim onward this rule applies even where the estate consists only of movable property. This explains why the Mechaber can cite here as an example a minor's sale "in a small estate." Do not object that a minor's sale is valid only for movable property while the Gemara's law of a small estate originally concerns land. This is clear.
But with land, etc. Transactions involving land require greater sharpness and commercial expertise than transactions involving movable property.
Meaning, the minor is thirteen years old. See below in seif 9, which distinguishes inherited land and requires specifically that he be twenty years old. See there for further discussion.
Even land given to him as a gift or purchased by him, etc. In the Perishah I explained the novelty. One might have thought the restriction applies only to land inherited from his father or other relatives, because a minor is naturally attracted to cash and may sell inherited land cheaply for money. This reason does not apply as strongly to land received as a gift, because people do not ordinarily give substantial gifts unless the recipient has worked to please them. All the more so it would seem not to apply where he himself, or his guardian, bought the land for money; that very purchase demonstrates that he values land more than cash. Nevertheless, Chazal ruled that even his sale of such land is ineffective, because land transactions require greater sharpness and commercial expertise.
Or that the guardian purchased. In the Derishah I wrote that this wording likely specifies a guardian because then the acquisition is fully binding and even the minor cannot retract. If the minor himself purchased the land, although Rambam holds that it is included in the rule of conferring a benefit outside a person's presence, as the Tur and Mechaber write below in seif 7, the minor can still retract if he wishes, as explained above. In such a case perhaps the property is not treated as a fully settled land acquisition, and may resemble movable property because the seller must return the money whenever the minor chooses to retract.
Even a gift of a dying person. One might have thought that since the Sages enacted that a dying person's words are treated as written and delivered so his mind not become distraught if his instructions might fail, and the same concern can exist where the recipient is a minor, the minor's gift should be effective even regarding land. The ruling teaches otherwise. Ramban concludes that with a minor one cannot invoke the separate principle that it is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. See Responsa Rosh, klal 1, seif 8.
Nevertheless, so long as, etc. Responsa Ramban, siman 2, writes: this matter is obvious and beyond doubt; a minor's sale and gift of land are completely void, even if the minor himself does not retract. Beit din or relatives can remove the purchaser from the land. So long as the minor remains silent and his relatives do not protest, or if he went overseas, the purchaser may not eat the produce. Nevertheless, if the minor wants his gift to stand and because of this permits the recipient to eat the produce, we do not recover from the recipient what he already ate. Even if the minor later retracts and says that he mistakenly thought his gift was valid and therefore allowed him to eat, this is a mistaken waiver of a kind that remains effective. We say similarly concerning one who sells future palm-tree fruit and the purchaser plucks and eats it; we do not recover it because mistaken waiver in a sale can be effective. A minor's waiver is like his gift, and a minor's gift of movable property is valid. All the more so if the minor expressly agreed that the recipient may eat the produce, for if he wished he could have given the produce itself. Even if he retracts after the produce has been harvested, we do not listen, because at the moment of harvesting it became movable property, and his sale or gift of movable property is valid. These are Ramban's words in substance; I have shortened them. His language implies that even if the minor did not expressly tell the purchaser to eat, but the minor and his relatives were present and did not protest, and later wish to recover the produce, they cannot, because such silence constitutes waiver even if mistaken, and even a minor can waive in this context. If so, Rambam's ruling cited by the Mechaber below in seif 14, that one under twenty who sold land can recover even produce already eaten, if it does not disagree with Ramban, must concern a case where the purchaser did not eat the produce in the minor's presence, such as where the minor went overseas, or where the minor or relatives protested and the purchaser ignored them. It does not appear reasonable to distinguish by saying that here Ramban speaks only where the minor expressly ratified what he did and wanted the gift to stand, while Rambam speaks where the minor was present but did not show consent. From ordinary reasoning, whenever the minor and his relatives are present and remain silent, we presume they consent; otherwise they would protest. This is why Ramban says specifically that if the minor went overseas or died, the purchaser may not eat, implying that if he remains present the purchaser may eat.
And the purchaser eats the produce. This is the correct text, with "and the purchaser."
He need not pay for what he took. See what I write below in seif 14.
A promissory note has the status of movable property. The novelty is that although the note includes a lien upon land and is treated like land for certain laws of oaths, ona'ah, and other matters, with respect to a minor's power of disposition it is treated as movable property. This excludes Ritva's view cited by Darkei Moshe, that a minor cannot sell a promissory note because the transfer is intangible and the rabbinic mechanism of writing and delivery was enacted only for an adult.
