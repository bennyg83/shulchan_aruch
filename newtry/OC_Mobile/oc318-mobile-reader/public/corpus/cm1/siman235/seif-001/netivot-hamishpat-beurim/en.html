And the buyer consumes the produce. See Sema, seif katan 14, who explains seif 14, concerning one under twenty who sold land and from whom even the produce is recovered, as speaking where the produce was not consumed in the minor's presence. This is extremely strained. Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber state their rule generally and do not distinguish. More fundamentally, how could one have thought that when a minor sells land, the buyer need not return the produce consumed? This is no better than the cases in Yoreh Deah siman 174 where a sale includes the condition, "When I have money I will return it to you," or, "You and I will divide the produce." Many poskim treat the produce there as fixed ribbis because the sale is ultimately void and the money functions as a loan. Here the minor can cancel whenever he wishes. Indeed, according to Ramban in responsum siman 2, the sale is void from the outset. All the more so, according to those poskim the produce should be ribbis. This is not comparable to one who sells the future produce of a palm, where if the buyer seizes and eats we do not recover it. There the buyer does not later receive back purchase money for land; he bought only the produce and consumed that very subject of the attempted sale. Here, however, the buyer returns the land and receives back the full money he paid for it. Any produce he consumed is therefore profit received because his money was outstanding with the seller, which resembles ribbis. Even according to those who hold in the analogous cases, and in asmachta in siman 183, that the produce is only avak ribbis, the reason is that the transaction resembles a mortgage without deduction. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 64 explain that a mortgage comes into the lender's hands in the form of a sale. None of that applies to a minor, who cannot validly rent or mortgage land in the same way. Rashba, cited by Mishneh LaMelech in chapter 9 of Hilchot Mechirah, writes that the money received by a minor who sold land is only an oral loan. Moreover, even if the produce were only avak ribbis, the poskim's reason for not crediting such produce against principal in an ordinary mortgage is that removing the mortgage without money is considered extracting property from the lender. They apply that specifically to a mortgage, as Shach in Yoreh Deah siman 177 explains. Nor is this comparable to one who sells stolen land, where the buyer can sometimes recover improvement and produce. There the sale is complete as between seller and buyer unless and until the true owner appears. If the robber later acquires title from the original owner, the buyer's purchase can become fully effective. The robber himself cannot retract from the sale. It is therefore not a loan and the produce is not ribbis. Here the minor's sale is no sale at all. Therefore the produce must be deducted. Ramban, who is the source of the rule Rama cites, states this nonreturn rule only concerning a gift, where ribbis is irrelevant. Likewise at the end of siman 246 the rule that produce need not be returned concerns a gift. Therefore the word "buyer" in Rama's gloss appears to be a scribal error and should read "recipient." Alternatively, Rama can refer even to a buyer only where the buyer has not yet paid the purchase money. In that case the buyer might otherwise have to pay the value of produce from his own pocket. Rama can correctly say he need not pay it separately. But once the purchase money was already paid, the produce must certainly be credited against the amount returned to him. On this basis seif 20 is no difficulty. There the purchase money had already been paid, and therefore we recover it from the buyer in the same manner as Bava Metzia 66, where in the case "you and I will divide" the Gemara says to calculate and remove the produce because there was never a valid sale. Even a written debt does not arise; Rashba says it is only an oral loan.
A debt instrument has the law of movable property. Ketzot HaChoshen asks that transfer of a debt instrument requires writing and delivery, while a minor cannot execute a valid deed. In my view there is no difficulty. We say a minor cannot execute a deed specifically where the deed itself requires witnesses as part of its legal form. Rambam similarly says ordinary kinyan sudar is ineffective for a minor because such a kinyan stands for documentary writing and a witnessed document cannot be created for a minor. But a debt instrument transferred through writing does not require a separate witnessed deed of transfer. The minor's own handwriting can be sufficient for the written transfer component, together with delivery. The reason in seif 5, that property is not extracted from a minor, also does not apply because the debt instrument is physically delivered. Rosh's responsum, kelal 85, is precise. He writes that because a debt instrument is like movable property, the witnesses properly signed even though the transferor was a minor. This shows that the minor's own written transfer can have legal effect. Still, Rosh's statement that the witnesses properly signed is difficult. A written evidentiary document against a minor should be subject to "from their mouths and not from their writing." Even when witnesses are not needed to create the underlying transaction, a proof document normally must be written at the instruction of the person being obligated. A minor's instruction has no full legal force. The case must therefore concern one where the minor did not deny the transfer, or where the minor's own handwriting also appeared, so the witnesses' writing was not needed as proof against him. The questioner asked only whether transfer of a debt instrument should be treated like land, and Rosh answered that the witnesses correctly wrote that the instrument was transferred because in law it is movable property. Their writing itself cannot serve as independent proof against the minor. This is especially true according to Razah, who explains a deed's evidentiary power through agency of the obligated party, and there is no ordinary agency for a minor. Ketzot further argues that because the transfer also requires delivery, and Rama in siman 197 requires delivery in the transferor's presence, while a minor is legally like one not present, the acquisition should fail. This is also incorrect. The requirement of presence is not a mystical legal requirement of adult awareness; it exists because delivery must be hand-to-hand or under the transferor's immediate assent. A minor can plainly make physical delivery. Otherwise one would have to say a minor cannot acquire a ship through mesirah, or a large animal according to those who require mesirah. Yet the poskim state broadly that minors can acquire movable property through the ordinary acquisition modes. Similarly, meshichah before the transferor can work without the words "go draw" because presence supplies consent. A minor can also supply that consent for movable-property acquisitions within the rabbinic framework.
