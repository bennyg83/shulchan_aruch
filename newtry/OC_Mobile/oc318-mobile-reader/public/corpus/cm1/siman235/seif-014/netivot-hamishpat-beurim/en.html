Or he planted and sowed. See Sema, seif katan 8, who is uncertain whether the buyer has the law of one who descended into the field with permission. Mahara Sasson, responsum 117, writes that whenever a sale is rescinded because of ona'ah or a defect, the buyer is treated as one who entered with permission. If he built and expended money without increasing value, Mahara Sasson compares the case to siman 103, where an appraisal is later reversed and expenses without improvement are addressed. Still, there may be room for doubt here because the buyer knew the seller was under twenty and that the sale of inherited land was not fully effective. Perhaps he resembles one who entered without permission. However, Mechaber above in siman 232, seif 14, rules that where the buyer improved property he receives the cost of excavation from the seller. This implies compensation even for labor, like one who entered with permission. It is strained to say that the case there necessarily concerned a field naturally standing to be planted. There is no source for making that distinction there.
He can no longer object. This is learned from the law of a minor girl: once she grows up and does not protest, she can no longer perform mi'un. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this because in kiddushin we rule that actual marital relations are required after she becomes adult, based on the principle that a person does not make his relations promiscuous. In my view the proof remains sound. The opinion in Yevamot that, once she grows up without protesting, her continued marriage establishes kiddushin, necessarily understands the lack of protest as evidence that she consented. Otherwise, from where would valid kiddushin arise? Similar reasoning appears in many places cited by Beit Yosef concerning support and in Ketubot, where if she grew up even one hour without protesting she can no longer protest. Why then do we rule in kiddushin that actual relations are required? Because kiddushin itself requires more than mere consent. It requires the husband's operative declaration, "You are betrothed to me." Even a compelling presumption that both sides consent is not enough without the proper kiddushin act and speech. See Chelkat Mechokek in Even HaEzer siman 27, seif katan 9. Even where he gives the money and she makes the declaration, the law can remain doubtful, although in ordinary sale a buyer's act plus the seller's acceptance can clearly complete a transaction. The husband's original words to the minor have no d'oraisa force. When she later grows up, they are like words said outside the effective moment of kiddushin. Therefore actual marital relations are needed because the legal presumption surrounding relations supplies the equivalent of present operative intent. In a sale, however, a compelling subsequent indication of ratification can suffice without a new verbal formula. Therefore once the now-adult minor knowingly allows the sale to continue without objection, he can be treated as having ratified it.
