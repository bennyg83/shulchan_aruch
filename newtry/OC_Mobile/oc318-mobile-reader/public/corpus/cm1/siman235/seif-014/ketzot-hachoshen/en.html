He can no longer protest. Rambam says that even a minor who sold inherited land and, after reaching age twenty, did not protest can lose the right to retract if he took the money and the purchaser used the land in his presence after he reached twenty. His silence shows that he accepted the sale. Maggid Mishneh derives this from the law of a girl whose kiddushin as a minor was subject to refusal. Once she becomes an adult and does not refuse, she can no longer refuse. That proof is difficult if the latter rule is only rabbinic. By Torah law, kiddushin performed during minority does not become full kiddushin merely because she grew and remained silent. Torah kiddushin ordinarily require later intercourse for the sake of marriage, based on the presumption that a person does not engage in promiscuous intercourse. Rambam, however, follows his own distinctive view. He writes that if she remained with her husband after reaching majority, the earlier kiddushin themselves become complete and she becomes a fully married woman without a new act of kiddushin. Maharik and Mahardakh explain Rambam to mean that if he cohabits after she grows and her conduct shows consent, the original money kiddushin becomes effective even though the original money is already consumed, analogous to kiddushin stated to take effect after thirty days. No new witnesses of seclusion are required under this approach. So too here. The minor originally received the purchase money. When, after reaching the age of legal capacity, he knowingly allows the buyer to use the land in his presence, his consent can validate the earlier monetary acquisition. This would imply that if the minor sold only on credit and received no purchase money at the time, later silence should not validate the sale through money. Yet the Shulchan Aruch does not follow Rambam's distinctive marriage theory and generally requires witnesses of later seclusion or intercourse. Maggid Mishneh himself also interprets Rambam's marriage ruling in a way closer to Tosafot. Why then is Rambam's land rule accepted here? The better source is the law of future palm fruits. Where a person purported to sell something not yet in existence, if the buyer later seizes and eats the fruits with the seller's knowledge, beit din does not remove them. The decisors learn from there that even where the original kinyan was ineffective, later knowledgeable acquiescence can validate the buyer's holding. So too, once the minor has reached full legal capacity and the buyer uses the land before him without protest, his conduct shows that he now accepts the sale. Rambam's specific rule still depends on the mode by which land can be acquired. Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch hold that use of fruits can itself constitute hazakah. Therefore the buyer's use in the now adult seller's presence is a fresh valid hazakah. Rama, however, rules in siman 192 that merely eating fruits or placing animals does not constitute land hazakah. According to that view, later use without protest cannot itself complete acquisition. The buyer would need to lock, fence, breach, or perform another recognized act of hazakah in the adult seller's presence. Thus Rama should apply Rambam's rule only where a legally effective hazakah occurred after majority.
