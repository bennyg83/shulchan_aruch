He can encumber them. The source of this rule is Rashba, part 3, siman 121, where the case concerns one under twenty who is already over thirteen. The questioner there was not uncertain whether beit din can collect from inherited property a debt the young adult incurred for his own needs. Why should it not be collectible? Once he reaches twenty, beit din could certainly collect, and there is no reason to distinguish collection shortly before or after twenty where beit din itself performs the appraisal. The concern that money might tempt the young person into an improvident sale does not apply to judicial collection. The real question was whether the lien created by one under twenty is legally valid against later purchasers or later creditors. Suppose he borrowed and purported to encumber inherited land while under twenty, then at twenty immediately sold or mortgaged the same land to another. If the earlier lien was invalid, the second party should have priority, analogous to a loan without a valid after-acquired-property clause where later creditors can prevail, or an earlier oral loan versus a later documented lien. Rashba answers that the earlier lien is valid. The creditor has the status of an earlier secured creditor and can collect from purchasers and later lienholders. Before age thirteen, however, there is certainly no valid lien instrument. A minor cannot create a deed. Where would the real-property lien arise? Accordingly, Rama's gloss, if read as referring simply to "a minor who borrowed," is misleading. If it meant only that free inherited property can be collected for the minor's debt, that is obvious where halachah obligates him to pay, even if the loan was for necessities. And the phrase "he can encumber them" would be inaccurate; it should say "they can be collected." The gloss therefore belongs to the preceding law of one under twenty, meaning one who is already thirteen and has signs. A later glossator cited by Knesset HaGedolah claims that Beit Yosef's version of Rashba is mistaken and that one can collect only inherited movable property, not inherited land. In my view this is plainly wrong. Once the debt is legally payable, inherited land is the debtor's property like any other asset and can be collected through beit din. The same gloss says Rashba's responsum in Toldot Adam concerns a minor under six. That too is impossible. The responsum discusses a minor who wanted to void a compromise because he had a guardian. A child under six has no effective transactions even without a guardian. Thus the correct practical rule is: One over thirteen with signs but under twenty can create a valid lien through his debt instrument even on inherited property. That lien has priority and can be collected from later purchasers or later secured creditors. One under thirteen cannot create such a lien instrument. His obligation, where one exists, has only oral-loan status. But free property in his hands can still be collected through beit din where he is legally liable, such as for money borrowed for commerce or necessities.
