Beit din sells their land to pay their father's debt. The same applies to the minors' own debt, as Rashba writes in responsa, part 2, siman 121. This power is specifically for payment of debt. If the minor himself needs land sold for some personal purpose, beit din cannot simply appoint a guardian to sell it for that purpose. We do not appoint an apotropos for an adult-like minor merely to manage discretionary sales, and beit din itself has no greater power. Chazal gave beit din authority over minors for defined protective functions, not an unrestricted power to sell their property. Beit Yosef cites a responsum of Ritva concerning a minor girl who gave a gift. Ritva writes that if the recipient wishes to protect himself from a future oath, he should have her gift and waiver of movable property made before three people, who should write a beit din record stating that they recognized her as intelligent and fit to make gifts of movable property. I find this very difficult. Any evidentiary deed written by witnesses without the instruction of the person obligated is "from their writing" rather than oral testimony. Surely three witnesses who see a gift, sale, or loan cannot simply transform themselves into beit din and write a judicial record. A group of three is beit din only where halachah itself requires beit din, or where they properly convened and summoned the parties, as explained in simanim 39 and 79. The rule in Bava Batra 113, that three who enter to visit a sick person can if they wish act as judges, is exceptional because the Torah describes that inheritance passage as "a statute of judgment," making the entire section judicial. Tosafot explain that there they can become judges even against the litigants' will. With a minor girl, however, her own instruction cannot create full judicial-document authority. A minor is legally like one not present for that purpose. Who made these three into beit din or into writers of binding evidence? They could testify to a guardian whom beit din properly appointed, because beit din has authority to appoint a guardian. Or they themselves could be appointed as guardians. But if a guardian is appointed, according to Ritva the minor may then be restricted from giving gifts. Accordingly, there is effectively no simple method for one under twenty to sell inherited land merely for personal need. One possible route is first to borrow money for himself and create a valid lien, as explained in seif 15 in the gloss. Beit din can then collect the debt through proper appraisal, because for payment of debt they do have power to sell. Rashba's responsum here contains a gloss quoted by Knesset HaGedolah claiming that Beit Yosef's version is mistaken and that collection can be made only from the father's movable property, not his land. In my view that gloss was written by a mistaken student. If the minor is legally obligated to pay, why should beit din not collect from land inherited from the father? It is the minor's property like any other asset. The gloss further says Rashba's responsum in Toldot Adam, siman 87, concerns a child under six. That is plainly impossible. There the minor sought to void a compromise because he had a guardian. A child under six has no valid transactions even without a guardian. The case must concern a child older than six. Rashba's part 3 responsum concerns one over thirteen but under twenty, for Rashba explicitly says "under twenty" and discusses selling inherited property. A person under thirteen could not sell even his own land. The practical result is that the present gloss concerning a lien refers to one under twenty who is already thirteen and has produced signs. His mortgage or debt instrument can create a valid lien even on inherited property, giving the earlier creditor priority and allowing collection from purchasers or later lienholders once the relevant conditions are met. A child under thirteen, however, cannot create a valid debt instrument. His debt has only oral-loan status. If the property remains free in his hands, beit din can nevertheless collect where halachah imposes payment, such as money borrowed for business or necessities.
