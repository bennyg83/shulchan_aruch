A minor who purchased land. Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 4, proves from Shekalim chapter 1 that a rabbinic acquisition can sometimes produce Torah-level consequences. The Mishnah says that although women, slaves, and minors are not compelled by collateral seizure to contribute the half-shekel, if they contribute, their money is accepted. How can minors validly transfer their coins to the public, when the gift of young children is effective only rabbinically? If Torah law still regarded the money as theirs, the communal offering would contain an individual's private share. It must be that once the Sages established their acquisition, it is effective even for Torah law. This supports Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 28, who rules that kiddushin effected through maamad sheloshtan may be Torah-level kiddushin because hefker beit din hefker, unlike Rabbeinu Yerucham who regards it as rabbinic only. Netivot discusses this proof. Shaar HaMelech had already raised the same question from Shekalim and answered differently. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 8, offers another important distinction. The statement that a rabbinic acquisition does not create Torah ownership applies while the very object acquired rabbinically remains in the buyer's hands. But if that buyer later sells or gives the object to another, the second recipient acquires it on the Torah level. The Sages certainly did not enact a form of ownership that would make every subsequent sale defective. Thus, although the minor's original title may be rabbinic, when the treasurer spends the shekalim and the seller receives the coins, the seller acquires the coins fully; the animals purchased in exchange are then acquired by the Temple treasury on the Torah level. This resolves the Shekalim problem without proving that every rabbinic acquisition is automatically Torah ownership. Beit Ephraim further develops the issue in siman 61. Chatam Sofer, Yoreh Deah siman 314, also discusses whether rabbinic acquisition is effective for Torah law. He concludes that where the Sages merely relaxed Torah law, such as acquisition by despair alone or money acquisition without meshichah where the Sages displaced the Torah mechanism, the rabbinic arrangement does not create Torah ownership for purposes of a Torah prohibition. By contrast, where the Sages affirmatively enacted a legal institution that one is obligated to follow, such as the maintenance rights of daughters in limited estates, the enactment functions with full legal force even for Torah consequences. Regarding maamad sheloshtan and acquisitions on behalf of a minor while the property remains in the original holder's hands, Rashi, Rif, and Ran regard the effect as Torah-level, whereas Tosafot regard it as rabbinic. This underlies the dispute between Shulchan Aruch and Rama concerning whether kiddushin by maamad sheloshtan is Torah-level or only rabbinic.
