Likewise a slave who bought or sold. Certainly when a slave buys or sells, the other contracting party can also retract before the master consents. The slave is no better than an ordinary person who buys on behalf of another, as explained in siman 184. Even if the principal later consents, a completed meshichah can no longer be revived after it ended; only an acquisition such as money, where the legal effect can still attach, may remain possible. The novelty is that even where the transaction is plainly beneficial to the master, such as buying at an exceptionally low price or selling at an exceptionally high price, we do not say automatically that the slave's hand is the master's hand for purposes of imposing the transaction. Maggid Mishneh writes that the rule "the slave's hand is like his master's hand" applies to acquiring benefit for the master, but not to creating a binding liability upon him, even if the transaction appears advantageous. Therefore the master can still reject the act.
