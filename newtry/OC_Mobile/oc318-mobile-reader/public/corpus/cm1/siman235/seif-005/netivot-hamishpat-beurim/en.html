Because we do not remove it from the minor's possession. See Sema, seif katan 23, who explains this as the reason the acquisition is ineffective: the movable property is still in the minor's possession and would have to be taken from him. If the buyer has already performed meshichah, no property need be removed from the minor, and the acquisition can therefore stand. One should not object that if orphans themselves perform meshichah but have not paid, they likewise should fail to acquire because collecting the money would require removing it from them. A minor who has a father is comparable to a minor with a guardian, and if he has no father or is not supported by his father he is comparable to orphans, as explained in Darchei Moshe. Independently, meshichah without payment does not acquire from orphans, as explained above in siman 199:4. However, according to the Sema's explanation, no reason is given why acquisition through a courtyard does not work. Moreover, if the buyer paid in one of the circumstances of siman 198:5 where money itself can acquire, such as where the buyer's house was rented to the orphans by their father, or in the circumstances of siman 199:2 and 3, the Sema's reason that "orphans do not acquire" would still seem to block the acquisition. Yet Gittin 54 explains that although orphans resemble consecrated property, and consecrated property is acquired by money, we do not make the power of consecrated property more stringent than that of an ordinary person. In those special circumstances even an ordinary person acquires by money. The rationale that ordinary property cannot be made more stringent therefore has no application, and there is no source why money should fail for a minor there. Moreover, why should property never be removed from a minor, when in ordinary commerce a minor is treated like an adult in the respects explained in siman 96:3? It therefore appears that Rambam and the Mechaber's reason, "we do not remove it from the minor," refers specifically to the case where the minor rented out the location of the movable property, and that rental was supposed to serve as the acquisition. The Sema explains that the minor lacks power to rent out the place because rental for a day is legally a kind of sale. But in seif 6, where a minor purchases movable property and rents the place from the seller, the acquisition likewise fails because a minor's courtyard does not acquire for him. If the only issue were that a minor cannot rent land, the two directions should be alike: just as he cannot rent out land, he should not be able to rent it in. Acquisition and disposition of land are reciprocal. Rambam's view therefore appears to be that although a minor lacks full capacity to buy or sell the corpus of land, rental also contains a personal obligation. If a landlord destroys the rented house he may be required to provide another house, as explained in siman 312:12, whereas a seller who destroys a sold house need only pay its value and is not obligated to provide a replacement. Likewise, siman 310:2 concerning a rented donkey shows a personal obligation to provide the rented use. Shitah Mekubetzet explicitly writes that an unspecified donkey resembles an unspecified apotiki and a specifically identified donkey resembles a specified apotiki. Thus rental creates a personal obligation tied to the property. An adult can undertake a personal obligation for the benefit of a minor. A minor, however, cannot create such an obligation toward an adult, since we do not enforce a self imposed obligation against a minor. Nor can the minor effectively transfer the body of land for its fruits. Therefore a minor cannot rent property out. But an adult can rent property to a minor, because that is like the adult obligating himself with respect to the fruits of land and encumbering that land for the fruits, which is effective from adult to minor. Accordingly, where a monetary kinyan is one that would work between adults, it can likewise work in favor of a minor.
And one does not make a kinyan from a minor. The principal reason, as the Sema writes in siman 96, is the verse "a man removed his shoe," which excludes a minor. Rambam's reason that kinyan sudar is analogous to a deed is explained below. With an even exchange, however, the law appears to depend upon the dispute in siman 203. According to Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that an even exchange does not require a vessel, that form of exchange is not derived from the verse "a man removed his shoe." Just as a shoe is unnecessary, the requirement of "a man" may likewise be unnecessary. Such an exchange is also no weaker than payment of the entire price, which can acquire even from consecrated property through money or money's worth. Orphans are compared to consecrated property in Gittin 34, and the rule that ordinary persons should not have greater power than consecrated property does not block the transaction where ordinary persons too acquire in that manner. Rambam's reason that kinyan sudar resembles a deed and a minor has no legal deed also does not apply to a true even exchange. Therefore, according to Rabbeinu Tam an even exchange should certainly acquire from a minor. According to the authorities who hold that even an even exchange requires a vessel and is derived from "a man removed his shoe," however, just as the verse requires the relevant object, it also requires an adult man.
