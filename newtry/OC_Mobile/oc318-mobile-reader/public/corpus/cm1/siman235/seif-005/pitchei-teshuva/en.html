And she produced two hairs. Noda BiYehudah, siman 4, discusses an orphan girl who, at exactly twelve years old, entered into a shidduch without her guardian's knowledge. At fourteen she was examined and found to have signs of adulthood. The question was whether the guardians could invalidate the engagement because it was unknown whether she already had signs of maturity when it was made. Rabbi Shmuel argued that her act should stand for two reasons: first, once a girl reaches the relevant age we ordinarily presume that she has developed signs of maturity; second, now that she is demonstrably an adult, one might apply the current presumption retroactively, as in a responsum of Rosh cited in Choshen Mishpat siman 35. Noda BiYehudah rejects both arguments. The presumption that one who has reached the age has developed signs is used only stringently, not leniently. Here, if the girl still wishes to honor the engagement, there is no issue. The actual case is that she now follows her guardians and wants to withdraw. To compel her to pay the half-dowry penalty would extract money from her and is more stringent than applying a presumption to permit a Torah prohibition. As for the Rosh's retroactive presumption, although Shulchan Aruch cites it without dissent, it is not universally accepted, and in any event a presumption of possession is especially strong. Therefore she cannot be compelled to pay the monetary penalty. The cherem attached to the shidduch is different. If she was in fact an adult in age and signs at the time, the cherem may have taken effect, creating a Torah-level doubt. She should therefore be warned that if she betrays the match she should be concerned for the spiritual consequences of the cherem. Beit din, however, cannot punish her for it, particularly because the prospective groom acted improperly in arranging the match without her guardians' knowledge. Although in strict law she could act for herself, his conduct was not equitable, and a compromise is strongly preferable. Noda BiYehudah adds that if it is not clear that the witnesses failed to examine her at the time, one may rely on the presumption that witnesses who are at least somewhat learned would not sign engagement conditions unless they knew the act was done by an adult. In siman 5, after learning that the groom's father was holding part of the bride's money and had issued a substitute written obligation for it, Noda BiYehudah rules that his possession is effective. Although Shach in Tekofo Kohen rules that where a creditor holds a valid document, the debtor cannot seize money owed under that document merely because he has an unrelated doubt of law, that means only that the debtor is not considered the legal possessor and the creditor is not treated as extracting from him. It does not make the creditor an affirmative possessor against every competing claim. In the present case, the dispute over the Rosh's rule turns precisely on monetary possession. Once neither side has an exclusive presumption of possession, the current signs of adulthood support an inference that she was already an adult when the engagement was written. The groom's father may therefore retain the debt in his possession against the penalty claim. Although in Even HaEzer siman 77, seizure for a wife claiming ma'is alai ordinarily works only when she herself seizes and not when her father does, here the groom's father is himself a party in interest. The penalty compensates his own embarrassment as well, and the engagement conditions define the penalty as payable from the defaulting side to the performing side. Since the father undertook the obligations and seeks to perform them, he is himself the performing party. This enables him to retain what he holds, but does not necessarily authorize beit din to remove the substitute note from the bride and tear it up. That final question Noda BiYehudah leaves without a definitive ruling.
