Or one who speaks but does not hear. Beit Yosef asks that the Gemara says both one who hears but does not speak and one who speaks but does not hear are like fully competent persons. He gives two answers. First, perhaps because such a person is not entirely like a competent person, since some examination is required, and because the Mishnah defines "one who speaks but does not hear" as deaf, Rambam can classify him like a full deaf mute for transactions not expressly covered by the Gemara. Second, perhaps the Gemara speaks of one who hears a little, while a person who hears nothing at all is treated like a full deaf mute. Both answers are very difficult. Chagigah 2 explicitly says that whenever Chazal speak of "deaf" together with mentally incompetent and minor, they mean one who neither hears nor speaks. This implies that a person who speaks but does not hear is generally like a competent person. The second answer is likewise difficult. If he can hear when people speak loudly to him, even if he cannot speak, he is already treated as one who hears but does not speak. Rosh's responsum cited by Tur says that whenever "deaf" is listed together with mentally incompetent, it means one who neither speaks nor hears at all. This indicates that if he can speak, even with no hearing, he is like a competent person. Moreover, if complete deafness made him equivalent to a mentally incompetent person, the poskim should have stated the same rule in kiddushin and divorce. Yet they do not. All the more so, such a person could not marry or divorce. In my view Rambam understands the Mishnah in Chagigah more narrowly. Its statement, "the deaf person of whom the Sages spoke everywhere," means specifically wherever they list deaf together with mentally incompetent and minor, as Rashi explains there. In those contexts the person must neither hear nor speak. But where a source mentions "deaf" alone, it can include one who speaks but does not hear, as Gittin says, "one who speaks but does not hear is deaf." The reason land is different is that land transactions require greater commercial acumen. A person over thirteen can be fully adult for kiddushin and divorce and yet, according to several authorities, still cannot sell even his own land unless sufficiently astute. Therefore Chazal could determine that even a person who speaks but does not hear, though legally competent in most areas, lacks sufficient acumen for selling land. For this specific law he can be treated like one lacking capacity. It therefore appears that if such a person needs land sold for his benefit, beit din can appoint a guardian, as with a mentally incompetent person for this limited purpose. Otherwise he might never be able to sell except by first borrowing and then allowing beit din to collect for repayment.
