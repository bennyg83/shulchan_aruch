His relatives came and challenged. In Perek Mi SheMet, Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute how purchasers prove that a deceased seller was already an adult when he sold inherited property. Rabbi Yochanan requires proof by witnesses; Reish Lakish says validation of the deed is enough. Rabbi Yochanan challenges Reish Lakish from the incident where relatives sought to inspect the corpse to determine whether the seller had signs of majority. If validating the deed alone sufficed, why inspect the body? They could simply validate their deed. Ramban notes that the Gemara could have answered that no witnesses were available to validate the document. He therefore infers that the relatives were not admitting anything about the deed and that witnesses to validate it were indeed available. Tosafot similarly says that ordinarily witnesses to validation would be found. This is difficult because Reish Lakish himself explains the Mishnah's rule, where one says the seller was a deathly ill person and the other says he was healthy, as a case where witnesses are not presently available to validate the deed. Why not establish the corpse case the same way? The answer can follow Rosh's responsum. Rosh rules that if we presently inspect someone after he has reached the relevant age and find signs of adulthood, we presume retroactively that he had already become an adult upon reaching the required years. He proves this from our sugya, where Rabbi Akiva says bodily signs can change after death. The implication is that if the person were alive and presently showed adult signs, the inspection would establish prior adulthood. We do not fear that the signs appeared only now, because the present status combines with Rava's presumption that one who has reached the proper years has ordinarily developed signs. Accordingly, if the relatives merely admitted that the deed was authentic but claimed the seller was a minor at the time of sale, their plea must be treated as a definite one, or beit din must advance for them the deceased's possible plea, that he had been a minor, through a migo that the document was forged. But if the inspection only supplies a present presumption of adulthood retroactively, such a migo would still overcome it. Reish Lakish himself accepts the strength of the migo against even the strong presumption that witnesses do not sign a document unless the maker was an adult. Therefore inspection based only on the present bodily presumption would not resolve the case if no validation witnesses existed. The Gemara's question is thus strong only if witnesses were available to validate the document independently. Then the relatives lose the migo and the inspection becomes meaningful. Ramban, however, interprets the case as one where the seller died immediately after the sale. Under that reading, present inspection would directly establish his status at the time of sale rather than merely create a retroactive presumption. Ramban therefore does not accept Rosh's broader retroactive rule, and Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer indeed cites Ramban and Rashba as disagreeing with Rosh. Tosafot can accept Rosh's retroactive principle and still make the Gemara's question work by reading the case, like Ramban, as an immediate death. In such a case the inspection is strong enough to contradict the minor claim directly. Under Rosh's broader approach, however, the need for available witnesses is the better resolution. This explains the different treatments of the sugya.
