Moreover, identifying signs can change. Beit Yosef asks why all three reasons given by the poskim are necessary. In my view each is needed for a different case. If there is a written deed, then even if bodily signs were not subject to change and even if there were no prohibition against degrading the dead, the single reason that witnesses are presumed to have acted properly can suffice. We presume the witnesses would not have written a valid deed for one lacking legal majority. Even if heirs now wish to prove that the deceased had no hairs, that does not help. Before age twenty his sale of inherited land is invalid anyway. After twenty, even if ordinary pubic signs are absent, he can still be legally adult if he has signs of being a saris. The witnesses are presumed to have acted properly and to have seen a sufficient saris sign, such as lack of bodily heat. But if there is no deed and only witnesses who saw the sale but do not know whether he then had signs, and the buyers now wish to inspect the corpse for hair in follicles in order to validate the sale, we need the reason that signs can change. The reason of not degrading the dead does not necessarily protect against buyers, because buyers can say, "We paid money for this inspection," as the Gemara says. Conversely, if the buyers wish to void the sale while the heirs demand the sale money and the heirs want to inspect the corpse for saris or aylonit signs establishing adulthood, then the reason that ordinary signs can change does not resolve the case. We need the prohibition against degrading the dead, because the heirs themselves may not dishonor him for their monetary benefit. If the person is alive and is now found to have hairs, there remains a question whether he also had them at the time of sale. According to Rosh, responsum kelal 33, where we presently see hairs we presume adulthood from the time he reached the relevant years. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 43 writes that Rashba and Ramban disagree.
