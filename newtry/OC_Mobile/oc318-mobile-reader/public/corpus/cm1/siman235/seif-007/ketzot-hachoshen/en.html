A minor who purchased land. Rambam rules that if a minor bought land, paid the purchase money, and made hazakah, the land remains his even though his own sale of land is ineffective. Maggid Mishneh cites the distribution of Eretz Yisrael through the tribal princes as proof that one may acquire for minors in their absence and also cites the case where Rav Bibi transferred a palm to his young son. He notes that some authorities hold that where another person's transferring intent is present, a minor can acquire even by Torah law. At first this proof appears difficult. The tribal princes acquired for the minors through the ordinary law that one can acquire a benefit for another person. It proves that an adult can acquire on behalf of a minor, not that the minor himself can perform an acquisition. Tosafot indeed distinguishes these ideas. A minor can sometimes acquire directly where another person intentionally transfers to him, and that is not necessarily the ordinary law of acquisition on behalf of another. Maharit raised this difficulty and explained Rambam through the separate doctrine of another person's transferring intent. In my view, however, Rambam itself indicates that the two ideas are connected. Rambam says that when a minor purchased land, paid, and made hazakah, the property remains his because "the minor is like one not before us, and one may acquire for a person in his absence." This shows that Rambam understands the power of another person's transferring intent as itself a branch of acquisition on behalf of the minor. Ordinarily a donor cannot simply acquire his own property for the recipient. The recipient must acquire directly or through another person. But where the recipient is a minor, his own physical act can function while the deficiency of legal intent is supplied by the adult transferor. The adult transferor is effectively acquiring for him. This is why a minor does not acquire a found object in the same way, since no transferor's intent exists. The same principle can apply to an adult who physically receives something while not intending to acquire, such as one who mistakenly thinks the object is already his. With ownerless property he does not acquire, but where another person intentionally transfers to him, the transferor's intent can supply the missing acquisition consciousness. A comparable rule exists in an unguarded courtyard. Where another person is transferring the property, the courtyard can acquire because the giver's intent supplies the missing element. Rosh explains that since the property is guarded by the giver's intention, that is enough. Accordingly, Maggid Mishneh's proof from the tribal princes is appropriate within Rambam's system. Since the minor's direct acquisition with another person's transfer is conceptually a form of acquisition on his behalf, a biblical source showing that acquisition on behalf of minors works by Torah law supports Rambam's rule. Rashba appears to understand the doctrine similarly. In Kiddushin he discusses whether acquisition for a minor is biblical or rabbinic, given that acquisition generally operates through agency and minors have no ordinary agency. He then asks from the case of the half slave where one owner transferred his share to his minor son and the court later had to appoint a guardian to induce the minor to free him. If the minor's acquisition were only rabbinic, the father's Torah ownership should have remained and the father could have emancipated him. Rashba's question makes sense only if the minor's direct acquisition where another person transfers is itself governed by the law of acquisition for a minor. This also answers Tosafot's question in Gittin regarding the Mishnah that says one may acquire for a minor but not for an adult. Tosafot asks that the same sugya says a minor himself can acquire. Under Rambam and Rashba, the minor's own acquisition in the presence of another person's transferring intent is still fundamentally the donor's acquisition on behalf of the minor. An adult, who is fully capable of acquiring for himself, does not receive that special mechanism in the same way. Maharit suggested proving from the half slave that a slave is like land for acquisition. But the passage itself shows that for rabbinic acquisition rules a slave can be treated as movable property. The court relied on the enactment validating a young minor's transactions in movables. Tosafot and Rosh explain that slaves are treated as movables for several rabbinic rules, including liens and related enactments. Therefore that passage does not prove that the minor acquired land directly. The result is that Rambam's theory is coherent: a minor can receive a gift by Torah law when another person intentionally transfers it to him because the transferor supplies the acquisition on his behalf. Whether that extends to a purchase, where the minor must also create a valid payment obligation, is the further question discussed in the next section.
Tosafot themselves do not understand another person's transferring intent as the ordinary law of acquisition on behalf of a minor. Therefore Rashba's question against the view that acquisition for a minor is only rabbinic does not arise for them. In the half slave case, the minor himself acquired the transferred share because another person intentionally conveyed it to him, and that direct acquisition can be biblical even if an adult's acquisition on behalf of a minor is only rabbinic. Even according to Rashba and Rambam, however, a separate difficulty arises from the accepted principle that a rabbinic acquisition can produce ownership effective even for Torah consequences through the power of beit din to confiscate property. If the minor acquired only rabbinically, why could the father later emancipate the slave on the theory that Torah ownership remained with him? Once rabbinic law transferred the ownership to the minor, the father should no longer own it even by Torah law for practical purposes. There is proof from Shekalim. The Mishnah says that although women, slaves, and minors are not compelled for the half shekel, if they contribute it is accepted. Bartenura requires that they transfer it completely to the community so that the communal offering not come from an individual's property. If the minor's gift were only rabbinic and had no Torah ownership effect, the communal offering would remain partly the minor's by Torah law. Yet the Mishnah accepts it. This suggests that rabbinic acquisition can indeed establish ownership effective for Torah law, as Beit Yosef writes concerning kiddushin through a three party arrangement. Bartenura interprets the "minors" there as those under twenty, which would avoid this proof. Rambam, however, understands the Mishnah as referring to children under thirteen, and Tosafot Yom Tov notes that the Jerusalem Talmud states this explicitly. According to Rambam, the proof remains strong. There is still a more direct difficulty in Rambam's own rule. Maggid Mishneh writes that in a gift a minor can acquire by Torah law where another person transfers, but in a purchase his acts are ineffective by Torah law because his payment does not count as valid payment. Rambam likewise says that a minor's purchase and sale of movables is effective only by rabbinic enactment. How then can Rambam say that a minor who purchases land, pays, and makes hazakah keeps the land? The enactment validating minors' transactions applies only to movables, not land. If the purchase of movables is only rabbinic because the minor's payment has no Torah force, how can his land purchase be effective? Ramah, cited by Rosh in Ketubot, gives the key principle. A mentally incompetent person cannot acquire by purchase even through another person's acquisition on his behalf, because purchase depends not only on the seller's transferring intent but also upon the buyer's legal capacity to obligate himself in the purchase money. If the buyer cannot become legally liable for the price, the seller never conclusively intends to transfer. A gift is different. Bach therefore correctly reads Tur as disagreeing with Rambam's statement that a minor who purchases land keeps it, following Rosh and Ramah. Beit Yosef's attempt to make Rosh agree is difficult in light of the explicit passage. Rambam can nevertheless be defended by combining the rabbinic enactment regarding minors' movables with the Torah law of acquisition for a minor. Once the Sages validated the minor's disposition of money as a movable transaction, the seller validly acquires the purchase money. At that point the obstacle identified by Ramah disappears. The seller has received legally effective payment, and the minor can then acquire the land through the Torah law of acquisition on behalf of a minor supplied by the seller's intent. Thus the land acquisition itself need not be part of the rabbinic enactment. The rabbinic enactment validates the minor's payment, which is movable property. Once the seller acquires that payment, the seller can confer the land upon the minor through the biblical rule of acquisition on his behalf. This also explains Maggid Mishneh's statement that purchase normally fails by Torah law because the minor's payment is not a valid payment. Before the rabbinic enactment there is indeed no valid purchase. After the enactment validates the money transfer, the remaining land acquisition can operate through the minor's biblical receipt of a benefit. Rambam also rules that where a seller transfers land or movables to an adult purchaser without his knowledge, the purchaser has the choice whether to accept, and the seller cannot retract if the purchaser elects to accept. One might ask why the seller's lack of actual receipt of the price does not create the same problem. The answer is that the seller transfers with the understanding that if the adult purchaser later accepts, the transfer is effective retroactively and the adult will then be fully bound to pay. With a minor, by contrast, until he reaches maturity he remains able to reverse his rabbinic transaction under the relevant rules, and there is no stable adult acceptance to which the seller can look. The enactment validating minors' movable transactions solves that payment problem sufficiently for Rambam's rule regarding land. This approach can also reconcile Ramah. His statement about a mentally incompetent purchaser concerns one for whom there is no rabbinic enactment validating ordinary market transactions. A minor of sufficient understanding has such an enactment for movables, and therefore can supply the necessary payment even though the land itself is outside the enactment. Rama in siman 195 says that acquisition for an absent purchaser works only in a gift, because a purchase may not be an unqualified benefit.
