A minor who bought land. See Sema, seif katan 29, who writes that the minor can retract while the seller cannot. Although the seller sold without explicitly saying so, we can say everyone knows a minor lacks ordinary land-acquisition capacity. The seller therefore sold on the understanding that the minor would retain the option to affirm or retract. If the minor retracts after reaching majority, he must repay the value of all produce he consumed. Ketzot HaChoshen discusses this at length, but I do not understand his objections. He asks how Maggid Mishneh can derive the rule from a case where property was transferred to a minor son, since that case was a gift. He further argues that movable-property transactions are effective for minors only through rabbinic enactment because their payment is not d'oraisa payment; why then should a land purchase become d'oraisa effective? There is no difficulty. Even in movable property, if a seller sells to a minor on terms expressly giving the minor a unilateral right to retract until adulthood while binding the seller, the minor can acquire d'oraisa because there is no greater benefit than such a one-sided advantageous acquisition. This is even more clearly a benefit than an ordinary gift, since although "one who hates gifts shall live," a purchase with a unilateral option is pure benefit. The ordinary reason a minor's purchase of movable property is not d'oraisa effective is that where the transaction is immediately binding on both sides, the minor's payment lacks d'oraisa efficacy and he has insufficient legal understanding. But when the seller transfers on terms preserving the minor's option, another mind consciously transfers a pure benefit. That can work d'oraisa, whether the subject is land or movable property. Maggid Mishneh learns from the gift case that when another mind transfers a pure benefit to a minor, acquisition can be d'oraisa. Once that principle is established, the same applies to a sale structured entirely for the minor's benefit. Ketzot further writes that once Chazal made minors' movable-property transactions effective, their payment should count as valid payment even for land, making a land purchase d'oraisa effective. His implication would be that the same is true all the more in movable property. I do not understand this. The Mishnah and Gemara state explicitly that a minor's purchase and sale are effective in movable property, implying precisely that land remains excluded even from the minor's purchase capacity. Chazal gave efficacy to the minor's payment only for acquisition of movable property, where less commercial sophistication is required. They did not make the same payment effective to acquire land, which demands greater judgment. Ketzot's question from Shekalim is better. Minor contributors' shekalim are accepted only if they transfer them fully to the community. How can minors transfer them completely? From this he proves that hefker beit din works d'oraisa, and uses it to support d'oraisa consequences for ma'amad sheloshtan in kiddushin. I have explained elsewhere that hefker beit din is d'oraisa only in its power to remove property from the former owner. The positive acquisition created by a rabbinic kinyan remains only rabbinic. Get Pashut, siman 130, writes similarly. Therefore, regarding shekalim, once beit din's enactment removes the property from the minors d'oraisa, the community can acquire it d'oraisa through the treasurer's meshichah as agent of the community, exactly like acquiring ownerless property. But where one gives something to a minor through a rabbinic acquisition, although the property can be removed d'oraisa from the transferor through hefker beit din, it does not thereby become the minor's d'oraisa property. The acquisition Chazal instituted remains rabbinic. This resolves Rashba's question concerning one who transfers a slave to a minor. Since a person who declares a slave ownerless still must provide a bill of emancipation, it does not help that hefker beit din removed the slave from the transferor d'oraisa. The slave did not become the minor's d'oraisa property, so the original owner's emancipation can still be effective in the relevant framework. Likewise, ma'amad sheloshtan in kiddushin does not give the woman d'oraisa ownership merely because the money left the husband's ownership d'oraisa through hefker beit din. If her positive acquisition is only rabbinic, the kiddushin is not thereby d'oraisa.
