Fifty is offset against fifty. A practical consequence arises if one of the animals is later lost. For example, Reuven's ox caused one hundred in damage to Shimon's ox, and afterward Shimon's ox caused fifty in damage to Reuven's ox. If Shimon's ox is then lost, one might have thought Reuven loses his claim because an innocuous ox pays only from its body, while Shimon can still recover fifty from Reuven's ox. The Mishnah teaches that the mutual obligations are offset immediately to the extent that they overlap, and only the excess remains payable. Another practical consequence concerns differences in collectibility. Ordinarily, two creditors who owe one another may each collect separately where there is a practical advantage, such as differing qualities of land. Here, however, we offset the liabilities because the injured party is entitled to collect from the tortfeasor's best property, and each person's own property is the best available means of satisfaction for the reciprocal liability. Nimukei Yosef writes this. The first explanation applies specifically to two innocuous oxen, because payment comes from their bodies. The second applies to two forewarned oxen, which pay from the owner's best property, and likewise to two people who injured one another, as explained in siman 421. The Ir Shushan's explanation that the debts are simply offset because there is no point in exchanging money is inadequate. Even where separate collection would create a real advantage for one party, we still offset them for the reasons explained.
Specifically where each ox is worth, etc. Nimukei Yosef writes that the rule of paying half damages on the excess applies only to the amount from which each side could actually collect from the other. If an ox worth two hundred damages an ox worth one hundred by eighty, and then the latter damages the first by one hundred, the first owner theoretically owes forty half damages, while the second owes fifty. But after the second ox caused the one hundred damage, its own value is only twenty. The first owner can collect from it only twenty because an innocuous ox pays only from its body. The first ox, by contrast, still has sufficient value. Therefore we offset only twenty against twenty, the amount each can actually collect from the other, and the second owner then collects twenty more from the first owner's ox as the remaining portion of his forty half damage claim. Thus an owner whose ox caused the greater damage can sometimes recover more than the other party because the collectible bodies differ in value.
For example, if a forewarned ox damaged an innocuous ox by one hundred, etc. The Tur gives the same example. In the Perishah I explained that he could have used a case where the forewarned ox caused only fifty damage. The forewarned owner's twenty five liability would offset the innocuous owner's twenty five half damage liability, and the balance would then be calculated. The Tur chose one hundred merely because he needed that figure for Rashi's interpretation concerning a case where both began the attack together, according to which the innocuous ox can in that special case pay full damages. Some say that all this, etc., through "and the Mechaber who ruled here according to the dissenting view." In the Derishah I argued that there is actually no dispute on the point Rama presents. Everyone agrees that if the animals did not begin together, but the second returned and attacked before its anger subsided, the second is exempt. The Tur cites the Rosh this way without dissent in two places, regarding animals and regarding people. Ri disputes Rashi only concerning Rashi's rule that when an innocuous and forewarned ox begin attacking together, the innocuous ox pays full damages like the forewarned one. Ri holds that even then the innocuous ox pays only half damages. The Mechaber does not mention Rashi's view either here concerning oxen or in siman 421 concerning people. He plainly follows Ri. The distinction between beginning together and responding afterward, which the Rosh records, is stated by the Mechaber in siman 421 and applies equally here. Rama therefore was imprecise in calling that distinction "some say," and from this mistaken assumption he further concluded that the Mechaber had not ruled properly. The two errors depend upon one another.
