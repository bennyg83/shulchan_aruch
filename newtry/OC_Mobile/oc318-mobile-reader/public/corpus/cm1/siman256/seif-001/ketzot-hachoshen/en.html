A shetuki has the law of a convert. Terumat HaDeshen discusses a shetuki with no descendants who made a deathbed gift of his house to his mother. After his death another person took possession of the house as ownerless property, claiming that a shetuki has no known heirs and is like a convert. Terumat HaDeshen ruled that the house should be divided because of the dispute whether a convert can make an effective deathbed gift. I do not understand the premise. Even if the mother is not believed to identify the particular father for inheritance purposes, she is believed to say that she conceived from a Jew because of the presumption that a woman who engages in relations is selective and seeks a permitted partner. Her testimony may not identify which Jew, but it should at least establish that the child was Jewish born and therefore certainly had heirs somewhere in Israel. A Jew whose family line is unknown is not thereby a convert without heirs. One cannot answer that this presumption works only for ritual law and not for money. Where the possessor came into the property only through the claim that it was ownerless, and that claim itself depends on the child having no Jewish father, the presumption of Jewish paternity directly undermines his possession. Majority and presumption can matter where possession itself is doubtful. There is also a procedural difficulty. Terumat HaDeshen says that because neither side was in possession the disputed property should be divided. But the stranger had already taken possession of the house. In land, seizure does not overcome a prior owner's presumption, but here there is no known prior owner after the supposed convert's death. Where no prior title exists, physical possession of land can matter. Rama therefore more precisely states the divided rule where both sides are in possession. The larger difficulty remains that the shetuki should not have the law of a convert at all once his mother is believed at least that the father was Jewish.
