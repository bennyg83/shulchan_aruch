And a shtuki. Ketzot HaChoshen questions this ruling. A shtuki should resemble one who arrives from a distant land and whose family is unknown, whose deathbed gift is certainly valid because he has heirs somewhere. Only a convert with no heirs at all is excluded from inheritance. Responsa Chavot Yair, end of siman 231, raises the same point. Netivot HaMishpat answers that the distinction is clear. Here it is impossible ever to establish the shtuki’s paternal heirs, because even witnesses that his mother committed adultery with a particular man do not suffice for purposes of inheritance. The estate is therefore like property abandoned in complete despair. Shevut Yaakov, part III, siman 175, cites She’erit Yosef, siman 3, who ruled that even one who comes from a distant land has no effective will, and questions that ruling, suggesting that perhaps She’erit Yosef actually meant a convert and intentionally avoided the word “convert.” Shevut Yaakov further inclines that if a shtuki, before death, directed that his movable property be given to his mother and she seized it, it should not be removed from her. Even according to the first answer in Kiddushin 75a, which says that a shtuki who seizes paternal property must return it because he himself does not know who his father is, the mother does know with whom she had relations and knows that the son has paternal heirs. If he made a will in her favor and she seized, the will should therefore stand. This differs from Terumat HaDeshen, who appears to equate a shtuki fully with a convert.
