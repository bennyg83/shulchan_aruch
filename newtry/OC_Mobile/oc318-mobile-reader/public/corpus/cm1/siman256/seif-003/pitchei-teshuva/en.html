We do not heed him, etc. See Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 46, concerning a person on his deathbed who distributed his estate and gave one heir an enlarged share on condition that he raise an orphan in his home, provide him food and drink from his assets, and teach him Torah. Noda BiYehuda questions whether such a will is legally sustainable. If the testator ordered support of an orphan without fixing either a term or a maximum amount, the recipient of the larger share might be obligated indefinitely, even if the total expense eventually exceeded the principal. This could create a concern of prohibited interest, because the recipient never acquires the additional principal absolutely; if he ceases support, the gift fails. It resembles giving another a sum on condition that so long as the recipient pays a fixed annual amount he cannot be required to return the principal, a structure that may constitute interest. Where the arrangement entails an aveirah, there may be grounds to invalidate the will entirely, as in siman 226, seif 3. Nevertheless, several complications remain. The rule that a deathbed instruction involving a prohibited act loses the rabbinic force of a deathbed gift is itself not unanimously established, as indicated by Mishneh LaMelech, Hilkhot Zechiyah chapter 9. Nor is it clear that the arrangement actually constitutes interest. Since this specific question was not submitted and the beneficiary was already in possession, Noda BiYehuda declined to invalidate the will retroactively. He further discusses the beneficiary, now elderly, who wished either to redirect the fund to another mitzvah because he feared his sons would not continue the will, or to place a specified sum with reliable parties under a permissible business arrangement and use the profits to have communal administrators raise an orphan in another household. From the wording quoted in the question, “on condition that he raise an orphan in his house, give him food and drink, and teach him Torah,” it appears that the testator imposed the condition on this beneficiary personally, not upon his heirs. He may voluntarily do some other worthy thing with the money, but throughout his lifetime he must fulfill the stated condition of supporting and educating an orphan. If he wishes to set aside a fixed sum with reliable people and feed the orphan from its profits, that is proper, but whenever the profits are insufficient he must make up the difference from his own funds each year for as long as he lives. If the will expressly imposed this obligation for all generations, then he must fulfill it during his lifetime and his sons and later descendants may choose whether to accept the additional inheritance together with the obligation. If they refuse, the additional share given over the other heirs lapses and reverts to the original testator’s heirs. They cannot be forced after his death to supplement insufficient income from their own property if they decline the extra share. The beneficiary himself likewise retains that choice during his life, although the facts suggested that he was a good and God-fearing man who wished to fulfill the will. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat siman 15, discusses a will that left four hundred gold coins in the hands of the testator’s son Rabbi Moshe, who was to pay twenty-six mint gold coins every year to Torah scholars who would study for the merit of the testator’s soul. The son’s house was to be permanently encumbered for this annual payment, and any purchaser of the house would have to assume the same obligation. After years of compliance the son claimed that four hundred gold coins did not yield twenty-six coins annually, that he had never accepted the money as an iska, and that the arrangement therefore involved interest. Chatam Sofer rejects the claim. Although the wording of the will appears internally inconsistent, siman 42, seif 5, instructs us to interpret a document so as to uphold all its provisions where possible rather than presume retraction. The will can be understood as follows: after transferring the house to the son, the father gave him four hundred gold coins as consideration for selling back to the charitable fund a permanent right in the house to an annual payment of twenty-six coins. The son also undertook the obligation by vow, which he had already performed for years. The four hundred coins were thus the price paid to the son for burdening his house with a fixed annual charge. If he sells the house, he need not separately pay the purchaser the principal or discount the sale, because he cannot sell what he no longer owns. A portion of the house has already been permanently dedicated to fund the annual twenty-six coins, and any purchaser takes subject to that right. Whether the son earns profit from the four hundred coins is therefore irrelevant. This interpretation preserves the later clauses of the document. Though it requires some pressure in reading the earlier wording, which sounds as though the annual twenty-six are the profits of the four hundred, the overall intent likewise supports this reading. The son must therefore fulfill his own vow and his father’s undertaking and pay the Torah scholars the stated amount every year whether or not he earns profit. There is consequently no issue of interest or need for an iska arrangement. See there at length.
