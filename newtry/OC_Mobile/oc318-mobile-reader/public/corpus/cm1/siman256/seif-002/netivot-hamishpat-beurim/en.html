If both are in possession, they divide. See Sema, seif katan 6, who says the same applies if neither is in possession. Sema follows his own interpretation that Rama's dispute concerns whether a convert's shechiv mera words are treated as written and delivered. On that understanding division can make sense even where neither side possesses the property. According to Shach, however, the dispute concerns whether the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased applies to a convert. Then, if neither side possesses the property, beit din certainly does not order division. Even if the mitzvah definitely applied, a sale by the heirs would still be legally effective, because the object remains theirs; they merely have a mitzvah obligation to carry out the decedent's instruction. Where it is uncertain whether that mitzvah obligation exists, beit din cannot compel its performance. This is no stronger than a true debt where both sides plead uncertainty, in which case the possessor is exempt. If the recipient himself is already in possession, however, he can say, "I rely on the authorities who hold that beit din compels fulfillment." The logic of kim li rests on a person's ability to protect his own claimed right where beit din could not otherwise compel the opposing party. Since he is already in possession, he may retain it on that basis. Rama therefore altered the wording of Terumat HaDeshen and limited division to a possession case.
