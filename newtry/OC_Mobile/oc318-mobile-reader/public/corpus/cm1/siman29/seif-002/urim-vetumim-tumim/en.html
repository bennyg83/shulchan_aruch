Witnesses who said, "We testified falsely," etc. Below, siman 38, Tur cites Rabbenu Chananel that if witnesses say, "We testified falsely," not only do they not pay on their own admission, but their original testimony remains valid, because once a witness has testified he cannot retract. This appears to contradict Tur in siman 46, where he follows Rosh that witnesses who admit they testified falsely can be liable for the damage they caused through dina d'garmei. Many therefore understood Rabbenu Chananel and Rosh to disagree. Shach in siman 38 proposes that Rabbenu Chananel speaks only where the witnesses first admitted falsity and were then formally refuted by hazamah. Since their own retraction is ineffective, the testimony remains in force and hazamah can still apply. He therefore explains that they are not liable on their own admission either as zomemim or through dina d'garmei because no actual monetary loss occurred before the hazamah. This is difficult. Rabbenu Chananel says nothing about later hazamah, and the central fact required by Shach is missing from the text. Nor is it much of a novelty that hazamah can still apply to testimony which, by law, remains valid despite the witnesses' attempted retraction. I initially thought to distinguish between testimony that merely creates a future possibility of collection and testimony that immediately destroys an existing right. Where false witnesses testify that Reuven owes Shimon, the harm is not yet certain until collection occurs. Where they falsely testify that a debt was already paid, they immediately extinguish the creditor's lien and can therefore be liable through dina d'garmei. This would fit one responsum of Rosh. But this too is not satisfactory, both because the distinction itself is doubtful and because Tur elsewhere applies Rosh more broadly. The better explanation is based on the Gemara in Makkot concerning the statement that a false witness pays his share. The Gemara asks what novelty this teaches and answers that the witness said, "I testified falsely." It then challenges: once he has testified, he cannot retract. Ramban asks: even if the retraction is ineffective for the original case, why should the witness not be believed against himself and required to pay through dina d'garmei? The answer is that dina d'garmei requires clear proof that his conduct actually caused the loss. Suppose one witness truthfully testifies that Shimon borrowed one hundred from Reuven, while Levi falsely joins him and thereby enables beit din to collect. Levi later admits he lied. Levi can say: "I did not cause you wrongful loss. The other witness still testifies that the underlying debt was genuine." Since one witness supports the existence of the debt, the supposed damage is not established with certainty. The same principle appears above in siman 28. A Jew who improperly testifies alone in a secular court and causes collection can sometimes avoid payment by saying, "I testified truthfully." Unless the injured party proves the underlying debt was not owed, the damage is not sufficiently established for dina d'garmei. So too here. If the second witness still maintains his original testimony, the first witness who admits lying cannot be charged because another witness still supports the substantive debt. If the first witness says that both he and his fellow testified falsely, while the fellow denies this, then, according to the authorities below in siman 410 concerning joint tortfeasors, he may have to bear the entire loss where recovery cannot be had from the other. But that is only where he knows the fellow also lied. If both witnesses admit that they lied and each knows the other lied, then each pays his proper half. But if each admits only his own lie and does not know whether the other actually saw the transaction, then neither can be made to pay. When Levi is sued, he can say: "Yehudah's testimony still stands and shows the debt was genuine. Although Yehudah now says he lied, his retraction is legally ineffective because once he testified he cannot testify differently." Yehudah can say the same based on Levi's standing testimony. Thus the loss is not established against either witness with certainty. This explains the Gemara's singular language, "a false witness pays his share." It describes each witness as a separate case. Each admits only his own falsehood and does not know that his fellow lied. The Gemara then asks: how can payment be imposed when the other witness's testimony still legally stands? Because of the rule that one cannot retract, each witness has another witness supporting the truth of the underlying claim, and the damage is not certain. Accordingly, Rabbenu Chananel's ruling follows directly from the Gemara. Rosh's ruling in siman 46 concerns a different case where the witnesses both know the entire testimony was fabricated, as appears explicitly in the responsum. There the damage is certain, and dina d'garmei applies. There is therefore no contradiction between Rabbenu Chananel and Rosh. The practical rule is that money cannot be extracted through dina d'garmei where the alleged damage remains uncertain and another witness still legally supports the underlying obligation.
