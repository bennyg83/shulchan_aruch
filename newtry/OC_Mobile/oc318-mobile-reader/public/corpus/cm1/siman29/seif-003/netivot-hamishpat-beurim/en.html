And they did not say, 'We do not know.' Taz challenges Sema's distinction between saying, 'I do not remember,' and, 'I do not remember now,' from the case where Rav Ashi initially said he did not know testimony and later remembered and testified. But Beit Yosef itself makes clear that Rav Ashi gave an explanation for his initial response; an adequate explanation permits later testimony, as Rama states. Urim VeTumim further asks from Shevuot, where repeated oaths of testimony inside beit din create liability only once because after the first denial the witnesses cannot return and admit. If saying, 'I do not remember now,' permits later testimony, why not establish multiple liability in that case? He similarly asks how Rama can permit witnesses who said, 'We do not know,' to return with an explanation even after an oath or ban. Rashi's explanation resolves both. The first oath already obligates them to come and testify so long as they remain capable of doing so. A later oath inside beit din is therefore merely an oath upon an existing oath whenever they still can testify. Multiple sacrificial liabilities arise only outside beit din, where each oath is potentially separable because even after an earlier denial outside court they can still come to court and testify. Thus, even where an explanation later permits renewed testimony, or where they originally said only, 'I do not remember now,' repeated in-court oaths remain successive oaths on the same continuing obligation. The Mishnah's statement that they cannot return and admit addresses a complete denial; once that denial is final, later oaths add nothing. Where later testimony remains possible, the first oath continues to bind them and later in-court oaths are superfluous. Rashi's framework therefore answers both objections.
