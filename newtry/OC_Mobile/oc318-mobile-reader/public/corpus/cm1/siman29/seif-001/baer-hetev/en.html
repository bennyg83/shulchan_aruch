After. The Sema writes that within the short period of immediate speech witnesses may retract. Even where two witnesses came together, the first testified and then the second testified, the first may retract within the immediate-speech period following the second witness's testimony, because the two constitute one unit of testimony. If a litigant interrupted a witness and the witness then says that he wished to complete his words but was interrupted, he is believed to complete them, provided he does not entirely contradict his original testimony. So writes the Ran. See Responsa Maharit, siman 75, and Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, part 2, siman 134.
They may not. Hagahot Mordechai writes that witnesses who testified before beit din and then gave contrary testimony are flogged, while their first testimony remains in force. Sema.
To add. The Sema writes that this applies specifically where the witnesses testified orally before beit din, or where their testimony is in a document and their handwriting is recognizable and their signatures can be authenticated by others. Otherwise, their later statement is supported by the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. See the end of siman 46 and the end of siman 82 regarding one witness who says there was a condition and another who says there was none. The Tur writes that according to the Rosh the witnesses are set one against the other and the defendant takes a hesset oath. The Sema's words, however, require study, because they contradict what he writes in siman 46, seif katan 104, where he says that our case concerns only oral testimony, whereas with a document the witnesses are believed to say there was a condition even if their handwriting is recognizable. Here he writes the opposite. There is a further difficulty: his statement that if their handwriting is recognizable they are not believed implies that if it is not recognizable they are believed. Why should that be so, when our case concerns a statement made after the immediate-speech period? In siman 46 he rules that even if their handwriting is not otherwise recognizable, they are believed only when they make the qualification within the immediate-speech period. This requires study.
And some say. The Shach writes that as a matter of practical law the Mechaber's view appears primary. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise writes that the leading authorities agreed that witnesses are believed only where their handwriting cannot be authenticated from elsewhere, but if it can be authenticated they are not believed. Nimukei Yosef states the same, and Ritva writes so in the name of Ri and other commentators and agrees with them. The Shach adds that in his view the same result applies even where the handwriting cannot be authenticated elsewhere, for our case necessarily concerns a statement made after the immediate-speech period. If the witnesses first said, "This is our handwriting," and only later, after that period, said, "There was a condition," they are not believed. See Giddulei Terumah, gate 21, part 3, and Responsa Maharshal, part 1, siman 12. Rivash writes in responsum 476 that where one witness says there was an oral condition and other witnesses testify that all the matters were written in the document, but admit that not everything discussed orally was included in the document, this is not treated as a contradiction. They may have understood that the oral matters did not constitute a legally significant condition. Beit Yosef cites a similar ruling from Rashba.
