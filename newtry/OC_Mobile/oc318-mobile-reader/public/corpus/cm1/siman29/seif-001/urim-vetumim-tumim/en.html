After the witness testified in beit din, etc. See Urim, where I cited Sema's statement that Tur and Rambam hold that so long as the examination of the witness has not been completed, he can retract his testimony. Beit Yosef writes that this is also learned from the Yerushalmi and Tosefta. I am surprised that he does not mention Tosafot, who clearly disagree. Tosafot in Pesachim 11 ask concerning two witnesses where one says the event occurred on the second of the month and the other says the third. The Gemara reconciles them by saying one knew the preceding month had been intercalated and the other did not. Tosafot ask: perhaps the reason their testimony remains valid is that they also agreed on the day of the week. They answer that without the principle that we follow the majority's ordinary lack of knowledge concerning intercalation, the two statements about the date would contradict each other. When they later align their testimony regarding the weekday, that would amount to retracting from their earlier contradictory testimony. Tosafot make the same point in Sanhedrin and elsewhere. Likewise, in Pesachim 12 they ask why witnesses must be questioned about the weekday at all. Once they already stated the date of the month, a later incompatible weekday answer would be a forbidden retraction. But the date of the month and the weekday are both among the seven formal interrogations listed in the Mishnah. According to Tur and Rambam, until all interrogations are complete the witness should still be able to retract and correct his testimony. Tosafot's entire argument would therefore fail. It follows that Tosafot do not accept Tur and Rambam's rule. The matter requires study. As for Tosafot's underlying question, according to Rambam and Tur the Gemara's proof can still be defended. If we did not follow the ordinary majority assumption that one witness erred about the calendar, then when one says the second of the month and one the third, their testimony would be contradictory. Even if their later weekday statements show which one was mistaken, the original date interrogation was not properly answered. The Torah requires seven interrogations. If the date response is effectively treated as an incorrect and unusable answer, that interrogation is missing. Saying later that the weekday matches does not transform the prior contradictory date answers into a proper completed interrogation. But once we follow the majority assumption that people commonly err regarding intercalation, the original answers "second" and "third" are themselves treated as compatible. The date interrogation was therefore properly completed from the outset. Thus the Gemara can still infer the majority principle even though witnesses may retract during the broader interrogation period. The Tosafot difficulty is therefore answerable under Rambam and Tur.
Within the time required for a short utterance, etc. See what I wrote in Urim. There is stronger reason to say that the first witness may retract until the second witness completes his testimony than to say that after the second witness has already finished, either can still retract merely because the short retraction period has not expired. The testimony of two witnesses is one evidentiary unit. So long as the second witness has not completed his statement, the combined testimony itself is incomplete. It is therefore as though the first witness's testimony has not yet reached full completion. By contrast, once the second witness finishes, the entire testimony is complete. There is then less reason to allow retraction merely because a few moments remain within the ordinary short period. This resolves Tosafot's question in Shevuot 32. The Mishnah teaches that the second witness can retract his denial within the short period. Tosafot ask: is that not obvious? Otherwise the second witness could never become liable for suppressing testimony, because the first witness had already denied before him. The only reason the second denial matters is that the first still has time to retract. According to what I have explained, the answer is clear. We already know that the first witness can retract until the second finishes, because until then the joint testimony is incomplete. That is enough for the second witness's denial to be legally meaningful. But we would not necessarily know that after the second witness has completely finished, he can still retract simply because he remains within the brief interval after his own statement. The Mishnah teaches this additional point. It therefore contains a genuine novelty.
But in a matter where there is clear proof, etc. Bedek HaBayit and Shitah Mekubetzet cite Ritva concerning the Gemara's statement that a person does not ordinarily bring a liability upon himself. Ritva asks why that reasoning is needed. Witnesses can retract within the short retraction period anyway. He answers that ordinary retraction within that period applies when the witnesses retract on their own. Here they changed their statement after the litigant intimidated or challenged them by saying, "Do you think I am a fraud?" In such a case we would suspect that fear caused the retraction unless there were an independent logical proof that a person would not deliberately impose liability upon himself. From Ritva one could derive two consequences. First, where witnesses came pursuant to a public proclamation, ban, or other pressure, and then retract, perhaps even within the ordinary short period their retraction is not accepted if there is reason to think fear or pressure caused it. Second, Ritva appears to understand the Gemara's incident as occurring within the short retraction period. The special reasoning that one does not bring liability upon himself would then justify accepting the retraction specifically within that period. After the short period has expired, that reasoning alone might not be enough to undo already completed testimony. However, Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber state the rule broadly and do not sound as though they accept Ritva's limitations. I think they disagree with his explanation. There is also a factual difficulty in Ritva's reading. The Gemara's language suggests that two witnesses originally described the person as a fraud. If the first witness testified that way in the litigant's presence, why did the litigant remain silent until after both witnesses spoke? If he immediately protested, "Am I really a fraud?" and the first witness retracted, why would the second witness then repeat the same accusation that had already been challenged and withdrawn? The more natural reading is that the testimony was initially received outside the litigant's presence, perhaps because he waived that procedural right where the testimony was for his own benefit. Both witnesses independently testified that he was a fraud. Later, when the litigant came before beit din and was told what they had said, he confronted the witnesses: "Am I really a fraud?" They then explained that they meant he was not a fraud, and Chazal accepted the clarification because a person would not ordinarily bring a liability upon himself. On this reading, a substantial period could already have passed since the original testimony. Nevertheless the strong internal proof justified accepting their clarification. There is no need to assume the event occurred within the short retraction period. Ritva's original question therefore falls away. Accordingly, the halachah need not follow his derived restrictions. Within the ordinary short period witnesses can generally retract. And where there is a compelling internal demonstration that the original words cannot reasonably have meant what they appeared to mean, even a later clarification can sometimes be accepted. Ritva's narrower rule therefore requires study.
And they did not say, "We do not know." But if witnesses expressly said, "We do not know," they cannot ordinarily return and say, "We do know." Ran asks from the Mishnah concerning the oath of testimony. The claimant says, "I adjure you if you know testimony for me," after the witnesses had said, "We do not know testimony for you." They answer amen and become liable. But once they already said, "We do not know," their testimony should be fixed under the rule that one who has testified cannot later testify differently. At the moment of the oath they should no longer have been capable of testifying, and there should be no liability for the oath of testimony. Ran therefore must explain that the oath followed within the short retraction period, while the witnesses still retained the ability to change their statement. One might ask that the latter clause of the Mishnah already teaches that where two witnesses deny successively, the second can be liable because the first can still retract within the short period. Why would the opening clause need to teach the same rule? The answer is that the opening clause teaches only that an ordinary statement, "We do not know," can be retracted within the short period. We might still have thought that once they add an oath, "We swear we do not know testimony for you," the solemnity of the oath makes the statement immediately final, just as certain severe legal acts such as kiddushin and divorce are not governed by the ordinary retraction interval. An oath is extremely severe, and the world trembled at the giving of the commandment against false oaths. The latter clause therefore teaches that even after an oath, the ordinary short retraction rule can still apply. This resolves the structure of the Mishnah. Sema cites a responsum of Rosh and distinguishes between saying, "We do not remember," and saying, "We do not remember at present." According to Sema, only the latter allows the witness later to say, "Now I remember." Taz objects from the Gemara's incident where R. Elazar initially said he did not remember the testimony for Rav Kahana and later remembered and testified. But Hagahot HaRosh already answers that the initial statement was made outside beit din, in private discussion between Rav Kahana and R. Elazar. The rule that one cannot retract completed testimony does not apply outside court. Still, Taz is correct that Sema's supposed quotation from Rosh is inaccurate. In the actual responsum, a witness was asked whether he knew how many years a certain person had lived in a house. He answered that at the relevant time he had never paid attention to how long the person had lived there. Rosh says such a witness cannot later say, "I have now thought about it and I know how many years he lived there," because he already admitted that he had no knowledge of the duration at the time. That is different from saying, "I do not remember now." A person who once knew and has forgotten can later remember. Thus the true distinction is not between the words "I do not remember" and "I do not remember now." It is between one who says, "I never knew or paid attention to this fact," and one who says, "I knew it but presently do not remember." The latter can later say, "I remembered." This creates a question concerning the Mishnah that says witnesses adjured five times in beit din are liable only once because after their first denial they can no longer return and admit. Why not find a case where they said each time, "We swear that we do not remember," and later finally remembered? If saying "I do not remember" remains retractable, they should be capable of becoming liable for each oath. The Gemara itself uses the impossibility of multiple liability in beit din to prove that the oath of testimony can apply outside beit din as well. If repeated statements of lack of present memory remained open to later recollection, the proof would fail. One could say that a witness who says, "I do not remember," can later testify only after enough time passes that genuine recollection is plausible. If he claims to remember moments later, the court does not believe such a sudden change. But the verse could still speak of repeated adjurations separated by meaningful intervals. That answer is therefore strained. Nor can one say that once the witness swears he does not remember, he can never later say he remembered because he should have concentrated before taking the oath. Rashba does say that one who takes a broad factual oath cannot later excuse himself by saying he forgot a fact he should have investigated. But where the oath itself is specifically, "I presently do not remember," and that statement is true at the time, there is no false oath. What was he supposed to investigate if he genuinely could not recall? Therefore the better answer is that a statement "I do not remember" does not create liability for the oath of testimony at all. The Torah obligates the witness for a definite denial: "I did not see," "I did not know," or "I know no testimony for you." Such a statement, according to its own content, excludes any future testimony. But "I do not remember" is not a complete denial. The witness is effectively saying, "At present I cannot testify, but perhaps later I will remember." That is like saying, "I will not testify for you until a year or two from now." It is not the absolute suppression of testimony for which the Torah created the oath-offering. Since he remains capable of returning later and saying, "I remembered," his present statement is not a complete denial and does not generate the offering. This resolves the Mishnah and the Gemara. It also explains why Mordechai cites the rule that after witnesses deny repeatedly in beit din they cannot later testify. His proof assumes a true denial of knowledge, not a statement of present lack of memory. The distinction is therefore sound.
Even if they said, "We do not know," etc. This requires study. Rama's placement and wording imply that the rule refers back to the case where a communal ban was proclaimed and the witnesses answered that they knew nothing, after which they offer an explanation and wish to testify. That cannot be accepted so simply. Beit Yosef derives his rule from the Mishnah where witnesses were adjured five times in beit din and denied. If a prior denial always made later testimony impossible, the Mishnah could have taught more simply that even where they first denied without an oath and were only afterward adjured, there would be no liability for the oath of testimony because their testimony had already become fixed. Beit Yosef infers that if they can provide a valid explanation for the original denial, they can still testify. But if so, why do witnesses who were repeatedly adjured not simply provide an explanation and regain the ability to testify? The answer must be that once an oath has been involved, an excuse is not ordinarily accepted. A person should have been careful before taking such a severe oath. Thus Beit Yosef's rule applies to an ordinary denial, not to one reinforced by an oath. A communal ban has the force and severity of an oath. If witnesses were under such a ban, denied knowledge, and thereby violated the ban, why should an excuse later allow them to change their story? The same concern applies as with an oath. There is another difficulty in Beit Yosef's proof itself. Rashi explains the Mishnah of five adjurations in beit din as a case where the witnesses were adjured five times and only afterward denied, not where they denied between each oath. Rashi writes that if silence were equivalent to admission, they would already be unable to testify; if silence is not admission, the repeated oaths can all precede one eventual denial. According to that reading, the Mishnah teaches a substantial novelty even without any rule about excuses. It is therefore unnecessary to infer that a prior ordinary denial with a later excuse remains retractable. Beit Yosef appears to follow Rambam's version of the Mishnah, whose wording implies denial between the adjurations. His inference then has more force. But according to Rashi there is no such proof. Therefore one should at minimum distinguish the case of a communal ban from an ordinary denial. Where the witnesses merely said, "We do not know," without oath or ban, a valid explanation can sometimes allow later testimony according to Beit Yosef's reasoning. But where they denied while bound by a ban comparable to an oath, the excuse should not ordinarily be accepted. This may also explain why Rashi preferred to interpret the Mishnah through silence rather than through repeated explicit denials between the oaths. He may have wished to avoid the difficulty created by an oath-backed denial. Accordingly, Rama's wording should not be read as permitting a later excuse after violation of a communal ban in all circumstances. The stricter interpretation is the better practical rule.
