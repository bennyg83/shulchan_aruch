Some say that he may add that a condition existed. The Tur writes this in the name of the Rosh, but I do not know the Tur’s source. The Beit Yosef says that the Rosh quotes the Gemara without qualification and does not distinguish, but this is not proof, for in many places the Rosh quotes the Gemara without explanation while adopting Rashi’s interpretation. The Rama’s statement that some say the witnesses may add that there was a condition is therefore also difficult. It appears that he intended the Ran in the name of Rav Hai Gaon, as Darkei Moshe explicitly says that the Ran writes like the Rosh. Yet it may be that the Ran disagrees only concerning written testimony, not oral testimony, as the Sema below, siman 46, seif katan 103, and the Bach explicitly distinguish. As a matter of practical law, although the distinction between written and oral testimony is not compelled, the principal ruling follows Maimonides and Rashi. The Ramban rules similarly in responsa. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that the leading authorities agree that witnesses are believed to say a condition existed only when their signatures cannot be verified from another source; if their handwriting is independently verified, they are not believed. The Nimukei Yosef states this explicitly in the chapter Mi Shemet concerning a document that said the grantor was ill and confined to bed. Ritva in the second chapter of Ketubot likewise writes this in the name of Ri and the other commentators and agrees with them. The Beit Yosef below, siman 46, also cites a responsum of Ritva to this effect. It appears to me that the same applies where their handwriting cannot be verified elsewhere, because the present case necessarily concerns a statement made after the time required for immediate speech, as the Rama writes. Thus, if they first say, “This is our handwriting,” and only later say, “Our statement was conditional,” they are not believed. Rashi says this explicitly in Ketubot 19b. The law is also explicit below, siman 46, seif 37, that even when their handwriting cannot be verified elsewhere they are believed only within the time of immediate speech. See what I write there and below, siman 82, seif 12. A work entitled Gedolei Terumah has now been printed. In gate 21, part 3, it cites Maharash Teitatzak in the responsa of Maharashach, part 1, siman 12, who asks how the Tur derived this from the Rosh and offers answers. Those answers are not convincing, as Gedolei Terumah himself refutes them. Gedolei Terumah proposes that the Rosh proves, concerning a trust document, that the case is where the signatures cannot be verified elsewhere because the Gemara bases the lack of credibility on the fact that the statement was not meant to be written. Had the signatures been independently verified, the Gemara would not have needed that rationale. He asks why the Rosh did not instead prove it from the Gemara’s question whether a condition resembles a trust document, since in a trust document itself the witnesses are believed when their signatures cannot be verified elsewhere. In my opinion this is no proof. The Rosh could not argue that way because perhaps the condition case concerns even independently verified signatures. Only after the Rosh establishes that the trust-document case concerns signatures not verified elsewhere may one infer that the condition case does as well. Moreover, the Rosh adduces a stronger proof from the trust-document case itself. In conclusion, nothing in the Rosh proves that he disagrees with Maimonides. Rabbeinu Yerucham’s unqualified statement that the leading authorities agreed, without mentioning any dissent by his teacher the Rosh, strongly supports this conclusion.
