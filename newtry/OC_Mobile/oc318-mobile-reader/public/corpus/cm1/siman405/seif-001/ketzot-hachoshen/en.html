An ox that injured a person. Rambam distinguishes payment for a free person from payment for a slave. An innocuous ox that unintentionally kills a person is exempt from execution and from kofer. Rambam also exempts it from ordinary damages for the free person's death, while if it unintentionally kills a slave he requires half the slave's value from the body of the ox. Raavad objects that the Talmud recognizes ordinary monetary value even for a free person. If such damages are unavailable for a free person, why should they exist for a slave? I think Rambam can agree that a free person's life can have monetary value in the abstract. The obstacle is procedural. Kofer and death-value become payable only after death. Half-damages of an innocuous ox are a fine, and a fine is not inherited before it is judicially established. Since the free person's heirs receive the claim only by inheritance after death, they cannot inherit an unadjudicated fine. A slave is different. The slave is the master's purchased property, like his ox or donkey. The master's property interest exists before death, so half-value can be assessed against the innocuous ox. This explains why a forewarned ox can generate ordinary monetary damages for a free person, as in the Talmudic passage, while an innocuous ox cannot. The forewarned ox's liability is compensatory rather than a fine. A remaining difficulty arises from the derivations of Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Akiva concerning "the owner of the ox shall be clean." Tosafot's explanation that ordinary damages are not explicitly written in that verse can answer it: the special exclusion concerns kofer and the thirty-shekel slave payment, not necessarily every theoretical value claim. The precise interaction of those derivations therefore requires care.
