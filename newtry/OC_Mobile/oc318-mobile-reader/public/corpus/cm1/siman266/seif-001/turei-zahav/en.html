(Seif 5, “Do not return it.”) Maggid Mishneh explains that the father told him to engage in interest-bearing business, to bring him fledglings, or the like. The son should return the lost article and not obey him. Rambam states in chapter 11 of Hilchot Gezelah: “If he obeyed his father, then while fulfilling the positive commandment of honoring his father he nullified the positive commandment of returning the lost article and violated the prohibition, ‘You may not hide yourself.’” It appears from Rambam’s language that this applies specifically where the obligation to return the lost article arose at the time he received his father’s instruction. Rambam therefore writes, “if he obeyed his father,” meaning that the father presently needs him and tells him not to return it. This is also the Gemara’s wording: “If his father said, ‘Do not return it,’ he should not listen.” By contrast, if he was already engaged in the mitzvah of honoring his father and then a lost article came before him, he is exempt under the principle that one engaged in a mitzvah is exempt from another mitzvah. In that case the father’s instruction neither adds nor detracts. The obligation of returning the lost article does not rest upon him at all. We find similarly that a guardian of a lost article is treated as a paid guardian because he benefits by being exempt from giving bread to a poor person while occupied with the lost article, as stated in Nedarim 33. One cannot distinguish the present case on the ground that returning a lost article includes both a positive commandment and a prohibition. Giving charity likewise involves a positive commandment, “you shall surely give,” and several other positive commandments, as well as the prohibition, “Beware lest there be a base thought,” which the final chapter of Bava Kamma applies to charity. Nevertheless, returning the lost article supersedes the immediate obligation of charity while one is occupied with it. Nor can one say that returning a lost article contains both a positive commandment and a prohibition, whereas honoring a father contains only a positive commandment. Rif, in Elu Metziot, when comparing which mitzvah supersedes another, counts only the positive commandment and not the prohibition, as is evident in the case of a lost article in a cemetery. The prohibition therefore is not the point. Indeed, there is no true displacement here at all. The exemption rests on the separate rule that one occupied with a mitzvah is exempt from another. The same applies here. Nevertheless, if the father is present, he is obligated to waive his service and allow his son to return the lost article, or to return it himself, for the Gemara derives from “I am Hashem your God” that both father and son are obligated in God’s honor. Even so, it appears to me that the son himself has no obligation to return the article while already occupied with honoring his father. One cannot argue that because the father is obligated in return, the son cannot claim exemption as one occupied with a mitzvah. If so, regarding charity in the case of a guardian of a lost article, we could likewise say that the owner of the lost article himself is obligated to give bread to the poor person. Evidently, that is not relevant. This appears correct.
