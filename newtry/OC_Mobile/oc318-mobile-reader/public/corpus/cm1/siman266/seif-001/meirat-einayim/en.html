A lost object belonging to a non-Jew is permitted. The Tur writes that not only before it reaches his hand is he free of any obligation to trouble himself to return it, but even if it comes into his hand it is permitted for him to retain. This is derived from the phrase "every lost thing of your brother that you find." "That you find" implies that it already came into his hand, and the Torah nevertheless limits the obligation to "your brother."
Because he strengthens the hands of transgressors. Rashi gives another reason: by returning it he appears to show that the mitzvah of returning lost property is not important to him as a command of his Creator, since he returns the property even of one regarding whom he was not commanded. In the Perishah I cited the Gemara's derivation from the verse "to add the watered to the thirsty; God will not be willing to forgive him." Rashi explains that the non-Jews, who are sated and do not thirst for service of their Creator, are called "the watered," while Israel, who thirst for service, are called "the thirsty." The person thereby equates returning a non-Jew's lost property with returning a Jew's. Rambam appears to follow the Targum's explanation, that a person adds unwitting sins to deliberate sins. Here too, this Jew who returns the lost property of a non-Jew views his act as innocent, yet he commits a transgression by strengthening transgressors, thereby adding this unwitting offense to his earlier deliberate sins. Where there is chillul Hashem. The Beit Yosef explains this as a case where the object was found in a place with a Jewish majority, so that the non-Jew would not assume it simply fell from him but would suspect that Jews present there stole it from him.
They bring in their utensils like the utensils of Jews, etc. The Tur writes, "In every place they bring their utensils inside to protect them from thieves together with Jewish utensils, because of peaceful relations." In the Perishah I explained that the Torah prohibited only the return of an actual lost object, which fell from a non-Jew without his knowledge and whose location and finder he does not know. If instead he intentionally left his tools or movable property in his courtyard or similar place, not expecting them to be lost, and a Jew learns that thieves are coming and will steal them, the article does not yet have the legal status of a lost object. The Jew may therefore move it to a guarded place because of peaceful relations. Rambam in the large printing omits "from thieves," though the Kesef Mishneh printing includes it, and the Yerushalmi in Gittin likewise does not use those words. Even so, their expression "bring in" itself indicates this meaning, for otherwise they would have said "return." The Tur adds "from thieves" as clarification. If not, why mention concern for thieves specifically? Even ordinary people might take an unattended item and justify themselves by assuming the owner despaired. Moreover, why would the Tur use this special formulation here more than elsewhere in the laws of lost property? The explanation given is therefore clear. For this reason the Tur says it is permitted "in every place," meaning even where there is neither chillul Hashem nor kiddush Hashem. Although with Jewish property too simanim 260 and 262 explain that an item deliberately placed in a designated location need not be returned, that applies only while it remains guarded there. Here thieves later arrive. The Tur sometimes says "together with Jewish utensils" and sometimes omits it. I reconciled the variants in the Derishah. The general rule in the Tur's view is that wherever the act is genuinely for peaceful relations, it is permitted.
