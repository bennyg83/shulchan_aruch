One who had a window in his wall and his fellow came and made a courtyard, etc. The Mechaber adopts the Rambam's language at the beginning of chapter 7 of Hilchot Shechenim. The phrase "and his fellow came and made a courtyard beside it" appears to mean that the window existed before the neighboring courtyard. This raises several difficulties. What was there originally? If it was ownerless land and the first person acquired the benefit of light by opening his window toward ownerless space, why does the ruling conclude, "because he acquired this nuisance right"? At the time he opened the window there was no nuisance to anyone. It should have said that he acquired from ownerless property. Rama's gloss also says, "all this where one builds a window over a courtyard," not over ownerless land. Moreover, if this were a special rule about a window opened toward ownerless land, the Rambam should have stated the more general law that anyone who has lawfully acquired a window right, whether toward ownerless land or toward an already existing courtyard, is protected according to the applicable rules. The Beit Yosef and Mechaber themselves understand the Rambam to recognize acquisition of rights even against visual intrusion. The Tur's formulation also says, "Reuven who acquired a right to a window opened toward Shimon's courtyard," which clearly assumes that the courtyard already existed. Nor can the Rambam mean a window opened toward the neighbor's ruin, because the Mechaber rules at the end of seif 16 that opening toward a ruin creates no prescriptive right. Still less can it mean a window opened toward the public domain, because Rama's gloss would not fit and the phrase "his fellow came and made a courtyard beside it" suggests construction immediately beside the window, while one cannot simply build a private courtyard or wall within the public way. It therefore appears that the phrase "and his fellow came and made a courtyard" is not literal. The ruling also includes a case where the neighboring courtyard already existed when the window was opened and the window owner later acquired a valid prescriptive right over it. The structure of the Mechaber's words is therefore as follows. A person had a window in his wall, meaning a window lawfully existing under any circumstance that gives it a valid right. His neighbor then comes to challenge it. If the challenge is based upon the fact that the neighbor now uses the adjoining courtyard and says, "Your window causes me visual harm, close it," he cannot compel closure, because the window owner has already established the relevant right. If instead the dispute concerns the neighbor's wish to build opposite the window, he must maintain the required distance so as not to darken it. This reading fits the concluding rationale and the rest of the siman.
