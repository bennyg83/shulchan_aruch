Rosh rules that because the owner of the tiled roof could not initially prevent the neighbor from opening a window over it where there was no present visual harm, the window acquires no presumption. Rivash challenges this reasoning. He argues that Rosh has reversed cause and effect. The more natural Talmudic structure is that the existence of a presumption determines whether one can protest, not that inability to protest determines whether a presumption can arise. Even if the roof owner could not initially stop the opening, he should be able to protest at the end of each three year period so the window user does not acquire a permanent right. If he fails to protest, perhaps the window should gain a presumption, and once it does, the owner can no longer build opposite it without leaving the required space. Rivash supports this from the opening of Perek Lo Yachpor. Rava says one may not place a potential nuisance close to the boundary even before the object that would be harmed exists. The Mishnah's various distancing rules imply that although there is no present harm, one may still have to refrain because later removal would create endless litigation. The person cannot simply say, "I will place it now and remove it when you build." Thus, says Rivash, a present absence of damage does not necessarily mean no right of protest exists. At first glance Rivash's point resembles the Gemara concerning land occupied only for fruits. Rav Ashi asks: if the possessor says, "I descended only for fruits," what was the owner supposed to do? Rav Kahana answers that he should have protested. Otherwise the same would be true of Sura mortgages: if the mortgage document were hidden, the mortgagee could later claim purchase. The Sages would not create an arrangement that could automatically cause loss; therefore the owner must protest. This seems to support Rivash that even where the user presently has permission, the owner may need to protest against future acquisition. After further consideration, however, Rosh's position is correct. Where it is definitively known that the user has a present right, no presumption arises at all and the owner need not protest. The Mishnah concerning a husband says there is no presumption in his wife's property, and the Gemara asks, "Obviously, since he has fruits he eats the fruits." Rashbam explains that everyone knows he entered for fruits, so his use cannot prove purchase. This differs from a mortgage, where over time people may forget that the entry was only as a mortgage, and therefore protest is required. So too here. If the law itself gives the neighbor an unquestioned right to open the window, his use is openly referable to that legal right. There is no adverse possession to protest. This is also why an artisan has no presumption in property that naturally came to him as an artisan: his possession is presumptively attributable to the permitted relationship, so no protest is necessary. Ramban himself in his commentary to Perek Lo Yachpor says similarly concerning nuisances placed before the injured object exists. Since the owner cannot presently protest, no presumption is created; when the injured object later appears, the owner may then protest. This supports Rosh. Ramban does write elsewhere concerning windows between brothers who divided that if one brother leaves the other's windows open after division, perhaps he should protest expressly lest a presumption arise. That appears to support Rivash. Yet this can be limited. Brothers who divide do not have an unavoidable legal right to those windows. Their continued existence rests on an implied understanding of the division. Had the brothers expressly agreed otherwise, the windows could have been removed. Therefore, after division, the user may later claim that he actually bought the window right outright, and a protest may be necessary. A tiled roof is different. There the window user has the right as a matter of law because forcing him to close an opening that presently harms no one would be Sodom like conduct. His permission does not arise from an ambiguous agreement that might later be characterized as a sale. His use is therefore like a husband's fruit right, an objectively known permission that creates no presumption at all. Rivash also relies on Rava's rule that one may not place a future nuisance in advance. Rosh answers that the cases in Perek Lo Yachpor involve things that are burdensome to remove or, in the case of a pit, already weaken the neighbor's soil. Because removal later would be difficult, the neighbor can object now. Opening a window over a tiled roof is not comparable. If the roof owner later builds, the window can be closed or built against without comparable burden. Thus the source does not undermine Rosh.
Rosh holds that if a dividing wall once stood between two properties and later fell, the window owner must close his windows. Responsa of the Geonim permitted the windows to remain because they were originally opened lawfully. The dispute parallels the early authorities in Perek Lo Yachpor concerning a tree planted near another's field where a rock layer originally separated the roots and later the rock layer disappeared. Raavad understands the case as allowing the tree to remain because it was planted permissibly when the rock layer existed. Ramban disagrees: if the only reason the planting was allowed was the existing rock layer, once that protection disappears the tree owner must remove the tree. The same applies here. According to the Geonic approach, once the windows were opened lawfully while the dividing wall protected privacy, their original lawful status allows them to remain even after the wall falls. According to Rosh and Ramban, the permission depended entirely on the existence of the protecting wall. Once the wall disappears, the windows must be closed. The present law follows that latter conception.
Rosh was asked about Reuven's house facing Shimon's house. Reuven already had windows opposite Shimon's house. Shimon later sold his house to Reuven. Levi owned a garden behind the house Reuven bought from Shimon, and that house had windows overlooking Levi's garden. Reuven's original windows did not overlook Levi because Shimon's house blocked the view. After the intervening house fell, Reuven's original windows now looked into Levi's garden. Rosh ruled for Reuven on this point. Levi had already been exposed to visual intrusion from the windows of Shimon's house and could not force them closed. Therefore the collapse of that house did not create a new type of privacy harm. Levi already had to conduct private activity with awareness that people in Shimon's house could see him. Furthermore, if Reuven wished, he could rebuild the house he had bought from Shimon and reopen the same windows. From Levi's perspective, there is no meaningful difference between being seen through the old windows of the purchased house and being seen through Reuven's original windows. This reasoning applies specifically because Reuven himself owned the intervening house. He could lawfully rebuild it and restore the very condition under which Levi was already exposed to observation. If the intervening house belonged to someone else, the result would differ. The fact that the third party owner could theoretically rebuild does not authorize Reuven to harm Levi now. So long as the house remains down, Levi can use his garden privately. Reuven cannot create visual intrusion merely because another person might later rebuild. The ability to restore the prior condition must be in Reuven's own hands.
