The Rosh's opinion is that one may open a window. Rivash, cited by Beit Yosef, explains the Rosh's position by referring to the dispute of R. Zeira and R. Ilai in Bava Batra 59 concerning an Egyptian window. His intent appears to be as follows. If their dispute concerned a Tyrian window, and everyone agreed that even above four cubits one may object, then R. Zeira, who holds that above four cubits there is no privacy damage, would nevertheless allow an objection. How then could the Rosh rule here that where there is no privacy damage one cannot object, apparently even to a Tyrian window? Rivash therefore understands the dispute as concerning an Egyptian window. This is very difficult. The Rosh in his rulings explicitly states how he understands the dispute, and Maharam Alashkar already notes the problem. Tur likewise follows both the Rosh's rulings there and the Rosh's responsum here. It therefore appears that the Rosh's responsum concerns specifically an Egyptian window. With a Tyrian window, which is permanent, one may certainly object and chazakah can arise even where there is no privacy damage, as seen from R. Zeira. He holds that above four cubits there is no privacy damage, yet still allows objection and recognizes chazakah in a Tyrian window because it is a permanent structure. This also follows from Ramban cited by Mechaber at the end of this seif. Ramban in his novellae establishes the Mishnah in a place where there is no privacy damage and nevertheless says that a permanent window can create chazakah and be objected to. Necessarily the present discussion concerns an Egyptian window, which is not permanent, and whose objection and chazakah arise only from privacy damage. The Rosh therefore rules that where there is no privacy damage, one cannot object and no chazakah arises. This also explains the Rosh's words concerning Shmuel's statement that a window made for light creates chazakah even if very small. The Rosh says it is reasonable that this applies even above four cubits. At first glance this is obvious, because according to the Rosh the dispute already concerns an Egyptian window and below four cubits even an ordinary Egyptian window creates chazakah. Shmuel's novelty must therefore concern above four cubits. According to what I have explained, however, an ordinary Egyptian window has chazakah only because of privacy damage. Where there is no privacy damage, there is no chazakah according to the Rosh. A window made for light, however, is permanent like a Tyrian window and always creates chazakah, even above four cubits. Ramban, cited by Mechaber, can likewise be explained consistently. Although Ramban in his novellae establishes the Mishnah where there is no privacy damage and nevertheless recognizes chazakah, that is because Ramban regards a window below four cubits as inherently permanent. Yet Ramban also writes at the beginning of Lo Yachpor that where one places a potentially damaging use before the vulnerable use exists, even though the future owner can object, no chazakah arises. According to our approach, since an Egyptian window is not permanent and the only issue is privacy damage, even if the future owner could object, the use may still fail to create chazakah. Ketzot HaChoshen questions Rivash's position that chazakah can arise merely because the owner had the ability to protest even when he could not legally prevent the act. Ketzot compares this to a husband, who cannot establish chazakah in his wife's property because it is known that he has the right to its fruits. Here too, it is known that he may open the window despite the neighbor's wishes. In my view there is a distinction. Chazakah is based on consuming another person's fruits. A husband with fruit rights is not consuming the fruits of another person, but his own. Therefore there is no chazakah. Here, however, the window owner benefits from the neighbor's airspace. That is a form of consuming another person's benefit. Since it is not known whether he enjoyed that benefit temporarily as a legally tolerated use or whether he acquired it permanently by sale, the neighbor must protest if he wishes to preserve his right.

In that event Reuven would be believed and would not have to close it, as explained below in seif 18, where if it is unknown which came first the possessor is believed. Why then should the courtyard owner be unable to protest now in order to prevent that future claim? The ruling must be understood as speaking where the present facts can later be established, or where the legal rules of proof will otherwise prevent the window owner from converting this tolerated opening into a stronger right merely by the passage of time.

But one who opens toward the public domain. See Sema, seif katan 38. Rashba and Beit Yosef discuss a case where the public domain is less than four cubits wide, and Rashba gives the reason that the person does not use any of his neighbor's property. At first glance this is very difficult. If the public domain is only one or two cubits wide, then the window necessarily also uses two cubits of the neighbor's airspace, because a window for light requires four cubits. If Rashba meant otherwise, then by setting his wall back one cubit inside his own property a person should be able to open a window toward his neighbor's ruin. Yet Rashba treats a ruin like a courtyard. Why should one be permitted to adjoin a window to a ruin more readily than to an existing courtyard? It therefore appears that Rashba's case concerns two houses that originally stood on opposite sides of a public domain, as is clear from the responsum. Reuven originally had windows and Shimon did not. Had Shimon wished to make a window in his wall opposite Reuven's window, Reuven could have prevented him because the one causing the damage must distance himself. Reuven's house then fell. We treat the ruin as though the house still stood, because an ordinary ruin is expected to be rebuilt. Shimon can nevertheless say: "Now that your house has fallen and I am not presently harming you, I may acquire use of the public airspace. The public air is ownerless. I was previously forbidden to make the window only so that I would not harm you. Now that I do not harm you, I may open the window." This resembles the rule above in seif 14 concerning a cattle shed where, if no storehouse presently exists, he may place the cattle shed there. Shimon is not claiming any use of Reuven's private airspace. He says: "I use only the air above the public domain. Keep your own airspace and build your wall if you wish. Indeed, now that my window came first, you may no longer make a window in your rebuilt wall that would damage me." Reuven may, however, rebuild a solid wall that darkens Shimon's window, because Shimon cannot acquire ownership of the air over Reuven's courtyard without Reuven's consent. If instead there had originally been a courtyard beside the public domain, Shimon may not open a window toward it merely because the courtyard later became a ruin. A ruin is normally rebuilt as the former courtyard, and once rebuilt the window would create privacy damage. Shimon would then have to close it. Although he does receive some public airspace from the two cubits of the public domain, he also takes some benefit from the neighbor's airspace. Reuven can say, "I do not wish to have to litigate with you later over the extent of your right." It is possible, however, that where Shimon can honestly say, "I do not intend to take any of your airspace. I use only the public air," he need not close the window until the courtyard is actually rebuilt. The stronger rule that one must close immediately may apply only where the present use necessarily includes private airspace. Shimon can say, "I benefit from the public domain alone. Build your own airspace if you wish. I should not lose the public air merely because you do not presently wish to build." Once the courtyard is rebuilt in a way that creates privacy damage, however, he certainly must close the window.
