But one who opens toward the public domain, etc., does not acquire a prescriptive right. Meaning that the neighbor living on the opposite side may later build upward from his own wall and darken the first person's window if the public way between them is less than four cubits wide. This is so even though, according to the Rosh, that opposite neighbor may not open a window directly facing the first window once the first person opened first, as stated above in seif 14. The distinction is explained in a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 155, and because it is a general principle governing many laws in simanim 154 and 155, I will summarize it. A person ordinarily cannot, through his own act and without consent, impose a servitude upon another person's land. Yet sometimes an earlier use does have the practical effect of restricting a later neighboring use. The difference is this. If a person is causing direct harm to his neighbor or physically uses the neighbor's property, the neighbor may say: You have no right to use my land or to injure me through your direct force. This includes projections, gutters, and similar intrusions. But when a person does nothing harmful and uses only his own property, he may do whatever he wishes there. Thus, if one co owner of a building first makes a storage room in his upper story, the lower owner may thereafter be unable to open a bakery or cattle stall underneath, because that later activity would damage the storage. The upper owner did not acquire the lower property by servitude; he merely used his own property harmlessly, and the lower owner may not afterward injure him. Conversely, if the lower bakery or stall came first, the upper owner cannot prevent it merely because he now wishes to make a storage room, because the lower owner is acting within his own property and was not presently harming him when the use began. One might therefore ask: if a person first opens a window above four cubits overlooking his neighbor's courtyard, where visual intrusion is not at issue, why does he not similarly gain priority and require the neighbor later to distance any building four cubits so as not to darken him? The answer is that the window takes and uses the airspace of the neighbor's courtyard. He cannot appropriate that use without consent. This differs from the storage room, which uses nothing belonging to the lower owner. From this it follows that one who opens a window toward a public domain is indeed diligent and benefits. The neighbor across the way cannot prevent the opening, because the first person is not opening onto the neighbor's property. The road belongs to the public, and anyone may open onto it. If the opposite neighbor later wishes to open a window directly facing the first, the first may object because the second would then cause him visual harm, just as the upper storage owner may prevent a later harmful use below. But if the opposite owner merely wishes to raise his own wall, he may do so even if it darkens the earlier window, despite the narrow width between them. He may say: To the extent your window makes use of the airspace of my property, you had no right to appropriate that use without my consent. Had the first window been opened directly over the second person's private property in a situation where the second could have protested, and the second failed to protest for three years with an accompanying claim, a true servitude might have arisen and the builder would then need to distance four cubits. But where the window opens onto the public domain, the opposite owner had no right to protest at the time of opening, and therefore no prescriptive right can arise from his silence. Consequently, even if the window owner now advances a claim of purchase, no prescriptive right exists, and the opposite owner may raise his wall, provided he raises it enough to prevent looking into the first person's premises. This is the Rashba's principle.
