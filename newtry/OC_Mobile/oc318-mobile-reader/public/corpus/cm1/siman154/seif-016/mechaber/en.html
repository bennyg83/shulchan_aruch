The Rosh holds that if a person opens a window toward his neighbor's property and it presently causes no invasion of privacy, the neighbor cannot prevent him by claiming that today or tomorrow he may build there and the window will then invade his privacy. Since the window presently causes no harm, the neighbor cannot prevent it; one benefits while the other loses nothing. Because the neighbor cannot prevent it, the window acquires no established right. When the neighbor later builds, he may compel the window's owner to close it so that it will not invade his privacy, and he may build directly opposite the window without leaving any space. Based on these principles, the Rosh ruled in the following case. Reuven extended his upper story outward and opened a window in it. Shimon, his neighbor opposite, later decided to extend his own upper story and Reuven claimed that Shimon must distance his building from the window so as not to darken it. The law follows Shimon. Since local custom permits projections and balconies into the public domain, it is like a jointly owned courtyard. Reuven improperly opened his window toward the airspace where Shimon's upper story could be extended. Until then, Shimon did not object because the window caused him no harm and he could not prevent it. Reuven therefore established no right, and Shimon may build opposite the window. For the same reason, the Rosh ruled that if Reuven seeks to open a window facing Shimon's ruin, Shimon cannot prevent him because it presently causes no harm. Since Shimon cannot protest, the window establishes no right. Thus, a window already open toward a ruin establishes no right unless its owner brings witnesses that he possessed it before the site became a ruin. A person whose window opens toward his neighbor's property and that property becomes ruined should arrange for witnesses to attest that he possessed the window before the ruin and should preserve their written testimony as proof. For the same reason, the Rosh ruled that a window may be opened toward roofs covered with tiles and not used. If the neighbor later opens his roof for use and the window then overlooks his courtyard, he may compel the window's owner to close it. Therefore, if a window opens into a neighbor's courtyard from a high place, and the neighbor wishes to build beneath it and make a tiled roof that blocks the view into the courtyard, the window's owner may prevent the building and roof. He may say, 'Today or tomorrow you may demolish your building and compel me to close my window, claiming I established no right because it opened only toward your roof, while I will be unable to find witnesses that I possessed it when it still overlooked your courtyard.' The Rashba, however, wrote that if Reuven wishes to open a window toward Shimon's courtyard in a place where it presently causes no invasion of privacy, and Shimon claims that he will build on his own property today or tomorrow and the window will then invade his privacy, the law follows Shimon and he may prevent the opening. Shimon need not subject himself to future litigation when he comes to build. Even if Reuven offers to write him an acknowledgment, he is not heard, because Shimon would then have to guard his receipt from mice. The Raavad agrees, as does the Rivash, who wrote that many later authorities, Rabbeinu Yonah, and the Ramban share this view. This is the accepted ruling. In a place with an established custom, local custom governs, as stated in Rivash 472. The Ramban nevertheless wrote that it appears the window cannot establish a right while it causes no harm. {Rama: Even according to the view that the window establishes a right because the neighbor could have protested, that applies only when it opens toward the neighbor's private property. If it opens toward the public domain or a similar place where the neighbor has no ability to protest, all agree that it establishes no right. If Reuven had windows in his house facing his neighbor's courtyard or garden with a wall between them, and the wall fell so that Reuven could now see into the neighbor's property, Reuven must close his windows although he originally made them lawfully. But if a house stood between them and belonged to Reuven or another person, and one could already see the neighbor's property from that intervening house, then if it fell and another house can now see the property, the windows need not be closed. What difference is there whether the view comes from this house or another? Some disagree and hold that invasion of privacy in this case is an injury that arose by itself; since the windows were made lawfully, the injured party must distance himself.
