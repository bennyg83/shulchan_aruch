For he cannot seal his windows. Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 152, states that the view of Rabbeinu Yerucham and those who agree with him applies only where the seller conveyed a house with an already existing open window. In such a case we say that a seller sells generously and did not intend the buyer to alter the house by sealing it. It does not authorize opening a new window after the sale. He further discusses a seller who retained a house opening to the street but sold a small house in the courtyard to Yehudah, together with the right to build upward, three cubits of adjacent vacant ground, and a right of passage through the seller's house to the street; the seller also undertook not to build in the courtyard except a small wood-storage room. Yehudah sought to add a heated room above the courtyard house, open a window into the courtyard, raise the roof, and extend it over the vacant area. The seller objected because of visual intrusion, increased traffic through his courtyard and house, and increased runoff. Yehudah argued that because the seller could not build substantially in the courtyard there was no meaningful visual-intrusion claim, and that the generous sale and the express right to build upward included these additions. Rabbi Akiva Eiger rules for the seller on all three grounds. The authorities dispute whether a generous sale includes waiver of visual injury, so the buyer cannot create a new window on the basis of that doubt. Even Rabbeinu Yerucham speaks of preserving an existing window, not opening a new one, and Ramban holds that even an express acquisition may not validate visual injury. The courtyard can still be used privately even if the seller cannot build extensively there. The added dwelling also creates additional traffic, and the proposed roof adds drainage into the courtyard. The extra phrase granting a right to build upward does not clearly prove permission to multiply residents and burdens; it can reasonably be read as authorizing construction for the buyer's own use or in a form whose access remains through his own rooms. The seller's objections are therefore strong and controlling.
