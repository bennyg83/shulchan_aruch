Reuven has a house adjoining that of a non-Jew and opened a window over the non-Jew's roof. Later the non-Jew sold his house to a Jew, and the purchaser seeks to raise the building in a way that will block Reuven's window. Reuven argues, "You are darkening the light of my window." Although the purchaser derives his rights from the non-Jew, he may not block the window, just as one Jew may not do so to another, because we do not find that a Jewish purchaser stands in the non-Jew's place except to the purchaser's disadvantage. Rama: Others say that since the non-Jew could have blocked the window, the Jewish purchaser who derives title from him may also block it. More generally, a Jew who derives title from a non-Jew receives all the rights that the non-Jew had. Maharam and Rosh ruled this way, and this appears the proper ruling, certainly in a place where dina d'malchusa applies. The purchaser must be careful to take possession of the non-Jew's land before paying him. If he pays before taking possession, the non-Jew immediately withdraws from ownership and the land is like ownerless property, allowing the other Jew to acquire the window rights before the purchaser takes possession. Mordechai, chapter Chezkas. This applies where it is known that the non-Jew's courtyard existed before these windows. If there is doubt whether the windows preceded the courtyard, and the window owner acquired rights while the property was ownerless, the purchaser who seeks to block them bears the burden of proof. Responsa Ramban, siman 16. A chazakah does not operate against the rights of a non-Jew from whom one later derives title unless the chazakah was established after the property was purchased from the non-Jew. Responsa Rashba. See below, siman 194.
