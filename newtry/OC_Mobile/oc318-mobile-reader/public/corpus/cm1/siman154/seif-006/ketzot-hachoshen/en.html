Although the window is high, the Gemara in Perek Chazakat HaBatim records a dispute whether a window more than four amot high acquires a presumption and whether the owner can protest. The Gemara initially suggests that the dispute turns on whether beit din compels one not to act like the people of Sodom, meaning where one party benefits and the other loses nothing. It concludes that everyone accepts that rule, but the owner can say, "Sometimes you may place a stool beneath yourself and stand upon it," creating a potential privacy harm. Ramban cites Raavad, who asks why this differs from a courtyard wall above four amot, where one neighbor cannot force the other to build higher merely because someday there might be a privacy concern. Raavad answers that people do not ordinarily place stools in a courtyard for that purpose, and the neighbor can see him and he is afraid, while inside one's house the other owner cannot observe him and he is less restrained. Were it not for Ramban's citation, I would say there is no difficulty. Without the doctrine against Sodom like conduct, the owner could certainly object to opening a window over the airspace of his courtyard, because the other party is using and benefiting from his airspace. The only reason to compel him to allow it is that one party benefits while the other loses nothing. The Gemara therefore says that everyone accepts such compulsion, but here the owner can answer that sometimes the window user will place a stool and gain a view, creating at least a small loss. Once there is any real possibility of loss, even a very small one, the doctrine of Sodom like conduct no longer applies. The same principle governs a courtyard wall above four amot. Tosafot explains that one who later uses the higher wall must reimburse the builder because this is not a pure case of one benefiting while the other loses nothing. The builder raised the wall above four amot precisely to avoid any possible privacy harm, so he suffered a real cost. Yet before the second owner actually benefits, the law does not compel him to build beyond four amot, because the potential harm is not sufficiently definite. Thus the two sugyot are consistent. Above four amot, the possible stool based privacy harm is enough to defeat the claim that the owner is acting with Sodom like selfishness. It is not enough by itself to impose an affirmative duty to build higher. Therefore he may object to the window even though he could not have compelled construction above four amot.
