Seif 16. (He cannot object because it opens toward Shimon's ruin.) The two opinions concerning building opposite another person's ruin and later removing the building may depend upon the broader dispute concerning one person benefiting while the other suffers no present loss. Even where there is no present measurable loss, the construction may create a future legal or practical disadvantage. That causation itself raises the question whether it is treated like direct consequential damage, which is subject to stricter liability, or like mere causation of monetary loss, which is not itself treated as money for all legal purposes. If consequential damage is only rabbinically stringent, as the Shach writes, while mere causation of monetary loss is not legally equivalent to money under Torah law, a doubt may be resolved differently than according to those who treat consequential damage as Torah law. One might also invoke the presumption that land remains with its owner. On the other hand, where one party merely benefits without presently diminishing the other's property, and the future causation is not itself legally equivalent to present money, perhaps the ordinary land presumption is not directly implicated. Still, the fact that one who causes the loss may remain liable in the laws of Heaven can support treating the owner's existing right seriously. Where a person opens a window toward another's ruin, local practice may follow the opinion that no permanent prescriptive right is acquired and the owner may later close it by rebuilding. The same conclusion does not necessarily apply to an open strip between houses used as a lane or passage. If one party never acquired that physical strip but the person who opened into it acquired a right in its airspace, the later owner may not stand in the same position as the owner of an undeveloped ruin. If a governmental deed grants the width of the area between the houses, perhaps it authorizes construction but does not automatically convey every preexisting airspace right where no construction or acquisition occurred. There may also be acquisition through fencing or locking an area with intent to acquire, even where it remains open from several sides. The actor's intent is necessary. If the physical act is performed through workers, Jewish workers can act for him. Netivot HaMishpat is reported to hold that even the act of a non Jewish worker can be treated as the principal's act for this purpose, though other authorities dispute this. The precise law requires further study. Similar considerations can apply to locally recognized enclosed strips or niches where effective acquisition is possible.
