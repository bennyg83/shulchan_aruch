This applies specifically in a place where people are not permitted to project beams, etc. The Mechaber's wording is very obscure. The phrase "this applies specifically" suggests that he is qualifying the immediately preceding rule, but seif 14 dealt with opening windows in the wall of an upper story without any projection, whereas this seif deals with projecting a structure. The earlier discussion also involved windows facing one another from opposite sides, while here the window is opened on the side toward an adjacent neighbor. The concluding language, "when he opened his window opposite the airspace before the upper story facing him," indicates that he projected a structure and opened a window on its side toward the adjoining neighbor, opposite the open air before that neighbor's upper story. Otherwise it should simply have said "opposite the airspace of the upper story facing him." The following seif likewise cites the Rosh in a case involving windows opened in a side projection, and the Tur's responsum is of the same type. Moreover, if the intended distinction were simply between places where projections are forbidden and places where they are customary, the Mechaber could have said so briefly. It therefore appears that the Mechaber's intent is this. At the beginning of seif 14 he discusses one who first wishes to open a window in an upper story facing other upper stories, teaching that even in such a case the neighbor may object. He then concludes that according to the Rosh, the person who opened first is like one who acquired from ownerless property. That conclusion applies both when he opened directly in the wall of his upper story facing the upper story across from him, without a projection, and when he projected a beam on the side toward the adjoining neighbor and opened a window in the side of that projection. In either case, according to the Rosh, the first person is treated as having acquired from ownerless property. This means not only that the adjacent neighbor may not later project his own structure and open a window directly opposite the first window, but even that he may not project a structure that would merely darken the first window. The Mechaber then qualifies this: the first person acquires such priority only in a place where people are not entitled to project structures into that airspace. In a place where projections are customary and permitted, the open air before the upper story is not ownerless in the same sense, because the neighboring owner retains an existing right to project into it. Therefore, when the first person opens a window toward that airspace, namely in the side of his own projection, he opened it without legal priority. The second person may later exercise his own right to project, even if doing so darkens the first window. If he wishes he may enlarge his projection opposite the first and open a window of his own, saying to the first person: Why should your earlier window deprive me of my equal right to open one? You close yours and I will open mine. The parties will then need a compromise. For this reason the Mechaber merely says that the first person opened "improperly" and does not state only that the second may darken him, because the point is broader than darkness alone.
