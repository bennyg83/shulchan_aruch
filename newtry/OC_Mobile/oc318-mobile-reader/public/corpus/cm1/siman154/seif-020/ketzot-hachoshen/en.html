The Shulchan Aruch adds that the purchaser can also argue that perhaps the gentile had closed the window. This seems difficult in light of Rama's ruling earlier that if the window owner says he himself closed it, the window owner remains the possessor of the window right. Sema attempts to reconcile the two. The distinction is that in the earlier case, Ritva explains that the window owner was already established in possession of the window right. The later physical closure does not by itself remove that existing legal presumption. Therefore the courtyard owner must prove that the right was surrendered. The window owner is the prior possessor of the easement. Here, however, there was no established possession against the gentile owner. If the present window is found closed, we do not know whether the Jewish claimant ever had a continuing right, because perhaps the gentile himself closed it. There is neither a prior legal presumption in favor of the window user nor a present physical state favoring an open window. Rama later rules that where there is doubt whether the window preceded the opposing owner, an existing open window is presumed to have been open earlier as well. That is a present status presumption: because the window is now open, we infer it was open before. But if the window is now found closed, there is no such present presumption. Nor is there a prior status presumption if the gentile may have closed it. Therefore the purchaser may say, "Perhaps the gentile closed it." If it is known with certainty that the window existed before the gentile acquired the property and that the claimant had already established the right, then the prior presumption remains and the purchaser cannot defeat it merely by saying, "Perhaps the gentile closed it."
