(Seif 5, gloss: Of two openings, each two cubits wide, and so forth). Beit Yosef writes that this is the conclusion of the Gemara. I am astonished, for neither the Gemara nor the Rif says this. Rather, it says only that one who has two openings, each four cubits wide, may not replace them with one opening eight cubits wide, and one who has one opening eight cubits wide may not replace it with two openings each four cubits wide. It is therefore difficult why Rama writes, “and we conclude.” Moreover, the substance of this ruling is difficult. Why did the Gemara not state the greater novelty that one who had one opening eight cubits wide may not make two openings each four cubits wide because the neighbor can say, “With one opening I could preserve my privacy”? According to this ruling, even one who had two openings each two cubits wide could not replace them with one opening four cubits wide, although there are two advantages: with two openings he received eight cubits of courtyard frontage, four for each opening, whereas now he receives only four; and with two openings privacy was more difficult than it is now with one opening. Rather, it is clear that this change is actually permitted, because it causes no injury at all. The Sema's statement in seif katan 17 that he will now use larger loads is exceedingly difficult. We are not concerned with visual intrusion while the courtyard is actually being used, for even without this change the neighbor must avoid him whenever he sees him in the courtyard. The essential concern is that even while one sits in his house the other can see him, as the Gemara states expressly, and in that respect there is no difference between a large load and a small one. Nor do I know the source for the Sema's additional reason that sight extends more forcefully through one broad opening. We should not create an unmentioned concern from an uncertain assumption. Nimukei Yosef, cited by Beit Yosef nearby, states explicitly that one may make one doorway in place of two doorways.
