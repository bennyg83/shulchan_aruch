(Seif 21: One who had a window, and so forth). It appears that these words of Rambam concern a place that was originally a ruin or an open valley. He teaches that one may acquire a prescriptive right even against an open valley, as Rivash writes in responsum 471, and likewise against a ruin. Rambam agrees with Rashba at the beginning of seif 17 that opening a window toward a ruin can establish a right. The neighbor afterward came and made a courtyard there. Rama therefore correctly writes that all this concerns one who builds opposite a window overlooking the courtyard, meaning that after the first owner made the window, the neighbor made a courtyard, thereby becoming subject to visual injury, and now seeks to build opposite the window. There is no need for the Sema's strained explanation of the phrase “and made a courtyard beside it.” The Tur cites a responsum of a Gaon concerning two brothers who inherited land. The outer half fell to one brother and contained a water channel by which their father had irrigated the entire garden. The inner brother continued carrying the water across the outer brother's land as it had passed during their father's lifetime. After a time, the outer brother blocked the channel. The inner brother claimed that he had already established a right because the other had remained silent. His claim was accepted. Since the Tur cites this ruling although in simanim 153 and 157, seif 15, he follows the Rosh's conclusion that all such uses require three years, and silence alone is not waiver except in the case of a baker, this water channel must be different. Their father had already possessed and established the use, and the heir can say, “I dwell as my ancestors dwelled,” as Scripture says, “In place of your fathers shall be your sons,” following Rava in the chapter HaShutafin. Although the scholars of Nehardea disagree there and the law generally follows them, here there is an additional favorable consideration: the son remained silent and waived his objection. We therefore say that he was content to fulfill the verse, “In place of your fathers,” and so the law appears, although the Shulchan Aruch did not cite it. This also seems to be the Gaon's intent when he calls such a case an erroneous waiver. Otherwise, whence could he know that it was mistaken? On the contrary, the burden of proof would lie on the one who denies the clear waiver. Rather, he means that where there is a favorable presumption that the heir wished to preserve his father's established arrangement, so long as no mistake is demonstrated, we assume that even had he known all the facts he would have acted the same way.
