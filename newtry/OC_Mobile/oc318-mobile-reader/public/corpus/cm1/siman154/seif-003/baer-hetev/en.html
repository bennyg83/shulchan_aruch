May object. The Gemara gives the reason: his partner can say, "Until now, while you were inside your own house, I did not need to conceal myself from you. Now you will see me through your window even when you remain in your house." See Responsa Maharit 87, 88, and 107.
Chazakah. The Tur concludes that the reason is that the opening was made in violation of a prohibition. This is learned from the verse describing Israel dwelling according to its tribes, which the Sages interpret to mean that their entrances did not face one another. For this reason, it appears that even if witnesses testify that the neighbor waived his objection, or he gave a document and kinyan, it is ineffective. According to the alternative opinion, which holds that chazakah can be established for a window opposite a window because the prohibition depends upon the neighbor's objection, and people do not generally object as strongly to windows, a complete waiver or a document and kinyan also works for an entrance opposite an entrance. Such cases are no more severe than smoke or a privy. Sema.
Into the public domain. One may likewise open opposite a ruined storehouse, and the neighbor cannot object on the ground that he may build it into a residence today or tomorrow. Maharam Galanti 36. See Responsa Rashach I, 144, and Responsa Ibn Lev I, 85. Whenever an opening could not have been compelled to close while the structure stood, its owner does not lose the right even if it later burns or collapses. Maharit I, 114. Knesset HaGedolah.
High. The Sema explains that where the open space of the entrance or window begins below four amot, even though its upper portion extends above four amot, the neighbor may open opposite it. In any event people in the public domain can already see through that opening, since the ordinary line of sight of people walking there reaches what is opposite them within four amot. The Shach writes that this explanation is incorrect.
Who says. See Responsa Rama 32.
Damage. Meaning that he causes a type of visual damage that people in the public domain do not cause, such as where the opening is higher than four amot or a similar circumstance. Darkei Moshe explicitly supports this. Shach.
Opposite. This is not literally limited to directly opposite. He must distance his opening far enough that he cannot see into the other person's house, as the Mechaber concludes. Sema.
To be careful. It appears that the Rama teaches that even where the observer does not intend to look or cause harm by seeing, and even if he does not actually see anything, he is nevertheless forbidden to stand opposite another person's house and look through that opening. People may suspect that he stands there in order to observe the household. This explains the Rama's comparison to one caught like a thief and his conclusion that the person has no valid explanation. Since, in the eyes of others, he has no reason to stand there, they will suspect that he intends to look inside. Sema.
