For an opening opposite an opening, chazakah does not help. See Sema, seif katan 10, who writes that according to the view that the prohibition rests on the neighbor's objection, a document or formal kinyan is effective even though ordinary chazakah is not, just as with smoke and a privy. Ramban explains that the harmed party can say, "I thought I could tolerate it, but now I cannot." This reason fits Ramban's own view that these rights arise through waiver. According to those who require a claim that the right was purchased by kinyan together with three years of chazakah as evidence for that claim, one might initially argue that the greater the injury, the stronger the proof from the neighbor's failure to protest. The Rosh in Bava Batra 23 therefore gives a different reason: the neighbor thought there was no need to protest because everyone knows that a person does not waive an injury of this kind. His intent must likewise be that people do not ordinarily sell such a right of injury.
We compel conduct that avoids the trait of Sodom. At first glance there is a difficulty. An opening gives its owner rights in the four amot opposite it. Why is this different from division of a courtyard in siman 174, seif 1, where one partner asks to receive his portion adjacent to his existing boundary? The Mechaber there treats refusal as the trait of Sodom, while the Rosh disagrees and holds that the requesting partner must increase the price until his fellow agrees. Here too a party should be able to say, "I want my opening here so that I will acquire the four amot in front of it. If the lot assigns the opening to you here, you would have to compensate me just as there." See Sema there, seif katan 7, and Tosafot, Bava Batra 12b, s.v. ma'alinan lei, who write that a party can say, "We will not give you the right we possess in this field except for a high price." The Rama's conclusion that if both are equally disadvantaged they should compromise is also difficult. Why not cast lots as in division of a courtyard? It therefore appears that the case here is where the courtyard was already divided, but no wall was erected between the portions and the parties waived ordinary visual privacy against each other. Even so, one may not create an opening directly opposite the other's opening. Since the objecting party owns no proprietary right in the specific location the other now wishes to use, refusal gives him nothing and we compel him to avoid the trait of Sodom. The rule that a person may demand a high price applies only where he owns an actual right in the place the other seeks to acquire. Alternatively, the case is one described in siman 172 where an opening gives no ownership of the four amot in front of it. Since the objector surrenders none of his property, he may be compelled. This also explains why the Rama does not require lots. A lot was instituted only for division of the body of land, derived from division of Eretz Yisrael, as explained in siman 173. Here no body of land is being divided, so there is no law of lots. If one party suffers a small monetary loss and the other a large one, then if the latter fully compensates the former for the small monetary loss, the former can be compelled to accept. But if the objector loses part of the actual land, he may say that the land is worth a great amount to him, as in Bava Batra 12.
If they are not four amot high. See Sema, seif katan 12, who writes that if the opposite opening begins within four amot of the ground, it may be made even though its height continues upward. The Shach in seif katan 4 says this is incorrect, and he is right. The occupant can say, "People in the public domain can see me when I use the lower area, but when I use the upper area they cannot see me, whereas you will see me even there." This resembles the Gemara's rule that one who has one opening may not make it into two, because with one opening the neighbor can conceal himself from that line of sight but with two he cannot.
