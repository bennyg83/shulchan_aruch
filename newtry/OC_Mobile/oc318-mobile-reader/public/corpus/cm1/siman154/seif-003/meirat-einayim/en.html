Even one of the partners, etc. One might have thought that since a partner already has a doorway from his house into the courtyard and enters it whenever he wishes, he may also open a window into the courtyard. The ruling teaches otherwise. The Gemara explains that his partner can say: until now, while you were inside your house I did not need to conceal myself from you; now you can see me through your window even while remaining in your house.
Chazakah does not help. The Tur explains that the opening was made in violation of law. Chazal derive from the verse in Parashat Balak, 'He saw Israel dwelling according to its tribes,' that their entrances did not directly face one another. It therefore appears that even witnesses to an express waiver, or a deed and kinyan, do not help where the arrangement itself involves prohibition. According to the opinion that chazakah does help for window opposite window, however, the prohibition depends on ordinary objection. Since people do not object as strongly to window opposite window, chazakah is effective there, and that view likewise holds that an express waiver or deed and kinyan can help even for doorway opposite doorway. Such matters are no more severe than smoke or a privy. See the Derishah.
Rather, he should distance it slightly, etc. Ramban explains why in a shared courtyard a slight offset suffices, whereas for a window opening into another's private courtyard one must distance it enough that he cannot see inside. In a shared courtyard, although he may still see the other, he does not see him continuously through his normal use. The other cannot say, 'You may intentionally observe me,' because he can respond, 'If I wanted to watch you deliberately, I could simply enter our shared courtyard and do so.' This differs from a window into another courtyard. The reason therefore is not that partners are already less careful with one another, but that the other cannot in any event fully avoid him.
If they are not four amot high, etc. Meaning, if the opening begins somewhat below four amot, even though its top extends above four amot, the other may open opposite it. People in the public domain can already see through such an opening in the ordinary course of walking, since ordinary eye level reaches within four amot from the ground.
A shop opposite an opening, etc. 'Opposite' is not literal. He must distance it enough that he cannot see into the other's house, as the Mechaber concludes.
Since he has no claim. It appears obvious that this does not mean he lacks a claim of purchase or waiver of the right to look into the house, because standing and staring is not something chazakah and a claim can permit, as Rama writes below in seif 7. Nor could the text simply assume he has no such claim. Rather, Rama teaches that even if the observer does not intend to look or cause visual harm, and perhaps is not actually looking, he should not stand opposite another's house and gaze in that direction, because people will suspect him of standing there to look. This explains why Rama phrases it in terms of being caught like a thief and says there is no claim justifying why he stands there. Nimukei Yosef derives precisely this point from the discussion of using a ledge, where one might have thought the user could face away and would be suspected as a thief if he turned toward the courtyard. From there one learns that even conduct that merely appears like watching another's private area should be avoided. The same applies to a courtyard as to a house.
