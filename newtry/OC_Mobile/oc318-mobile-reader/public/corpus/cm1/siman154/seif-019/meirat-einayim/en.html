The Rashba states this explicitly, and the Mechaber alludes to it by saying, "for such is the law of the gentiles," whose law allows this even after the window has remained open for years. This is difficult, because in the preceding seif 18 the Mechaber ruled that a Jewish purchaser from a gentile may not even build in his own courtyard in front of Reuven's window once Reuven had established his window right, all the more so that he should not be able to force Reuven to close it. One cannot resolve this by saying that seif 18 concerns a Jewish window that had existed for years before the gentile built beside it, so that the Jew had already acquired a right under Jewish law, whereas here the gentile's property existed from the very moment the window was opened and therefore the gentile always retained a right to build against it. That cannot be correct. Seif 18 itself implies that the gentile's property already existed when the window was opened, for it says, "Reuven has a house beside a gentile and opened a window over the gentile's roof." Rama also explicitly adds there that the ruling concerns a case where the gentile's courtyard preceded the window. The difference becomes clear from the Mechaber's precise wording. In seif 18 he speaks of a window opened over the gentile's roof, while in seif 19 he speaks of a window opened over the gentile's courtyard. In seif 18 he says that the purchaser wishes to build opposite the window; in seif 19 he says that the purchaser wishes to force the window owner to close it. Although these differences may reflect the original incidents, the Mechaber is stating legal rules and would not have varied the facts without purpose. A typical flat roof does not involve substantial visual intrusion, as the Tur and Mechaber explain in simanim 159 and 160. Thus in seif 18 the window owner was not directly harming the gentile through observation. The purchaser merely wishes to raise his own building and thereby darken the window. Since this would directly cause loss to the established window owner, we do not apply here the rule that one who derives title from a gentile acquires all the gentile's rights, and the purchaser may not actively damage the window by darkening it. In seif 19, by contrast, the window overlooks a courtyard in which people ordinarily conduct activities, and every act of looking creates visual harm. The window owner is therefore himself directly injuring the courtyard user. For that reason the Jewish purchaser may compel him to close the window, and certainly may build opposite it. This does not rest merely upon the rule that one who derives title from a gentile has the gentile's rights, because the gentile himself could not compel literal closure. Rather, the point is that the window owner has no right to continue direct visual harm. Although according to the Rosh visual intrusion can sometimes be subject to prescriptive rights, that requires possession accompanied by a claim of acquisition. Here the Jewish window owner made no such claim, and even if he wished to make it he would not be believed, because under gentile law visual intrusion gives no basis for protest. Where there was no right to protest, silence cannot establish possession, even between Jews. Likewise, according to the Rambam, who ordinarily treats silence after opening a window as waiver, that applies between Jews who are particular about visual intrusion, not between a Jew and a gentile who does not object under his law. In seif 18, although the same general principle that absence of protest prevents acquisition could be raised, the facts show that the gentile seller did not build against the window and that the Jew used it openly throughout the gentile's ownership. We therefore preserve that established use as though the gentile had waived or sold the relevant right, because there was no ongoing direct visual harm. The Gaon cited by the Tur, who says we do not always apply the rule that one deriving from a gentile has the gentile's rights, likewise speaks of a purchaser seeking to build in front of the window and darken it, not of a case where the window owner is directly harming the courtyard by observation. See the Perishah and Derishah, and what I wrote above in siman 45:17 and simanim 66 and 67 regarding the rule that one who derives title from a gentile has the gentile's rights when a Jew would otherwise directly deprive the purchaser of his property.
