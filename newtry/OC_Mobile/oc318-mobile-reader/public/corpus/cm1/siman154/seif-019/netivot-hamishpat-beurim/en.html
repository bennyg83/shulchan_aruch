It has already been explained that some disagree. See Shach and Taz, who question this reference because the dissenting view in seif 18 actually supports the second Jew. In my view the Rama refers instead to the dissenting authorities in seif 15, who hold that whenever a person initially opened his window permissibly, the harmed neighbor bears the obligation to distance himself. Here too the opening was created permissibly, since it was permitted while the neighboring property was held by a non Jew.
If the non Jew was required to close it under their law. This means that the written instrument was drafted in a manner legally effective under their law. Likewise, if the instrument records an admission, then even if the admission was written in a form lacking force under their law, a written admission by a non Jew can confer rights under our law. The continuation, "and he obligated himself in what he had not previously owed," is explained as follows. A personal obligation is not a kinyan in the body of the object. It takes effect on the person who undertakes the obligation through the lien on his assets, while the object itself remains his property and can be sold. Therefore, this type of obligation does not require the non Jew's signature in the same manner as a conveyance. Rashba expressly writes this. The law of collecting from purchasers does not apply to an obligation to close a window.
