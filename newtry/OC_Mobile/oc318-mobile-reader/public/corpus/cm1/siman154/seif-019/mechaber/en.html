If Reuven owned a house adjoining a non-Jew's courtyard and opened windows toward it because non-Jewish law permitted this, and the non-Jew then sold the courtyard to Shimon, Shimon may tell Reuven, 'Close your windows.' The law follows Shimon, even if Reuven claims he purchased the right from the non-Jew. {Rama: It was explained above that some disagree.} If Reuven owned a house adjoining a non-Jew's property, the non-Jew opened windows toward Reuven's courtyard, Shimon purchased the house from the non-Jew, and Reuven seeks to close the windows while producing a document in which the non-Jew wrote that Reuven could close them, and Shimon claims the house he purchased had previously belonged to a Jew who established the windows before selling it to the non-Jew, then the following rule applies. If the non-Jew was required under their law to close the windows, or Reuven's document states that the windows have no established right because they were made unlawfully or only by permission, Shimon, who derives his right from the non-Jew, has no greater right than the non-Jew. But if the non-Jew was not required to close them and did not admit that they lacked an established right, but merely wrote, 'I will close them whenever you wish,' thereby obligating himself beyond the law, Shimon need not close them. A non-Jew's use of windows does not establish a right when their law gave the neighbor no ability to protest, because such possession is not legally significant.
