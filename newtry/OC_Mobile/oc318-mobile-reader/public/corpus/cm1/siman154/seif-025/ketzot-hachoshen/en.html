Beit Yosef cites Rashba concerning a dispute over reducing the air reaching Reuven's wine and oil storeroom. Rashba writes that the facts showed these storerooms required airflow to cool the wine. If that was indeed the local usage, then once the storeroom existed first, even if no wine or oil was presently stored there, it remained designated for that use and a neighbor could not do something on his own property that damaged the storeroom. Rashba's language suggests an already existing dedicated storeroom from which the wine or oil happened temporarily to be absent. Rama's wording, however, can be read as a case where a person merely built a room intending later to use it as a wine and oil store, before ever placing wine or oil inside. That is difficult because the opening of Perek Lo Yachpor leaves unresolved several analogous questions, such as one who builds an upper story, adds windows, or places fruit there before the susceptible use has actually begun. In siman 155 the practical law is that initially the neighbor may protest, but if the other already acted the installation may not necessarily be removed. One might answer that according to Rava, who holds one may not place a future nuisance even before the injured use exists, the wall must be removed, and the Gemara's exception for a cattle stall before a storeroom is because ordinary residence is special. Since a wall is not residence, perhaps it should be removed even before wine is stored. That answer is not satisfactory. Tosafot explains that Rava forbids anticipatory placement only where the damaging force comes directly from the object itself. A wall placed nearby does not actively damage. It merely reduces airflow, like reducing the beneficial trampling near another wall. Ramban similarly writes that where one merely prevents a benefit rather than causes direct damage, the neighbor may act until an actual injured use exists. Accordingly, if the wall came first and there was not yet an established storeroom, the wall should be permitted even though it later reduces air. It is only withholding a benefit, not producing direct damage. Indeed, I find difficulty even if the wine storeroom already existed. Why should a neighbor be obligated to preserve his own airspace merely to cool another person's wine? The law of leaving space for beneficial trampling near a wall applies only where a prior legal right to that space exists, such as where the first owner acquired land from the king or from ownerless property in a manner that included the necessary strip, according to Maggid Mishneh and Rama in siman 155. Without such a prior right, a neighbor may build close to the wall because the first owner never acquired another's airspace. Hagahot Maimoniyot says similarly that, absent a prior acquired right, one person cannot force another to leave open air merely so light or air reaches his property. Preventing light is not the same as actively damaging with mustard, soaking pits, or similar sources. It is only withholding a benefit arising from the neighbor's own airspace. Rashba's ruling can therefore be understood according to the opinions that require a neighbor to leave the beneficial airspace even without a special prior acquisition. But our practical law follows Rama that one need not preserve such space unless the first owner acquired an established right, such as from the king or ownerless property, or otherwise obtained a presumption. More likely, Rashba's own language supplies the limitation. He says, "If this is so and such is the local practice." Where local custom gives established storerooms a recognized right to the necessary airflow, the neighbor must respect that customary servitude. Without such custom or other acquired right, one need not preserve his own airspace merely to cool another's wine. This is the proper practical distinction.
