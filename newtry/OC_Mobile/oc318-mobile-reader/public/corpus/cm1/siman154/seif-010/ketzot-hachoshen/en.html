Ramban writes that where a small window has no presumption, for example a narrow window or one positioned high, the courtyard owner need not refrain from building opposite it merely to avoid darkening it. Nevertheless, he must still avoid building in a way that directly allows him to look into the window from above or below, because if we permit that visual harm we effectively force the window owner to close the opening. Even where the window itself has no prescriptive right, the neighbor may not inflict direct visual intrusion like arrows. Ramban criticizes later writers who ruled otherwise. Ramban appears to follow the opinion cited by Rosh that when a high window has no presumption, the courtyard owner cannot compel its closure, though he need not maintain the usual four amot clearance opposite it. Under that approach, Ramban can say that the owner may build close enough to darken the window but cannot create direct visual intrusion, because the window owner remains entitled to leave the window open. According to Rosh himself, however, where a window has no presumption the courtyard owner can compel its closure. The Tur and Beit Yosef follow this. On that view it is obvious that the courtyard owner may build above or below even if the construction creates visual intrusion, because he could have required the window to be closed entirely. Ramban adds that even if both parties were building at the same time, it would be unjust to permit the courtyard owner to build so as to create visual intrusion. Yet according to the accepted ruling like Rabbi Ilai, even a window above four amot may be protested from the outset. Certainly, if both are building simultaneously, the courtyard owner may object to the window itself. Perhaps Ramban referred specifically to a narrow Egyptian window, because in his own novellae he writes that for such a window, when positioned more than four amot high, all agree the owner cannot protest. In that setting he can say that when both build simultaneously, the courtyard owner must still avoid direct visual intrusion. For our accepted law, however, even a narrow or high window can be protested from the outset. Therefore, when both build at the same time the courtyard owner can prevent the window entirely. All the more, according to Rosh and Tur, if the window was already installed without a valid presumption, the owner may compel its closure and may certainly build above or below even if the construction creates visual harm. Even according to the opinion that the courtyard owner cannot compel closure, however, I would still rule like the later authorities that he may build above and below. The Gemara's basis for allowing him to build in his own airspace is that compelling him to leave his airspace unused would itself be an extraordinary concession. Once his building is ordinary use of his own property, forcing him to refrain merely to preserve the other party's unauthorized view is not the prevention of Sodom like conduct. It is simply requiring him to surrender his own use. "What is mine is mine and what is yours is yours" is not Sodom like conduct in such a case. The later authorities are therefore correct.
