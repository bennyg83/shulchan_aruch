One of the partners in a courtyard. This is the Mishnah.
For each one. Bava Metzia 117b discusses one owner wishing to alter the structure. The Rosh, citing Rabbeinu Yonah, distinguishes a structure originally built jointly from one merely purchased or inherited. Raavad instead makes the rule depend on local custom, without distinguishing how ownership arose. The Tur here follows Raavad, and likewise in siman 164.
Even if he built. This is explicit in the Mishnah there.
According to. The Gemara there.
It becomes. This is the Rambam's wording.
But he may... Ramban, citing the Tosefta, and Maggid Mishneh, as well as Kesef Mishneh citing the Rif, explain that where the added room has no separate entrance to the courtyard, it is not treated as an independent residence and does not create an added traffic burden. The Gemara's discussion of an upper room is thus speaking where it opens only into his own house.
Needless to say, if... The Mishnah and Gemara say he may open the upper room into his house and divide an interior room into two. The Shulchan Aruch copies the Rif's responsum concerning an upper room. Ramban adds that even a newly built internal room is permitted when it has no separate exterior opening, because both portions remain functionally one residence.
When is this said... Tosefta at the end of Bava Metzia, cited by Ramban, Maggid Mishneh, Nimukei Yosef, and Kesef Mishneh in the name of the Rif.
Provided that he closes. This follows the law of a resident of one alley seeking to open into another. The residents of his original alley may object while his opening remains there.
Some disagree. Tosafos and the Rosh disagree regarding the scope of this permission. The Tur indicates that they nevertheless agree regarding an internal upper room or chamber when everything remains within his own courtyard.
