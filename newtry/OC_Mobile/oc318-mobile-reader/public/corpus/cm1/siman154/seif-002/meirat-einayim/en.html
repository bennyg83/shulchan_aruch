Some say that a partner, etc. The ruling speaks specifically of a co-owner. A tenant may not do this during his rental term, as explained below in simanim 315 and 317 in the Tur and Mechaber.
Even though he subdivides his house for residents, etc. This phrase connects to what follows, 'the other cannot say, etc.' Mordechai at the end of chapter Chazakat HaBatim writes that even though they share a privy and Reuven wants to divide his house so that additional residents result, Shimon cannot prevent the subdivision. Therefore the conjunction before 'cannot' should be deleted.
Although in a courtyard people do not object to such use, etc. Mordechai explains that although we rule like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov that one who is forbidden by vow to benefit from one partner may nevertheless enter the jointly owned courtyard and say, 'I am entering my fellow's portion, not yours,' that applies to a courtyard because people do not object to ordinary passage there. With matters people ordinarily waive, even one prohibited by vow may benefit. But people do object to passage through a house. This does not conflict with the Gemara's ruling that one partner may not open from the shared courtyard into another courtyard through a house he owns there because he increases traffic. That case involves regular, permanent traffic from a fixed house in the other courtyard, unlike occasional visitors coming from the street. For the same reason there is no difficulty according to Rambam, who prohibits filling one's house with other residents because of increased traffic in the shared courtyard. There too the use is permanent.
However, in a house people object. It appears that even if the other partner does not live with him in the house, he may object to bringing additional residents into the house because increased occupancy damages his share. But if one partner merely wishes to place someone else in his own stead, without increasing the number of residents, the other cannot prevent him even if he also lives there and says, 'I can live with you, but not with the person you wish to put in your place.' Such a claim applies to two tenants, as explained below in siman 317, not to co-owners of the house itself. A co-owner could in any event sell his share to that other person. If his fellow does not wish to live with the purchaser, they may divide the house or proceed through the rule of buy or sell. This differs from two tenants. This appears clear to me, unlike Ir Shushan, who explains the rule as based on unwillingness to live with those additional people. The wording of Mordechai does not support that explanation either.
