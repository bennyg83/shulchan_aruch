Rivash discusses one who acquired an ownerless ruin after another had already placed a potentially harmful use nearby while the neighboring land was still ownerless. He writes that a person who placed a nuisance lawfully before any injured owner existed ordinarily need not later remove it when someone acquires the neighboring property. However, where the later user's activity itself would harm the first user, the second party must keep the required distance. Rivash explains the mustard and bees sugya in this way. Even according to Rabbi Yosei, who ordinarily places the burden of distancing on the damager, where the mustard was planted first at a time when there were no bees, the mustard owner acted lawfully and acquired his position. When the bee owner later arrives, the bees themselves damage the mustard. Therefore the later bee owner must distance the bees. The first user need not now move, because his original lawful priority has already fixed the location. Ramban reads the sugya differently and holds that where each use harms the other, both must distance themselves. Rivash acknowledges Ramban's interpretation but prefers the approach just stated, which corresponds to Tosafot's reading of the mustard case. I will discuss that dispute in siman 155, seif katan 12.
