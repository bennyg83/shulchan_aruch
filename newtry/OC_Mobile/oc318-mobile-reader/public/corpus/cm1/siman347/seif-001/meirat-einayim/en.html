To the lender's ox. Meaning, the particular ox that was borrowed from the lender was damaged by another ox belonging absolutely to the borrower.
But not that it should not be damaged. This is also the Tur's wording. In the Perishah I wrote that even if the borrower did not expressly say this, once ordinarily he would be responsible both to prevent the borrowed ox from damaging and to protect it from being damaged, but he specifically accepted only that it not cause damage, it is as though he expressly stipulated that he did not accept responsibility to protect it from injury. This is also implied by Rashi at the end of the first chapter of Bava Kamma. As a matter of law, however, it appears that even if he expressly stipulated that he would not guard it against being damaged, if his own innocuous ox damages the borrowed ox he must still pay half-damages, because he is independently obligated to guard his own ox from harming others. The lender's ox is no worse than any other person's ox. This is straightforward.
The correct reading is: "and the borrower's ox damaged it." Not "to the ox."
If it damaged an ordinary third party's ox, the borrower must pay. It appears that he pays according to the amount the lender himself would have been required to pay, namely half-damages if the borrowed ox was tam and full damages if it was mu'ad. The borrower accepted only to stand in the lender's place and assume the lender's liability.
But not that it should not cause damage, etc. Here too the meaning is as explained above. Once he specifically said that he would guard it only from being damaged, it is as though he expressly said that he did not undertake to prevent it from damaging. See the Perishah.
