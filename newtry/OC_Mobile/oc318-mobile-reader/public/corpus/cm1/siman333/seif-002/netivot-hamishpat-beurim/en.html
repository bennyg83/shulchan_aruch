Maharam Schiff asks: according to those who hold that even in monetary davar ha'aved, if the employer does not actually hire replacement workers he need not pay the resulting damage, why here, when the employer retracts and the workers did not hire themselves elsewhere, must he pay them from his own pocket? In my view there is no difficulty. The rule that one may hire replacement workers against the defaulting party up to the amount of the original wages is necessarily not pure Torah law. It rests either on a rabbinic enactment or on an assessment by Chazal that workers and employers implicitly obligate themselves to cover the other side's loss up to that amount. One cannot ask a technical damages question against the scope of such an enactment or implied undertaking. Indeed, it must be a special rule of workers. If a person agrees to sell merchandise but no kinyan occurs, even if the other merchant loses an opportunity because of the withdrawal, he is ordinarily exempt. Workers are different because Chazal instituted special liability. Tosafot write that according to R. Meir, the employer can be liable under dina d'garmi. At first glance that might imply full ordinary damages law. But this cannot be their intent in the strict sense. If one locks another person in a room for half a day, he must pay for the actual half day of unemployment. He need not also pay for a speculative inability to obtain another job for the second half of the day. Yet where a worker was hired for the entire day and the employer retracts, the worker may receive payment for the entire day's lost employment. This cannot be ordinary shevet liability. When one does not intend to injure, the four additional categories of personal injury do not ordinarily apply in the same way. Rather, Tosafot mean that once Chazal established that in davar ha'aved one side is treated as though he directly caused the other's loss, there is no reason to distinguish between a worker who retracts and an employer who retracts. According to R. Meir, causation and direct damage are sufficiently alike for the special worker-enactment to apply to both.
Only heavier work. The same rule appears in siman 335 in the gloss. Mordechai implies that if the employer retracts and the workers can hire themselves out to a different, heavier job, even at increased wages, the employer need not compensate them for refusing that job. One might ask from siman 316 concerning a tenant who wishes to leave a house but cannot sublet it to a family with more occupants. There, even if the replacement family offers higher rent, the original tenant may still be liable. Why not say the same here, that the workers cannot be forced into a materially different job merely because it pays more? The distinction is that the worker's recovery after the employer retracts rests on the special damages or enactment rule just discussed. If a different heavier job is available at higher wages, and there is a reasonable assessment that had the original employer never hired them they would have accepted that job, the original employer is not considered to have caused their loss. A tenant's obligation is different. The rental was acquired by law, and the tenant owes the rent as a contractual debt. His right to sublet is itself limited. He never acquired a right to place a materially larger household in the premises, and therefore inability to do so does not cancel his rent obligation.
