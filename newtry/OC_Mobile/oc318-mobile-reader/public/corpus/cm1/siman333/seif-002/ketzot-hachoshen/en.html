But if they could have been hired yesterday. Tosafot asks why an employer who causes workers to lose a day's work is not liable under the law of indirect damage, since Rabbi Meir imposes liability for consequential harm. Tosafot answers that the case must be one where they can still find work today, though with extra effort. My son, Rabbi Yosef Dov Ber, asked that even if they can no longer find work, why is the employer liable? Preventing another from making a profit, like tying up his money or closing his shop, is ordinarily exempt. I answered that bodily loss of work differs from loss involving property. One who injures a person is liable for lost labor, and even confinement without bodily injury can create liability for lost work. Thus causing a person to lose his workday can be viewed as consequential liability for bodily lost labor. Yet elsewhere I wrote that liability by the law of consequential damage extends only to ordinary damage, not to the additional categories of bodily injury such as lost labor. If so, indirect prevention of a worker's earnings should likewise be exempt. I later found Maharam of Rothenburg stating explicitly that consequential damage requires actual loss and does not include merely preventing anticipated profit. He proves this from the Mishnah of workers who retract from one another. Accordingly, Maharam appears to hold that even if the workers could have found work yesterday but cannot now, the employer is exempt from damages because the loss is only prevention of profit. This is a powerful monetary authority and the employer is in possession of the money. The Shulchan Aruch therefore does not frame the employer's liability as ordinary consequential damages but as the special law of a matter that will be lost. That special doctrine itself may depend upon commencement of work or another contractual basis, as discussed in the following seif katan.
This is like a matter that will be lost. Nimukei Yosef writes that if workers could have hired themselves out on the previous evening, and the employer's commitment caused them to lose that opportunity, the employer can become liable because their wages are now a matter that will be lost. If they had no employment opportunity from the outset, however, they acquire a stronger contractual right only once they actually begin the work. The language of "matter that will be lost" rather than ordinary consequential damage is deliberate. As I wrote above, lost profit is not ordinarily actionable as damages. A difficulty remains. Nimukei Yosef himself says that when workers abandon an employer's matter that will be lost, even before beginning the work, the employer may hire replacement workers against them up to the amount of their wage. But if he simply waits and suffers the loss, they do not pay damages, because they undertook to complete the work, not to reimburse the loss. Why then, when the employer retracts from the workers, should he have to pay wages merely because their opportunity was lost? The distinction can be that the workers' undertaking is to perform the work. Therefore, in a matter that will be lost, the remedy is to compel completion economically by hiring others against their wage. The employer's undertaking, by contrast, is to pay wages. If his retraction turns the workers' opportunity into a matter that will be lost, fulfillment of his own undertaking consists precisely of payment. Still, Maharam of Rothenburg appears to reject even this. He treats the workers' missed earnings as mere prevention of profit and exempts the employer unless a stronger contractual acquisition already arose. That view is persuasive, because when workers abandon an employer's flax in soaking, they cause actual destruction, while an employer who retracts merely prevents profit. The case of musicians hired for a funeral, sometimes classified as a matter that will be lost even before beginning, can be explained by the special concern for the honor of the deceased rather than ordinary tort law. Mordechai, however, cites Rabbi Meir that there is no distinction whether work began, which points toward Tosafot's stricter view. The practical issue therefore remains difficult.
He pays them the reduction. Sema explains that if the workers now find only lower paying work, the employer pays the difference according to the value of an idle worker measured from yesterday's hiring. The Shach asks what an idle-worker deduction has to do with this case, since the workers are in fact doing work. The case can be understood where the market wage for the same labor did not fall. Rather, the heavier work for which they had been hired is no longer available, and they can now find only easier work that naturally pays less. The employer pays the difference according to the amount a worker would accept to exchange the heavier work for the easier work. That is the sense in which the idle-worker valuation is relevant.
