He may retract in the middle of the day. Beit Yosef cites Ritva in the name of his teachers that a worker's statutory right to retract applies only where he hired himself verbally. If he bound himself through a kinyan, the kinyan strengthens the undertaking and he may not retract. The Shach argues that such a kinyan is merely a kinyan on words. In my view, where a person expressly subjects his body to labor, the kinyan is not a mere kinyan on words. Raavad says that a person can bind his own body through kinyan like a servant. His criticism of Rambam concerning artisan partnerships is based precisely on this. Rambam too can agree that explicit subjection of the body is effective. His ruling that a kinyan to travel in commerce is a kinyan on words speaks where the body itself was not expressly subjected, only the future act was promised. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ritva that all the Mishnah's rules of a worker's retraction concern verbal hiring without kinyan. If the worker obligated himself by kinyan, the work rests upon him and even an unavoidable event can require him to complete through others or reimburse the employer. The same applies if he already received the entire wage. Nevertheless, the Shulchan Aruch rules that even one who has already received the whole wage can retract, and money is itself an acquisition mode for a Hebrew servant. Therefore, even though a kinyan upon the worker's body is legally meaningful and not a kinyan on words, the Torah's principle that the Jewish people are servants of Hashem and not servants of servants can still allow a worker to retract. The kinyan does not necessarily defeat that statutory liberty.
And he has no money with which to repay. Maharik writes that a worker may retract even after receiving his wages and even if he presently lacks funds to repay the employer. The money becomes an ordinary debt. He distinguishes a Hebrew servant, who acquires his freedom before the end of his term only through the Torah's redemption rules. If he lacks the money for redemption, he cannot simply leave. The worker, however, retains the right to retract. According to Responsa Maimoniyot, however, the worker's retraction itself is learned from the Hebrew servant's redemption by deduction of money. On that theory, perhaps the worker also needs money or property with which to repay what remains unearned. There is an additional reason for restriction. If the worker has no means to repay, the employer faces a present certain loss. A doubtful possibility that the worker may later become wealthy does not necessarily remove the status of a matter that will be lost. We find a similar rule in mourning laws where a presently collectible debt that may become unavailable later is treated as a matter that will be lost even though future collection remains possible. Thus according to Responsa Maimoniyot the worker may be unable to retract when he cannot make the necessary monetary deduction and the employer will otherwise lose. On the other hand, Tosafot says the distinction between a Hebrew servant and an ordinary worker is precisely that the worker may retract in the middle of the day, whereas a Hebrew servant requires formal redemption. This supports Maharik even where the worker has no money in hand. Therefore Tosafot appears to favor Maharik's rule.
Three years. Chavot Yair discusses a cantor who bound himself by a handshake pledge to a community for six years and then wished to retract, arguing that a person should not hire himself for more than three years because that resembles servitude to another person. Chavot Yair distinguishes a cantor, whose essential work is sacred communal service, from an ordinary laborer. He argues that leading prayer is service of Hashem and therefore does not make him a servant of servants. I do not accept the distinction. He also says that the wage of a teacher is only for guarding children and for cantillation, not for the mitzvah of Torah itself. Yet Nedarim shows that cantillation too is a component of Torah instruction. Rav would even forbid taking payment for it, and Rabbi Yochanan permits payment only because he treats that aspect as rabbinic. Prayer likewise is rabbinic according to most authorities. If a teacher may retract despite serving Hashem, the same is true of a cantor. The fact that the work itself is sacred does not mean his contractual submission to another person ceases to be employment. Nevertheless, if he swore not to retract, the oath can bind. The prohibition against hiring oneself for more than three years is rabbinic, and an oath can take effect upon a rabbinic obligation. Where keeping the oath requires remaining passive and not retracting, the rabbinic law can yield rather than requiring an active violation of the oath. Moreover, once he already entered the overlong employment, the original rabbinic violation has already occurred. A later retraction does not undo that past act. Therefore the oath can prevent him from retracting afterward.
