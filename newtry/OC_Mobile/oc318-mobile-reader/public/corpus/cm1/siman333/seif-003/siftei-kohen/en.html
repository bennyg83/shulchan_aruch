He may withdraw, etc. The Beit Yosef cites the Ritva in the name of his teachers that a worker’s right to withdraw applies only where he hired himself by verbal agreement, but if he bound himself by a formal kinyan, the kinyan was intended to strengthen the obligation and he may not withdraw. Darkei Moshe cites this without qualification. In my view, the other authorities, who make no such distinction, disagree, and their view is primary. The verse, ‘For the children of Israel are servants to Me, and not servants to servants,’ indicates that the worker may withdraw in every circumstance. The Rosh, Tur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, and others likewise rule that although drawing the craftsman’s tools completes the hiring for a contractor, a worker may still withdraw. It is strained to distinguish meshicha from a formal kinyan, since they are generally equivalent, and Hagahot Ashri expressly compares them. Moreover, the obligation is a mere kinyan devarim, as the Rambam and Semag rule regarding one who formally undertakes to travel in commerce with another. Even the Raavad appears to agree except in the special law of partners. Perhaps the Mechaber himself reconsidered the matter, for he made no distinction here between a worker who made a kinyan and one who did not. I later found this stated expressly by the Rivash, siman 476: there is no distinction between a worker hired for a day, week, month, or year, or a contractor hired to reap a field or harvest a vineyard. No formal kinyan is needed; beginning the work effects the hiring. Nevertheless, a worker may always withdraw, even after beginning and even after a kinyan, because Scripture states that Israel are God’s servants. It appears that the Ritva may have referred only to a contractor, who after a kinyan cannot withdraw even by accepting a monetary disadvantage, although the plain wording is not so. Responsa Mabit suggests that had the Rivash seen the Ritva he might not have disagreed, but in my view the Rivash’s position is primary.
He does not have the money available to pay, etc. Maharik’s wording is that the money is not in his possession and available for payment. This may mean specifically that the money is not presently liquid, although he owns assets from which payment can ultimately be made. If he has no means to pay at all, perhaps the worker cannot withdraw because the matter would be one subject to loss. The Mechaber’s wording, however, indicates that even where he has no means to pay, the worker may withdraw. This is unlike an ordinary case of loss, where one may hire replacements at the worker’s expense or mislead him, but even there the worker cannot be physically compelled to labor. Alternatively, inability to pay does not itself render the matter one subject to loss. Although above, siman 37, seif 10, we do not rely upon the possibility that a person may become wealthy with respect to testimony, here the work is still not deemed irretrievably lost, since the employer may later acquire funds.
He is not allowed to sell himself to a Hebrew slave, and he is not allowed to sell himself to a Hebrew slave, and he will not be able to sell himself to Hashem’s command, and he will not be able to do so with him
For a fixed period of three years, etc. Darkei Moshe at the beginning of siman 331, Be’er Sheva, and Agudat Ezov state that the text contains an error and should read ‘more than three years,’ as is clear from Hagahot Maimoniyot. Their correction is sound. Hagahot Maimoniyot asks: if a worker is called a servant, why may a Jew hire himself at all, just as he may not sell himself as a Hebrew slave? It answers that a worker is called a hired laborer, not a slave; the derivation from a slave is only to grant him leniency. Scripture states of the Hebrew slave’s six years, ‘twice the hire of a hired laborer,’ while Yeshayahu defines the term of a hired laborer as three years. Therefore a teacher, scribe, or other worker should avoid hiring himself to live permanently in an employer’s home and eat at his table without interruption for more than three years. Beyond three years he leaves the category of a hired laborer. Although he does not become a full slave for all laws, he violates the principle that Israel are God’s servants. A supporting indication is the account of the man who came from Upper Galilee and hired himself for three years. The Ateret Zekenim and Sema misunderstood this and wrote that a three-year term itself is forbidden because the person is then called a hired laborer. They overlooked the Hagahot Mordechai, which says the opposite: being a hired laborer is permitted; the prohibition begins only beyond three years, when he leaves that category. Be’er Sheva notes, however, that Tosafot imply that even more than three years is permitted. Tosafot explain that the prohibition applies specifically to a Hebrew slave, who cannot withdraw and does not leave before his term except through a bill of release. The Mordechai likewise follows Tosafot.
