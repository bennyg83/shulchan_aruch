Ketzos HaChoshen writes that according to the authorities who explain a worker's right to retract on the basis that he is no worse than a Hebrew servant, who leaves through proportional monetary redemption, actual money is required. On this basis he answers the Shach's question in seif katan 19 concerning the ruling that when a worker retracts after wages have fallen, the employer gives him six. Why should the worker receive more than the value of the work actually performed? Ketzos answers that because the right to retract is modeled on proportional redemption, and Kiddushin states that if a Hebrew servant's value falls, the calculation is made according to the remaining years, the same calculation applies here. In my view this issue is actually a dispute among Amoraim in the Yerushalmi. On the Mishnah stating that whoever retracts has the lower hand, the Yerushalmi records: Rav said, "For the children of Israel are servants to Me," meaning Jews do not acquire one another. Rabbi Yochanan said that the worker is like a Hebrew servant. According to Rav, both the worker and the employer may retract. According to Rabbi Yochanan, the worker may retract but the employer may not. I wrote long ago that Rav's view is that Torah law recognizes no proprietary acquisition of the person of an ordinary Jewish worker, only of a Hebrew servant when the Jubilee system applies. Consequently, the employer too may retract, and if his retraction causes loss he pays only under the law of causative damage. He therefore pays only the damage actually caused. If the employer was prevented by circumstances beyond his control, he is not liable under causative damage and need pay only for work already performed. All the more so when the worker himself retracts and thereby causes his own loss, the employer need pay only for work actually done. Even if wages later fell so that the remaining half of the work can now be completed for less than half the original price, the worker has no claim to the difference. Rabbi Yochanan, however, holds that a worker has the status of a Hebrew servant and is subject to an acquisition. His right to retract derives from the fact that even a Hebrew servant leaves through proportional redemption, and a hired worker should certainly be no worse, as stated in Teshuvos Maimoniyos to Sefer Kinyan, siman 31. Accordingly, if wages fall one calculates the remaining obligation in the manner of redemption and deducts only according to the lower current rate. Since the Babylonian Gemara rules like Rav that a worker may retract, and formulates the rule in terms of retraction, this implies that we do not treat an ordinary worker as a Hebrew servant with respect to the mechanism of leaving employment. A Hebrew servant is bodily acquired by the master and must acquire his freedom through monetary redemption, so actual money is required. A hired worker, however, is not bodily acquired and simply possesses a right to retract. Therefore, even if he has no cash with which to pay any amount he owes, the amount can remain as an ordinary debt, just as with one retracting from a sale not completed by a binding acquisition. For the same reason, if wages fall the worker receives only according to the work he actually performed. The Shach likewise concludes several times in this siman that an employer has no bodily acquisition in the worker.
