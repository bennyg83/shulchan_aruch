The employer accepted him back. Rosh uses this to reconcile two sources. Perek HaOmanin implies that a worker prevented by illness is paid only for the work actually done, while Kiddushin says that a Hebrew servant who was ill for three years and worked three years need not make up the missing time. Rosh explains that in the servant case the employer accepted him back without reservation after the illness. Rosh also cites Maharam that if the worker was paid in advance, the result can differ. These distinctions apply where the illness covered only half of the employment period. But if he was ill for most of the term, even prepayment or silent reacceptance should not entitle him to full pay. The baraita itself says that a Hebrew servant ill for four years must make up the illness, despite the same assumptions. Rambam writes that if a Hebrew servant's total illness is less than four years it counts toward the six-year term, but if it reaches four years he must make up all the sick time. Maharit explains that an ordinary hired laborer's term is three years, while the Hebrew servant serves double a hired laborer's period, six years. Once he has performed the equivalent of an ordinary three-year hiring, the Torah's phrase "like a hired laborer" allows the remaining illness to count. On this reading the servant law is special and does not prove a general rule for workers. Accordingly, ordinary workers can have the sick days deducted according to the work actually performed. The Shach understands Rambam as requiring literally four full years of illness, while Mishneh LaMelech says Rambam merely used the Gemara's terminology and agrees that more than three years constitutes the relevant majority.
Or the teacher, his wage. The Shach follows Tosafot, Mordechai, and Tashbetz that there is no distinction whether the worker was prepaid or later accepted back without reservation. An ordinary worker who becomes ill receives only payment for the work actually performed. Tosafot says the law of a Hebrew servant cannot be compared to a teacher. The Hebrew servant's body is acquired by the master, while the teacher's body is not owned. The teacher merely hired himself to teach for a stated period. If he cannot teach throughout that period, he is paid only for what he actually did. Ritva gives another distinction. A worker or contractor hires himself for a particular work or defined service and receives pay for that performance. A Hebrew servant is not hired for one defined task. His entire labor capacity is subjected to the master. Illness therefore falls upon the master's field. According to the Tosafot approach, even if a worker hires himself for all kinds of work and receives money, document, or hazakah, he remains only a worker nowadays because full Hebrew-servant bodily ownership does not operate when Jubilee is not in force. His relationship is a personal obligation, not ownership of his body, and sick time is deducted. Ritva's own theory may treat a worker who truly subjects himself for all labor more like a servant. But under the prevailing Tosafot approach there is no current bodily acquisition of the Hebrew-servant type, so an ordinary worker is always paid only according to the work actually performed.
He may hire others against them. The Shach says that when the work is a matter that will be lost, the employer may hire replacement workers against the original workers even if they never began the work. This follows Nimukei Yosef. The Shach further says that a contractor can be deceived even where the work is not a matter that will be lost and even before beginning, relying on a responsum of Rosh. I find that incorrect. If neither worker nor contractor began the work and there is no matter that will be lost, each may retract and the employer has only a grievance. There is no basis to hire against him or deceive him. Rosh's responsum involved a contractor who had already begun weaving a garment and then demanded a higher wage because the yarn was poor. Since he had already begun, the question was different. It does not prove liability before commencement.
What he finally agreed to pay them. The Shach explains a responsum of Rosh as allowing the employer to deceive a contractor even if replacement workers were available, unless the contractor expressly told him, "Go hire those others." He uses this to reconcile Rosh with Nimukei Yosef's rule that where a worker could lawfully retract but only incurs a grievance, a later reconciliation is presumed to be on the original wage unless a new higher wage was expressly fixed. The responsum itself is more difficult. Rosh says a contractor who already began the work could not lawfully retract even if another worker was available, and therefore the employer could deceive him into finishing even where the matter was not otherwise lost. That formulation conflicts with Rosh's own halachic rulings, under which a contractor who began can still retract when suitable replacements are available. The better course is to follow Rosh's halachic rulings rather than the responsum. Where suitable replacement workers are available, the contractor can retract subject at most to grievance. There is no automatic right to deceive him. Rashba's phrase, "They tell him, Go hire from these," means that the availability of replacements must be clear and provable. If the parties dispute whether replacement workers were actually available, the retracting worker bears the burden of proof. It does not mean he must literally utter those words in order to gain the right to retract. Accordingly, if replacements were admittedly available, the contractor may retract even if he never said, "Go hire them." There is also no contradiction with Nimukei Yosef regarding the final wage. If the parties merely reconcile without specifying a new amount, and the retraction created only a grievance, we presume they returned to the original wage. But if the employer expressly promises a higher amount in order to bring the workers back, and he had no legal right to deceive them, he must pay the newly agreed amount. Only where the law itself permits deception can he later refuse the extra amount. This resolves the issue.
