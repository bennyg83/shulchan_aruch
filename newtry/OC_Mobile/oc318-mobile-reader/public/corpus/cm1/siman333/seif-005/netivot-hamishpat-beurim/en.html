A maidservant. See Shach, seif katan 23, who notes that Terumat HaDeshen leaves the matter requiring study. In my view Terumat HaDeshen was uncertain only whether a maidservant should be considered monetary davar ha'aved in the special sense that one may hire replacements against her even if no bundle or property of hers has come into the employer's hands. But it is certainly at least non-monetary davar ha'aved. There are household needs that an employer cannot reasonably perform without a maidservant. Thus the withdrawal can create the special urgency of davar ha'aved even if the precise monetary remedies require distinction.
Only for what they did. This applies specifically when they were hired to complete a task from which the employer derived some benefit through the portion already performed. Even if the employer must pay more to finish the remainder, the completed portion still constitutes benefit, comparable to the renter of a donkey that died after part of the journey in siman 310. But if replacement workers cannot be found and the entire undertaking is thereby lost, so that the employer receives no benefit at all from the portion performed, he need not pay even for that part. This resembles the rule in siman 335, seif 2, where an agent was sent on an errand and found the relevant merchant unavailable.
Or he may mislead them. See Shach, seif katan 29, who cites Rosh that with a contractor, even when the matter is not davar ha'aved, the employer may mislead the contractor. At first glance this is surprising. The Mishnah appears to teach the right to mislead only in davar ha'aved. Even if the contractor already began the work, he may still retract, except that the accounting is against him. Why should the employer be allowed to deceive him into continuing when no such rule is stated in the Mishnah? In my view Rosh follows Ritva cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. Ritva explains that where a contractor wove only half the wool and wishes to retract, he can do so with the accounting against him specifically where he had drawn only the half of the wool that he already worked on and now retracts from the portion he never drew. But if he drew all the wool at the outset, neither the employer nor the artisan may retract. Meshichah acquires the contractual relationship as to the entire material. Rosh's case concerned such a situation, where the contractor had already drawn the entire object or material. The contractor could no longer retract, and the employer could therefore use the same right to mislead that applies in davar ha'aved when necessary to secure performance of a binding undertaking. Thus Rosh does not create a new right to deceive in every ordinary contract. He speaks where a kinyan has already made the contractor unable to withdraw.

A worker who works for free. See Shach, seif katan 31, who writes that if the employer later agrees to pay him, the employer can become liable. At first glance this is difficult. Maharik's reason for exempting the employer is that the worker caused his own loss: when he undertook the work gratuitously, he should have anticipated that he might later retract. Maharik carefully distinguishes this from an ordinary paid worker, even though a paid worker too may often retract. According to that reasoning, even if the employer later offers payment, why should the employer become liable for the earlier reliance? The worker originally created the risk himself by undertaking the work for free. It therefore appears that Shach is speaking according to Hagahot Ashri, which says that if a person declares, "I am lent to you," he can create liability. Even then, however, the employer's liability for davar ha'aved applies only if replacement workers could actually have been hired. Ordinarily one cannot assume others would perform the work for free. Therefore Shach properly requires that the employer now be willing to pay wages, because otherwise there was no realistic substitute labor whose loss can be attributed to the worker's withdrawal.
