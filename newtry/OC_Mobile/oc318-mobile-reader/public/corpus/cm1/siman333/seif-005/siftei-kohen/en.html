If the employer retracted and then accepted them again, etc. The distinctions recorded by the Rama, between a case in which the employer retracted and later accepted the workers again and one in which he did not, and between a case in which he prepaid their wages and one in which he did not, derive from the Rosh. The Rosh used them to reconcile the passage in Bava Metzia, which implies that workers who were prevented from completing the work are not paid their entire wage, with the passage in Kiddushin concerning a Hebrew servant who was ill for three years and worked for three years, who need not make up the lost time. Maharam is also cited as distinguishing between prepayment and nonpayment, and the Hagahot Maimoniyot relates that this distinction was revealed to him in a dream. In my view, dreams neither establish nor negate law. The Gemara itself rules that even where a dream identified hidden money as second-tithe money, the dream has no legal force. Moreover, there is no sound reason to distinguish between prepayment and nonpayment. The wage was advanced only on the understanding that the worker would perform the agreed work for the agreed period. This is unlike the cases of a specified ship with specified wine, or a tenant who dies during the lease term. The Rosh's further distinction, that the employer's unqualified acceptance waives the unworked time, is also unclear. Why should acceptance without express qualification waive compensation for work not performed? His explanation is also difficult in light of the Gemara's statement that if the Hebrew servant was ill all six years he must make up the time. On the Rosh's approach the Gemara should have distinguished, even within illness lasting three years, between illness at the beginning and illness at the end. The Rambam and Sefer Mitzvot Gadol likewise write that whether the illness was continuous or intermittent, so long as it totaled less than four years it counts toward the six-year term. Their language indicates no distinction between illness at the beginning and at the end. In truth, with an ordinary worker the employer deducts for all time not worked. The Tosefta states that if a hired worker dies or is stricken with fever, his wage is appraised according to what he performed, whether he was hired by the month or as a contractor. It does not limit this rule to an interruption at midday. A Hebrew servant is different because his person is acquired by money, deed, or possession, and illness occurring while he is under his master's authority is the master's loss. An ordinary worker's person is not acquired. Tosafot explicitly reject the claim that teachers of children who are ill for half the term receive the rule of a Hebrew servant. A teacher hired to teach until a fixed time receives only the proportional wage for what he taught. The Mordechai, Tashbetz, and Ritva all rule similarly. The Ritva explains that a worker or contractor was hired for a particular task and therefore receives compensation only for the work done, whereas a Hebrew servant was not acquired for one defined task but to place all his labor at his master's disposal. Consequently, his illness is the master's loss. This remains true even if an ordinary worker hired himself for all kinds of work, because his person was not acquired through the modes by which servants are acquired. This is the correct ruling.

If he found other workers and misled the original workers, etc. The Gemara states that he tells the worker, 'Go and hire from among these.' The Rosh explains that where substitutes are available, the worker may direct the employer to hire them; if the employer instead misleads the original worker, he must pay the added amount. This appears to apply when the worker knows of available substitutes and explicitly tells the employer to hire them, thereby placing another in his stead. Accordingly, the Rosh's responsum permitting an employer to mislead a contractor who retracts even when another worker was available concerns a case in which the contractor did not direct him to hire that substitute. This also reconciles the Rosh with the Nimukei Yosef, who writes that where the employer could have hired others and did not, he may not charge the original workers for the resulting loss. The critical point is whether the retracting worker actually supplied or designated an available substitute.
