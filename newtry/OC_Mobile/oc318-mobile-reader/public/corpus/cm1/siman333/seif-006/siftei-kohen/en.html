Whatever he adds, etc. This means that the employer may add for the replacement workers up to the amount originally agreed with the first workers, and recover that addition from the first workers even if he had not yet paid them anything. Thus, if he hired the first workers for two sela and they retract, he may hire others for four sela and collect the additional two sela from the first workers. This is plainly the view of the Rambam, Sefer Mitzvot Gadol, the Rosh, and the Tur. Rashi explains differently. Rabbeinu Yerucham cites one view limiting recovery to wages already held by the employer, and another allowing recovery up to twice the original wage, and he prefers the first. This is surprising, since he thereby rules against his teacher, the Rosh, particularly where the principal authorities support the latter view.

Property belonging to them, etc. This is the language of the Rambam and Sefer Mitzvot Gadol, and it implies even other property, not only their tools of trade. The Tur likewise writes that if the employer has any of their property in his possession, he may retain it. Rabbeinu Baruch in Sefer HaChokhmah also indicates this. In my view Rashi agrees. Although the Mordechai, citing Maharam, speaks specifically of the workers' bundle, and Rashi also uses that expression, Rashi explains that the employer has much of their property because craftsmen commonly bring their tools to the employer's premises. His point is therefore not to limit the rule to tools, but merely to describe the usual case. Any property of theirs in the employer's possession may be retained up to the amount recoverable from them.

Some say, etc. The phrase means that the cited opinion disagrees with the preceding ruling, as will be explained below. It is therefore correct to read 'and some say,' with the conjunction, unlike the wording printed in Ir Shushan. Perhaps that text contains a scribal error. In practice, however, the law does not follow this opinion, as explained below.
This applies specifically where he seized their tools of trade. Because the employer relies more securely upon such tools, he may retain them even beyond the workers' original wage, up to the full amount for which they are legally liable. Other property, however, may be used only up to the amount of their wage. Even ordinary property may be seized to that extent, as is evident from the laws of seizure in simanim 72 and 75 and elsewhere. This is Maharam's view in the Mordechai. Some understood Maharam to permit seizure of any property for the entire loss, but that reading is incorrect. The distinction is between ordinary seizure, effective up to the wage already due, and seizure of tools of trade, upon which the employer particularly relies and from which he may recover the additional replacement cost.
Specifically in the case of an irretrievable loss that is not monetary, etc. The Rama appears to distinguish the Nimukei Yosef's case as one involving a nonmonetary irretrievable loss. Darkei Moshe also suggests this. In fact, however, the Nimukei Yosef indicates that even where the employer suffered monetary loss, the workers need not compensate him for the damage. There is nevertheless no dispute. The Ramban and Nimukei Yosef address a case in which replacement workers were available but the employer delayed hiring them, and therefore the original workers are not liable for the loss caused by his delay. The Hagahot Ashri and Terumat HaDeshen address a case in which no replacements were available, or the loss occurred immediately.
