If the employer performed meshichah. See Shach, seif katan 3, who writes that the employer can no longer retract for any amount of money. One might ask what Shach adds, since the meshichah must necessarily be a kinyan preventing complete retraction. If it merely placed the worker in the stronger position, what difference would there be if he could still retract, since the worker is not yet entitled to any wages and the tools have already been returned, as Sema writes? The novelty is that one might have thought this was no stronger than a case of davar ha'aved, where one hires replacement workers only up to the amount of the original wages. Shach therefore teaches that after this kinyan the employer cannot retract at all merely by paying money. Shach seeks proof from the sugya in HaZahav. In my view the opposite is implied there. Meshichah of the barber's tools is needed only according to Reish Lakish, who holds meshichah acquires. According to R. Yochanan, who holds money acquires, no meshichah of the tools is needed. We rule like R. Yochanan. Although R. Yochanan agrees that Chazal suspended money-acquisition for movable goods, that was because of the concern that the seller might say, "Your wheat burned in the attic." Where that concern does not apply, such as rental of movable property, money still acquires, as explained in siman 198. All the more so, hiring a worker can be acquired through money. A worker also resembles land for certain laws, as explained in siman 227 and elsewhere, and land is acquired with money. Rambam likewise supports this. In Hilchot Me'ilah, chapter 6, he distinguishes between buying an object from a Jew and from a non Jew. When money was given to a Jew for an object, he rules there is no me'ilah, because Chazal removed the money-acquisition and hefker beit din is effective. But where the money was given to one of the tradesmen, Rambam rules simply that me'ilah occurs and does not distinguish Jew from non Jew or require meshichah of the barber's tools. This indicates that hiring labor is acquired through money even rabbinically. Why then did the sugya require meshichah of the barber's tools according to Reish Lakish, if a worker can be hired through money even under all views? Shitah Mekubetzet, citing Ritva, explains that one who hires a worker together with the tools of his trade has two distinct acquisitions: the worker's labor and the rental of the tools. The tools themselves require the ordinary mode of acquisition applicable to movable property. This resembles one who hires a worker together with a horse, as in siman 227, seif 33. Each component has its own kinyan. If one component of a combined transaction fails, the other can also fail, as in siman 216 concerning two items sold together for one price. Accordingly, under Reish Lakish, meshichah was needed for the tools. Under R. Yochanan, money acquires the rental of movable tools where the burning concern does not apply, and it also acquires the worker's labor. This explains Rambam's ruling and resolves Kesef Mishneh's question why Rambam does not require meshichah according to R. Yochanan. Rama therefore correctly records the meshichah rule as "some say," because the Gemara itself proves only the case of payment, while Shitah Mekubetzet gives no basis to say meshichah of the tools alone acquires the artisan himself. Rabbeinu Tam may also have spoken of payment together with meshichah and held that rental of movable property still requires meshichah. Rama's agreement with this opinion should therefore be limited to the practical point explained immediately below. If one hires a non-Jewish artisan, money certainly acquires his labor where the law recognizes acquisition of a non Jew's labor. If the artisan is hired together with tools or animals, the acquisition of those movable items depends on the rules in siman 194. According to the view that a non Jew's movable property is acquired specifically by meshichah, one must also draw the tools. If the artisan himself draws the object given to him for repair, however, all agree that the hiring is acquired and neither party may retract, as Ritva writes in HaOmanin. Darkei Moshe also writes that where three people hire a teacher, no kinyan is required because a matter undertaken by the public does not require kinyan. It is surprising this principle was not cited more broadly for workers hired by three people. Perhaps it applies specifically to a teacher because teaching is a mitzvah matter and resembles a vow made publicly and for the public.
He can retain it. See Shach, seif katan 5, who says Nimukei Yosef wrote this only according to Rashba and Ran. In my view even Rashba, Ran, and Mordechai mean only that the employer may retain the scissors until the barber uses them to cut his hair. They hold, as explained above from Shitah Mekubetzet, that rental of the scissors and hiring of the artisan are two separate acquisitions. They may also follow the view cited by Taz in siman 216 that where two things are acquired for one price and the kinyan fails as to one, it can nevertheless remain effective as to the other. Since the scissors were validly rented, the employer may keep them until the contemplated haircut. He can say, "I rented these scissors to remain in my possession until you cut my hair with them." But there is no source saying he may sell the scissors and use their proceeds to hire someone else. There is proof. These authorities compare a worker to an eved Ivri, who can redeem himself proportionally and leave. Certainly one cannot compel an eved Ivri to continue working merely because his tools are in the master's possession. Rama too can be understood this way. His phrase that the employer may retain the tools and hire others means that he can hire another worker with his own money and have that worker perform the task using the original artisan's tools, while paying the original artisan whatever portion of the fee represented rental of the tools. Rama does not say that the employer may sell the scissors and hire another from the proceeds. His deliberate language of "retain the scissors" supports this reading. In my view this is clear.
