A grievance. The Tur writes that either party may withdraw before work begins. The employer can say to the worker, "Hire yourself elsewhere," and the worker can say to the employer, "Go hire another worker." The withdrawing party nevertheless causes a grievance because of the trouble imposed upon the other. This wording suggests that there is not even a problem of acting in bad faith, because that concept applies only where one withdraws from a commitment without a legitimate benefit, as the Rama writes at the end of siman 204 that when the market price changes there is no issue of acting in bad faith. The same would apply where the employer withdraws because he no longer needs the workers or because cheaper workers are available. According to this approach, the authorities who disagree there would disagree here as well. The phrase "they have only a grievance" would then exclude a monetary claim of the type discussed in the next seif, while the moral fault of acting in bad faith could still remain. So writes the Sema. The Shach writes that the wording also implies that there is no grievance merely for a change of person. Although at the end of siman 311, regarding a ship, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state that there is a grievance because of the change of person, that case differs because the ship was unloaded midway and the owner had already become accustomed to the first renter. Here the withdrawal is immediate. Accordingly, where there is no inconvenience, such as where the workers can readily find another employer, they do not even have a grievance. Nimukei Yosef cites the Ramban similarly in chapter HaOmanin regarding a fall in wages.
He drew it. Even if the employer later returned the craftsman's tool after drawing it, the act of meshichah is nevertheless a kinyan preventing either party from retracting. The worker is an exception because the Sages gave a worker the right to withdraw even in the middle of labor, based on the verse "For the children of Israel are servants to Me." When the Rama concludes that the employer may nevertheless withhold, he means that the worker's tools are still in the employer's possession. So writes the Sema. The Shach agrees that the opinion cited as "some say" is primary, and many poskim rule so.
Contractor. Hagahot Ashri, chapter HaOmanin, writes that Rabbeinu Tam appears to hold that after a binding monetary kinyan the contractor cannot retract at all. His phrase that "he performed an act" may mean that beginning the work itself is not a kinyan, and only meshichah or kinyan sudar creates the stronger obligation. Thus, although a contractor who merely began work may withdraw and then has the lower hand in the accounting, as stated in seif 4, that remedy does not apply where a formal kinyan has been made. Once the employer drew the work tool or the contractor obligated himself by kinyan sudar, no monetary adjustment permits withdrawal; if the employer insists, the contractor must complete the work. This also appears explicitly from R. Baruch in Sefer HaChochmah, cited by the Mordechai in chapter HaOmanin regarding the employer taking possession of the worker's bundle: after the employer draws the craftsman's tools, the worker cannot withdraw even if he is willing to have the lower hand in the accounting. He hired them. Since the employer was negligent and the workers' going to the job is treated like the beginning of work, the employer may no longer withdraw. If he does, he must pay them as idle workers. This is true even where the workers suffered no actual monetary loss from the cancellation, because even had he not hired them there was no other employment available. The Tur explicitly writes this in the name of the Rosh. The Ir Shushan's explanation, that liability is because the employer caused them damage by preventing them from being hired elsewhere and is therefore liable under the law of indirect damage, is incorrect. It also contradicts his own next seif, where he explicitly says that once they went to the job the employer is liable even where no alternative work was available. So writes the Sema, and his practical ruling is correct. Nevertheless, this is not a valid objection to the Ir Shushan, because Tosafot, the Rosh, Tur, and other poskim explain that the Gemara mentions the workers' going as the usual case: before they go, they generally can still find other work, whereas afterward they cannot. The Ir Shushan can be understood in the same manner. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 28. Shach.
