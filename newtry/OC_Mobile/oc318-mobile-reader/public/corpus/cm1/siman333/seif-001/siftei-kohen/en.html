They have only a grievance. The employer may say to the worker, ‘Hire yourself elsewhere,’ and the worker may say to the employer, ‘Go hire another worker.’ The party who withdraws is subject to grievance because of the trouble caused. The Tur, following the Rosh, indicates that the grievance is due to the trouble, not merely to a change of mind. Although in siman 311, seif 6, regarding a ship unloaded midway, the Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber mention grievance due to a change in the parties’ expectations, that case differs because the parties had already traveled together for half the journey. Here the withdrawal occurs immediately. Accordingly, if no trouble is caused, such as where the workers can readily find another employer, there should be no grievance at all. Nimukei Yosef cites later authorities in the name of the Ramban that if the employer withdraws after work began, but the contractors can find equivalent work elsewhere, they have not even a grievance. The Maggid Mishneh, however, in the name of the Ramban and Rashba, and the Mechaber in seif 2, imply that the workers still have a grievance against the employer. Tosafot, the Rosh, Tur, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Mordechai, and Aguda likewise indicate that even where they can find other employment they have a grievance, because doing so ordinarily entails effort. This raises a difficulty from the Gemara’s question regarding a ship: if another charter is available, why is there a grievance? The Gemara could have answered that another charter was available only with effort. The matter can be reconciled with difficulty. In any event, where equivalent employment is immediately available without effort, all appear to agree with Nimukei Yosef that there is not even a grievance. One practical consequence of a grievance concerns a second wage agreement, as explained above in siman 332.

But not the worker, etc. Hagahot Ashri cites Mahari’ach that Rabbeinu Tam’s words imply that the worker cannot withdraw at all for money once the employer has performed an act of acquisition. Perhaps beginning the work is not a complete acquisition for this purpose, whereas meshicha of the craftsman’s tools or a formal kinyan sudar is. Thus, although a worker who merely began the work may withdraw with the financial consequence that his hand is on the lower side, where the employer drew the tools or made a formal kinyan the worker cannot withdraw even by accepting that consequence. He must perform the work if the employer demands it. Rabbeinu Baruch in Sefer HaChochmah, cited by the Mordechai, likewise states expressly that once the craftsman’s bundle came into the employer’s possession, the worker cannot withdraw even if he is willing to bear the lower-hand calculation.
Nevertheless, he may retain the craftsman’s tools, etc. This requires study. Nimukei Yosef states this only according to the Rashba and Ran, and the Mordechai only according to Ri, who entertain the possibility that drawing the tools does not complete the hiring but merely permits the employer to retain them. According to the authorities who hold that drawing the tools completes an acquisition, and who limit the passage in Bava Metzia to a contractor, it may follow that in the case of a day laborer the employer may not retain the tools at all. Even if those authorities might agree externally, what is the source? Logic also indicates that since the worker may withdraw and no binding acquisition remains, the employer should not retain the tools, for where there is no underlying debt there can be no collateral. Tosafot in Kiddushin supports this. Tosafot, the Rosh, and Aguda also state simply that a day laborer may withdraw even after the tools were drawn, without adding that the employer may retain them, which implies a complete right of withdrawal. The Maggid Mishneh, citing the Ramban and Rashba, rules that where the worker’s bundle came into the employer’s possession, even in work that is not subject to loss the employer may hire replacements or mislead the worker up to the value of the bundle. That may concern only a contractor, who cannot withdraw, or may reflect the Rashba’s distinct view. Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise first cites the Rosh’s general rule and then adds that some great authorities hold that although the worker may withdraw, the employer acquires a right in the tools. This implies that the Rosh and his followers disagree. Although I am not worthy to dispute the Rama, it appears to me that a God-fearing person should act stringently and return the worker’s property.

He gives them their wages. Since the employer was negligent and the workers’ travel is considered the beginning of the work, the employer may no longer withdraw without paying them as idle workers. This applies even if the workers suffered no direct monetary loss from the cancellation because, had he not hired them, they would not have found other employment. The Tur states this expressly in the name of the Rosh. The Ateret Zekenim instead writes that the employer is liable because his act caused them loss, since otherwise they would have been hired by others, and that he is liable under the law of indirect damage. The Sema objects that this is incorrect and internally inconsistent, since the Ateret Zekenim itself later obligates the employer after the workers traveled even where no other work was available. The Sema is correct as to the law, but this is not necessarily a criticism of the Ateret Zekenim. Tosafot, the Rosh, Tur, and other authorities explain that the Gemara’s mention of travel reflects the ordinary case: before traveling workers generally can find other employment, whereas afterward they generally cannot. The Ateret Zekenim may likewise have described the ordinary case. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 28.
