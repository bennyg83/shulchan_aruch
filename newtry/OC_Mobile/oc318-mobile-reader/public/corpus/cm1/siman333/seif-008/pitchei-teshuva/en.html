A maidservant, etc., is a matter subject to loss. VeShav HaKohen 13 explains that Terumat HaDeshen's doubt concerns whether the employer may hire replacements at the worker's expense and extract the excess cost, since it is not as clearly a case of imminent loss as the Talmudic examples. But initially the servant may still be forbidden to retract when her withdrawal may cause the employer loss. Thus Rama's designation of a servant as a matter subject to loss can mean only that she may not retract initially, not that excess replacement costs can always be collected from her. Whether a household servant is truly such a case depends on the circumstances and the judge's assessment. Chavot Yair 103 discusses a maid who fled three months before the end of her term while the mistress held her Shabbat clothing and hired a more expensive replacement. Without possession, the maid would have a strong claim because the classification is doubtful and the mistress had previously managed for years without a servant. Possession, however, strengthens the mistress's position. If the maid could have supplied an equally suitable replacement, the mistress cannot rush to hire another and charge the excess. A servant used only minimally for honor is also not necessarily a matter subject to loss. Chavot Yair further discusses local custom concerning a maid's pledged property and favors compromise in such disputes. If an employer wrongfully dismisses a servant, he ordinarily owes the agreed wage but not necessarily food costs, though Shevut Yaakov distinguishes a female servant whose access to food and personal safety may differ. Chatam Sofer 122 discusses a maid who pledged gold earrings and disappeared. If she returned at the appointed time, demanded them, and knowingly abandoned the claim after the employer refused, her conduct can amount to waiver, not necessarily mistaken waiver, especially where she wished to preserve her reliability. If she never returned, one must consider that an accident prevented her and that her failure to claim was based on a mistaken assumption. The holder must then make serious efforts to locate her, including public notice. If she still cannot be found, Chatam Sofer suggests appraising the earrings, recording their value in the communal register, and permitting use subject to paying depreciation and reasonable use value should she later appear.
