He said to a craftsman, "Make this for me." It is difficult why liability is limited here to something that will be lost if the customer does not immediately accept it, meaning a case of actual loss. In siman 336 it is explained that even where one instructs another to perform work upon ownerless property and says, "Your wages are upon me," he must pay and cannot say, "Take what you made as your wages." It therefore appears that siman 336 concerns ordinary hiring of a worker, which does not require a separate acquisition. Here, by contrast, he did not tell the craftsman to make the item as hired labor, but told him to make it for himself and that afterward he would purchase it from him at its value. The craftsman therefore did not immediately become his agent in a manner generating an ordinary wage obligation. Only where the finished item is subject to loss does liability arise under causative damage.
If he stood with them silently. See Shach, seif katan 44, through his statement that otherwise he should not have allowed the person to continue living in his house. One cannot argue that if market rents later fell, the tenant should still have to pay the original higher rate, because the landlord remained silent, while the tenant can say, "I remained because what loss was there to you in allowing me to live in the house?" The cases differ. Occupying another's house without payment implicates unlawful use of another's property. We therefore assume that the tenant intended to pay rent, and when no new rate was fixed we infer that he continued on the basis of the original rate. With a prayer leader, however, listeners do not unlawfully take anything merely by hearing him pray, and they may say, "What loss did we cause you?"
He is satisfied with what the father did. See Shach, seif katan 45, who writes that Ramban's responsum concerns a son who knew that his father had fixed a wage. The Shach infers that the same should apply to any other person who knew a rate had been fixed, accepted the arrangement, and began the work. Ramban's responsum, however, expressly states that this rule is specific to a son. There is an additional difficulty. What practical effect is achieved by saying that the son receives the father's fixed rate until he retracts? Even if the father's agreement were not binding, the son's work would simply be labor performed without a fixed rate, in which case he receives the lowest customary wage. We must therefore be discussing a case where the father fixed a rate even lower than the lowest customary wage. This creates a major distinction. If an unrelated person knows that another person fixed a wage on his behalf but does not know the amount, and he nevertheless begins work relying on that agreement, he is no better than an ordinary principal whose agent made a disadvantageous bargain. If the agent was overreached, the transaction is void even by a minimal amount, because the principal can say, "I sent you to improve my position, not to harm me." Once the agent's price is void, the worker is considered to have worked without a fixed wage and receives the lowest customary wage. With a father, however, we presume that the son accepts whatever arrangement his father made. It is as though he authorized his father both to improve and to worsen his position. The father's rate therefore remains binding, and the son receives only that amount.
The employer may retract. See Shach, seif katan 46, who cites Maharshal distinguishing between a verbal release and a written one. This must follow the first reason of Rashba, cited by the Shach in seif katan 47, that the rule applies to every worker because the worker's person is acquired and release is ineffective without an act of acquisition. Maharshal therefore writes that a written document can effect the release. According to Rashba's second reason, that one cannot waive the young person's existing lien, even writing would not help. The Shach disagrees and holds that the phrase "go away from me" is an inferior expression of release, and even writing does not cure it. Kiddushin 15 states that a master may say before two witnesses, "Go," and Tosafos explain that even though the servant's person is acquired, a document is effective. That applies specifically to the language "you are your own," which is the statutory language of emancipation. An inferior expression does not become effective merely because it is written, as is evident from HaKosev. By contrast, the phrase "I have no claim upon you" operates as abandonment and is effective even orally, as explained in Kerisos and siman 245, seif 10. The same applies to rental of a house when the tenant says, "I do not want the house." I recall that this is stated in Machaneh Efraim, though the work is not presently before me.
