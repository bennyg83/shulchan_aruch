He is liable. The Sema infers from the Rosh's responsum that the craftsman bears the burden of selling the finished article, while the employer must cover the deficiency. This is correct only where the craftsman supplied the materials. If the employer supplied them, then since the law is that a craftsman does not acquire title through the improvement he adds to the object, the article belongs to the employer, and the burden of selling it does not rest upon the craftsman.
He thereafter hired himself without specification. This means that he hired himself to remain with them for another year without restating the terms. Since he made no new stipulation, we assume that the renewed employment was on the original conditions, and the Rivash's proofs apply. But if he fixed a new wage and did not mention the earlier condition, the fixing of a new wage constitutes a new agreement, and we properly infer that the former condition was not renewed. The Ir Shushan's assertion that the prior condition continues whether the new wage was increased, reduced, or left unchanged has no adequate source.

But if he remained with them in silence, etc. The Rama's ruling requires study. The Rivash does not establish that mere silence always renews prior conditions. He distinguishes a prior arrangement that was valid and was simply extended from a prior act that was void from the outset, such as an additional marriage settlement written for a minor. In the former case, continuation may indicate renewal of the existing terms; in the latter, silence cannot validate what never took effect. Maharik likewise does not prove a broader rule. Accordingly, where the parties merely continue their relationship after the original period, one must examine whether their conduct shows an extension of the first contract or a new arrangement. Mere silence alone is not invariably sufficient, especially where the earlier condition concerned a matter that ordinarily requires express renewal.
He certainly is agreeable, etc. The Ramban's responsum states that where the worker knew the condition and the wage fixed by his father, and nevertheless entered the work without protest, he is presumed to have accepted what his father arranged. The Rama likewise speaks of a case in which the worker knew of the prior arrangement. The rule is not limited to a father; it applies equally where another person hired him and he knew of that person's agreement. Nor must he know the precise amount. If he knew that the employer or another had fixed terms with him and he began work, he is presumed to accept whatever was fixed.
The employer may retract, etc. Maharshal distinguishes between an oral waiver and a written one, maintaining that writing is effective to waive the obligation. His distinction does not appear correct. The opening of Ketubot and the authorities there indicate that writing alone does not create such a distinction.
However, with another worker, etc. Although Maharam, the Rashba, and the Mordechai imply that even an ordinary worker is not released merely when told to leave, because his person is acquired like that of a Hebrew servant, we do not rule this way. A worker is compared to a Hebrew servant only leniently, in that he may retract, not because his person is literally acquired. Tosafot explicitly state that an ordinary worker's person is not acquired. Maharam himself appears not to have relied principally upon that rationale, but only added it as support for another reason. Therefore, with an ordinary worker, once the employer unambiguously dismisses him, the continuing personal obligation ends unless some independent contractual liability remains.

He is exempt even without a waiver. That is, even if he already received the entire wage, he need not return any portion. The Beit Yosef cites the Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of Sefer Mitzvot Gadol that one who hires a teacher subject to a penalty must pay the penalty if he breaches, even without a formal act of acquisition, because this resembles the undertaking, 'If I leave the field fallow and do not cultivate it, I will pay.' The Beit Yosef leaves the matter requiring study, but the rule is explicit in Sefer Mitzvot Gadol and derives from Tosafot. The Hagahot Ashri, Agudah, Mordechai, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Rabbeinu Yerucham rule similarly. An undertaking to compensate for a defined loss caused by nonperformance is enforceable and is not treated as an unenforceable asmachta.
