What is the law of a worker who retracts? The Tur gives a fuller presentation of these rules. I will restate it with slight changes for clarity. A day laborer may retract even in the middle of the day and his hand is on the upper side. For example, if he was hired for eight dinars for the day and completed half the day, then retracted, even if wages rose so that the employer must pay another worker six dinars to finish the remaining half, the first worker is still entitled to four dinars for the half day he actually worked. We do not reduce his payment to two in order that the employer's total cost remain eight. This assumes he retracted for an ordinary reason. If he retracts solely because wages rose, we do not listen to him, as Rama explains, because the Sages did not enhance the worker's power in order to let him profit from the increase. Conversely, if wages fell so that the remaining half day can be completed for two dinars, the first worker is entitled to six. The employer withholds only two, the amount needed to complete the full day's work within the originally agreed total of eight. A contractor is different. He accepted responsibility to complete a particular job for a fixed amount and is not like a servant obligated to work continuously for his master throughout the day. He may choose what hours he works, like an independent owner producing work for sale. Therefore he cannot retract with the special protection of a laborer. If he accepted a job for eight, completed half, and retracted when the remaining half now costs six, he receives only two, so that the employer can complete the work for the original eight. If the remaining work now costs eight, he receives nothing. If the market rose so dramatically that completion will cost more than the original total, the contractor is not required to add money from his pocket. If he had not yet begun the work but merely traveled to do it, and then retracted after wages rose, the employer has none of the contractor's earnings in his hands because no work has yet been performed. The contractor therefore loses only the trouble of his travel, so long as the employer holds none of the contractor's property. From the Tur's wording, "since he holds nothing of his," we learn that if the employer does hold property of the contractor, even something other than tools, he may use that property to hire another worker so that the job is completed according to the original agreement. If wages fell and the remainder can be completed for two, the contractor still receives only four for the half already performed. The employer's retraction follows the inverse rule and his hand is on the lower side even with a contractor. If wages fell and the work can be completed for two, he must pay the original contractor six for the work already done and retain only two to finish the job. If wages rose, he pays four. From these principles the Mechaber's language becomes clear. When he says regarding a laborer, "we appraise what he did and he takes it," that primarily describes the ordinary case where the work became more expensive after the worker retracted. If wages fell, one instead appraises what remains to be done. The Tur follows the baraita's language, which mentions only appraisal of what was done for a laborer, probably because a worker ordinarily retracts when wages rise. Rama's words, "whether the work became more expensive or cheaper," mean that in both directions the worker has the upper hand, but the method of calculation differs according to the case. Similarly, when the Mechaber later says of a contractor that "we appraise what remains to be done," that mainly describes a rise in wages. His phrase "whether it became cheaper or did not, etc." should be understood according to each circumstance. Whether the work was initially cheap and later became expensive, or initially expensive and later became cheap, or remained at one level, the contractor's hand is always on the lower side. When wages rise, we appraise what remains; when they fall, we appraise what was already done. This is the opposite of the laborer's rule. Rama also writes that an employer who hired a teacher and later found another teacher better than the first cannot retract during the year once the first has begun, so long as the first is not negligent. The Rosh rules this in responsum, klal 104, siman 4. Darkei Moshe also cites Ramban that where the employer retracts he must pay in full because workers weaken when idle. See also siman 335.
