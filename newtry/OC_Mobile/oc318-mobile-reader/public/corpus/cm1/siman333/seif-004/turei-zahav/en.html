(Seif 5: If the employer took him back without specification, etc.) The Tur, citing the Rosh, distinguishes whether the worker's unavoidable interruption occurred at the beginning or end of the term. If the interruption occurred early and afterward the employer accepted him back without reserving a deduction, the employer is presumed to have waived the lost time; if it occurred at the end, there is no such proof. The Rosh derives this from the law of a Hebrew servant who was ill for three years and served three years. Yet the reasoning is difficult. If the employer has not paid, silence cannot be called “waiver”; it would be a new gift of money, which requires an acquisition. Even an express statement that he will not deduct appears to be a promise of a gift, not a new hiring. Why may the employer not say, “I assumed you would later make up the time”? Moreover, the Gemara concerning a convert and his wife teaches that when an obligation was extinguished, silence alone does not recreate it, and the Rivash applies that principle generally. Maharam therefore distinguishes instead according to possession: if the worker already received all his wages, he need not return them; if not, he cannot collect for the time he did not work. The Rama cites both views, creating an internal contradiction, since the Gemara's Hebrew-servant case itself involved illness first and later service. If the Rama accepts Maharam that advance payment may be retained, he should also allow the employer who has not paid to rely on Maharam despite later acceptance of the worker. The sources clearly dispute one another. In practice money should not be extracted from the possessor, and Maharam's view is primary. The result therefore depends on possession, not on whether the illness came at the beginning or end.
