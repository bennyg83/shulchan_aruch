Or the price fell. Tur rules that if a worker was hired for eight dinars for the day and, after half the day, market wages fell so that another worker can complete the remaining half for two, the first worker who retracts receives six. The employer withholds only the two needed to hire the replacement. The Shach objects. Since the agreed daily wage was eight, the worker should receive only four for the half-day actually worked. Why should the worker benefit from the later wage decline? Beit Yosef says the rule follows by symmetry from the case where market wages rose. The precise conceptual basis is developed in the next seif katan.
Tur's ruling is correct according to Responsa Maimoniyot, which derives a worker's right to retract from the law of a Hebrew servant who goes free through monetary deduction. The Torah calculates redemption in the manner favorable to the servant. If his value rose, the calculation follows the original lower sale price; if his value fell, it follows the current lower value for the remaining years. So too with a worker. If market wages rise, he may retract in the middle and accounts with the employer according to the original hiring rate. If market wages fall, the unearned remaining obligation is calculated according to the current lower value. The employer therefore deducts only what it now costs to complete the work. I found this explicitly in Responsa Maimoniyot concerning a teacher who retracts. If another teacher can be found for less, the first receives his full agreed wage minus only what must be paid to the substitute. Maharam of Rothenburg rules similarly. This is not based on agency between the two teachers. Even where the employer himself must hire the substitute, once another worker is available the matter is no longer a matter that will be lost, and the original worker's retraction is governed by the favorable redemption-like accounting. Therefore the principal halachah follows Tur.
