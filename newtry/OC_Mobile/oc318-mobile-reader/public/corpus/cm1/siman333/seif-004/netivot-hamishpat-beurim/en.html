And an employer who retracts has the law of a contractor. This implies that if the market price increased so that completing the work now costs six dinars, while four dinars were originally allocated to the half already completed, the employer who retracts must pay the worker four dinars for the completed half, as Tur states and Sema cites in seif katan 16. At first glance one should ask why the law is not stronger. If the contractor now has no other work available, why should the employer not pay him the entire contract price, less the amount he benefits from being idle, just as with an hourly worker in seif 2? Even if the case assumes other contract work is readily available, the distinction remains difficult. An hourly worker hired for the day suffers no loss if he can hire himself elsewhere for the remaining half-day. Had he stayed with the first employer he could not have performed the second job simultaneously. A contractor, however, does not have a fixed finishing time. He can say: even if your work had remained with me, I could still have accepted this other contract and performed it afterward when I finished yours. Therefore availability of another contract does not necessarily mitigate his loss. If the employer himself gives him another equivalent job from his own property, or procures another comparable contract for him, then the contractor can be required to accept it for purposes of reducing damages, but the employer may not ordinarily force him to perform a different job merely to avoid paying what is already due on the completed work. This illuminates siman 335, seif 1, which allows an employer, in some circumstances, to offer a worker equivalent work or even somewhat harder work with increased compensation where the worker demands full lost wages as damages. That rule applies only when the worker seeks payment for work not performed on the theory that the employer caused him loss. If the employer can eliminate the loss by offering comparable employment, the worker cannot demand damages. But where the worker seeks only the agreed compensation for work he already completed, he is entitled to that compensation. The employer cannot say, "Instead of paying you for what you did, perform another job for me." The worker was hired for a specified task and cannot be forced to substitute a different one. This is all the more true where the substitute is harder. Accordingly, the contractor receives the amount due for the work already completed, with the employer treated as the party whose position is weaker in the accounting.
