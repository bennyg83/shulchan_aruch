Or the wage decreased. The Tur writes that if he hired the worker for eight dinars and the market wage fell so that the remaining half-day can be completed for two dinars, the employer must pay the worker six dinars and may retain only two. The Ateret Zekenim and Sema copy this ruling, but I have found it in no other authority, and it appears incorrect. Although the worker may withdraw and his hand is not on the lower side, he is entitled only to the agreed rate, not to more than that rate. The agreement was eight dinars for a full day, namely four for a half-day. Why should the withdrawing worker have the upper hand and receive six? The Beit Yosef says that the rule follows simply by analogy from a rise in wages, but the cases differ. Where wages rose, the worker receives the agreed half-day amount, as though hired for half a day at four. Here the proposed six exceeds the agreed half-day rate. The Bach’s assertion that the Rosh equates a fall in wages is also incorrect; the Rosh discusses a fall where the employer withdrew, not where the worker withdrew. Tosafot and Rashi support my view. I later found an old parchment manuscript of the Tur that omits this ruling.

He gives him a shekel. A shekel is two dinars. Alternatively, the workers must complete the job. If the remaining work cannot be completed for less than eight dinars, the employer gives the withdrawing worker nothing. If the price has risen so greatly that completion will cost more than the original total wage, the worker need not pay the excess from his own funds. Therefore, if the worker withdrew before beginning, even if he had already traveled, he loses only the value of his effort in traveling, since the employer holds none of his property and the worker need not pay from his own pocket. The Beit Yosef and Sema explain that had the employer possessed the worker’s property, he could have collected the entire loss, relying on the Ramban and Rashba that where the craftsman’s tools are in the employer’s hands, even in work not subject to loss he may hire replacements or mislead the worker up to the value of the job. The Bach objects that the Tur later limits this rule to work subject to loss. Moreover, the Ramban and Rashba speak specifically of the craftsman’s tools, not any property. The Tur’s phrase ‘since he is not holding any of his property’ sounds broader than tools. It also would have been phrased as a qualification had that been the intent. The Bach therefore explains that the Tur means the worker is not holding the employer’s property. If the worker had already received wages from the employer, he would have to return them all. This is supported by the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef, who state that the worker need not pay from his own pocket more than the amount fixed. This implies that up to the agreed amount he must return funds already received.
If the remaining work was worth two dinars, etc., he gives only a sela, etc. The Raavad comments that he gives only a sela worth two dinars. The Maggid Mishneh and Kesef Mishneh do not explain his intent, and the Maggid Mishneh’s supercommentary confuses the issue. In my view, this law is not stated in the Bavli, Rif, or other authorities. Since a contractor’s account is calculated according to the value of the work remaining, and that remainder is worth only two dinars, he receives only a sela of two dinars, not four. The Raavad may be explaining the Rambam in this manner. I found the Tosefta and Yerushalmi stating expressly: Rabbi Dosa says the remaining work is appraised. If it is worth six dinars, he is given a shekel or completes the work; if it is worth a shekel, he is given a shekel. The Yerushalmi rules like Rabbi Dosa, as do the authorities. Although the Rambam’s wording is difficult to read this way, it is hard to suppose that he disputes the Tosefta and Yerushalmi. The Tur similarly writes that if the price fell and the remaining work can be completed for two dinars, the worker nevertheless receives four. He should instead receive only two. Perhaps the Tur understands the Tosefta and Yerushalmi to concern a case where the remaining work was originally worth only two but the contractor had been hired for more; then he receives only a shekel. Where the market fell after he performed part of the work at the earlier higher rate, the Tur may hold that he is not paid solely according to the present lower value. This is strained, for the plain Tosefta indicates that because the worker withdrew and his hand is on the lower side, he receives only two in every case. Perhaps the Rambam and Tur reason that since the Bavli cites the baraita but omits the clause ‘if it is worth a shekel, he is given a shekel,’ the Bavli rejects that clause. The matter requires study.
