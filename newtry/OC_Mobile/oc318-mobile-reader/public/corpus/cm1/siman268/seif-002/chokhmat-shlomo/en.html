Seif 3. Maharshal holds that one's courtyard acquires a lost object even though the object entered the courtyard before the owner's despair. A scholar asked me whether this contradicts Bava Metzia and Chullin, which say that it cannot be that a person's hand fails to acquire while his courtyard acquires. There is no contradiction. The Gemara's principle is parallel to the general rule that an agent cannot perform an act that the principal himself cannot perform. If a courtyard functions as the owner's hand, it cannot do what his hand cannot do; if it functions as an agent, an agent cannot do what the principal cannot do. This applies when the very act now being attempted is one the person could not lawfully perform in any manner. Thus, with sending away the mother bird, the Torah itself forbids taking the mother at that moment, so neither his hand nor his courtyard as agent can acquire it. By contrast, where a lost object physically entered his domain before despair, the reason his hand cannot acquire it afterward is historical: it previously came to him in a prohibited state and he became obligated to return it. An unattended courtyard does not create that personal obligation merely because the object entered there before despair. For liability, the courtyard was not acting as his agent without his knowledge. It can therefore be viewed as becoming his acquiring agent only now, after despair. The present act of acquisition is one his own hand could perform if the object first came to him now after despair. Hence there is no violation of the rule that an agent cannot do what the principal cannot do. This distinction is logically clear. In siman 198, however, I brought proof supporting the Shach against Maharshal.
