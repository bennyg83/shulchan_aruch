Seif 1, gloss: Unlike those who say otherwise. See above siman 198, seif 12, where the rulings appear contradictory. Mishneh LaMelech seems to note this, and I resolved it in my responsum concerning sale of chametz, Orach Chaim siman 448.
There, seif 1. From Ran to Nedarim 34 one can derive a novel rule. If ownerless or lost property lies within Reuven's four cubits such that he could acquire it for himself, he may direct that those four cubits acquire it for Shimon. The principle is the same as one who lifts a found object for another: since he could acquire it for himself, he can acquire it for his fellow. The same reasoning can operate through four cubits. From Rabbi Eliezer of Metz, cited by the Rosh there, one may derive another rule. If ownerless property lies near Reuven but outside his four cubits, yet Reuven is the nearest person and could immediately take it, he may declare it consecrated before actually lifting it, because it is within his power to acquire. By the same logic he may sell it. However, this declaration takes effect only if he later actually acquires the object. The fact that it was within his power prevents the declaration from being treated as dealing with something not yet in existence, but it does not itself make the object his against another claimant. If another person acquires it first, the consecration or sale is void. If Reuven later acquires it, the prior declaration can then take effect. It is doubtful whether he may retract before he acquires it. Logically he should be able to retract. This distinction has practical significance mainly for consecration. On rereading the Rosh, it is explicit that the case is where he says, "When I acquire this, it shall be consecrated." Thus before acquisition nothing takes effect; only after he acquires it does the consecration attach. There, same seif. Yoma 22 asks whether the four cubits used in the contest for removal of ashes are measured together with the cubit of the foundation. I find the question difficult. If the rule is that the priest within four cubits of the ashes acquires priority, one can understand the four-cubit measure by analogy to acquisition through four cubits. But if the total distance is six cubits from the altar, what legal significance would six cubits have rather than one hundred? This requires study.
