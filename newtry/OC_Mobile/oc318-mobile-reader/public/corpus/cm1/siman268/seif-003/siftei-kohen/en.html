A person's courtyard acquires for him, etc. If the item entered his courtyard before the owner despaired, Maharshal writes in Yam Shel Shlomo, Perek Eilu Metziot, that Tosafos proves that with respect to a courtyard we do not apply the rule of despair without knowledge together with the principle that the item came into his possession unlawfully. Otherwise, what proof could Tosafos bring from the cases of a shop and a moneychanger's table? This is contrary to the Nimukei Yosef and the Rosh, who hold that a courtyard is like the person himself. Maharshal writes this, but Maharsha there challenges him, explaining that Tosafos understands the case of the shop as involving a majority of non-Jews, as Tosafos writes later regarding the shop, that one may retain the reading "bundled" and explain the case as one involving a majority of non-Jews. I disagree with Maharshal, though not for Maharsha's reason. According to Maharsha, Tosafos itself only writes later that it is possible to retain the reading "bundled" by explaining the case as one involving a majority of non-Jews. If so, what proof can Tosafos bring from the shop? Perhaps Rashi's reading, which omits "bundled," is correct and the case involves a majority of Jews. Rather, there was never any difficulty at the outset. It is obvious that the reason in the case of the shop is not despair without knowledge, for otherwise the Mishnah concerning the shop would contradict Rava, who holds that despair without knowledge constitutes despair. How could Rava disagree with a Mishnah? Likewise, in the Gemara on page 22b, where Rava is refuted from the teaching of Rabbi Yochanan in the name of Rabbi Yishmael ben Yehotzadak, the refutation is relatively weak, as is evident to one who studies the passage. Why was he not refuted from the Mishnah concerning the shop, which would be a strong refutation? Rather, it is obvious that the Mishnah accords with all opinions. Tosafos therefore proves that the reason for the Mishnah is that a courtyard does not acquire an item that its owner might never find. One must also say that the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef did not mean that there is no other explanation besides this one. They themselves first cite Tosafos's explanation. This is also explicit in the Nimukei Yosef on the Mishnah of one who finds an item in a shop: he first explains the Mishnah according to Abaye, and then explains it according to Rava as a courtyard that is not guarded. Therefore, the primary ruling follows the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef, that despair without knowledge also applies to one's courtyard. The Ran likewise writes, regarding the man who found pitch in a winepress: "If you ask, why should the winepress not acquire it for its owner, one may answer that it was there before the owner despaired. His courtyard is no better than his hand, and wherever an item came into his possession unlawfully, he never acquires it." The Nimukei Yosef cites this there. The Raavad likewise writes in his glosses on Rambam, chapter 9 of Hilchot Gezelah, law 4: why should his courtyard not acquire it for him? Because it is not a guarded courtyard, and he did not say, "Let my shop acquire it for me," etc. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes that some say that even if he says, "Let my shop acquire it for me," he does not acquire it because the lost object came there before despair and his courtyard is like his hand. Rashba likewise writes this. Nevertheless, the Maggid Mishneh appears to have understood that the Raavad intended to challenge Rambam on this point and accepted that challenge. I do not agree. There is no challenge here at all, and the Raavad did not intend to challenge Rambam. It is entirely possible that Rambam also holds as a matter of law that one's courtyard is like one's hand, but he could not explain the Mishnah concerning the shop that way, because the Mishnah must also accord with Rava, as explained above. Moreover, Rambam explicitly speaks of a majority of non-Jews and reads "bundled and placed" in the case of the shop, following the reading of Tosafos, and explains that the case involves a majority of non-Jews. He therefore could not explain the reason as despair without knowledge. The Raavad likewise cannot have intended to challenge him for this reason. Rather, he meant only that according to the law as it applies to us, since we rule like Abaye, a courtyard does not acquire for him in a case involving a majority of Jews when the item arrived there before despair. Thus, as a matter of practical law, no one disputes that his courtyard is like his hand. This is the primary ruling, contrary to Maharshal. Examine carefully.
Acquires for him, etc. Meaning, even if he is not standing beside it. This is the Sema's explanation. The same applies even if he is not in the city at all, as is clear from Rashi and Tosafos in Perek Shor Shenagach Et HaParah, page 49b, and is also implied by the poskim.
When does this apply? In a guarded courtyard, etc. This implies that with a guarded courtyard, according to all opinions, he need not say, "Let my field acquire it for me," because it acquires even without his knowledge. What the Tur writes at the end of this siman in the name of Rambam, that even when it is guarded while he is inside it he must say, "Let my field acquire it for me," must be a scribal error. The Gemara proves in several places that he need not say anything, and Rambam's statement applies only to an unguarded courtyard when he is standing beside it. The Bach likewise writes that this is a scribal error.
Rather, when he knows, etc. See Raavan at the end of Perek Shnayim Ochazin, page 93d.
His courtyard does not acquire for him, etc. The same is true of his hand: it does not acquire for him without his knowledge in this type of case. This is clear from the poskim who are the source of this ruling, and it is also implied above, siman 232, seif 18, in the gloss. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 148, and the cited work, page 102a.
If judgment has already been rendered, etc. This is from Terumat HaDeshen, siman 310, who brought proof from Perek HaCholetz, Yevamot page 36. See my Sefer Tekfo Kohen at its conclusion, where I challenged Terumat HaDeshen both conceptually and from the Gemara, and demonstrated from the passage there that even after judgment has been rendered, he must give him one item of merchandise by virtue of a double uncertainty. This applies specifically when both incidents occurred with the same person. But where one claimant comes through another person, since there are two different persons, we do not apply a double uncertainty to the property, and there is no distinction between whether judgment has already been rendered or not. See there, where I discussed this at length.
And some say, when he is standing, etc. What the Maggid Mishneh writes, that the Yerushalmi states that he must say, "Let my field acquire it for me," is, in my humble opinion, incorrect. The Yerushalmi contains the same rule as Perek HaShoel, page 102a. See there.
