A person's courtyard acquires for him without his knowledge. Maharit argues that a person's own hand should acquire without conscious intention all the more so than his courtyard. If a courtyard acquires without knowledge, surely an object actually in one's hand should. This depends on the dispute between Rashi and Tosafot. Rashi in Ketubot requires a three handbreadth lifting even when the object is in a person's hand. Tosafot objects that one's hand should be no worse than one's courtyard, citing divorce delivered into a woman's basket. According to Tosafot, a person's hand is itself a domain and can therefore acquire like a courtyard without a separate conscious lifting act. According to Rashi, however, the hand is not treated as a courtyard for ordinary monetary acquisition. It acquires only through the recognized kinyan of lifting, and ordinary acquisition acts require intention. Therefore an object merely placed in a person's hand without his awareness does not automatically become his. Shitah Mekubetzet defends Rashi. The Torah's law of divorce, "he shall place it in her hand," does not necessarily define ordinary property acquisition. Monetary acquisition is learned through the established modes of lifting, meshichah, and delivery. The woman's basket can work as her domain, but an ordinary hand may still require the act of lifting. Accordingly, under Rashi, Maharit's a fortiori argument from courtyard to hand does not stand.
