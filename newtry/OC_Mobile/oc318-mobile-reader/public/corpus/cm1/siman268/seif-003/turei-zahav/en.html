(If judgment has already been rendered.) I have room for considerable analysis regarding Terumat HaDeshen’s proof. He derives it from Yevamot 37, concerning a doubtful son and the sons of a yavam who came to divide the yavam’s estate after the yavam had earlier divided the deceased brother’s estate. Initially, the doubtful son and the yavam litigated over the estate of the yavam’s deceased brother. The doubtful son claimed, “I am the deceased’s son and inherit everything,” while the yavam claimed, “You are my son and have no share in my brother’s estate.” They divided the estate because property subject to such a doubt is divided. Later the yavam died leaving substantial property of his own. The doubtful son then litigated with the yavam’s sons, arguing: “In either event, if I am your brother, give me an equal share with you. If I am the deceased’s son, your father previously took half the deceased’s estate improperly; return it to me.” Rabbi Abba rules that the prior judgment stands. Rashi explains that once judgment was rendered concerning the deceased’s estate, the doubtful son cannot reopen it, and regarding what he now seeks from the yavam’s estate we tell him to bring proof. Terumat HaDeshen writes that logically the doubtful son should always receive something through his either-or claim, but where the prior division was made according to law, he removed it from his mind and despaired of the share taken by the yavam, and it became the yavam’s property. Likewise, if judgment was already rendered that an item taken by one person from another’s courtyard belongs to the taker, the courtyard owner removed it from his mind from that time. This is difficult in light of the Tur’s ruling in siman 31 concerning two groups of witnesses that contradict each other. If a creditor has two notes against one debtor, one supported by each group, he may collect on the larger note and then collect on the other before a different beit din. Why must he use a different beit din? Even before the same beit din he should collect, because the prior judgment stands once he already collected according to law. One cannot distinguish that in siman 31 the beit din did not initially know of the second note and the first collection was therefore somewhat mistaken, for Yevamot compares its case to one who purchased a field from one of four possible owners and another came with a claim, concerning which the Sages and Admon dispute. One may answer that Yevamot itself contains a dispute, and one opinion holds that the judgment is reopened. Rosh’s words imply that the law was not conclusively decided in favor of “the judgment stands,” and that we leave the property with the yavam’s sons only because of doubt. This explains siman 31, where the creditor now seeks to extract with the second note, and perhaps the prior judgment should be reopened. According to this, it appears to me that if the first purchaser later seized property from the second purchaser, we would not remove it from him. Nor can one say that this is a doubt in law for which seizure is ineffective, because neither the first nor second possession was unlawful. The entire matter is doubtful. Terumat HaDeshen himself says at the end of the responsum, in the gloss, that the latter seizure was not unlawful. The same should apply when the first party later seizes. This follows Terumat HaDeshen’s reasoning. In siman 280, however, I challenged the very basis of his proof from another angle. In my view this ruling is not clearly established, particularly since many great authorities rule like the opinion that the judgment is reopened, as Beit Yosef writes in Even HaEzer 163 in the name of Baal HaMaor.
