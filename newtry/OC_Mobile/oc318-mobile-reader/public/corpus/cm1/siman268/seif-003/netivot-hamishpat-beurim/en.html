A person's courtyard. See Shach, seif katan 3, concerning an item that enters one's courtyard before the owner has despaired. See what I wrote above in siman 262. Maharit, volume 1, siman 150, writes that one's hand likewise acquires without his awareness. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that according to Rashi in Ketubot, who holds that lifting three handbreadths is required, the hand cannot be compared to a courtyard. In my humble opinion, one's hand certainly is no weaker than one's courtyard. Rather, the entire object must lie within the space of his hand, in which case the hand is like his courtyard. If he grasps only part of the object while the rest lies outside the space of his hand, then just as a courtyard does not acquire an object lying partly outside it, his hand does not acquire by virtue of being his hand. He can acquire only by lifting, and for that specifically a lifting of three handbreadths is required.
Where he knows of the found object. I am troubled, for the Mordechai derived this rule from an item found in a ruin and from one found in a shop. Rambam and Rosh must therefore reject this reasoning, since the Rosh explains those cases as despair without awareness and Rambam explains them as property lost from everyone. Tosafot likewise explain that the item was not ordinarily likely to be found. Furthermore, Mordechai derives the rule from the case in the chapter Shor Shenagach concerning a convert's pledge in the possession of a Jew. For this reason he rejects Rashi and Tosafot there, who explain that the case specifically concerns an unguarded courtyard. According to Mordechai, even a guarded courtyard does not acquire unless the item is of a kind ordinarily likely to come there, or like fledglings that commonly occur in a field. Money and objects are not normally found there, and therefore the courtyard does not acquire them. Yet the Mechaber in siman 72, seif 39, rules that in a guarded courtyard the owner does acquire. Mordechai states explicitly that according to his view even a guarded courtyard does not acquire, and even if the convert dies in the Jew's courtyard, the Jew does not acquire the gold in the convert's belt. There is another difficulty. Darkei Moshe states that Nimukei Yosef agrees with Mordechai, but Nimukei Yosef expressly adopts Tosafot's view, namely that only an item that is not ordinarily likely ever to come to the courtyard owner's attention, such as treasure hidden in a wall, is excluded. This implies that if a found item would certainly reach the owner unless another person takes it, the courtyard acquires it even though such a find, such as money or objects, is uncommon. Nimukei Yosef thus disagrees with Mordechai. It therefore appears to me that Darkei Moshe and Rama, in saying "a matter that does not ordinarily come there," mean a circumstance in which for that very reason it may never reach the owner's attention. Their statement that he "considers it possible" means that he sometimes considers going to that place and therefore the item is destined eventually to be found and come into his possession. By contrast, treasure hidden in a ruin or wall is a place where he never considers searching, and it may never reach him, so his courtyard does not acquire it. In this manner Mordechai too can be interpreted, albeit somewhat strained, in order to harmonize him with Rambam, Rosh, Rashi, Tosafot, Nimukei Yosef, and the many other authorities cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. If this is not the interpretation, then in my humble opinion the Rama's gloss is missing the words "some say," because the broader rule would represent only Mordechai's isolated opinion, while all the early authorities disagree. The decisive factor is whether the item is ordinarily likely to be found, as explained.
Merchandise at a low price. At first glance this is difficult. Even if the buyer physically took the merchandise, so long as the seller had not transferred it to him, the seller could sell it to another. Moreover, a seller's utensil located in the buyer's domain does not effect acquisition, as explained in siman 200. It therefore appears that the case concerns merchandise brought into the buyer's courtyard without any further act, and that the seller brought the merchandise and fixed its price, declaring that he wished to sell it for that amount. Proof is that the text specifies merchandise brought cheaply. If no price had been fixed, how would we know that it was cheap? The seller therefore stated the price at which he wished to sell. Siman 200, seif 11, rules that if a buyer takes an article from a seller and its price is fixed, the seller cannot retract once the buyer lifts it intending to acquire. See Sema, seif katan 13, who explains that a price expressly fixed by the seller is equivalent to a generally fixed market price. Since the seller could not retract if the buyer had taken the merchandise into his hand, the latter view holds that the same applies when it enters his courtyard and he says, "my courtyard shall acquire for me." A courtyard is derived from the legal concept of one's hand, and just as the seller cannot retract when the buyer takes it in his hand, so too when it enters his courtyard. The first view holds that the Torah expanded courtyard to function as one's hand only for a found object, where no payment is lacking. Here, since the buyer was present but had not yet set aside the item for himself and had not paid, we say that perhaps he will not obtain the money, and he does not acquire. According to this, if he says, "my courtyard shall acquire for me," has the money in his possession and wishes to pay, and another person then seizes the article and performs meshichah, the first buyer acquires.
