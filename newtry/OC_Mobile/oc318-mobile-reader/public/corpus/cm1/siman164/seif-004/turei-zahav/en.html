(Seif 5: The owner of the upper story, etc.) The Maggid Mishneh reports that the Rashba asks why the upper owner cannot compel the homeowner to rebuild. He writes that the Yerushalmi establishes the case where the homeowner is absent and the court does not descend to his property. This appears to be the Rambam’s position: where compulsion is possible, the court compels him, as stated at the beginning of the chapter, while here the upper owner chooses not to compel him. This is also the Tur’s view, as the Beit Yosef explains. The Rashba himself, however, does not agree, as the Beit Yosef cites from Nimukei Yosef. The Maggid Mishneh likewise writes that according to the Rashba the court never descends to the homeowner’s other property, because only this particular house was subjected to the obligation. This contradicts the Sema’s distinction in seif katan 1 between seif 7 and seif 1, where he maintains that the lower owner cannot be compelled here to rebuild. According to the Rashba, the inability to descend to his property applies here as well. According to the Tur, he may be compelled and the court may descend to his property. The Tur therefore necessarily rejects the Rashba, for with respect to the lien upon the owner’s property there is no distinction between ordinary deterioration and total collapse. Rather, the Rambam and Ramban understand the rule that the upper owner descends to live below as applying where he does not wish to compel the lower owner. A further difficulty arises from siman 165, where the upper owner plants below, meaning vegetables and similar produce, as the Sema explains. It is obvious that he previously had other crops above. How can this limited substitute suffice, and why should he be unable to compel rebuilding? The answer must be that the Mishna discusses a case where he chooses not to compel. The two laws in the Mishna are therefore entirely parallel.
