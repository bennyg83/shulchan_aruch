The owner of the upper story can compel him. Meaning that beit din may descend to the lower owner's property to enforce payment. This differs from seif 5, where the entire house and upper story fell and the lower owner cannot be compelled to rebuild. Here only the walls deteriorated and remain repairable. Sema. See the Beurim for the reason. One should not interpret the compulsion as forcing the upper owner to move downstairs as in seif 5, because once the walls have deteriorated the lower area is not fit for residence:
If he wishes. The house owner will ordinarily not object if repairs are not made. On the contrary, he may prefer it, because then the upper owner will not live there and burden the structure. Sema. If there is danger that the damaged upper structure will fall onto the ceiling and break it, the upper owner must repair it. Taz:
And the ceiling itself. Since the upper owner must also maintain the roof, he can say, "I accepted the expense of the roof on condition that I have a stable place to stand in my upper story just as you have ground beneath your lower story. Therefore we should divide the structural expense, I above and you below." The plaster layer, however, serves only to level depressions for the benefit of the upper resident and also to prevent his spilled water from damaging the lower resident. Its expense therefore rests on the upper owner. Sema:
The house owner is not obligated. The reason is that the upper owner presumably entered the arrangement knowing that the lower owner would not repair the ceiling for him, because the lower owner is not concerned about the ceiling when the roof protects him. The upper owner also cannot remove the roof, because the roof is subject to the lower owner's rights. This applies where only the ceiling deteriorated. But if the entire house and upper story collapsed and they wish to rebuild, the lower owner must certainly build the house and ceiling, while the upper owner builds his upper story and roof. Sema.
