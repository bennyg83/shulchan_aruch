The distinction between walls that have merely deteriorated and a structure that has entirely fallen can be understood from the general law of partners. Where repair is necessary to prevent loss to both parties and neither can remedy the problem without benefiting the other, each can compel the other to contribute his share. But where the matter is merely an improvement and no imminent loss will result, one cannot force the other. Thus if the house has already fallen and is now being rebuilt, it is like creating a new improvement. The rules of seif 5 apply. If the walls remain but are deteriorating, failure to repair will harm both parties, so they can compel one another. Here, however, the lower walls are the responsibility of the house owner and the roof the responsibility of the upper owner, because the parties are presumed to have entered their arrangement on that basis. Each therefore repairs the portion under his control. Where only one party would suffer, the other is not obligated to assist.

But regarding a landlord and tenant. See Sema, seif katan 4. The view of the Levush appears to follow Ramah in siman 312, seif 18, concerning one who rents "this upper story over this house." According to that opinion the landlord need not repair the upper-story walls, only the ceiling. Levush does not cite Rama's opposing view there, indicating that he follows Ramah. The present case necessarily concerns that formulation. If one rented merely "this upper story," collapse terminates the lease. If one rented an unspecified upper story, the landlord must provide another. Therefore, this case fits Ramah's distinction and the Levush is consistent.
