Seif 7. (We do not listen to him because he increases the load.) If a person owns a house or shop above his fellow's house or cellar and wishes to divide his upper premises into two by adding a partition, the lower owner may object if the added wall and the increased traffic will weaken the intervening ceiling. This is so even though the upper owner could ordinarily place very heavy objects in his own premises, perhaps heavier than the wall and the additional occupants. The ordinary use of the premises without the division is not expected to create this continuing additional load, and an ad hoc decision to store heavy articles is not as permanent as a structural division. If there is no immediate weakening of the lower owner's property and the only concern is that vibration over time may eventually contribute to damage, the case may fall under the general rule that the injured party must distance himself unless the harm is comparable to direct arrows, in which case the actor must distance himself. If the vibration itself causes no present injury, but only after many years, once one of the lower building's wooden supports has decayed, will the earlier vibration contribute to collapse, and at that later time the vibration will no longer be occurring, there is room to say that the lower owner cannot prevent the construction now. This may depend upon the broader dispute discussed elsewhere concerning one person benefiting where the other presently suffers no loss. According to the practice that allows the owner to build as he wishes while there is no present loss, subject to removing the cause once a concrete injury develops, the upper owner may erect the partition now and remove the additional burden before the lower structure deteriorates. The earlier vibration would not have been direct harmful force. Because the matter is doubtful, however, the one who seeks to change the existing condition bears the burden of proof. Therefore, when permitting present benefit on the ground that the other presently loses nothing, one should also ensure that the future dispute will not be left to an uncertain burden of proof merely because the builder will then be in possession. He should provide an effective written undertaking that if a reasonable doubt of damage later arises he will remove the added burden. This is particularly appropriate in places where parties customarily rely upon the court's register and are not expected to preserve private documents indefinitely.
