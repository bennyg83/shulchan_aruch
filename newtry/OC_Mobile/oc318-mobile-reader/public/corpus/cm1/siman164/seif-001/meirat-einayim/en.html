The owner of the upper story can compel him. In the Perishah I explained that this coercion means through bans or collection from his property. Only in seif 5 below, where the entire house and upper story collapsed, can the lower owner not be compelled to rebuild. Here the walls did not collapse; they merely deteriorated and remain subject to repair. Nor can the coercion here mean that the upper owner descends and lives below, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 165 regarding a damaged olive press, and in siman 312:8 regarding a damaged upper story. Since the walls themselves are deteriorated, the place is not fit for residence.
The owner of the upper story may repair it if he wishes. This is made dependent on his choice because the lower owner certainly has no objection if the upper walls remain unrepaired. On the contrary, he may prefer their deterioration so the upper owner cannot live there and burden him. The ruling teaches only that the upper owner cannot compel the lower owner to repair the upper walls, or even to participate in the expense. He may repair them himself if he wishes.
The ceiling itself belongs to the owner of the house. The Tur explains: the upper owner can say to the lower owner, 'I received an upper story corresponding to your house. Just as you stand on the ground that fell to your share, provide me with my ground.' The floor of the upper owner is the ceiling. One might ask why the lower owner must bear the entire ceiling expense. He should at least own half the structural floor and the upper owner only the other half. The answer is that the essential reasoning is reciprocal allocation of structural expenses. The upper owner must provide the ceiling or roof above his own upper story. He can therefore say: I accepted the expense of the upper covering on condition that I receive a complete standing surface for my upper story, just as you receive the earth below. Thus each bears the corresponding structural layer, lower and upper. This reasoning applies only to the ceiling itself, without which the upper owner cannot stand. The plaster or fill above it serves mainly to level depressions for the upper owner's benefit and to prevent his spilled water from damaging the lower premises, which would be direct harm. Therefore its expense belongs to the upper owner. This is also why the Mechaber, following Rambam, says 'the plastering belongs to the upper owner,' meaning its cost is his because it was made for him.
The owner of the house is not obligated, etc. The Tur explains that an upper-story owner ordinarily enters on the understanding that the lower owner will not repair the ceiling for him, because the lower owner does not care about the ceiling when a roof above protects him. Even if the upper owner threatens to remove the roof so that the lower owner will need the ceiling, he may be prevented, because the roof is also servient to the lower house. Rain falling on the ceiling will eventually penetrate below. From the outset their division assumed that the upper owner would maintain his roof so rain would not damage the lower premises, but the lower owner undertook no separate obligation to provide the upper owner's footing. This explains Rama's gloss. In the Derishah I cited Rashi on the Mishnah of a house and upper story that both collapsed. Rashi says the lower owner must build the lower walls and ceiling, while the upper owner builds from the ceiling upward and provides the upper roof. Thus Rashi too places the ceiling over the house on the lower owner. This must refer specifically to total collapse, where when rebuilding from scratch the lower owner must construct a complete house and a house inherently has a ceiling. The upper owner then builds his upper story and its roof. The Tur and Rama's statement here that the upper owner repairs the ceiling concerns an already existing building where only the ceiling later became damaged. Since each entered initially on the understanding that he repairs what he needs, and the lower owner does not need the ceiling because the roof still protects him, repair falls on the upper owner. Rama's conclusion that in a landlord-tenant relationship the lower owner must repair is based on a different rule: a landlord renting an upper story must provide a complete dwelling, including its ceiling, roof, and walls. Ir Shushan's distinction between ceiling and upper walls in an ordinary rental is therefore not convincing. If he argues that the dwelling was originally sound and later deteriorated because of the tenant's fortune, the same argument could be made concerning the ceiling. This is clear.
