In the case of a house and upper story that collapsed, if the owner of the house refuses to rebuild, the upper owner may build the house and live in it until reimbursed. Nimukei Yosef asks why the lower owner is not simply compelled to rebuild. He cites the Jerusalem Talmud, which establishes the case where the lower owner is absent. Ramban accepts this, while Rashba argues that according to our law, which allows collection from an absent debtor in appropriate cases, even absence would not prevent compulsion. Rashba therefore holds that the lower owner's other assets were never personally encumbered to rebuild the house. The upper owner's right is only against the building itself. Beit Yosef, however, says Rambam and the Tur follow Ramban, that the lower owner can in principle be compelled because his property is personally obligated for the rebuilding. This underlying dispute affects the later analysis of whether the upper owner's expenditure is treated as a loan to the lower owner.
Rabbi Yehudah says the upper owner who rebuilds and then dwells in the lower house must pay rent because otherwise the arrangement resembles interest. Rivash asked how the Gemara can cite this as proof that one who benefits while the other does not lose must pay. Perhaps Rabbi Yehudah's reason is only the interest concern: the upper owner advanced building costs and then receives free use of the lower owner's house in return. Rivash answers that the continuation of Rabbi Yehudah's statement shows the rule is not based solely on interest. Rabbi Yehudah says instead that the lower owner rebuilds the house and the upper story is rebuilt, and the upper owner dwells until reimbursed. According to the Gemara's initial assumption, that too is a case of benefit without loss. If every such free dwelling were prohibited as interest, the arrangement would remain impermissible. Thus the passage indicates that this involuntary rebuilding arrangement does not automatically have the status of an ordinary loan for interest purposes. Rashi nevertheless explicitly mentions interest and also says Rabbi Yehudah holds that one who benefits while the other does not lose is liable. The two points can coexist. If the dweller is legally obligated to pay rent because he received benefit, then waiving that rent for him would indeed amount to interest, even though the underlying advance was forced. But if such benefit were legally exempt, one might argue the free dwelling is merely an uncompensated benefit rather than interest arising from a voluntary loan. Rashi therefore gives both grounds. Tosafot objects to Rashi that if the house burned before reimbursement, the lower owner would not necessarily owe the expenditure, showing that there was no true loan. This suggests the transaction is conditional upon the building surviving and the owner choosing to reimburse. Yet once reimbursement occurs, the courtyard can be viewed as having acquired the construction retroactively, much like Nimukei Yosef's explanation of a neighbor who later pays for a wall built on his land. That retroactive acquisition creates a difficult interest question. If the owner acquires the building from the earlier time once he pays, the upper owner's free use meanwhile can look like payment for extending the time until reimbursement. One can answer that because the lower owner had the option never to reimburse and the upper owner would then bear the loss if the house burned, the arrangement is at most a contingent form of interest. According to the authorities who permit one sided contingent interest, this may be allowed. Rashi's analysis is especially strong according to Rambam, Ramban, and the Tur, who hold that the lower owner's assets are actually obligated to rebuild. In that view a debt exists immediately. The upper owner, by building from his own funds and giving the lower owner time, is effectively extending credit. Free dwelling can then be genuine interest. Even if the specific burned building would not be reimbursed because the upper owner chose to build without forcing the lower owner first, the underlying duty to rebuild remains. The tension between Rashi and Tosafot therefore depends upon the nature of that duty.
