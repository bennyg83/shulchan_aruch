And he sits and dwells in it. Mechaber abbreviates the law and does not explain what happens if the house owner wishes to rebuild but the upper owner refuses to rebuild the upper story and roof it. The Yerushalmi appears to address this. Since the roof is ordinarily the responsibility of the upper owner, can the house owner compel him to roof it? The Yerushalmi proves from a case of two adjoining ruined houses with a shared wall that, despite the shared need, one cannot compel the other to rebuild. Rather, if one party builds, he may recover the other's expenses afterward. The same applies to the roof. The lower owner cannot force the upper owner to build the roof. He may build it himself and then remain in possession until the upper owner reimburses his expenses.
All his expenses. Why does he receive only his expenses and not the increase in value, when one who descends into another's property without permission may receive the increased value once the owner reveals that he desires the improvement? The answer is that such recovery of increased value applies where the improver intended to improve for the owner. Here his primary intention was his own need, namely to obtain a place to live. He did not intend to improve the other's property. Shach in siman 391, seif katan 2, likewise requires intent to improve for the other. Although Mechaber says that he "sits and dwells in it," it appears that he may also rent it to others and take the rent, and even rent it to the house owner himself. Although ordinary Sura mortgages can raise interest concerns, here the arrangement is not a loan at all. Tosafot explain that if the house burns the other party does not owe the expenses, and Rivash likewise explains that the arrangement is imposed against his will and therefore is not a loan. Renting it in this manner is therefore permitted.
