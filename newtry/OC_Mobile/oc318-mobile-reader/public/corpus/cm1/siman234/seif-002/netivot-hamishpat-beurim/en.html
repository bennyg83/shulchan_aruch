And it became known that it was tereifah. If the buyer mixed this meat with kosher meat, causing the remaining meat to become prohibited, it appears that according to the Rama above in siman 25:1, who follows Tosafot and the Rosh in ruling like Rabbi Meir, the seller is liable. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 100, s.v. Tihar Et HaTamei, write that according to Rabbi Meir one who wrongly rules impure food pure is liable even where the owner himself later mixed it with his produce. Here too the seller acted intentionally, since he knowingly sold the meat as kosher. Even according to those who exempt in that other case, the Shach there, seif katan 2, distinguishes it from showing a coin to a money changer. Once a person has shown a coin to a professional, he has no reason to show it to another; whereas after asking a halachic authority, the owner might still have reason to seek further clarification. Also, in the money changer case it is evident that the owner relied upon his appraisal, while in the halachic case the authority might have ruled under intellectual compulsion without the owner necessarily relying fully on him. None of those considerations applies here. The seller intentionally represented the meat as kosher. What reason did the buyer have to make any further inquiry? It therefore appears that the seller is liable according to all opinions.
And he returns their money. See Sema, seif katan 4, who gives two reasons why one who sold a Torah prohibited food must return the purchase price. First, the Sages penalized him. Second, eating something prohibited by Torah law is not treated as a compensable benefit to the buyer. A practical difference arises if the buyer mixed the prohibited liquid into sixty times its volume of permitted liquid and then consumed it after the mixture became permitted. He certainly received benefit, but there can still be a penalty upon the seller for intentionally causing the prohibition to be nullified. If the buyer mixed it but the mixture still exists, it appears that the buyer can recover his money and the seller can take his proportional share of the now permitted mixture. Kosher and tereifah are treated as two different types, so the original sale was void. There is, however, room to question whether the mixture is itself prohibited to the seller because it was through his intentional causation that the prohibited matter became nullified. One who intentionally nullifies a prohibition may not benefit from the result. The question is whether indirect causation is treated like intentionally nullifying the prohibition. It seems more likely that the mixture is prohibited to the seller.
