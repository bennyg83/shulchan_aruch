The seller returns nothing to him. Baer Heitev cites the Sema. Shach, Yoreh Deah siman 119 seif katan 57, asks why the seller should return nothing at all. Even if a penalty was not imposed, he should at least return the amount received above the market value of non-kosher meat, which is due under ordinary monetary law. Shach answers that the Sages did not enact the prohibition in order to extract money; for monetary purposes they left the parties to Torah law. Netivot HaMishpat discusses Shach's conclusion that even the excess above actual value need not be returned. He asks: if the transaction is void, the buyer is like one who consumed property outside any valid sale, so why should he pay more than its real value? He suggests that one who unknowingly eats a Torah prohibition still requires atonement and teshuvah, so the harm to him exceeds his benefit. By contrast, one who unknowingly eats something prohibited only rabbinically requires no such atonement; as far as his own benefit is concerned, he ate as though the food were kosher. He therefore pays the full value of the benefit received, as in the law of one who benefits while causing another a small loss. This implies that even if the buyer bought on credit, he must pay the full kosher price. Shaar Mishpat states this expressly, comparing it to the law of pidyon haben where a written obligation to give five selaim to a kohen remains collectible even though rabbinically the child is not thereby redeemed. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 180, understands the matter differently. In his view, only if the seller already received the kosher price need he not return the difference, because in any case the buyer owed at least the value of non-kosher meat. If the buyer has not yet paid, he need pay only the lower value. Chatam Sofer there addresses a seller who knowingly sold stam yeinam as kosher wine on credit, while the purchaser, unaware of the defect, resold it on credit to many retail customers. Since the first purchaser never acquired the wine because the sale was void for mistake, the second purchasers are regarded as having acquired from the first seller's property. The case resembles stolen property where the owners did not despair and a third party consumed it: the owner may collect from either party. The retail buyers acquired only when the wine reached the place where it could no longer be returned, and then they became liable to the original seller for the inexpensive value of stam yeinam. The original seller may demand from his purchaser the return of the wine or that value, and the purchaser may collect that same value from his own customers. This appears the proper ruling. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch say that “the seller returns nothing,” which naturally refers to a case in which the seller already received the kosher price. Moreover, Pri Chadash holds that even then the seller must return the difference between kosher and non-kosher value, and interprets “returns nothing” as referring only to items whose market price does not differ, such as certain non-Jewish cheeses. Thus where the buyer still holds the money, he can certainly say kim li in accordance with Pri Chadash and Chatam Sofer and pay only the lower value.
