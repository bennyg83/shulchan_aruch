And the seller returns nothing to him. Shach in Yoreh Deah siman 119 rules that even the amount by which the price exceeded the true value need not be returned. At first glance this is difficult. If the sale is void, why should the consumer pay more than actual value? He should be no worse than one who damaged or consumed property outside a sale, where liability is limited to actual value. Perhaps the reason is this. Even though with a d'oraisa prohibition one who violated inadvertently may still require atonement and repentance in order to protect himself from punishment, an inadvertent violation of a d'rabbanan prohibition requires no such atonement. It is treated as though no prohibited act occurred. Proof appears from Eruvin 67, where regarding a d'rabbanan issue the rule is to act first and only afterward challenge the ruling. If an inadvertent d'rabbanan act itself carried punishment, how could we intentionally allow a person to perform it first and thereby expose him to punishment? Evidently, the inadvertent d'rabbanan act does not generate such punishment. Therefore, from the consumer's perspective, eating the food was like eating kosher food. He received the same practical benefit and must pay the full value of that benefit, under the principle that one who benefits while another suffers some loss pays according to the benefit. This differs from a d'oraisa prohibition, where even inadvertent consumption entails a spiritual detriment and the consumer's loss can outweigh or fundamentally alter the benefit.
