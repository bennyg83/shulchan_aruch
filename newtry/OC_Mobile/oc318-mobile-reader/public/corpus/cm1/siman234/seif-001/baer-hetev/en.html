To an expert. This teaches a novelty. Although it is possible the animal has a blemish, an ordinary animal is presumed unblemished, and if it is a bechor the prohibition is therefore of Torah severity until the blemish is verified. This differs from checking an ordinary animal for treifot, where in a case of doubt it retains its presumption of being non-treifah and examination is required only rabbinically, as the Rama writes in seif 3. Therefore the Tur and Mechaber write in seif 2, 'and it became known that it was a treifah,' meaning it was definitely treifah. Furthermore, a bechor that was not examined, even if it is later discovered to have had a blemish, is nevertheless rabbinically forbidden for benefit, as is evident from the Mishnah in the chapter Ad Kamah and is explained in Yoreh Deah siman 310. Therefore its value is not deducted from the price, as stated at the end of this siman. An ordinary animal that was not examined is not prohibited for benefit. Sema.
He shall bury it. Meaning, the buyer must bury it and may not return it to the seller for burial, because the seller is not trusted concerning this. Perhaps he will sell it to a non-Jew and derive benefit, although it is prohibited for benefit. This differs from a treifah in seif 2, where benefit is permitted and the meat may be sold to a non-Jew. Therefore the remaining meat is returned to the seller. We do not fear that he will eat it himself, because one suspected of the lighter prohibition of placing a stumbling block is not thereby suspected of the more severe prohibition of eating treifah, as stated in Yoreh Deah siman 119. Nor need we fear that he will again sell it to another Jew for monetary gain, since kosher meat is worth more than treifah meat, because any butcher from whose hand such a failure occurred is removed from his position and publicly announced so that no one purchase from him, as stated by the Tur and Mechaber in Yoreh Deah at the end of simanim 19, 64, and 119.
