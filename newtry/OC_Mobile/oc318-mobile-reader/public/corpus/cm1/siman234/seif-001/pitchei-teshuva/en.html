He returns nothing to him. See Baer Heitev in the name of Sema. See Shach in Yoreh Deah, siman 119, seif katan 57, who questions Sema's rationale: why should the seller return nothing? At least he should return the amount he received above the value of the nonkosher item, because that monetary excess is owed by ordinary law and not merely as a penalty. Shach therefore explains that the Sages did not impose their prohibition in a manner that creates additional monetary extraction; for monetary law they left the matter on its Torah footing. See there. Netivot HaMishpat cites Shach's statement that even the amount above the nonkosher value need not be returned and writes that at first glance this is difficult. Since the sale is void, the purchaser's consumption is like holding or consuming the seller's property without a valid sale. Why should he pay more than its actual value? Netivot suggests that although one who inadvertently eats a Biblical prohibition requires atonement and repentance to protect against suffering, one who inadvertently violates a rabbinic prohibition requires no such atonement. For the eater, therefore, the rabbinically prohibited food is treated as though it were permitted food; he received the same benefit as from kosher food. He must therefore pay the full value of that benefit, just as where one person benefits and another suffers even a small loss, the beneficiary pays according to the benefit, below siman 363, seif 7. By contrast, one who inadvertently ate a Biblical prohibition still bears spiritual punishment, and his loss from the transaction is substantial relative to his benefit. Netivot's wording implies that even if the purchaser bought on credit he must still pay the full kosher value. Shaar Mishpat writes this explicitly. Even though the obligation arose under a mistaken assumption, he analogizes to Yoreh Deah, siman 305, seif 4, where one writes to a kohen that he owes him five selaim for redemption of his son. He must pay, even though rabbinically the son is not thereby redeemed. Thus, an obligation can remain effective where by Torah law the underlying result is valid. So too, once the purchaser already ate a rabbinically prohibited item, he benefited from it like kosher meat and owes the entire price he undertook to pay, because by Torah law it is entirely permitted. Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 180, however, indicates that this applies specifically where the seller already received the price of kosher food; he need not return the difference, because the buyer would at least have owed the nonkosher value. But if the buyer has not yet paid, he need pay only the lower nonkosher value. Chatam Sofer there defends Sema against Shach. His case involved one who intentionally sold stam yeinam as kosher wine. The first purchaser bought on credit and then, unaware of the problem, resold portions on credit to many individual customers. Chatam Sofer discusses the payment obligations at length and concludes that because the first purchaser did not acquire due to mistaken sale, the later customers are treated as though they received the wine from the original seller's property, comparable to a robber where the owners did not despair and another person then ate the stolen item, in which case the owner may collect from either. See Bava Kamma 111b. The later purchasers likewise did not acquire until the wine reached a point of consumption where return was impossible. At that moment they became liable directly to the original seller for the inexpensive value of stam yeinam. The original seller may demand from his first purchaser either return of his wine or the inexpensive value of stam yeinam, and that purchaser may in turn demand the inexpensive value from his customers. Chatam Sofer regards this as straightforward. In my humble opinion, this is the proper practical ruling, for the simple language of Rambam and Shulchan Aruch, "the seller returns nothing to him," implies that the seller has already received the price of kosher goods. Moreover, Pri Chadash in Yoreh Deah there, cited by Shaar Mishpat, holds that even where the seller already received the money he must return the difference between kosher and nonkosher value; the phrase "returns nothing" refers only to items whose market price does not differ, such as certain non Jewish cheeses.
