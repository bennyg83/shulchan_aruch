And it became known that he had not shown it to him. This is unlike an animal that was not examined, discussed in seif 3. There the animal stands with a presumption of kashrut and examination is required only rabbinically. With a physical blemish, by contrast, the animal has no presumption that it lacks such a blemish, and the prohibition here is Torah law. Further, an unexamined firstborn animal is forbidden for benefit, and where benefit is forbidden, even by rabbinic law, the purchase money is returned, as explained in seif 4.
It shall be buried. Meaning, the purchaser himself must bury it, and he may not return it to the seller for burial, because we fear the seller may sell it to a gentile and derive benefit from it. This differs from one who sold tereifah meat in seif 2, where the meat is returned. There we do not fear that the seller himself will eat it, because one suspected of the lighter prohibition of placing a stumbling block before another is not thereby suspected of the more severe eating prohibition. Nor do we fear that he will sell it to another Jew, because when a butcher is found to have sold tereifah, a public announcement is made that no one should buy from him.
