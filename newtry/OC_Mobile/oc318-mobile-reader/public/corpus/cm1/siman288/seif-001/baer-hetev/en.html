It means that what is in shame in them that they are doing so in their sons and daughters, is still in the eye, and that they are wearing Shabbat clothes, and that they will not be able to bring them to the hospital and that they will not be able to do anything in their hands to be oppressed. A:
They acquired. This is the Rambam's reason. At first glance, the Sema suggests comparing it to siman 97, seif 25, where a creditor does not collect clothing that the debtor purchased for his wife and children because they have already acquired it for themselves. There too, this applies specifically to weekday clothing and not Shabbat clothing. Yet that law concerns garments bought for them before the loan, while garments bought after the loan may be collected, as the Tur writes there in the name of the Ramban. Here the garments were made from the undivided family estate, which resembles something made after the debt arose. Still, the Tur explains there that collection after the loan is only where the creditor's document includes a clause covering after-acquired property together with land. Here the brothers wrote no such obligation to one another. Nevertheless, the Sema concludes that the reasoning that the recipients 'already acquired them' fits only a loan, because a loan is given to be spent. From the outset the lender permits the borrower to use the money as he wishes and to repay something else when the due date arrives. Therefore, the borrower's wife and children are no worse than other recipients of a valid gift. Brothers sharing an estate, however, do not hold one another's money as a loan. They are partners and paid bailees for one another, and a partner has no authority to give partnership property to another as a gift. If he does, it may be recovered from the recipient. Therefore, the rule here must rest on the reason stated by the Sema, that the brothers themselves waive such claims against one another. The Maggid Mishneh also gives this reason. See there, and Responsa Lechem Rav, siman 109.
