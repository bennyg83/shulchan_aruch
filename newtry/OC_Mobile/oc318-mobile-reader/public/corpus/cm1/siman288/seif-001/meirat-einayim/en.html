We appraise what is upon them. Meaning the clothing actually being worn by the brothers themselves is appraised when they divide. The rule differs regarding clothing of their sons and daughters. Even if such clothing still exists, there is a distinction. Clothing not being worn at the time, such as Shabbat and festival garments that can be brought to beit din and appraised, is appraised. Ordinary weekday clothing they are presently wearing is not appraised because it would be degrading to make them appear before beit din for valuation, and we presume the brothers waived such claims. For the brothers themselves there is no comparable embarrassment in appearing before beit din. Clothing that has already worn out or been lost is not appraised even for the brothers themselves. We presume they waived claims regarding things no longer in existence, unless at the outset they protested against one another that any expenditure from the common estate would be deducted from the spender's share. The Tur explains this. The Mechaber's reason, that the wives and children already acquired the garments for themselves, follows the Rambam. At first glance this resembles the rule that a creditor can collect the debtor's own clothing but not clothing already acquired by the debtor's wife and children. Yet the analogy is imperfect. A loan is given for expenditure and the borrower may transfer the money to others, while brothers holding common estate property are partners and one partner has no right to give common property away. Therefore the true reason here is that the brothers themselves are presumed to waive such claims, as the Maggid Mishneh writes.
Some say that regarding the eldest brother, etc. The Tur writes that even the clothes he is wearing are not appraised, because the brothers are pleased that he dress well so that his words will be respected. Initially they can object, but so long as they did not object we presume they were satisfied. This is Rav Pappa's statement in the Gemara. The Mechaber omitted it because he followed the Rambam, whom the Beit Yosef understood not to rule like Rav Pappa. The Beit Yosef reasoned that although the brothers benefit from the eldest brother's respectable appearance before division, perhaps they do not waive the value after division. In the Derishah I rejected this. It appears all agree that what the eldest brother wears is not appraised. The Rambam had no need to repeat it in chapter 10 of Hilchot Nachalot because at the end of chapter 9 he already wrote that if the eldest brother wears fine garments and the brothers benefit because his words are thereby respected, he may dress from the common estate. Once they permitted him to make those garments from the estate, it is natural to assume complete waiver even at division.
