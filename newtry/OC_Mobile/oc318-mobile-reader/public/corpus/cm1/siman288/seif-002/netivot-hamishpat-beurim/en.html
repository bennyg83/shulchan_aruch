See Sema, seif katan 5, who asks from the cases where a new brother appears or where a creditor takes the share of one brother, under siman 178, where any brother can undo the division. Sema answers with difficulty. In my view the distinction is simple. When a new brother appears, we hold brothers who divided are purchasers because there is no retroactive clarification. Therefore each existing brother can say: perhaps the field I gave you in exchange for your share actually belonged to the third brother. When the third brother appeared and I had to divide with him, it became clear that at the time of our first division I had transferred to you a field that was not mine. This resembles an attempted transfer of "this field when I later acquire it," which is ineffective. The creditor case is similar where the creditor collected through an explicit apoteiki. Siman 150 explains that with an explicit apoteiki collection works retroactively. Therefore, when the creditor collects the field, it becomes his retroactively. Accordingly, when the brother originally divided and gave this field in exchange for his share, he had effectively given land that later proves to have belonged retroactively to the creditor. But this applies where the creditor took the brother's entire allotted share so that nothing from that division remains with him. If the creditor took only part, for example the brother received a field of one kor and the creditor took half a kor, then if the affected brother wants to preserve the original division as to the remaining half-kor and receive money or equivalent land for what was taken, the other brother cannot undo the whole division. This is no worse than the case where half a field is found stolen, cited by Nimukei Yosef, or the rule "acquire one kor for me and he acquired half a kor" in siman 182. Only the buyer can say, "I want the complete transaction," while the seller cannot invalidate the portion that can still be completed. Siman 232, Sema seif katan 2, likewise says that where the deficiency can be completed the transaction stands, and even with land it can be completed through adjacent land. Therefore the opposite brother cannot cancel if the affected brother is willing to receive compensation and leave the remaining land in place. Certainly, if the opposite brother wishes to satisfy the deficiency with land, he may do so. Thus here, where one brother returns the palm tree and asks for money, the proper understanding is that he says: either give me another palm and I will restore the necessary adjustment, or give me money for the palm I transferred. He cannot force the other brother specifically to pay money if another equivalent palm can be given. Even if a particular palm had been named, the other brother may be able to satisfy with another equivalent palm under the principles above. If the claimant insists on money only and the other brother has none, the latter can cancel the division. But if the claimant says, "Either another palm or money," the other brother cannot cancel the division as to the remaining property. This is the clear rule.
