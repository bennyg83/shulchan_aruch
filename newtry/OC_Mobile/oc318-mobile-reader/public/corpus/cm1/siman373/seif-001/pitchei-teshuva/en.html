The principal together with the increase. See Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Bava Metzia, s.v. Amar Rava hilcheta. He writes that where the purchaser did not know that the land was stolen, the seller is liable to him for the increase whether a creditor repossessed it or the true owner recovered it, and the two laws are entirely alike except where the land declined in value. In that case the purchaser's protection is stronger when the true owner recovers it than when a creditor repossesses it. If the seller sold it for one hundred and it fell in value to fifty, a creditor who repossesses it obligates the seller to pay only fifty, its present value. But if it is discovered that the land never belonged to the seller, it is now revealed that there was never a valid sale at all and the purchase money was effectively a loan in the seller's hands. When the land is taken from the purchaser, the seller must therefore return the full sum. See Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 18, who writes that Ran in his novellae, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet there, says the same as Nimukei Yosef. Their view is nevertheless not universally accepted. Shitah Mekubetzet there, s.v. Hikir bah she-einah shelo, cites Ritva that the purchaser receives only the money he paid, regardless of whether the land later declined or increased in value, since it is as though there was no sale at all and the money was given only as a deposit. This differs from a case where land is found not to belong to the seller, or is repossessed by a creditor, where the purchaser receives according to the value at the time it left his possession. See also what I wrote above in siman 116, seif 1, seif katan 1. Beit Ephraim further writes that according to Ran and Nimukei Yosef, if the land declined in value the seller returns the original purchase money because the sale is void, yet the purchaser also collects from the robber any increase beyond his expenditures, even an increase that arose by itself. This must be because responsibility for the increase is a monetary obligation that remains effective. Accordingly, he raises the following question. One sold land to another for one thousand, land values subsequently fell so that comparable land was worth only five hundred, but this particular parcel accumulated alluvial soil and is now worth one thousand. The purchaser argues: I paid one thousand, which you must return, and you also undertook liability for the increase, another five hundred. The seller responds: if you claim the increase under my warranty, you may receive no more than the present value of this land, and even after the alluvial increase it is worth only one thousand. How is this case to be adjudicated? See there carefully.
