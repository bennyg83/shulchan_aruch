Only the principal. The Tur explains: Since the purchaser knew at the time of purchase that the property was stolen, the money he gave was not truly given as purchase money but effectively as a loan. If he were to recover more than he gave, it would appear like interest. Regarding appreciation that occurs automatically, everyone agrees he receives nothing. Regarding appreciation produced through expenditure, they disagree. The Rif holds that he recovers his expense, with his hand on the lower side, because that amount was an additional outlay beyond the purchase principal. Recovering what he actually expended does not look like interest. The other view, that of the Rosh, holds that even those expenses are not recovered, as Rama states.
If he expressly accepted liability, etc. The Tur cites the Ramah, who holds this view, as saying that the seller must have expressly accepted liability for the appreciation, and a kinyan must also have been made on that obligation, where the seller has land. In that case collecting from land does not appear like interest, unlike collection of money resembling the original loan. But if he did not expressly accept liability for the appreciation, even if he accepted ordinary sale warranty, he accepted only what he specified and not what he omitted. The Tur implies that even the Rosh, who disagrees and holds that express acceptance of liability for appreciation does not ordinarily entitle him to the appreciation, may agree where the seller has land, for the reason stated. It is surprising that the Mechaber and Maharik omitted entirely the rule where the seller has land.
