The principal together with the appreciation. Nimukei Yosef, beginning of Bava Metzia, writes that where a purchaser did not know that the seller lacked title, the seller must indemnify him for appreciation whether the land was later taken by a creditor or by the true owner, and the two cases are generally alike. There is, however, a difference if the land depreciated. If the buyer paid one hundred and the land later fell to fifty, then where a creditor takes it the seller need reimburse only its present value of fifty. But where it turns out that the seller never owned the land, the supposed sale was void from the outset and the money remained in the seller's hands as a loan. When the true owner takes the land, the seller must therefore return the full one hundred. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 18, notes that Ran's novellae, cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, say likewise. This view is not unanimous. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ritva that where the buyer knowingly purchased land that was not the seller's, he recovers only the money he gave, regardless of later appreciation or depreciation, because the transaction is treated as though there was no sale at all and the money was merely entrusted. By contrast, where the buyer innocently purchases land and it later proves not to belong to the seller, or where a creditor takes it, recovery may be measured according to the land's value when it leaves the buyer's hands. See what I wrote above in siman 116, seif 1, seif katan 1. Beit Ephraim further notes that according to Ran and Nimukei Yosef, where land depreciates the seller returns the original price because the sale is void, yet must also indemnify the buyer for appreciation beyond expenses, even passive appreciation. This implies that liability for appreciation is a separate monetary obligation. He therefore raises a question where land was sold for one thousand, comparable land later fell to five hundred, but this particular parcel itself increased through sediment deposits and is now again worth one thousand. The buyer argues that the seller must return the original one thousand because the sale was void and also pay five hundred for appreciation liability. The seller responds that if the buyer claims appreciation, he should not recover more than the land's present total value, which is only one thousand even after the new sediment. Beit Ephraim leaves the proper resolution for careful study. See there.
