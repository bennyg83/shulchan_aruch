Samekh) This is the language of the Rambam there, at the beginning of chapter 18, and the Tur, seif 5. Rambam explains there that an Israelite and a gentile are equal regarding this prohibition of deceiving another's mind.
Ayin) This is the language of the Tur, seif 6, based on the incident involving Shmuel in Chullin 94a. Rambam explains there, halachah 3, that the prohibition applies even though the carcass is as valuable to the recipient as properly slaughtered meat.
Peh) A baraita in the name of Rabbi Meir there.
Tzadi) The Sema writes that in his view the prohibition applies specifically to excessive urging beyond normal courtesy. But it is permitted to invite him once or twice, saying, "Come eat with me," because otherwise his fellow may be embarrassed before onlookers, who might think that the host refrained from inviting him out of contempt, since not everyone knows that he will not eat there.
Kuf) In our versions of the Gemara the text refers to barrels sold to a shopkeeper. Rashi explains that their barrels were ordinarily sealed. When an important guest came, one would open a barrel of strong wine for him. If a complete barrel had already been sold to a shopkeeper but was still physically in the seller's possession, he may not open it for a guest, because he thereby deceives the guest into thinking that the host incurred a substantial loss on his behalf, since the barrel will remain partially empty and the wine will spoil, when in fact it will immediately be delivered to the shopkeeper for sale.
Reish) From the incident involving Mar Zutra son of Rav Nachman, Rava, and Rav Safra, who encountered one another, and from Rava's response to Rav Safra there on 94b.
