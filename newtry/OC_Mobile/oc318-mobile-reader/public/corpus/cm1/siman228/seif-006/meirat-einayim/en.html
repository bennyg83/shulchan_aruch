If there is a defect in his merchandise, etc. Meaning, even if there is no monetary ona'ah, he must nevertheless disclose the defect. This resembles geneivat da'at, which is prohibited even where there is no monetary loss.
He should not urge him excessively. Meaning, he should not press him repeatedly. Since the text uses this language and afterward says, "and he should not increase offerings to him," I wrote in the Perishah that the prohibition applies specifically where he presses repeatedly or offers more than customary. To invite him once or twice by saying, "Come eat with me," is permitted. To the contrary, if he says nothing, his fellow may be humiliated, because observers will see him enter and leave without being honored with an invitation and may assume it is because he is of low standing. Not everyone knows that the host refrained only because he knew the guest would not eat. This is clear.
He should not open barrels already open for the shopkeeper, etc. The Gemara's text is "barrels sold to a shopkeeper." Rashi explains that their barrels were ordinarily sealed. When an important guest arrived, the host would open one in order to serve him strong wine. If he had sold an entire barrel to a shopkeeper and it was still physically in his possession, he should not open it for the visiting guest, because he thereby steals the guest's goodwill. The guest assumes the host has suffered a significant loss for his sake, since an opened barrel remains partly empty and the wine may spoil, whereas in truth the host will immediately deliver it to the shopkeeper who already purchased it. The Gemara concludes that if the guest is so beloved that the host would have opened a barrel for him anyway, it is permitted. Beit Yosef cites this and wonders why the Tur omitted it. The wording of the Tur and Mechaber, however, says "barrels that are open," implying that they were already opened for the shopkeeper and the householder presents himself to the guest as though he is opening them now specifically for him. That too is prohibited. We do not say that the guest deceived himself because he could have noticed the barrels were already open. Perhaps their Gemara text read "the open barrels" rather than "the sold barrels." This version is also more plausible, because ordinarily a shopkeeper would not buy wine without tasting it first.
