Seif 18. Likewise, he may sell below the prevailing market price. Note: I was asked about one who lives in a village, whether he may sell liquor cheaply to people of another town. I answered simply that there is no prohibition at all, for he is acting with his own property. Indeed, it is an a fortiori argument: if in the town where he lives he may sell more cheaply than others, all the more so may he do so in another place. I was told, however, that Responsa Panim Meirot, part I, rules stringently, and that Responsa Gur Aryeh Yehudah, Choshen Mishpat siman 22, cites and disputes him. I examined the matter there. In that case the two villages were within one Sabbath boundary, where it was permitted to walk from one to the other on Shabbat, and for that reason they were treated as one town. Even this is disputed by the Gur Aryeh Yehudah. But where the village is outside the same boundary, even the Panim Meirot agrees that it is permitted. The words of the Panim Meirot are also difficult, for he distinguishes between grain and liquor on the basis that with grain there is a benefit because prices are lowered, as the Gemara says. This is difficult, for the Shulchan Aruch and Tur state without qualification that one may sell more cheaply, and they do not distinguish between grain and other commodities. It is therefore evident that there is no distinction. As for the Gemara's question, "What is the reason of the Rabbis?" he overlooked Rashi there, who explains that the question is only why the Rabbis bless him for doing so; the answer is that he lowers prices. The basic permissibility itself does not depend upon lowering prices. The Gur Aryeh Yehudah sensed part of this difficulty, yet brought his proof from afar from the Nimukei Yosef and did not note that Rashi says it explicitly there. For this reason, the Shulchan Aruch states generally that it is permitted and does not qualify the rule or give the reason of lowering prices. Further, the Panim Meirot's claim that two villages within one Sabbath boundary are as though their residents live together is difficult. What is his source that being within one boundary renders them one city for this law? One cannot say that this was merely the factual setting of the case and that he would prohibit even where they are not one city, for then what proof could he derive from the Gemara? Even according to his understanding that the Gemara's question concerns the basic permission, that discussion relates only to one town. Where they are not one locality, the matter is permitted regardless, and even the first Tanna would agree. Necessarily, he assumes that two places within one Sabbath boundary are treated as one city for this purpose, and there is no source for that. His words are therefore difficult. As a matter of halachah, it is certainly permitted even in one city, as is explicit from the wording of the Shulchan Aruch and from Rashi's explanation. This is clear in my humble opinion.
