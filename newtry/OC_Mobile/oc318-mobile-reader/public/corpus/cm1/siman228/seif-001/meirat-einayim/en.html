So too verbal ona'ah. This is derived from the verse, "You shall not wrong one another, and you shall fear your God." It cannot refer to monetary ona'ah, because Scripture already states concerning sales, "When you sell property to your fellow or buy from the hand of your fellow, do not wrong one another." The verse adds "and you shall fear your God" because verbal oppression is a matter entrusted to the heart: only the speaker truly knows whether his intention was to cause pain.
He is answered immediately. The Gemara derives this from the verse, "Behold, Hashem stood upon a wall measured with an anach, and in His hand was an anach." The word anach is understood as related to ona'ah. "Standing upon the anach" implies readiness to hear the victim's cry, and "in His hand was the anach" implies that the sin is not set aside to await later punishment, but is, as it were, held in His hand for immediate response. From here Chazal said that all gates may be closed except the gates of ona'ah.
Only to those who fear Hashem. They derive from "do not wrong your fellow" that "your fellow" means one who is with you in Torah and mitzvot.
One who wrongs himself may be wronged. Nimukei Yosef at the end of chapter HaZahav writes that this is stated in the Midrash and concludes, "such a person is not called your fellow." I heard some explain that this means: if someone wrongs you, you too may wrong him, and "himself" refers to the person whom the Midrash permits you to wrong back. This does not appear correct. The wording "wrongs himself" does not mean "wrongs you"; it should have said "one who wrongs you." Moreover, why limit it to one who wrongs you personally? If he wronged others, according to that explanation you too should be permitted to wrong him. Rather, it appears that it means one who degrades his own dignity and person. The Torah did not warn regarding such a person in the same way, and others may likewise treat him with reduced honor. At first glance the reason might be that such a person is not God-fearing, and Nimukei Yosef immediately before this likewise writes that the prohibition does not apply to one who is not God-fearing. But that does not appear correct, for then it should not have said simply that he "is not called your fellow"; it should have said that he is not with you in Torah and mitzvot. Rather, the Midrash appears to mean literally that since normal civilized people do not humiliate themselves, one who does so is outside the ordinary social framework of "your fellow" and proper conduct. For this reason Rama states the two laws separately. The Levush confuses them, writing that some say one is warned only with respect to a God-fearing person, because "your fellow" means one who is with you in Torah and mitzvot, but if he is not God-fearing he wrongs himself and may likewise be wronged. He thereby treats the two statements as one. This is incorrect, and his explanation makes a teaching that is actually stated depend upon one that is not stated in that form.
