The primary view appears to be. See Baer Heitev, who concludes that the matter is a doubt in law. Shevut Yaakov III:178 writes that he repeatedly ruled in practice that if the owner incurred medical expenses to heal the animal, the guardian must reimburse them, because he thereby saved the guardian from a loss for which the guardian would otherwise have been liable. As to loss of work, although compensation for idleness is not ordinarily collected for an animal where the authorities dispute it, the guardian must at least pay the animal's food during its illness. Before the injury it ate while working, whereas now it must be fed without producing, and that is actual damage. He need not pay what it would have earned beyond its maintenance, for that is compensation for idleness, which does not apply to an animal. He also rules like the Sema that where the flesh itself was not naturally emaciated, one appraises the reduction in the animal's value; but where the loss of flesh occurred naturally, the rule differs.
