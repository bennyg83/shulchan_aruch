And the neighbors warned him to remove the wheat. Tur's reference to the neighbors' warning is difficult, because siman 400 concerning a wall that fell into the public domain requires warning specifically by beit din, not merely by private persons. If the case is comparable instead to one who dug into a wall and thereby caused it to fall, meaning the walls were already weak from the beginning and could not support the wheat for long, then no warning is required at all. Tur himself says the grain was placed against weak walls and that this was his direct force. Rosh likewise compares the case to other forms of property damage where payment is due even without prior warning. His mention of a warning is therefore only by way of greater force: all the more so when he was warned. Rosh's closing comparison requires explanation. Tosafot give a fallen wall the law of a pit. One might therefore say that since the wall belonged to the landlord and the tenant's wheat merely caused it to collapse, the tenant should resemble an ox that dug a pit and be exempt. Rosh answers that a wall which was originally sound and only later deteriorated is unlike a knife or burden that was dangerous from the outset and was moved by an external force. That is why warning matters there. Here, however, the wheat was placed against walls that were already weak and liable to fall from the burden. This resembles one's rope or other object left where the ordinary movement of an animal will cause damage. The owner of that object is liable because he placed it in a setting where ordinary forces would predictably cause damage. Accordingly, if the walls were weak from the outset, no warning is needed. If they became weak only later, after the wheat was placed there while the walls were still fit to stand, then a formal warning by beit din is required just as with one's own deteriorating wall.
