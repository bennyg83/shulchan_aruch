(Seif 3, at the time he consecrated the property.) The Sema, seif katan 4, explains this phrase in the manner written by Kesef Mishneh, but the Sema himself acknowledges that the explanation is forced. It is also difficult, for we find no distinction between a healthy person and a sick person except with respect to collusion, not with respect to retraction. Although a sick person may retract his gift throughout his illness, as stated in siman 250, we do not find that in the present context his period for retraction extends longer than that of a healthy person. Moreover, Sefer HaTerumot, cited by Beit Yosef, states explicitly: “Some answer that the case is where, at the time he consecrated the property, he acknowledged the debt within the time required for speech. After he consecrated it, however, he cannot do so, for consecrated property is no worse than ordinary property, concerning which he cannot later create an obligation by his admission. Rambam likewise rules.” This is plainly the reverse of the proposed explanation. It appears to me that Rambam’s words may be resolved as follows. After property has been consecrated, the donor is never believed to remove it from the consecration, whether he is healthy or sick. The reasons, however, differ. For a healthy person the concern is collusion. For a dying person, concerning whom there is no concern for collusion, the reason is that once the consecration took effect he lacks power to remove property from consecration, just as he cannot remove it from encumbered property, so long as the creditor’s document has not been authenticated. This is the difficulty raised by Baal HaMaor against Rif, as Beit Yosef cites from Sefer HaTerumot. If, at the time of consecration, he made a complete admission, it certainly takes effect, whether he was healthy or dying, and even if it followed the consecration within the time required for speech, for we hold that speech within that period is considered a continuation of the first statement, except in the cases of betrothal and blasphemy. If, however, the admission was not complete, because it could be understood as an attempt by a healthy man not to appear wealthy, as Nimukei Yosef explains, there is a distinction between a healthy and sick person. Where his statement at the time of consecration was not in itself a complete admission, but he later corrected it by saying, “Give the one hundred to so-and-so,” thereby showing that he had not spoken merely to avoid appearing wealthy, this correction is ineffective for a healthy person because of the concern for collusion. For a sick person, however, there is no concern for collusion. His only lack of credibility is that he cannot remove property from an existing encumbrance, and he is therefore believed to clarify and correct his earlier words. Rambam’s ruling is thus clear. A healthy person is never believed to remove property from consecration unless he made an unequivocal admission at the time he consecrated it. A sick person, however, is believed where at that time he said, “I owe one hundred to so-and-so,” although there remains a concern that he spoke merely to avoid appearing wealthy, because he may later correct his words by saying, “Give it.” This is Rambam’s meaning when he continues, “Therefore, if he said, ‘Give,’” meaning even if he said it some time after the consecration, it is effective as a correction of the concern that he had spoken merely to avoid appearing wealthy. The money is therefore given even without a document. If he did not say “Give,” but there is another factor that removes the concern that the original admission at the time of consecration was merely to avoid appearing wealthy, namely, that the creditor holds an authenticated document, he may collect from the consecrated property itself by virtue of the testamentary instruction, unlike an ordinary creditor, who collects only from the one who redeems the consecrated property, as Rambam explains there concerning other creditors. The sequence of Rambam’s words is therefore as follows. With a healthy person the distinction is between an admission made at the time of consecration and one made afterward. After consecration, even a complete admission is ineffective, which implies that an unequivocal admission made at the time of consecration is effective. “When does this apply? To a healthy person,” meaning that for him the only distinction is between the time of consecration and afterward. With a sick person there is an additional distinction. If his original statement was not phrased as an unequivocal admission, a concern remains unless he later says, “Give,” and Rambam therefore concludes, “Accordingly, if he said, ‘Give,’” and so forth. Rambam’s final clause, “If after he consecrated it he said, ‘Give,’” means that at the time of consecration he said nothing at all, and only afterward said, “Give one hundred to so-and-so, which I owe him.” Such a statement cannot defeat the power of the consecration, because the property has already become fully consecrated, and he is like any other creditor who may collect only from the redeemer. This appears correct to me after careful analysis of Rambam’s words.
