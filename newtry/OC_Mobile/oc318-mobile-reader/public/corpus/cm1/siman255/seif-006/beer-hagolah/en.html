Tur, seifim 10 and 11, in the name of Responsa Rosh, klal 86, siman 5.
See siman 41, seif 3, and siman 253, where the Rama writes in the gloss the opposite. The Sema reconciles them by saying that here the dying person admitted that he owed so-and-so one hundred, while there it concerns a gift of a dying person. The Bach objects that the language 'to give one hundred' sounds like a gift. He answers that here the donor gave the recipient the testamentary document during his lifetime, in which case it is certainly a full document; above, however, the document was written after death. The Beit Yosef cites the Nimukei Yosef that testamentary documents are written after death so that they may record that the testator died from his illness and passed to his eternal home. The Sages authorized such writing and treated the documents as acts of beit din, which may be written without agency from the litigant.
