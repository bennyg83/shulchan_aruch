Alternatively, he said this after consecrating. See Sema, seif katan 8, who establishes the case where the parties were still engaged in the same matter. This is very difficult. Why should there be any distinction whether they were still discussing the same matter, and what difference is there between a healthy person and a shechiv mera? The primary explanation follows Ir Shushan and Shach, that the statement was made within toch kedei dibbur. Still, we must explain why a healthy person and a shechiv mera differ. In my view Rambam's ruling rests on two principles. First, he follows R. Eliezer in Arakhin, chapter Shum HaYetomim, who is concerned for collusion even where the person could theoretically obtain release from the consecration, because making a verbal claim is easier than arranging formal release, as the poskim cited by Shach explain. Second, Rambam holds that one cannot retract from hekdesh even within toch kedei dibbur. Therefore, when a healthy person says within toch kedei dibbur, "I owe one maneh to so-and-so," we remain concerned that he is attempting collusion and that this is why he made the statement. A shechiv mera is different. We do not ordinarily suspect him of collusion, and therefore we assume he is telling the truth. Since the statement came within toch kedei dibbur, we interpret it as clarifying his original intention: when he consecrated all his property, he meant all except this maneh. This is comparable to Temurah 24, where R. Yose says that if this was his intent from the outset his words stand. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 73 write that even according to the view that toch kedei dibbur is not generally treated as continuous speech, it can still serve to explain and clarify one's first statement. Accordingly, the shechiv mera can collect this maneh from hekdesh even if the underlying obligation is only an oral loan, because that maneh was never included in his intention to consecrate. After the interval of toch kedei dibbur, however, he can no longer explain the first statement in this way. The hekdesh is complete, and even if the debt is true, an oral loan cannot be collected from hekdesh. Only a verified written debt can be collected. Even if the donor says there is a document, his admission alone is not enough. We are concerned that even a witness can speak from mistaken assumption; all the more so a litigant. Until the document itself is produced, the debt does not have the status of a written claim against purchasers or hekdesh.
But an ill person who consecrated. See Shach, seif katan 6 and 8, who discusses at length the questions raised by the earlier authorities. There are three principal questions. First, a shechiv mera who consecrated should be able to retract. Second, even a healthy person should not create concern of collusion because he can seek release from the consecration, as Arakhin 22 indicates. Third, according to the initial assumption that no debt document is in hand, an oral loan cannot be collected from encumbered property. Shach answers, in substance, that when a person says merely, "I owe one maneh to so-and-so," we should understand him as referring to a deposited maneh. His purpose is to enable that maneh to be recovered from hekdesh, and if he meant an ordinary loan he would accomplish nothing because an oral loan cannot be collected from hekdesh. Therefore the statement must concern a deposit. If so, the second question also falls away: the possibility of seeking release is only a migo, and once we establish by chazakah that he means a deposit, the migo is against that chazakah. Shach also cites the earlier answer that the property had already been delivered to the hekdesh treasurer, after which release is unavailable. In my view Shach's explanation is very strained. Precisely because an oral loan cannot be collected from hekdesh, there is no danger that his admission will steal from hekdesh. One cannot argue that there was no reason for him to admit unless he meant a deposit, because he may have wished to admit the debt so the creditor could collect from assets that would later come into his estate. There is a stronger difficulty against Shach and Ramban, who interpret the case as one where he expressly said, "a deposited maneh." The author of the teaching is Rav Huna, while in Gittin Rav says that if a person merely says, "I have one maneh belonging to so-and-so," we do not deliver it because perhaps he means buried money. Tosafot there indicate that this concern can be even stronger with a deposit. Rav Huna would not ordinarily dispute his teacher Rav. Rama, responsum 92, likewise writes that unless the donor says "Give," we are concerned for a buried maneh. Just as beit din pleads claims for heirs, it pleads for purchasers, and hekdesh has the legal position of a purchaser. Shach's argument that release is merely a migo against a chazakah also seems incorrect. The case cannot be analyzed as a migo used to extract property. Even if the litigant himself had a migo, he would not thereby extract from purchasers. Rather, the concept is the one stated in Bava Metzia 13: when the borrower admits the debt, the admission can allow collection even from purchasers unless we are concerned for collusion. The Gemara asks that where the debtor admits, even a document containing responsibility clauses should be returned. This shows that admission can be effective against purchasers when collusion is not suspected. If the person can simply obtain release from the consecration, there is no reason to suspect collusion. Why fabricate a debt if he can undo the consecration directly? His admission therefore has force against purchasers or hekdesh, not because of migo but because the special concern of collusion is absent. Eruvin uses the same reasoning regarding a husband who need not vow not to benefit from his wife after divorce when release is available. That too is plainly not a migo. The answer that the property had been delivered to the treasurer is also difficult when combined with the deposit interpretation. Once he delivered everything to the treasurer as his own property for hekdesh, how can he later say some of it was not his without contradicting his earlier conduct? Shach further says, "It appears Ramban intended this," meaning that Ramban spoke of a kinyan. This is clearly not Ramban's intent. Ramban answers Tosafot's first question by establishing the case as a shechiv mera who consecrated from now, in which case he cannot retract, because speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person and "from now" gives it the force of a healthy-person transfer. Alternatively, he consecrated while healthy and afterward became ill. Ramban then separately says that the consecration was made on the understanding of the public, in order to answer the second question concerning release. Ramban never meant a kinyan. In my view the questions of the earlier authorities can be resolved more directly. Tosafot ask why the shechiv mera is believed, since he can retract from his consecration anyway. It appears that the Gemara's uncertainty whether one who consecrated his entire estate can retract concerns principally what happens if he recovers. During the illness itself, however, he cannot retract. Nimukei Yosef says this explicitly. The same follows from siman 125. If a shechiv mera says "bring," which is like "acquire," he cannot retract during the illness. Siman 250 likewise states that once the property has been delivered, he cannot retract during the illness, although if he recovers the gift can fail. Taz in siman 125 explains that where recovery defeats the gift, this is because the transfer operates like a condition that the donor die from this illness. But where the acquisition during illness rests on delivery rather than merely on the special law of shechiv mera words, he cannot retract during the illness itself. Acquisition through another person is like the recipient's own meshichah and therefore cannot be revoked during the illness. Sema in siman 250 says similarly. Hekdesh is even stronger. "Speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person." The acquisition of hekdesh through speech is not merely the shechiv mera enactment; it is an independent form equivalent to delivery. It should therefore be comparable to zekhiyah through another, which cannot be revoked during the illness, even if recovery later allows the donor to undo it. There is another route. Even if we treat it as an ordinary shechiv mera gift that vests only after death, Nedarim 29 states that if one says, "This ox shall be an olah after thirty days," he cannot retract, although one who betroths a woman after thirty days can retract. Rosh and Ran explain why. The same principle should apply if one says, "This shall be charity after thirty days." Accordingly, consecration set to take effect after death can be irrevocable during the illness. Sema in siman 250, seif katan 11, says the donor can retract during the illness, but I find his words difficult. Tur says no such thing. Rama's gloss can be understood differently: he first states the rule regarding the poor and even where they have seized, and then says the law of hekdesh is similar. He need not mean that retraction during illness is permitted. If one insists that a delayed hekdesh can be revoked, one would have to follow Rashba, cited by Ran in Nedarim, who holds that one can retract from consecration after thirty days, a dispute among Amoraim in the Yerushalmi. Rav Huna can then follow the opposing view that one cannot retract from delayed hekdesh, which would fully resolve Tosafot's first question. The second question, why we need the rule against collusion when release from the consecration is available, is likewise resolved. Where the shechiv mera's consecration is structured as taking effect only if he dies from the present illness, it is a conditional or time-delayed vow. A vow cannot be released before it takes effect. Yoreh Deah siman 228, seif 17, rules that one does not release a vow before its term of applicability, and all the more so before a condition has been fulfilled. According to Rosh in Nedarim 29, the reason one cannot retract from delayed hekdesh is not that it is considered effective from now, but because of the law of vows. Then certainly release cannot occur before the vow takes effect. Even according to Ran, who explains that "speech to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person" gives delayed hekdesh an effect resembling "from now," it still appears to remain before full vesting for release purposes. First, Sema in siman 195 writes that "from now" can mean the acquisition begins now but is completed only later. Before completion, the vow has not yet fully taken effect. Second, Ran explicitly says the comparison to "from now" is only for the purpose that the donor cannot retract. The reason is that ordinary speech cannot cancel an act of acquisition once speech to Heaven is treated like delivery. Ran does not mean the property is literally consecrated retroactively from the first moment. If it were, one would commit me'ilah retroactively in a case of consecration after thirty days, and redemption by others during the interval could not prevent the future sanctity, contrary to the Gemara. Thus before death the vow has not yet fully taken effect and cannot be released. One might then argue in the opposite direction: if a shechiv mera's consecration is irrevocable, then his position is like that of a healthy person who consecrated immediately. Why should his later admission be believed? Since ordinary immediate hekdesh can be released, collusion is not suspected. The answer is that the Gemara's uncertainty about revocation concerns a person who consecrated his entire estate. There we may infer that he did not intend to leave himself with nothing against hekdesh. But one who gives instructions because of impending death can certainly have a different rule and can be unable to retract. Rav Huna's case can concern such a death-related instruction. Alternatively, Rav Huna himself may have been uncertain. Tosafot's question assumes that if the law is uncertain, then practically the property remains with the original owner and he can retract, as Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 250 by placing the property in its prior chazakah. But according to what I wrote, the very reason the doubt gives him practical power to retract may itself prevent release of the vow before death, because full vesting has not yet occurred. Thus the apparent migo from release still does not exist. The poskim who rule that a healthy person is not believed can be speaking where he consecrated on a condition that takes effect only after death, like the shechiv mera, or where he consecrated in a form that cannot be released, such as on the understanding of the public, or after delivery to the treasurer. Their explanations of the Gemara remain strained, but Rav Huna's teaching itself is straightforward according to this approach: the novelty is that although a shechiv mera cannot presently seek release from such a consecration, we nevertheless believe his admission because a person does not engage in collusion in that setting. This also answers Tosafot's question in Arakhin concerning the statement that the dispute concerns release from hekdesh. The third question, that an oral loan cannot be collected from encumbered property, is also resolved. That objection arises only according to the approach that the donor consecrated while healthy, or consecrated from now in an irrevocable manner. Then a preexisting oral loan cannot precede and defeat the hekdesh lien. But according to my explanation, the donor is a shechiv mera whose hekdesh does not vest until death. Bava Batra 175 asks whether, when a shechiv mera admits a debt, he must say "Write" before witnesses may prepare a document. Nimukei Yosef explains that with a healthy person witnesses may not write merely from an admission unless told to do so, because that would convert an oral loan into a written loan. The Gemara resolves that a shechiv mera need not say "Write." This shows that from the time of the shechiv mera's admission and authorized writing, the debt can have the legal status of a written loan, comparable to a healthy person who explicitly said "Write," and it can collect from assets encumbered afterward. Sema in siman 253, seif katan 77, likewise says that a shechiv mera's admission can have the force of a fully written document for collection from encumbered property. The Gemara's question against Rav and Shmuel, that an oral loan is not collected from heirs, must therefore concern a stage before the witnesses have actually written the document. Once written, it becomes a written claim, as Ramban cited by Sema indicates. Accordingly, after the shechiv mera's admission the witnesses may write, and from that point the debt precedes the hekdesh, which vests only after death. The creditor can therefore collect from it. According to Rosh, who holds that delayed hekdesh is not literally "from now" and only becomes irrevocable because of vow law, this is obvious: the creditor's written lien precedes the later vesting of the hekdesh. Even according to Ran, who compares delayed hekdesh to "from now" only for purposes of irrevocability, the property is not actually consecrated retroactively. Therefore a debt document written during the donor's life can still precede the hekdesh that does not become fully effective until death. Thus, even under the Gemara's initial assumption that no prior document was in hand, it is meaningful to ask whether the donor is believed when he says, "I owe a maneh to so-and-so." If he is believed, witnesses may now write the debt instrument without his saying "Write," and the creditor can collect from the hekdesh. If he is not believed, they may not do so.
At the time he consecrated. This means within toch kedei dibbur. See Shach, seif katan 8, who questions this from Bava Kamma 73, where the Gemara compares testimony to Temurah. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that the sugya in Bava Kamma was rejected by the beginning of Nazir chapter 2. There the Mishnah says that if one says, "I am a nazir from dried figs," Beit Shammai hold he is a nazir, consistent with their view that there is no release from hekdesh in nezirut. Tosafot establish the case within toch kedei dibbur. Since release is ineffective, retraction within toch kedei dibbur is likewise ineffective. Ketzot reasons from Rambam, Hilchot Shevuot chapter 2, that retraction within toch kedei dibbur works because it resembles correction of a mistake. According to Beit Shammai, mistaken hekdesh remains hekdesh, so retraction cannot work. He then argues that Temurah 17 teaches mistaken substitution is effective and Rambam says there is no release from temurah. Therefore retraction within toch kedei dibbur should not work in temurah either. R. Yose's statement in Temurah 28 that retraction does not work would then refer even to a short toch kedei dibbur. The opening clause concerning hekdesh would likewise concern the short interval because of the special stringency of hekdesh, and the Bava Kamma sugya would be rejected. This is an ingenious explanation, but in my view it is not correct. Mishneh LaMelech can properly say that the Bava Kamma sugya is rejected because there is a dispute among Amoraim whether the relevant interval is the shorter or longer form of toch kedei dibbur. The sugya can follow one view while we rule like the other. But to say the sugya follows no accepted view and that an explicit Mishnah in Nazir was entirely overlooked is difficult. Moreover, what basis would Rambam have for rejecting Bava Kamma in favor of Nazir? In my view, although Temurah 17 includes mistaken temurah from the word "it shall be," that means only that one cannot entirely uproot the status of temurah and make the animal ordinary property. The Torah includes an accidental substitution within the law of temurah. In that respect, even retraction within toch kedei dibbur cannot make it ordinary. But if, within toch kedei dibbur, he changes only the sacrificial designation while leaving the animal in the status of temurah, the retraction can work. For example, he says, "This is temurat olah," and within toch kedei dibbur says, "shelamim." He has not removed the name temurah. He has only clarified the type of korban, and that can be effective. As for Menachot, where one says, "I undertake a todah from ordinary funds and its bread from ma'aser funds," and the second statement within toch kedei dibbur does not help, there is a separate reason. He can certainly retract entirely from the consecration within the relevant rules. But here he did not retract from the obligation of todah. He only attempted to make the bread come from ma'aser funds. It is as though he said, "I undertake a todah on condition that I not bring its required bread from ordinary funds," which is a condition against what the Torah requires. He remains obligated in the todah and must bring the bread from ordinary funds. The same applies where one says, "I am a nazir provided I may drink wine." Even without using the formal words "on condition," a formulation such as, "I am a nazir that I may drink wine," has the same legal defect. Therefore the primary answer to Rambam follows Mishneh LaMelech. My own full resolution of Rambam's wording belongs elsewhere.
