One who consecrates all his property. Ir Shushan writes that even within the brief interval of immediate speech the consecrator cannot retract. Sema objects, but Ir Shushan follows Rambam's Commentary to the Mishnah, Bartenura, and Tosafot in Menachot, which list consecration and substitution among the exceptional matters for which retraction within immediate speech does not operate. Shach challenges this from Bava Kamma, where the Gemara discusses Rabbi Yosei's view concerning testimony and compares it with his ruling in Temurah, apparently recognizing two measures of immediate speech. Shach concludes that consecration and substitution should follow ordinary retraction rules. Mishneh LaMelech answers that Rambam rejected that Bava Kamma passage because it conflicts with other sugyot. The clearest basis appears in Nazir. Beit Shammai treat "I am a nazir from dried figs" as a binding nazirite declaration because a person does not utter his words for nothing and, more fundamentally, because their theory of consecration does not allow ordinary retraction through error. Tosafot explains that retraction within immediate speech works by treating the first declaration as mistaken. Where error itself does not undo the sacred status, retraction cannot help. Rambam says similarly in the laws of oaths that retraction within immediate speech uproots the first declaration because it is treated like an error. But substitution is effective even when made in error, and one cannot seek annulment from it. Therefore retraction within immediate speech cannot undo substitution. The Mishnah of Rabbi Yosei speaks of consecration and substitution together with the same term "he reconsidered." Since in substitution even the shorter measure of immediate speech cannot undo the first declaration, the parallel consecration case must likewise be read as not allowing retraction. The Bava Kamma passage is therefore displaced by the Nazir and Temurah sugyot. Tosafot in Menachot gives a complementary conceptual explanation. In ordinary private transactions, meshichah by itself does not transfer unless accompanied by the transferor's words of acquisition, and the words can be retracted within immediate speech. In consecration, however, speech itself is treated as an act, like delivery to an ordinary person. Once the sacred act has been accomplished, it cannot be undone by ordinary verbal retraction. This special severity applies to Temple consecration and bodily sanctity. Our contemporary charitable dedications are not true Temple consecration but obligations to the poor. They do not operate through the rule that speech to Heaven is like actual delivery. Their force is the vow of charity. A vow can be retracted within immediate speech like ordinary vows. Therefore the exceptional rule does not apply to our charitable dedications.
Lest they collude against consecrated property. Bava Batra says that a deathly ill person who consecrated all his property and then says, "I owe a maneh to so and so," is believed because a person does not sin for no benefit to himself. A healthy person is not believed because of concern for collusion. The early authorities ask why the healthy donor is not believed through a migo that he could seek annulment of the consecration. Ramban establishes the case where he vowed on the understanding of many and cannot seek annulment. Rashba establishes it after delivery to the Temple treasurer, when ordinary annulment is no longer available. Rambam nevertheless rules both like Rav Huna and like Rabbi Eliezer, who is concerned for collusion in another consecration case. Ran explains that there is a distinction between a mere statement and an actual divorce. A healthy person might prefer the easy lie, "I owe a maneh," rather than undergo formal annulment, so that is not a good migo. But where the alternative is an actual divorce, a serious act, perhaps the migo structure differs. A further difficulty arises from Yevamot, where one is believed concerning consecration because the matter is "in his hands" to redeem or seek annulment. That rule, however, belongs to ritual prohibitions. A single person is trusted in ritual law regarding something within his control even against an existing presumption. It is not ordinary migo. Here the statement, "I owe a maneh," is a monetary claim. One witness does not establish money merely because the matter lies within his power. To defeat monetary possession he needs an actual migo. Redemption is not a migo because he may not wish to spend money, and verbal admission can be easier than seeking annulment. Thus the ritual principle does not make him credible monetarily. I nevertheless remain uncertain about the distinction between the weak migo of annulment here and other cases where a person is believed through the power to divorce or otherwise perform a significant act. The point requires further study.
But an ill person who consecrated all his property. Rif explains Rav Huna's law and adds that even where the creditor has no document, if the deathly ill donor says, "Give him," payment is made. Ramban was surprised, because an oral loan does not collect from encumbered property. He therefore interprets Rif as speaking of an admitted documented debt, or perhaps a deposit or land. In my view Rif can be understood literally. An oral loan can collect from monetary consecration even where movables of heirs would not ordinarily be subject to the creditor, because consecrated monetary property and ownerless property differ from ordinary inherited movables. I discussed this in siman 104. If one instead follows Ramban and says the case is a deposit, Shach uses the normal presumption that everything in a person's possession is his to argue that no migo against that presumption should help. But the structure of the sources remains difficult. A further issue concerns the rule that if the dying person holds a document, an admission is not treated as mere speech made to avoid appearing wealthy. Tosafot suggests that a valid document removes that concern. If the debtor can still say the document was repaid, or in a deposit can say it was returned through a migo of unavoidable accident, perhaps the document is not strong enough to defeat the "not to make his children appear wealthy" defense. Still, one can say that any existing written instrument makes the declarant afraid that outsiders will take the admission seriously, and therefore his admission is less likely to have been made merely for appearance. This can be true even if the document is not fully conclusive. Shach also argues that migo of annulment or retraction is a migo against the presumption that property in a person's possession belongs to him. Ran, however, distinguishes ordinary alternative pleas from a migo based on something literally within one's power. A person who could actually perform the controlling legal act has a stronger migo that can work even against a presumption. Therefore the objection is not decisive. See siman 99.
