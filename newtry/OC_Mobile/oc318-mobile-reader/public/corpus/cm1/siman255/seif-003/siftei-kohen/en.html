One who consecrates all his property, etc. This entire seif is the language of the Rambam in chapter 7 of the Laws of Valuations. The Ir Shushan writes that one who consecrates all his property cannot retract even within the brief interval ordinarily treated as part of the same utterance. The Sema objects that throughout the Torah a retraction within that interval is effective, except in the familiar exceptional cases. In truth, however, Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, part 4, implies as the Ir Shushan does, and the Rambam himself states explicitly in chapter 15 of the Laws of Sacrificial Procedure that there is no retraction from consecration even within that interval. He repeats the rule at the end of chapter 2 of the Laws of Substitution, and in his Commentary to the Mishna in Temurah, listing consecration and substitution among the exceptional cases together with blasphemy, idolatry, betrothal, and divorce. The Bartenura writes the same. The source is the Gemara in Temurah, chapter Keitzad Ma'arimin, which teaches that even where he changed his declaration immediately, the first consecration remains. Tosafot in Menachot likewise write that retraction is ineffective in consecration because a declaration to Heaven is like delivery to an ordinary person. Accordingly, the Ir Shushan's ruling is correct, and the Sema's objection is unfounded.
Lest they collude against consecrated property, etc. Since the poskim contain considerable confusion on this subject, it must be explained carefully. Rav Huna teaches that when a dangerously ill person consecrated all his property and then said, 'I owe one hundred to so-and-so,' he is believed, because a person facing death is presumed not to sin for another's benefit. A healthy person, by contrast, may collude against consecrated property. Ramban, Rashba, and Nimukei Yosef explain that this distinction applies where the consecration cannot readily be annulled, such as where it was undertaken with public consent or the property was delivered to the Temple treasurer. If ordinary annulment remains available, even a healthy person has a migo, for he could annul the consecration entirely, and one does not suspect collusion where that lawful course is open to him. Although a vow made with public consent may be annulled for a mitzvah, that does not help unless the alleged debt is first believed. Otherwise the claim of a debt may itself be the collusive device by which he seeks annulment. Where the debt is established, repayment of a creditor is a mitzvah and annulment is available. It further appears that delivery to the treasurer ends the power of annulment, just as terumah in the kohen's possession can no longer be annulled. A declaration to Heaven is treated as delivery only for acquisition; it is not literally identical to physical delivery. Therefore, before delivery, speech may be undone through the Torah's institution of annulment, whereas after the treasurer acquires through possession, speech cannot undo the completed transfer. The same reasoning applies where consecrated property was acquired through barter or another completed act of acquisition. Ramban also explains that Rav Huna's case is properly understood as a claim that a specific sum or object in his possession belongs to another as a deposit, because an oral loan ordinarily does not collect from consecrated property. This also resolves the challenge that the dangerously ill person has a migo to retract his gift: a migo does not prevail against the presumption that property found in a person's possession belongs to him. The Rambam, however, rules that even where a healthy person could annul the consecration, we remain concerned for collusion. He therefore requires a woman divorced after consecration to vow that she will not benefit from her former husband before collecting her ketubah, and he likewise does not accept a healthy person's later assertion that he owes another money or that a particular article belongs to another. The Ran strongly questions this ruling, because the conclusion of the Gemara in Arakhin explains the dispute as depending upon whether consecration may be annulled, and the Tosefta and Yerushalmi likewise indicate that the view requiring the vow follows Beit Shammai, while the view dispensing with it follows Beit Hillel. Since the halacha follows Beit Hillel, ordinary consecration that remains subject to annulment should not create a concern for collusion. This is also the position of Ramban, Rashba, Nimukei Yosef, Tosafot, and the Ran. The ruling of the Rambam, the Rosh, and those who followed them therefore requires substantial difficulty. The proper practical ruling is that where annulment remains available, we do not suspect collusion merely because of the later admission. Where annulment is unavailable because the consecration was completed by delivery or another binding acquisition, a healthy person is not believed, while a dangerously ill person is believed because he is presumed not to sin for no benefit of his own. The most satisfactory reading of Rav Huna is that he refers to money held as a deposit, not an ordinary oral loan. This preserves the plain language and resolves the several sugyot consistently.
I later found in Gidulei Terumah, gate 42, part 4, that he expresses surprise at Kesef Mishneh and the Ran for questioning Rambam's ruling that a healthy person may collude against consecrated property. He argues that the Gemara in Shevuos explicitly establishes the anonymous Mishnah on that basis. With respect, he did not examine the Ran's words carefully. The Ran explains that mere speech is easier than a formal act such as divorce, and therefore we may fear collusion in a verbal admission even where we would not suspect a person of carrying out a substantial legal act merely to deceive hekdesh. Kesef Mishneh himself cites this distinction. The passage in Shevuos is precisely a case of speech and therefore does not answer the broader difficulty. I also found the relevant Baraisa explicitly in the Tosefta of Arachin, chapter 4, and in the Jerusalem Talmud, Nazir chapter 5. The Jerusalem text plainly contains a reversal of Beit Shammai and Beit Hillel and should be emended so that Beit Hillel are the view permitting inquiry into and release from hekdesh. Once corrected, the sources support the distinction of Ramban and Rashba: where the consecration can still be annulled, there is no need to suspect a fabricated debt, because the owner has a simpler route by releasing the consecration; where that route is unavailable, the concern of collusion applies.

But a sick person who consecrated, etc., at the time he consecrated, etc. The language of the Rambam and the Mechaber here is difficult. The Raavad objects that even if the sick person made the admission after the consecration he should be believed, while at the time of consecration every person is believed. The Kesef Mishneh and Lechem Mishneh attempt to explain the Rambam, and Migdal Oz cites Rabbi Yosef Halevi, who understood the phrase 'at the time he consecrated' as necessary because the unresolved question whether a dangerously ill person may retract a consecration would otherwise prevent reliance upon a later direction to pay. This explanation cannot stand. The Rambam himself rules that a dangerously ill person may retract such a consecration, and even if he could not retract, saying that a particular sum belonged to another would not be a retraction from the consecration but a clarification that the property was never included. Baal HaMaor therefore rejects Rabbi Yosef Halevi's explanation, and his rejection is correct. Rav Huna's teaching must be understood as applying even where the admission was made after the consecration. The sugyot in Arakhin and Shevuot likewise indicate this. The attempt to distinguish a statement made shortly after consecration while still discussing the same matter is unconvincing once the consecration itself has become irrevocable. The Rif's position is also clear when read correctly. His distinction is not that a later admission is never believed, but that where the sick person merely says, 'Give,' after an earlier ambiguous statement, payment is not made unless the circumstances establish a genuine admission and eliminate concern for repayment. The Rosh, Ramban, Rashba, Tosafot, and other authorities support the broader reading of Rav Huna. Moreover, the Gemara's concern that the statement may have been made merely to avoid appearing wealthy presupposes that Rav Huna speaks of a later declaration, not only one incorporated into the act of consecration. The practical conclusion is therefore that a dangerously ill person is believed even when he makes the admission after consecrating, provided the statement is made as a genuine admission and there is no independent reason to suspect repayment or another defect. The Rambam and Mechaber's restrictive phrase should not be read as excluding every later admission.
