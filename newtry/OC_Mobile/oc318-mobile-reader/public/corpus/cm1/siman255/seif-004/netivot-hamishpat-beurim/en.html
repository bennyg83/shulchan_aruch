Some say in every case. At first glance the opinions need not disagree. Rambam can be speaking where the father made a full, formal admission. He holds that even a full admission can be ineffective after death in this context. Beit Yosef, in the name of Ri Migash, explains the reason: because the admission could have been made merely so that people would not think his children wealthy, even if there is proof that he spoke in the form of an admission, those present could not know that such proof would later be produced. But if a claimant demanded payment and the father admitted the claim in response, Rambam may agree that the admission is binding. In that circumstance the rationale of avoiding an appearance of wealth does not apply in the same way. The other opinion can therefore speak specifically of a demanded claim followed by admission. There is another distinction. Rambam may rule only where the heirs admit that their father gave them no further instruction and that they paid solely because of this recorded testament. In that case they are not believed. But if they say their father also privately instructed them and said, "Give," or that they themselves knew independently that the obligation was true and paid accordingly, they are believed. If they claim merely that they paid because of the public testament and rely on a migo, Rambam may hold that the migo is against witnesses or against the legal force of the record and therefore ineffective.
