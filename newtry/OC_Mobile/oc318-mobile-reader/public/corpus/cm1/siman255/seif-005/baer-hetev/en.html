They are believed. This implies that they are believed even if they say simply that their father told them, 'I paid him,' without indicating that he paid only after his earlier admission. Nor is there a distinction between a case where witnesses were present when he said that he owed the claimant one hundred and no witnesses were present when he said that he had paid, a case where witnesses were present for both statements, or a case where witnesses were present for neither. This follows Rabbeinu Yitzchak, cited by the Tur, who holds that even if the heirs say their father told them that he reviewed the account, found that nothing remained due, and that his admission had been mistaken, they are believed. This is unlike the Ramah, who distinguishes among these cases, as the Tur records in his name. So writes the Sema, and the Shach agrees. See above, siman 81, seif 23.
A heset oath. The Beit Yosef writes concerning this, 'This is obvious,' and the Maggid Mishneh writes similarly. To me it requires study, because the claimant does not know that the father did not tell the heirs this, and a heset oath is not imposed on a doubtful claim. One might say that the case is one where the claimant never left the father's presence and asserts with certainty that the father did not subsequently tell them this. If so, the rule is obvious, but the plain language of the Rambam and Mechaber does not sound that way. Alternatively, since according to the claimant the debt is certainly due, we presume that the father did not later make such a statement, and this is treated like a definite claim. Or perhaps because the claimant is certain that the debt is due while the heirs are certain that it is not due, they must take a heset oath. None of this is obvious, however, and the matter requires study. Shach.
They are not. This requires study, because the Tur writes here in the name of Rabbeinu Yitzchak that this applies specifically where the father said that he paid before making the admission. If he said that he paid after the admission, he is believed even though he initially said, 'Give.' The Mechaber rules without qualification here as well. His unqualified language therefore appears to follow Rabbeinu Yitzchak in the first clause, where the father did not say 'Give,' but to follow the Ramah in the latter clause, where he did say 'Give.' So writes the Sema. The Shach responds that in his opinion there is no proof from the Mechaber's language, because the Mechaber merely copied the Rambam, whose words are those of the Gemara. Whatever qualification can be read into the Gemara can likewise be read into their words. Indeed, Hagahot Ashri implies something stronger: even if the heirs do not themselves claim that their father told them he had paid, the beit din raises on their behalf the possibility that he paid afterward. At the end of Bava Batra it writes in the name of Or Zarua that collection is allowed only where there is no concern that he might have repaid, such as where the heirs remained with him from the time of his admission until his death. This also accords with the general rule in the beginning of siman 108 and elsewhere that one does not collect from heirs unless the debtor admitted while ill close to death in circumstances where subsequent repayment could not have occurred. This appears primary. Shach.
