And he said at the time, etc. Ri Migash writes concisely: Rav Huna taught concerning a deathly ill person, etc. We hold that if, when he consecrated the property, he said, 'I owe one hundred, etc.,' and said, 'Give it,' payment is made, even without a document. Otherwise payment is made only where he holds a validated document. This applies specifically when he spoke at the time of consecration. If he spoke afterward, even if he said, 'Give it,' payment is not made, for Bava Batra 148b asks about one who consecrated, etc., and every unresolved monetary question is treated stringently toward the claimant and leniently toward the possessor. Once he consecrated the property, the consecration acquired it, and his later statement is treated as a retraction that cannot defeat consecration without clear proof. Therefore payment is not made, because we say he retracted. This is also precise from the comparison to Rav and Shmuel, where the property remained his. But afterward, even if he says, 'Give it,' collection requires a validated document like any creditor. Baal HaMaor and other decisors challenge Rif and Ri Migash, who say that when he says, 'Give it,' payment is made on an unvalidated document or even without a document, since an oral loan does not collect from encumbered property. See Ramban and Raavad, who offer several resolutions. Baal HaTerumot, gate 42, part 4, cites an explanation that the statement was made at the time of consecration, and Rambam writes similarly. Although Rambam rules leniently in the unresolved question of consecration, as stated in siman 250, seif 3, that concerns recovery after the patient arose, where the gift is void from the outset and consecration did not certainly acquire. Here consecration already acquired and he now seeks to retract, so doubt is treated against the claimant in both settings. Ri Migash's language is precise. This is unlike Tur and the gloss there. Rambam's language nevertheless requires study, for if the statement was made at the time of consecration, the same should apply even to a healthy person, since the property was then his. Raavad raises this question. Kesef Mishneh's answer, that the case was after the time required for speech but while they remained engaged in the same matter, is untenable, for continued engagement helps only to effect an acquisition and not to permit retraction, as Rambam and the other authorities rule and as explained above in siman 195, seif 7. Rambam's formulation also requires study: he begins, 'one who consecrates, etc., after he consecrated, etc.,' but concludes, 'when does this apply? to a healthy person; but, etc., at the time, etc.' Ri Migash's objection is likewise difficult to fit, for he asks that perhaps the patient retracted, whereas the Gemara avoids that objection by stating a presumption, etc. Tosafot there, s.v. 'a deathly ill person,' ask the opposite: resolve from here that he cannot retract, etc., and answer that the consecration occurred while he was healthy, when he could not retract. Certainly according to Ri Migash, who treats the unresolved question stringently, the practical ruling is clear even where he consecrated while deathly ill and afterward spoke and died. Moreover, if the statement had to be made at the time of consecration, why does Shevuot challenge Rav Huna? Rav Huna's rule would then be limited to that moment, as stated in Arakhin. Lechem Mishneh's resolution is extremely strained. Rather, the passage concerns a statement after consecration, as all the decisors understand. The challenge that an oral loan cannot collect from encumbered property also does not overturn Rif. The Gemara explicitly first assumes Rav Huna's rule applies without a document, yet allows collection from consecrated property, and challenges only from the concern that the statement was made merely to avoid appearing wealthy. The entire discussion requires a validated document only because of that concern, as Tosafot explain. When he says, 'Give it,' that concern does not apply, as Rav and Shmuel teach, and payment is therefore made even for an oral loan. The objection from encumbered property is unfounded. An oral loan does not collect from purchasers for two reasons: first, to avoid harming purchasers where there is no publicity, as explained in Bava Batra 42a and 175a and Kiddushin 13b. That concern does not apply to a gift or consecration, where the recipient gave no value. Second, because of possible collusion, as in Bava Batra 128b. That concern does apply to gifts and consecration, but where there is no concern of collusion, collection follows because we rule in Kiddushin that biblical law creates a lien. Here there is no collusion, as indicated by the presumption stated by the Gemara, and collection therefore follows even for an oral loan. There is no need to force the case into one of a deposit. Ri Migash's inference from the comparison to Rav and Shmuel is likewise answered, since absence of collusion makes the consecrated property equivalent to property still in his hands.
