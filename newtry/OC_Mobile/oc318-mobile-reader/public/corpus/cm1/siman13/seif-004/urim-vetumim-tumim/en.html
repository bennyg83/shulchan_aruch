Even if one witness testifies, etc. Tur rules this way, and the Gemara initially appears to support it. At the beginning of Zeh Borer, Ravin cites R. Yochanan's explanation of the dispute between R. Meir and the Sages. One litigant seeks to disqualify the other's judge and the other's witness. The case is arranged so that the witnesses are disqualified but the judges are valid, or the judges are disqualified but the witnesses are valid. R. Meir says that since the litigant is believed to disqualify the witnesses, he is also believed regarding the judges. The Sages disagree. This implies that even where there is a migo supporting his allegation, he is still not believed to disqualify the judges, although his personal interest is limited because other courts are available, as the Gemara itself notes. But there is a difficulty. According to the Gemara's first explanation, the opening clause concerning disqualifying the other's judges is established as a case of Syrian tribunals, while the clause concerning disqualifying witnesses concerns a family disqualification. Why did the Gemara not establish the opening clause too in the same type of case, where one witness joins the litigant in challenging the judges? Both clauses would then teach useful points. The first would demonstrate the strength of the Sages, that even regarding judges, where another court is available and therefore personal interest is reduced, the litigant is not believed. The second would demonstrate the strength of R. Meir regarding witnesses. That would parallel Ravin's later explanation in the name of R. Yochanan. The fact that the Gemara instead establishes the judges in a case of Syrian tribunals suggests that according to the first explanation, where one independent witness joins the litigant, everyone may agree that the judges can be disqualified. There is little personal interest because another beit din is available. Rambam in his commentary to the Mishnah, Raah, and Ramban all cite the explanation involving Syrian tribunals in a manner suggesting that it is retained as halachah. Likewise, according to one reading in Rashi, R. Dimi explains the dispute as concerning whether a party who was required to clarify his proof remains bound to that proof. R. Meir holds that because the litigant must produce another valid set of witnesses, he lacks a meaningful personal interest in disqualifying this particular set. The same logic should apply to judges, because other judges are ordinarily available in the city and Torah law is the same for all of them. It would be strained to answer that the Gemara avoided establishing both clauses in the same manner merely because then the Mishnah could have combined them into one sentence, "This one disqualifies the other's judge and witness, and the Sages say he cannot disqualify them," instead of separating the two disputes. That textual consideration does not seem sufficient. Accordingly, there is room to say that under the first explanation, where one witness joins the litigant in challenging a judge and another beit din is readily available, the challenge may indeed be accepted according to everyone. This point requires further study in relation to Tur's unqualified ruling.
