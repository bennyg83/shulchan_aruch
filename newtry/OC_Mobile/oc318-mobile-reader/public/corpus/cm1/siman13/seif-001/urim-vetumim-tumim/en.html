The Mishnah teaches: civil cases are heard by three, each litigant selects one judge, and the two selected judges choose the third. The phrase "civil cases by three" plainly governs the procedure that follows and explains how the panel of three is formed. If the two selected judges disagree, it is obvious that a third is required to decide between them. Even according to Shmuel, who holds that two judges can adjudicate, if the two do not agree no judgment can emerge without a third. If they do agree, however, then according to the disputed ruling two are sufficient. How then can the Mishnah state categorically that the case is heard by three? The Gemara also asks why the procedure is arranged so that each litigant chooses one judge. According to the cited view, the answer would be simple: this procedure avoids unnecessarily increasing the number of judges when the two agree. Yet the Gemara gives no such answer. Necessarily, when each litigant selected one judge to adjudicate according to Torah law, a panel of three is still required, just like any other beit din. The purpose of three is not merely to break a tie. Even when the first two incline to the same result, the third may offer a new reason that causes both of them to reconsider and agree with him. Through three judges the law is clarified more completely. Sema explains that three are required only where judges impose judgment upon the parties against their will. Here, since each litigant voluntarily accepted one judge, two can suffice. According to this reasoning, if plaintiff and defendant jointly accepted two judges to decide their dispute according to Torah law, those two should likewise suffice. But Tosafot in Sanhedrin 5b state explicitly that even where litigants come voluntarily before two people and say, "Judge us according to the ordinary law," a valid Torah-law judgment still requires three. The same must therefore be true where each party selected one judge. Acceptance does not reduce the normal number required for a Torah-law tribunal. One might instead say that according to Rambam, following R. Acha son of R. Ika, d'oraisa one judge is valid and three are required only because of concern for unqualified street-corner judges. Where each side deliberately selects a judge, we need not fear that problem because presumably they will choose Torah scholars. But this too is insufficient. The same reasoning would apply where the parties jointly choose two judges, yet Tosafot still require three. Therefore I see no firm basis for the ruling. Its source is said to be a responsum of Mahari Weil. But aside from Mahara Sasson's understanding that Mahari Weil was speaking of arbitrators for compromise rather than judges, Mahari Weil's own language appears to concern a case where the parties explicitly accepted those two alone to judge between them. If they specifically accepted a two-person tribunal, their acceptance governs and they cannot afterward demand a third so long as the two act within the accepted terms. The index to the responsum likewise indicates that one may not use stratagems to enlarge the panel after such acceptance. But where the ordinary arrangement was simply that each party selected one judge, with no explicit agreement that these two alone would decide according to Torah law, the normal requirement of three remains, exactly as if the parties jointly selected a beit din. Taz likewise writes that the cited ruling is not correct. This requires substantial study.
