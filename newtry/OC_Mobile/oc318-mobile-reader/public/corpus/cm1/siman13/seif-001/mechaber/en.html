How Judges Are Selected When the Litigants Do Not Agree. Seven seifim. If one litigant says, 'So-and-so shall judge for me,' and the other says, 'So-and-so shall judge for me,' those two judges, one selected by each litigant, select a third judge. The third judge need not be acceptable to the litigants. All three judge the case, and in this way the true judgment will emerge. Even if the judge selected by one party is a great sage who possesses semichah, he cannot compel the other party to litigate before that judge alone; the other party may also select whomever he wishes. {Rama: Since each party selects one judge, the litigants heed them. Each judge examines every lawful argument in favor of the party who selected him, while the third hears the arguments of both, and together they rule truthfully (Tur). If the two sides cannot agree on the third judge, the city leaders appoint three judges for them. If there are no city leaders, the claimant goes before three judges, who compel the defendant to litigate before them. Likewise, if the defendant schemes to select an unfit judge to sit alongside a fit judge, he is compelled either to litigate before three judges, as explained above in siman 3, or to select a fit judge. Some say that if the two selected judges can reach agreement, they need not select a third (Mahariv, siman 11). Others say that if the defendant proposes that each party select two judges and those four select a fifth, he may demand this, because the greater the number of judges, the more likely the true judgment is to emerge (Ramban in his commentary on the Torah).}
