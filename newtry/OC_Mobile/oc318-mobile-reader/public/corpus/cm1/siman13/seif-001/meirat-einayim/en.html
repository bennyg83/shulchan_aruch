One of the litigants, etc. Kesef Mishneh explains Rambam to mean that if one litigant happens to request the procedure whereby each side selects a judge, we listen to both parties, and the two selected judges choose a third. This is beneficial because the judgment will thereby emerge truthfully. The Mishnah is not imposing an absolute obligation that every case must begin this way. The Mechaber likewise has already ruled repeatedly that three ordinary judges, or one expert where applicable, can adjudicate without consent. Here he teaches only that where each party chooses one judge, the selection is honored and those two then choose the third. Indeed, the better reading is that once each party has selected one, those two judges themselves select the third even against the parties' wishes.
They choose, etc. See the Perishah.
Even, etc. This means where the party actually spoke with and persuaded the person to serve as his selected judge. If he merely said, 'Come, let us litigate before the sage of our city,' that sage is not his partisan selection but the judge of both parties.
Whomever he wishes. Whether greater or lesser in stature; see the Perishah.
They obey them. Each litigant says, in effect: I chose this judge because I trust him to identify and present any legitimate argument in my favor. Therefore he is less likely to question the eventual ruling.
Each judge also turns over every side of the case, etc. The selected judges may not act as advocates for arguments they themselves consider unsound. Rather, each carefully understands the position of the litigant who selected him and raises any genuine legal merit. In this way no valid point is overlooked. The two then deliberate before the third, who decides. The Rosh, Tur, and Nimukei Yosef explain that each selected judge studies the case patiently and does not rush to rule against the party who selected him before the matter has been fully clarified.
If the defendant schemes, etc. If the claimant instead chooses an unfit judge as a delaying tactic, the defendant need not use formal coercion. He may simply refuse to answer until the claimant selects a suitable judge.
There is no need to select a third judge. This is because the requirement of three applies when judges adjudicate a defendant against his will. Here each party accepted one selected judge. If those two agree, their agreement suffices. This is not because a third would necessarily agree or because there is already a majority, for majority rule applies only after all members have actually deliberated and expressed their views. A third judge might offer a reason that would persuade both.
If the defendant, etc. It appears the same applies to the claimant. Ramban writes generally. If the defendant may impose the trouble and expense of additional judges, all the more so the claimant can in an appropriate case. Rama mentions the defendant because the source was discussing compulsory jurisdiction and because ordinarily the defendant is the one who seeks additional procedures to delay the case.
