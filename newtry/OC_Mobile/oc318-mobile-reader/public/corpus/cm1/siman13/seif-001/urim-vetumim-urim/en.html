When one says, "So and so shall judge for me, etc." Be'er Sheva writes that if the defendant says, "I will not go with you to beit din until you tell me your claim," the plaintiff may answer, "I will disclose my arguments only in beit din." Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 143, agrees. Shach above, siman 11, seif katan 1, disagrees. It appears to me that the plaintiff must at least state the amount he claims, such as one hundred or two hundred, because the defendant may say, "Perhaps I will simply pay you, and why should I suffer the indignity of beit din?" But if the plaintiff states the amount, and the defendant then demands, "Tell me how and why I owe you, from what transaction the obligation arose," and similar details, Be'er Sheva is correct that the plaintiff need not disclose them before coming to beit din. Otherwise the defendant may learn how to prepare false and deceptive answers. Even in beit din, where there is no indication of fraud, a litigant need not clarify more than necessary, as explained below in siman 75, seif 1. All the more so outside beit din, where a person may properly refrain from revealing the full theory of his claim.
These are two judges, etc. The novelty is that once each litigant chooses one judge, those two judges immediately choose a third, even against the will of the litigants. The two selected judges make that choice themselves. More generally, whenever monetary law requires three judges, it is proper and preferable for each side to choose one judge and for those two to choose the third, because the truth is more likely to emerge. Sema.
The third judge need not be, etc. This applies where each party selected a judge for adjudication according to Torah law. If each selected one for compromise, however, the two cannot add a third without the litigants' consent. The power to appoint the third against their will applies to adjudication, not compromise. Bach, based on the Tur's wording.
Even if he was, etc. "A great sage and ordained, etc." is Rambam's language. Tur asks: a sole expert judge may adjudicate against a person's will, as explained above in siman 3. Perishah and Sema answer that if one merely says, "Come, let us be judged before the sage so and so," without first persuading that sage to act as his chosen arbitrator, the sage is not a "chosen judge" but an ordinary judge, and as an expert he may compel adjudication. But once the litigant approached and persuaded him specifically to serve as his chosen judge, the opposing party may say, "Then I too will choose one." This is strained, as Shach notes. Bach answers that a sole expert can compel only one who refuses all adjudication. If the defendant agrees to be judged but asks to choose an additional judge so that the case will be more carefully clarified, his request is heard.
He too chooses whomever he wishes. The person selected must nevertheless be learned, or one whom the public accepted as competent, though he need not be as great as the other side's chosen judge. Shach in the name of Rabbeinu Yeruham.
The litigants will accept their decision. Rabbeinu Yeruham explains that the parties will not question the judgment afterward, because each one's selected judge searched diligently for every legitimate argument in his favor. In addition, the judges themselves will not act hastily, but will deliberate carefully and reach a just ruling. Sema.
If the defendant schemes, etc. If the plaintiff is the one scheming, the defendant may answer, "I will not respond to your questions until you choose a proper judge." Sema.
