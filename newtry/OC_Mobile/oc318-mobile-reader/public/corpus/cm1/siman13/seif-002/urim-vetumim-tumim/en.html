Wherever he cannot retract, he cannot add judges. This implies that once the litigants have already presented their claims and can no longer retract their selection, they likewise cannot demand additional judges. In Shev Yaakov a difficulty is cited in the name of the late Gaon R. Baruch Kahana, Av Beit Din of Fürth. Ramban, in his commentary to Devarim, derives the rule permitting a litigant to add judges from Sifrei's exposition of Moshe's words about Israel's burden: when a litigant saw his opponent prevailing in the case, he would say, "I will add judges." But this clearly describes a case after the parties had already presented their claims, since the litigant had already seen that his opponent was prevailing. How then can Rama say that once the claims were presented the litigant cannot add judges? Shev Yaakov considers distinguishing between plaintiff and defendant, but correctly rejects that distinction. His own answer, however, is also very strained. He suggests that the litigant's demand to add judges was legally ineffective because it came too late, but the very making of such an ineffective demand was itself part of the burden imposed on Moshe. This is difficult. If every legally useless argument uttered by litigants counts as a special burden, there are countless such statements in litigation. Parties constantly make lengthy arguments, bring irrelevant proofs, and assert things that make no difference to the law. Why would Sifrei choose specifically the request for additional judges as the paradigmatic burden? Moreover, the generation of the wilderness was a generation of knowledge. Would they really not know a basic rule that judges cannot be added after the case has already been argued? What burden is there in a claim everyone knows is legally meaningless? According to that explanation, Ramban's own writings would also contradict each other. His Torah commentary would imply that after claims were presented one cannot add judges, whereas in his novellae he states explicitly that judges can be added until a written record is made. At first glance, however, there is no difficulty. Even after the parties have presented their claims, in a place where the custom is to write a formal record of the selected judges, they can still retract until that document is written. Rama says expressly that only where there is no custom to make such a writing does presentation of the claims itself prevent retraction. This implies that where the custom is to write, the parties can still retract after presenting claims until the writing is completed. The Sifrei can therefore speak of such a circumstance: the litigant saw the other side prevailing after arguments had begun but before the selection was formally recorded, and therefore demanded additional judges. But this answer works only for the authorities who allow retraction after argument until a writing is made. According to Rashba and Ran, once the parties have presented their claims they cannot retract even if nothing has yet been written. How can they explain Sifrei? It would be strained to say that according to those authorities Rama's law is simply rejected and one may always add judges. If so, Rama should not have stated the matter without qualification. Moreover, Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and Shitah Mekubetzet indicate that Ramban himself agrees with Rashba and Ran that after claims are presented one cannot retract even before anything is written. Yet Ramban in his novellae to Bava Batra, in Perek Get Pashut, writes explicitly: "Even though he selected one judge, he may retract until they sit in judgment, meaning until the claims are presented before them, or until after final judgment," reflecting Rif's view that even after claims have been presented there may still be retraction in certain circumstances. He then adds: "Once they have written the selection, he cannot retract." Ramban continues: "So too, regarding selection of judges, as I have said, they may add beyond three until the selection is written, as Sifrei says: 'When he saw his opponent prevailing in judgment, he said, I will add judges.' Although the Mishnah teaches civil cases by three, meaning that they can compel each other to choose one and one, if a party says, 'I want to litigate before a larger panel,' judges can be added two at a time. The matter requires decision. Certainly, however, if they made a kinyan or wrote the selection, nothing can be added or removed." Thus Ramban there adopts Rif's view for this purpose: until the formal writing, even after arguments, the party may add judges. The Sifrei poses no difficulty for that position. Still, Rashba and Ran remain difficult, and Ramban himself says the matter requires decision rather than stating it conclusively. How then did Rama rule without qualification? The answer, in my view, lies in the reason for adding judges. A litigant who asks for a larger panel is effectively saying, "I wish to go to a more authoritative court." Ramban explains the principle through the verse "Justice, justice shall you pursue," which Chazal interpret as "go after a superior beit din." From Ramban's Torah commentary it appears that he treats a local beit vaad and the Great Beit Din as conceptually equivalent for this purpose. He begins with the beit vaad and then cites the verse concerning pursuit of a superior beit din, indicating that increasing the panel is a form of seeking a more distinguished tribunal. If adding judges is legally analogous to saying "I will go to the Great Beit Din," the matter becomes clear. Even after arguments have begun, a litigant before an ordinary compulsory court can still say, "I wish to go to the Great Beit Din." Bava Kamma states that although the local court can initially open the case, once proceedings begin the litigant may still say he wishes to go to the Great Beit Din. Yam Shel Shlomo explains "opened" as meaning the parties already presented their claims. Therefore, in an ordinary three-judge court imposed upon the litigants, even after claims have been presented the party can still seek a superior tribunal. Likewise he can demand additional judges, because that request is a form of seeking a more authoritative court. But a zabla tribunal is different. Where each litigant voluntarily chose one judge and they accepted this particular arrangement, Rashba and Ran can hold that once the parties have presented their claims they can no longer withdraw and say, "I will go to a different beit din." They voluntarily selected these judges and began litigating before them. If they can no longer say "I will go to the Great Beit Din," they likewise cannot demand additional judges, because adding judges is legally the same type of request. There is therefore no contradiction from Sifrei. The wilderness case did not involve a zabla panel voluntarily selected by both litigants. It involved the ordinary courts appointed to judge compulsorily. In such a court, even after arguments began, a litigant could still invoke the right to seek a greater court, and correspondingly could ask to enlarge the panel. Ramban's uncertainty in his novellae concerned specifically the zabla context: whether, before the selection was written, one could still demand a larger tribunal. That is exactly where Rif and Rashba differ. Rama's ruling is therefore precise. In zabla, whenever the litigant has reached the stage at which he can no longer retract, whether after written selection according to Rif or after presentation of claims according to Rashba and Ran, he likewise cannot add judges. Sifrei speaks of an ordinary court, not zabla. This also resolves a well-known question against Rif and those who hold that in zabla one may retract until the selection is written. In an ordinary valid court, once the litigants have presented claims they generally cannot simply withdraw. Why should zabla be more retractable? The answer is that even in an ordinary court, although a litigant cannot merely abandon adjudication altogether after beginning, he can still say, "I wish to go to the Great Beit Din." According to Rif, zabla remains similarly open until the written selection: the party cannot refuse judgment altogether, but he may still move the case to a superior court or enlarge the panel. Once the selection is written, even that option is closed. Rashba and Ran hold that the special strength of zabla is greater: because the parties voluntarily chose these judges, presentation of the claims itself fixes the forum, and they can no longer invoke the Great Beit Din or enlarge the panel. This also explains Ramban's apparent inconsistency. When he writes in his Torah commentary that after argument one cannot "retract," he means complete withdrawal from adjudication, which is indeed no longer available. When his novellae say that until the writing one can "retract," the retraction in question can mean moving to the Great Beit Din or enlarging the judicial panel, not refusing adjudication altogether. Rama's words concerning zabla are therefore exact according to all the positions. A practical consequence follows. Even where a city has a permanent beit din and a litigant cannot insist on abandoning it in favor of zabla, as Rama writes above in siman 3, he can still seek a larger judicial panel if he possesses the same right to say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." We have not found a distinction saying that one may appeal from a nonpermanent local court to the Great Beit Din but not from a permanent city court. Rama below, siman 14, writes that because of our sins we no longer possess the Great Beit Din in the full sense, but aside from that circumstance the legal principle remains. Since adding judges functions as the analogue of seeking the Great Beit Din, a litigant in a permanent local court can likewise request additional judges where that avenue remains legally open. Rama's statement here that one cannot add judges applies where he can no longer retract in the relevant sense, meaning he can no longer say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." Therefore Shev Yaakov's ruling that a permanent city court can never be enlarged is not correct. He did not distinguish between complete withdrawal from adjudication and transfer to a superior beit din, and that distinction resolves the entire matter.
