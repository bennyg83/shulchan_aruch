Responsa Avodat HaGershuni, siman 108, addresses Shimon’s claim that the third judge cannot be chosen until each litigant has first selected his own judge, while Reuven refuses. Shimon is plainly correct. At the beginning of Zeh Borer, all agree that the judges’ consent is required; the dispute concerns only whether the litigants’ consent is also required. How, then, can a third judge be selected before each side has chosen its judge, when those two selected judges must agree on the identity of the third? Shimon further argued that even if Reuven agrees that each side will first choose its judge, Reuven insists that the third be selected only with the consent of both litigants, whereas Shimon wants the two judges to select the third without the litigants’ consent. At first glance Shimon is correct here too, since we rule like the Sages that the litigants’ consent is unnecessary. In truth, however, that rule means only that once each party has selected a judge without qualification, those two judges may choose the third without consulting the litigants, even if the litigants object. But if the defendant initially agrees to adjudication through zeh borer lo echad, on condition that the third judge be chosen jointly by the two selected judges and the litigants, he is entitled to impose that condition. The Sages never said that the third judge may not also be chosen with the litigants’ consent. Although Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura’s language at the beginning of Zeh Borer suggests a concern that requiring the litigants’ consent could cause the third judge to incline toward one party, Tosafot Yom Tov already questions this. The Levush explains the rule on the ground that if the parties had to agree on the third, they might never agree and the defendant could indefinitely delay the plaintiff. That explanation might imply that the defendant cannot insist on such a condition. Even according to the Levush, however, if the plaintiff insists that the third be selected with the litigants’ consent, he should be heard. In the case discussed, each party regarded himself as plaintiff, so Reuven should be heard when he asks that the third be chosen with the litigants’ consent. Avodat HaGershuni nevertheless concludes that the Levush’s explanation does not fit the sugya well, and therefore even a defendant may impose this condition without being considered recalcitrant, so long as he agrees to zeh borer lo echad and merely requires that the third judge also have both litigants’ consent. Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 149, offers an explanation of Rabbi Ovadiah of Bartenura that answers Tosafot Yom Tov and suggests that the Levush may be understood similarly; he writes that Avodat HaGershuni’s arguments on this point are not compelling. Noda BiYehudah, siman 2, explains that according to the Bach, compromise does not require three judges and may be made by two or even one, so the two arbitrators cannot appoint a third without the parties’ consent, because the parties accepted only those whom they selected. In strict adjudication the rule differs. Noda BiYehudah considers whether, even in strict adjudication, this freedom to choose the third without the parties’ consent applies only where each side selected one judge, whereas where each side selected two, the four judges could not appoint a fifth without the parties’ consent because there are already enough judges to constitute a court and the parties can say, “We accepted only your judgment.” He rejects the analogy. In compromise, decisions do not ordinarily follow the majority, so the parties can plausibly say that they accepted only the selected arbitrators’ own views. In adjudication, however, even four selected judges are an even number and a beit din cannot remain evenly divided. The parties necessarily knew that a decisive fifth judge would be needed and selected the four on that understanding. Therefore the four may choose the fifth without the parties’ consent. By the same reasoning, perhaps even in compromise, if each side selected two arbitrators and the parties expressly stipulated that the majority would control, the four may choose a fifth without further consent because the compromise has thereby been structured like adjudication. Noda BiYehudah leaves this point requiring study. All of this concerns choosing a decisive additional judge. If the judges merely wish to consult another scholar about the law, the litigants’ consent is certainly unnecessary, as Rama states below, siman 18, seif 1. This is unlike the Sema here, seif katan 19, who derived the freedom to consult from the rule at the end of this siman that the selected judges choose a third acceptable to them. Were the Sema correct, whenever they could not choose an additional judge they likewise could not consult freely. But Rama in siman 18 explicitly says that even where they cannot add a deciding judge they remain free to consult whomever they choose. Noda BiYehudah concludes accordingly. I find some difficulty, however, because Rama’s gloss in siman 18 appears also to contradict Noda BiYehudah’s own suggestion that if the parties expressly agreed to follow the majority, the arbitrators may appoint a fifth without consent. Rama there says that if a community said to follow the majority and the judges are evenly divided, we do not say that additional judges should be brought in. This requires study. Noda BiYehudah, siman 3, further discusses two parties who each selected one arbitrator to decide between them either according to law or according to compromise, with authority, if the two could not agree, to choose any third deciding arbitrator they wished, “whoever he may be.” A written arbitration agreement was executed with a kinyan sudar and a solemn undertaking. The two could not agree and selected a third. After the decision, the losing party discovered that the third arbitrator was related to the other litigant and sought to void the ruling, claiming he never intended to authorize a relative or otherwise disqualified person. The other party argued that the agreement authorized the arbitrators to choose whomever they wished without excluding relatives, and that the extra phrase “whoever he may be” expressly included even a relative or disqualified person. Noda BiYehudah rules that if the original two arbitrators themselves ultimately agreed with the third, then the decision plainly stands. Remove the third from consideration and the case was still resolved by the agreement of the two selected arbitrators. Their later agreement is their own judgment, not the decisive vote of a relative, comparable to the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 13. If, however, the two remained divided and the relative’s vote created the majority, then if both litigants themselves appeared before that third arbitrator after his selection and argued their case before him, the decision likewise stands. They saw that a relative had been selected and accepted him; they cannot object only after the decision. Even before final judgment, their appearing and presenting claims before him reveals retrospectively that the phrase “whoever he may be” included a relative, as though they admitted that the original kinyan was made on that basis. If the litigants never appeared before the third arbitrator and the original arbitrators merely relayed the claims to him, ordinarily his decision would be invalid even if he were fully qualified, because one cannot decide based on claims not heard directly from the litigants, as stated in siman 13, seif 6. But in the case presented, the arbitration agreement expressly authorized the two arbitrators to select a deciding third without further admission or consent by the litigants and then to issue judgment according to his determination. Thus the parties expressly accepted that they need not argue before the third, and that acceptance was reinforced by kinyan. His not hearing them directly therefore does not invalidate the decision. The remaining issue is only his kinship. Here Noda BiYehudah distinguishes between a decision that extracts money from the person in possession and one that leaves money with the person in possession. The surplus phrase “whoever he may be” does suggest inclusion of a relative, but Rashba, cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 61, warns us not to derive new inclusions merely from extra wording by analogy. Although Knesset HaGedolah cites responsa that draw various distinctions and Beit Yosef in siman 42 cites Maharik in a manner that appears contrary, Rashba expressly rules that such analogical inferences cannot be relied upon to extract money. Therefore if the relative’s deciding vote was used to take money from the person in possession, one cannot rely on the extra words against Rashba. If, however, his vote merely leaves the money with the person already in possession, the decision stands. Rashba said only that we may not invent such an inclusion with certainty; he did not establish that no inclusion can ever be inferred, and other responsa do interpret extra wording. Kenesset Yechezkel, siman 101, cited below in siman 42, seif 10, seif katan 10, reports in the name of Radbaz that Rashba himself distinguishes similarly: for extraction one cannot add beyond what the Gemara expressly recognizes, but to uphold possession one may do so. This all assumes that the two original arbitrators knew at the time of selecting the third that he was related. If they learned of the kinship only after final judgment, their selection was made in error, and the third arbitrator’s decisive vote is certainly void.
