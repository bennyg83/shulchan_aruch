For the litigants must be standing, etc. Below, at the end of siman 18, Mechaber writes that in civil cases beit din may issue judgment in the absence of the litigants. Sema already notes the apparent contradiction and answers that there the parties had already presented their claims before the court, and only before final judgment did one party transfer his right to another person. In such a case, civil judgment may be completed in the litigant's absence, as Rashba writes. Here, by contrast, the Great Beit Din never heard the parties' claims at all, so it may not rule in their absence. Bach objects that in the case below the right was transferred to another person, and the court never heard the claims of that new person either. Bach therefore distinguishes differently. Ideally, even in civil cases judgment should be rendered in the litigants' presence, but after the fact their absence does not invalidate the judgment. Rashba's distinction is that only in capital cases does Scripture make the litigant's presence indispensable, from the verse concerning the ox that is stoned, whereas in civil cases absence does not invalidate after the fact. Shach, seif katan 8, objects to Bach. What difference does it make that the right was transferred? The original party already presented the claims, and the successor acquires no better position than the person from whom he derives his right. The later stage is therefore merely final judgment. Rashba distinguishes that in capital law even final judgment requires the accused's presence, whereas in civil law it does not. But initially, Shach maintains, even civil litigation requires the parties' presence, even where another arrangement would otherwise be difficult. With respect to Bach's specific proof from the transfer of rights in siman 18, Shach's answer is indeed correct. The court heard the claims, and it makes no difference that during the litigation one party transferred his right to another. Yet a difficulty remains from siman 124. There, with distinguished women and with Torah scholars, the court sends its scribes to them, the scribes hear their claims, and beit din rules on the basis of the claims relayed by those scribes. Why should the lower court that transmits the parties' claims to the Great Beit Din be inferior to scribes of the court? Why may the Great Beit Din not decide on the basis of the claims reported by the lower court? We must therefore accept Bach's principle that where there is no practical alternative, even commencement of civil adjudication can proceed without the judges personally hearing the litigants, through agents who faithfully transmit the claims. In siman 124 the alternative is unavailable because of the dignity of those litigants. Here, however, there is an available solution: the Great Beit Din can send its legal conclusion to the lower court, and the lower court can issue the judgment in the parties' presence. Accordingly, Bach's distinction is sound. It may further follow that even final judgment should not be rendered in the litigants' absence where it is readily possible to render it before them. Rashba's distinction between civil and capital cases is derived from Bava Kamma 45, which implies that civil judgment can be completed without the litigant present, but that passage itself concerns a situation where no other practical arrangement is available. Thus, ideally, civil judgment too should always be rendered in the parties' presence when possible. Bach's words are therefore correct.
