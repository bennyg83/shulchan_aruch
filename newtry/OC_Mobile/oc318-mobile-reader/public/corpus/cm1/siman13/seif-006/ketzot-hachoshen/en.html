For we require this. Later, at the end of siman 18, the Shulchan Aruch rules that monetary judgment can be issued when one litigant is absent. The Sema distinguishes: there the parties already argued before the judges and stood before them, whereas here the proceeding has not yet reached that stage. Bach objects that according to Rashba's reason, the Torah's requirement of presence was revealed only for capital cases and not monetary cases, and therefore the distinction should instead be only between initial procedure and after the fact. The Shach agrees with the Sema and says there is no distinction between monetary and capital cases regarding final judgment. I find this difficult from the discussion in Arakhin as to why beit din does not collect from the property of minor orphans. One opinion fears hidden bundles of money and another fears an undisclosed receipt. If monetary judgment cannot be issued in the party's absence, there is a simpler reason: a minor's presence is legally like absence, just as testimony cannot be received against him because it is as though received outside his presence. It should follow that judgment cannot be entered against him either. The fact that a document is considered already investigated by Torah law removes only the need for receiving the testimony, not the separate need for a judgment in the party's presence. Likewise, Perek HaKotev expressly allows collection from an absent debtor. How could the judgment be entered if his presence were indispensable? The authorities also rule that within the due date collection can even be made from the property of minors. Even if there is no concern of payment, a judicial ruling is still required. It therefore appears from all these sources that Bach is correct: presence is required only as the proper initial procedure. Where appearance is impossible, the case is treated like an after the fact situation and judgment can be entered.
