They do not compel, etc. Nimukei Yosef writes that not only can one litigant not compel the other to give him a written version of his claims, since he can say, 'I do not want you lying in wait like a lion, studying my pleadings in order to fabricate an answer,' he cannot even compel him to submit written pleadings to beit din. Even after oral pleading, reducing the claims to writing fixes them so that they can no longer be changed, as Rama writes below.
The judge should not, etc. This refers to before the parties have orally presented their claims. Rivash explains that one should not burden the other side to obtain a written copy and prepare written responses, and that judges should hear the claims directly from the parties. Their manner of speaking can reveal truth or falsehood, unlike polished pleadings arranged by others to make a false claim appear strong.
They instruct the scribe to write them. Meaning the judges themselves direct their scribe to record the claims and supervise the wording so that it does not depart from what was actually said.
And they should not write, etc. The reason is that once the claims are formally written, the parties cannot retract them.
If they wish to plead in writing, etc. If both sides consent and do not fear that the other will use a scribe to formulate deceptive pleadings, beit din need not object, since they have waived that concern toward one another.
They cannot retract. Nimukei Yosef writes that even an explanation or excuse for changing the written claim is not accepted. Rama cites this in Darkei Moshe to siman 17.
