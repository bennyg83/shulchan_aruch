Even though he benefited through the prohibited produce, this constitutes a chazakah. This is the view of the Geonim and the Rambam. The commentators object that in the Gemara in Ketubot, in the chapter HaIshah, it says that even consuming bundles of branches counts, and this is connected to Rabbi Yehudah's view that one who consumed produce of orlah thereby establishes a chazakah. If, as the Rambam explains, the orlah case means that he consumed full fruit in violation of the prohibition, there is no proof from it that bundles of branches are called consumption. The Maggid Mishneh attempts to resolve this by saying that regarding orlah the statement may mean either consumption of bundles of branches or consumption of prohibited fruit, whereas regarding kilayim it must mean prohibited consumption, because in kilayim even the wood is forbidden. Yet this does not fully resolve the difficulty. How can the Gemara infer from the ruling that consumption during orlah establishes a chazakah that consumption of wood is considered consumption? Perhaps wood is not considered consumption at all, and the orlah case refers only to actual fruit consumed in violation of the prohibition, just as in the case of kilayim. We must therefore say that with orlah and Shemittah, prohibited consumption of the fruit itself does not establish a chazakah. Why should the challenger protest? What does he lose, when those fruits are themselves forbidden to him and destined for destruction? The chazakah that applies there must therefore be through consuming bundles of branches, which are permitted wood. With kilayim, however, where no permitted wood can be found because even the wood is forbidden, the baraita must refer to prohibited consumption. Nevertheless, that consumption establishes a chazakah, because we say to the challenger: why did you not protest? Had the field truly been yours, you could have consumed its produce permissibly. You were under no obligation to sow kilayim and render it prohibited for benefit. Why, then, did you allow your field to remain in another person's hands? Ramban writes similarly. I would add another reason. Ordinarily, prohibited consumption is not regarded as a meaningful act of consumption for chazakah, because when the challenger sees someone eating in violation of a prohibition he may regard him like a robber and pay no attention to protesting. Kilayim is different. We can say to the challenger: either way, your claim fails. If, as you say, the land belongs to you and not to the possessor, then the possessor is not consuming prohibited kilayim at all, because kilayim does not become prohibited when planted in property that is not his. Rambam writes in Hilchot Kilayim, chapter 5, that if one trained his vine over another person's grain, neither becomes prohibited, because a person cannot prohibit property that is not his. It also appears there that apart from an employee acting for the owner, even the one who performed the act is not penalized and the produce remains permitted for benefit. If so, the possessor consumed permissibly, and you should have protested. This resolves the matter well. According to this, when the baraita says that prohibited consumption establishes a chazakah, it refers specifically to kilayim. With orlah and Shemittah, prohibited consumption would not suffice; only consumption of wood would. This, however, is not the implication of the Rambam or Maggid Mishneh. It is therefore possible that the Rambam holds, in accordance with the plain implication of the Gemara, that prohibited consumption itself establishes a chazakah. We must still explain why the challenger should protest when the produce is forbidden for benefit. The answer is that orlah and kilayim are among the prohibited items that must be burned, and the Rambam likewise writes regarding Shemittah that its prohibited remains are subject to destruction. We hold that the ashes of items that must be burned are permitted. Likewise, if one cooks over coals made from orlah or kilayim, the resulting benefit can be permitted. Thus the challenger does have a potential benefit in the prohibited produce, namely the use of the wood after burning, and therefore he should protest. If so, the Gemara in Ketubot properly infers from this rule, concerning a husband who incurs expenditures on his wife's property, that even a small consumption can cause him to lose reimbursement, because consumption of wood itself is called consumption. After all, the challenger's entire reason to protest in the orlah case is the benefit obtainable from using the wood. We thus see that such use is considered benefit and consumption. This is satisfactory. Moreover, even this opinion must agree that if he did not consume prohibited fruit but merely consumed ordinary wood, this can constitute a chazakah. The Gemara asks from where we know that three years establish chazakah, and derives it from the prophet, who, while standing in the tenth year, according to Rabbenu Chananel's reading, the ninth year, warned concerning the eleventh year, "Fields shall be bought for money." The Gemara asks that perhaps this was merely good advice. But the Temple was destroyed immediately after a Shemittah year. Eruvin states that the Temple was destroyed in year 36 of the Jubilee cycle, meaning immediately after Shemittah, since year 35 was Shemittah. Thus the tenth year referred to there was itself Shemittah. What sort of chazakah could be established in that field during Shemittah? Surely the prophecy was not directed, Heaven forbid, to sinners who would consume Shemittah produce in violation of the prohibition. We must therefore say that even during Shemittah there can be permitted consumption, namely of wood, and that such consumption can establish a chazakah. This is clear.

Ramban's novellae explain the Yerushalmi concerning the dispute between Rabbi Yishmael and Rabbi Akiva, where it says that the years of orlah are a practical difference between them. If the possessor consumed leaves, tendrils, and the like, which according to Rabbi Yehudah are not considered fruit, their consumption is even less significant than consuming bundles of branches. See Ramban in the passage beginning "a major crop," etc. It appears that even those who disagree with the first opinion do so only regarding orlah and Shemittah. With kilayim they agree that prohibited consumption establishes a chazakah, for the reason I wrote above in explaining the Rambam. Ramban explicitly writes in his novellae that kilayim establishes a chazakah because, in any event, the challenger sustained a loss and should have protested. Nimukei Yosef writes likewise. This is also evident from the Tur. He writes that consumption in violation of a prohibition, such as orlah and Shemittah, does not constitute a chazakah. The Derishah and Bach struggle to explain why he omitted kilayim, which is likewise listed in the baraita. Their suggestion that it is difficult to find branches of kilayim is strained. Since the baraita itself lists kilayim, the Tur should also have listed it. Moreover, when discussing prohibited consumption that does not establish a chazakah, the Tur is speaking of eating the fruit itself, so that answer does not address the omission. Kilayim should have been counted precisely there. Rather, it is clear that the Tur agrees with the Ramban. Therefore, when he writes that prohibited consumption does not establish a chazakah, he mentions only orlah and Shemittah. Kilayim is different and does establish a chazakah. The Tur's wording is thus exact and clear. Since, in addition, the Geonim and Rambam hold that prohibited consumption establishes a chazakah in every such case, it is certainly clear that regarding kilayim the law follows the Ramban and Nimukei Yosef as stated.
