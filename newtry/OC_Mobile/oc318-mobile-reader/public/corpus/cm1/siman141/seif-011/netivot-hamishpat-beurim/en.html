He consumed orlah produce. Everyone asks on Rambam, who explains the case of consuming orlah as consumption of prohibited produce. If so, how does Ketubot 80 derive from this that eating vine shoots counts as consumption for chazakah? In my view, the Gemara's proof comes from Shemittah. Regarding Shemittah, even if consumption of prohibited produce counts as consumption, Shemittah produce is ownerless, as Rashbam writes, and consuming ownerless produce certainly does not establish chazakah. We must therefore say that the case concerns eating vine shoots, which are not ownerless, and this proves that consumption of shoots counts as chazakah. One should not object to Rashbam, who explains the Shemittah case in terms of ownerlessness rather than forbidden consumption, that the possessor must then have refrained from sowing and plowing during Shemittah and merely eaten aftergrowth. If he sowed, there would be forbidden consumption. Why is this different from a place where fields are customarily left fallow, where a year in which one refrains from plowing and sowing does not count toward chazakah, and certainly eating aftergrowth would not help? It can be answered that he plowed and sowed before Shemittah, but the crop had not reached the stage of tithing before Shemittah. The produce was therefore permitted and became ownerless in Shemittah, as explained by Rambam in Hilchot Shemittah.
He ate vine shoots. See Sema, seif katan 19, at the end, where he resolves the case of kilayim, in which everything is prohibited. Ramban answers that with kilayim the claimant could himself have eaten the produce permissibly by simply not planting kilayim. He therefore should have protested, and his silence creates chazakah. With orlah, by contrast, the produce is prohibited even to the claimant, so no chazakah arises unless the possessor consumed the vine shoots. Tumim adds that kilayim is easier for another reason. According to the claimant's own allegation that the land was stolen from him, the produce is not prohibited at all, because one who trains his vine over another person's grain does not render the grain forbidden. According to this, however, if the claimant alleges rental or mortgage, in which the possessor can render the produce prohibited, there should be no chazakah, yet the Gemara states the rule without distinction. Furthermore, if the claimant is believed to say that he gave the possessor the fruits under a rental arrangement despite the possessor's kilayim consumption, then a claim of theft should likewise defeat chazakah, because the possessor must preserve his deed against a possible claim that he was given only produce rights under a rental arrangement, as Tosafot prove in Bava Batra 38b. Moreover, the dispossessed owner could claim that the land was robbed and that he despaired of recovery. Ordinary robbery produces owners' despair, and despair helps at least regarding the d'rabbanan prohibition of kilayim even though land itself is not acquired through despair, as Tosafot write at the beginning of Sukkah, chapter Lulav HaGazul. Tumim further proves that eating vine shoots establishes chazakah because the principal source for three-year chazakah is learned at the beginning of the chapter from Yirmiyahu, according to Rabbeinu Chananel's version, and the year of the destruction was a Shemittah year. Yirmiyahu surely did not instruct people to establish chazakah through prohibited consumption. It must therefore have been through eating vine shoots. This can be answered because permissible consumption can occur in the third year even if it is Shemittah, for example by sowing before Rosh Hashanah of the third year so that the crop reaches one third of its growth before Rosh Hashanah. It then has the law of sixth-year produce and is permitted, as explained by Rambam in Hilchot Shemittah. For purposes of chazakah, that consumption still counts as the third year's crop, because the third crop was completed during the third year. There is no reason that sowing the third crop before Rosh Hashanah should invalidate the chazakah.
