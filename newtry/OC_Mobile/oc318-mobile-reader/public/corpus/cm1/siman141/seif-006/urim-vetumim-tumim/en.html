But if he incurred expenditures, etc. See Sema, seif katan 10, who writes in the name of the Rashba that otherwise all chazakot in our times would be nullified, because, due to our many sins, taxes have increased until the produce does not suffice to cover them, etc. It appears that this refers to expenses that do not recur every single year, but arise only intermittently, such as wartime taxes and the like, whereas in times of peace those taxes are absent. If, however, the expenses are constant, what chazakah can there be? It is advantageous to the owner that another person consume the produce and bear the expenses, thereby sparing the owner the loss. This also resolves the Rashba's proof from a field consumed during the years of orlah, where the expenses certainly exceed the value of the wood that is consumed. If those years of orlah were to continue forever, what fool would incur expenses merely for the wood? It is only because after the years of orlah end he will consume the full produce, and he cannot reach those profitable years without spending money during the years of orlah, even though during those years the expenses exceed the benefit. By contrast, with a field whose expenses permanently exceed what can be consumed, it is a field of loss. What concern should the owner have if others consume what the field produces while paying expenses that exceed the benefit? Certainly, then, the Rashba speaks where the expenses are not constant, comparable to orlah. This also accords with reality. If taxes and other heavy expenses were constant, the price of produce would rise accordingly, since market prices reflect the cost of production. It is generally not possible for expenses to permanently exceed the value of the yield. Rather, the case is as stated above, where at times an extraordinary tax increases, while the price of produce remains low because it is set by ordinary years. This is straightforward. Nevertheless, this ruling itself requires study, and I am surprised that the Rama cites it as established law without noting disagreement. The Rashba's entire proof is from the case of orlah. According to the Rambam, and as the Mechaber rules in seif 11, the case of orlah means that the possessor consumed the forbidden fruit itself, and no proof can be brought from there. All the more so according to the Tur, who holds that even if the orlah case refers to consuming the wood itself, the value of the wood must still exceed the expenses. According to him the Rashba's rule is not established. Although the Bach, and likewise the Shach in seif katan 1, rule like the Rashba against the Tur, I do not know why they reject the Tur, whose reasoning is compelling. Moreover, the Rambam agrees with the Tur on this point. His position is that consuming mere branches, whose value does not exceed the expenses, does not constitute a chazakah. Were he to hold otherwise, there would have been no need to interpret the orlah case with difficulty as consumption of prohibited fruit. One cannot say that the Rambam holds that consumption of branches is not called consumption at all, for Ketubot 80 explicitly treats it as consumption. His reason must therefore be that ordinary branches are not worth more than the expenses, as the Rashba himself explains, and the Rambam does not consider that sufficient for chazakah. If the Rambam and the Tur agree in both ruling and rationale, how can we rule against them solely on the Rashba's reasoning? This is why the Mechaber omitted the Rashba's ruling, since he follows the Rambam. The Rama's ruling therefore requires study.
