Thirty trees. See Sema, seif katan 23, who writes that the same applies if there were ten trees in a beit se'ah and he consumed the produce of three each year. His intent is that in the third year he must consume from four trees, because he certainly must eventually consume from all ten trees. This is also explained in Perishah.
Planted at ten per beit se'ah. See Sema, seif katan 27, concerning a case of nine mature trees. The Tur expressly rules that this is ineffective, as do Tosafot, because the acts of consumption during those years must themselves be sufficiently dispersed. If there are only three mature trees in every two beit se'ah, the consumption is not dispersed in the required manner and does not establish chazakah. The Tur does not explain the law where there are nine or ten mature trees in each beit se'ah and the possessor consumes from three each year in a dispersed pattern. Tosafot in Bava Batra 36, s.v. hayah lo, write that the case cannot involve mature trees because with mature trees the proper density is only three to a beit se'ah. Their language somewhat implies that just as young trees require specifically ten, and if there are more they are treated as planted too densely so that consuming from three each year does not establish chazakah because the excess trees stand to be uprooted, so too mature trees numbering more than three might be treated as excessively dense. Nevertheless, it appears clear to me that chazakah is effective in such a case. Otherwise the poskim would not have remained silent, but would have expressly distinguished young trees from mature ones. Tosafot themselves write, s.v. mimata asarah, that ordinary people do not space trees more than sixteen amot apart, for otherwise a person with a large field might plant only two or three trees. According to ordinary planting practice, therefore, chazakah of mature trees would virtually never be possible if more than three mature trees per beit se'ah invalidated it. Likewise, although we rule regarding a sale that when trees are planted too densely the buyer does not acquire the underlying land, the poskim do not distinguish a sale of three trees according to whether mature trees are planted at a density greater than three per beit se'ah. Plainly ordinary people do plant them in this fashion. Rather, Tosafot intend only to exclude the following case. With young trees, there must be ten per beit se'ah; if there are fewer than ten, even consuming from all of them does not establish chazakah because they are considered too dispersed. With mature trees, however, even three can establish chazakah. When there are ten mature trees, Tosafot would likewise agree that chazakah can be established. The reason fewer young trees do not suffice, even though they are not standing to be uprooted, appears to depend on the rule of Shemittah that when there are ten young trees one may plow the entire beit se'ah for their sake. Chazakah of an orchard is not established by consuming fruit alone; one must also perform the cultivation of the land required for the trees, as Shitah Mekubetzet writes at the beginning of Perek Chezkas HaBatim. If it is an orchard, three fruit crops are insufficient unless they are crops of three years that also require a full year's cultivation, because the entire field is plowed for the trees. Thus, with ten young trees or three mature trees, for which the entire field is properly plowed, all the land is considered orchard land and cultivation of the whole field is cultivation for the trees, establishing chazakah. With fewer than ten young trees or fewer than three mature trees, the land is not regarded as an orchard field for this purpose. Even if he plowed it, where he did not sow the intervening land the plowing does not establish chazakah because that cultivation was not needed by the trees. One might ask that if ordinary practice is to place the zones of nourishment closer together, why does having more than ten trees per beit se'ah fail to establish chazakah? Tosafot themselves later write that in a sale one does not acquire the land where the trees are spaced in the proportion of ten per beit se'ah, because people commonly bring their zones of nourishment closer. Tumim asks this. There is in fact no difficulty. People may plant trees closely enough that their zones of nourishment approach each other while still leaving substantial nourishment for every tree. But no one plants more than ten trees in each beit se'ah, because then each tree receives too little nourishment and the trees deteriorate. Ten may therefore be planted even when mature, because ordinary practice is to bring the zones of nourishment closer, and chazakah is effective.
More than a beit se'ah for ten. See Shach, seif katan 4, who writes that even if he consumed from all the trees, he does not thereby establish chazakah over the remaining land. This applies only to young trees, as explained above. With mature trees, even if there are only three and he consumes from all of them, chazakah is effective. Likewise, if there are nine or ten mature trees in every beit se'ah, chazakah is effective even where consumption is dispersed, as explained above. Nevertheless, the trees must not be more than sixteen amot apart. Otherwise he does not establish chazakah over the body of the land, just as in a sale. Although Tosafot distinguish between chazakah and sale, even according to them the trees must at least not be spaced farther apart than the density of ten per beit se'ah, meaning that no tree should have more than sixteen amot separating it from the next relevant tree. The words of Tosafot are, however, very difficult to me. It appears that chazakah, sale, and consecration should all have the same measure. If trees are sixteen amot apart, it is physically impossible to plant more than about ten in a square beit se'ah of fifty by fifty amot. The fact that each tree draws nourishment in a circle does not permit another tree to be planted in the spaces between those circles without mingling their nourishment zones. Certainly more than ten cannot fit, and even with ten the spacing is slightly less than sixteen amot. That is not problematic, because the trees may be placed as close as eight amot apart. All these laws therefore converge. For chazakah, the trees certainly cannot be spaced more than sixteen amot apart, just as in a sale. The novelty here is that even where they are close enough, such as eight amot apart, so that many trees could physically fit, if more than ten per beit se'ah are actually planted and he consumed from only three, there is no chazakah because the excess trees stand to be uprooted; people do not ordinarily plant more than ten per beit se'ah. The same should apply to one who sells or consecrates only three trees in a beit se'ah where the overall planting density is greater than ten, for he does not sell or consecrate the land because the trees stand to be uprooted. The Yerushalmi's expression concerning the spacing of a planting of ten should likewise mean that they must not be planted more densely than ten in the disqualifying sense just described. As to both minimum and maximum spacing, the measure for chazakah should therefore be the same as that explained for sale in siman 216, from four to sixteen amot. The Mechaber's failure to mention maximum spacing here is no more significant than his failure to state that the trees may not be closer than four amot. Certainly, even if exactly ten trees occupy a beit se'ah, if some are less than four amot from each other they stand to be uprooted. It further appears that the Rama's gloss, stating that where there is more than a beit se'ah for ten trees he establishes chazakah only over the land necessary for the trees, applies only where the trees are nevertheless no more than sixteen amot apart from one another but there are only, for example, eight trees and unused open ground remains. Since there are not ten young plantings in the field, one does not plow the entire field for them, only the area required for harvesting with basket and picker, as explained by the Rambam in chapter 3 of Hilchot Shemittah. Chazakah therefore extends only to that area. But where the trees are more than sixteen amot apart, no chazakah in the land is established at all. At minimum Tosafot require the density of a planting of ten, and widely separated trees are treated like isolated individual trees that do not acquire the land. Chazakah likewise does not extend to the land.
The same applies if they were planted densely. The wording is imprecise. Where they are truly densely planted like a forest and stand to be uprooted, consuming the produce of three trees per beit se'ah establishes no chazakah in the land at all. This differs from the dispersed case mentioned above, where he at least establishes chazakah over the land required for the trees.
