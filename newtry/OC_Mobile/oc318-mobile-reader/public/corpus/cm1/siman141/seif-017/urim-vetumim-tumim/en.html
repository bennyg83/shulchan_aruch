And some say that the same applies if they were planted consecutively, etc. The Tur, in the name of Ri, challenges this view from the rule that if one consumed them consecutively it is a chazakah. The Derishah and Bach ask that this can be answered as the Tur himself later writes regarding trees planted less than four amot apart: there is a distinction between consuming all of them and only some of them, since he must consume those not destined to be uprooted. Tosafot indeed use this answer to defend Rashbam. Yet I do not understand even that. Tosafot later ask, in the passage beginning "from planting," that in a sale, when trees are planted ten to a beit se'ah, we rule that the purchaser does not acquire the land, because the spacing must be from four to sixteen amot, measured in circles. The Milchamot Hashem in the chapter HaMokher implies that this measure is greater than the area of nurture, two hundred fifty amot, and thus he must understand the amot as square; Tosafot in Eruvin initially considered that as well, but conclude as Tosafot do here that they are circular. Tosafot answer that people ordinarily do not plant as sparsely as ten per beit se'ah. They are not particular about the trees drawing nourishment from one another and plant them closer together. Therefore, in a sale, one does not acquire the land unless the trees are planted as closely as people ordinarily plant, whereas for chazakah, even if they are separated by the full measure of nourishment, there is a chazakah. If so, how can we say that trees planted closer than ten per beit se'ah are destined to be uprooted, when that is precisely the ordinary way people plant? If ten per beit se'ah are planted, the buyer does not acquire the land at all until they are planted more closely. How then can we call the closer trees destined for uprooting and say that possession of them is ineffective? On the contrary, the law of sale is that when one buys three trees he acquires the land specifically when they are closely planted. Therefore the Tur did not give this answer except where the trees are less than four amot apart, where even in a sale one does not acquire the land because they are destined for uprooting. Tosafot's own wording is precise: "but regarding chazakah, even though they are distant," implying that if they are closer, all the more so the rule is like a sale. Tosafot's defense of Rashbam therefore requires study, since it appears to contradict their subsequent words. The Tur's formulation, however, is clear. Consider this carefully.
