Unless they rented it by a written instrument. See Shach, seif katan 17, who agrees with the Bach. According to that explanation, the Raavad, who is the first opinion, requires a written instrument even though the tenants lived there for all three years, because two persons lived there. This is difficult. The same distinction would then apply to purchasers: if a field were sold to two persons in partnership and they jointly held it for three years, a written instrument would be required. We have never heard such a rule, nor does it appear in the Gemara or the poskim. What difference should there be between one person and two? The Gemara required a written instrument only with three successive purchasers, each of whom possessed it for only one year. In truth, there is no difficulty. There, the purchaser who possesses the field claims that it belongs to him. Therefore, either way, if the challenger heard of the possessor's sale to the purchaser, he should have protested; and if he did not hear of the sale, nevertheless this purchaser openly claims that the field is his, so he should have protested. The purchaser is occupying what the challenger claims is his property for three years and declaring that it was purchased by him. How could the challenger fail to protest? Therefore, when he possesses it for three consecutive years without protest, the challenger loses his claim. Here, by contrast, the tenants say that the land is not theirs and that they merely pay rent. The assertion that they rented it from this possessor is something the challenger may never have heard, because a rental does not generate publicity. What, then, should he protest against them? They openly declare before thousands of witnesses that the house is not theirs and that they have no residence there of their own. The challenger can think that ultimately they will pay the rent to him, since this is a courtyard that is ordinarily rented out. This is straightforward. The Sema in seif katan 15 also intended this point, although his wording is somewhat confused. In my opinion there is actually no dispute here between the Rashba and the Raavad. When the Tur says that the Rashba disagrees, he does not mean that the Rashba disagrees with the Raavad in part. Rather, he means that the Rashba explains the Raavad's statement and resolves its apparently general wording, namely, that although the Raavad stated the rule without qualification, his intent was as explained above. A proof is that the Tur, after citing the Raavad, writes, "And so wrote the Baal HaItur." In the Itur, under the entry concerning protest, it says: "Three purchasers combine; the same applies to a mortgage and to a rental when there is a written instrument. But with witnesses alone there is no chazakah until each one has consumed the produce for three consecutive years." This is the wording of the Itur. It is evident that where one person consumed the produce for three consecutive years, no rental document is required. If the Rashba actually disagreed with the Raavad, why would the Tur cite the Baal HaItur after the Raavad? He should have cited him after the Rashba. The matter is therefore clear.
