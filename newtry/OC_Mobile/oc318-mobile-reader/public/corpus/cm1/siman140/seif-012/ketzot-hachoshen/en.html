Raavad says that if tenants are used to establish a landlord's three year possession, the rental arrangement should be evidenced by a document. His reason is that tenants are like successive purchasers, and a chain of purchasers can establish possession only where each transfer has publicity through a document. Sema asks that even one purchaser who lives there all three years establishes possession without a document. He answers that a purchaser is himself claiming ownership, and the challenger's failure to investigate and protest is therefore meaningful. Tenants do not themselves claim ownership, so the challenger can say he trusted them and did not know about the landlord's adverse claim. This still leaves difficulty, because a single purchaser likewise did not necessarily buy directly from the challenger. Why should the challenger know the purchaser's legal basis but not know the tenant's? The better explanation follows the Gemara concerning three successive purchasers. Rav says all must have documents. The Gemara asks whether Rav therefore holds that a sale before witnesses alone lacks publicity, despite his ruling elsewhere that a witnessed sale can support collection from encumbered property. The answer is that purchasers who failed to investigate caused their own loss, while a challenger seeing three different occupants in succession need not investigate because he can assume each is merely staying temporarily and is unwilling to possess the field long enough to make an adverse claim. A single purchaser who occupies continuously for three years is different. An owner who watches his land has reason to investigate the continued occupation. A witnessed sale ordinarily creates enough publicity that investigation would reveal the sale. If he does not investigate and protest, he loses. Raavad holds that a witnessed rental does not create comparable publicity. Even if the challenger investigates, he may not learn that these occupants are tenants of a landlord claiming ownership. Therefore, tenants establishing the landlord's possession need a written rental instrument, just as three successive purchasers need written instruments. A document creates the publicity needed to charge the challenger with knowledge. This is the more precise basis of Raavad's rule.
