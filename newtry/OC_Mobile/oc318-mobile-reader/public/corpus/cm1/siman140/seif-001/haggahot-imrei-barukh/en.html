(Shach, seif katan 16) And the Tur disagrees. Note: See the Beit Yosef and many later authorities who ask why the residents are not believed by a migo that they could have said, "we did not live there." One who studies Rivash, siman 333, will see that he understood the case in Ketubot 84 concerning the cattle keeper as follows. If the keeper had said that the creditor seized the cattle from him, he would have become liable to the orphans, because the creditor had no right to seize after death. Even though the keeper has a migo removing any concern of personal interest, since had he remained silent no claim would have arisen against him, once his own testimony makes him a litigant he no longer has the status of a witness. A litigant is not a witness even when he has a migo, just as a relative cannot become a witness by means of a migo. The Ran and Ritva there likewise indicate this. The same principle appears in the Sema above, siman 37, in the name of the Ir Shushan concerning an interested witness. This may explain the Tur here. When the witnesses testify that they lived in the house, their own liability for rent now depends on the judgment between the claimant and possessor. If the possessor loses, they too must pay rent. They are therefore direct litigants in the very adjudication whose outcome their testimony seeks to establish. Their migo that they could have remained silent does not remove that status. Nor can they afterward say, "we know with certainty that the house belonged to the possessor because we lived there for three years," and rely on a migo that they could have concealed their residence. That is a retrospective migo. When they came to testify they expected their testimony to help the possessor and did not anticipate that it would expose them to a rent claim. Tosafot, Bava Batra 31a, similarly state that a person may fail to anticipate a claim he will later need.
