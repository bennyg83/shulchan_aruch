In the inner room. According to this, if a house is known to belong to Reuven and Reuven and Shimon lived there together for three years, and Shimon claims that he purchased a partnership share in the courtyard from Reuven based on having lived there with him for three years, Shimon is plainly not believed. This is not chazakah, because Reuven himself continued to live there. Even the claimant's mere right of passage over property held by the possessor can negate chazakah. This is explicit in responsa of Bach, Maharam Alshich, Maharshal, Maharshdam, and Maharit. Ketzot HaChoshen attempts to reject their rulings from siman 153, concerning beams inserted into a wall, where one can claim purchase of a right in the wall after three years even though the owner also uses the wall. Ketzot also questions Bach's statement that even chazakah of use requires a defined location, citing chazakot involving windows, plaster, drains, gutters, dripping and pouring water, all of which coexist with the owner's continuing use of the courtyard, as well as rights of residence where the original residents remain. Although Mordechai indeed writes that no chazakah arises in the latter case for this reason, Rama rules in siman 156 that a chazakah of residence can be effective. Ketzot therefore concludes that only where the possessor claims to have purchased the entire house does even the claimant's right of passage negate chazakah, because it functions like a protest. But where the possessor claims that he expressly left the owner a right of passage, or claims only to be a partner, even joint residence can establish chazakah. I do not know how he could bring himself to disagree with all these great authorities. Nimukei Yosef also proves against him. In questioning Rashbam, he asks: if so, when one sells land but reserves a road, how can the buyer ever acquire? According to Ketzot there would be no difficulty, because if the possessor claims precisely that arrangement he should have chazakah. Evidently, according to Rashbam, even in such a case he does not. Ketzot's proofs can be rejected in several ways. First, a drain, gutter, or window is a fixed installation whose permanence is visibly demonstrated by the act of construction, unlike mere residence or use. Second, the claimant's objection in those cases is specifically to removal of the drain, gutter, or window, and at the precise location occupied by that installation the claimant is not himself using the space. By contrast, in chazakah of a house, the possessor seeks to establish ownership over the same place the claimant himself continues to use. Third, and most importantly, if the claimant and possessor alternated use of a property for six years, one year after another, and the possessor claimed that because he used it for three alternating years he had purchased a partnership share, it would certainly not be chazakah, because there were no three consecutive years, the claimant having interrupted between them. The same applies to simultaneous joint use. At the moment one uses a particular place, the other's use is interrupted, so there is no uninterrupted chazakah. A drain, gutter, or window that the claimant seeks to remove remains continuously in the possessor's use without interruption. Rashba's responsum cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 153 states that a right of ingress and egress certainly requires three-year chazakah, because it diminishes the owner's land: the claimant actually uses the other's courtyard to enter and leave, and if the owner wishes to build there and block the passage the claimant can stop him in order not to lose his right of access. This implies that even a right of passage can acquire chazakah although the homeowner uses the house when the claimant is not passing. Possibly Rashba disagrees with Rashbam regarding the inner room case, whereas Rama, who rules here like Rashbam, would not accept that result. Alternatively, Rashba may refer to a case where the claimant performed a visible act showing a permanent right of passage, such as constructing a bridge or similar structure. Without something of that kind, there is no reason to say that merely walking through another's open house creates chazakah, because many people routinely pass through open houses and owners do not object. In such a case the visible permanent act makes it like a drain or gutter. Another possibility is that a right of way differs because he does not claim ownership of the land itself but only a servitude over it, for which three years can suffice. Nevertheless, Ketzot's reasoning is needed to answer Taz's question that there are portions of the house over which the claimant never walked, so why should there be no chazakah over those places, unlike the rule in seif 15 concerning a defined area? The answer must be that here the possessor claimed purchase of the entire house. The claimant's passage over part of it functions as a protest against that overall claim, after which the possessor should have guarded his deed. His failure to do so proves that he did not buy the house and that his claim is false. In seif 15, by contrast, regarding a defined area, there was no protest at all against the portion actually claimed.
