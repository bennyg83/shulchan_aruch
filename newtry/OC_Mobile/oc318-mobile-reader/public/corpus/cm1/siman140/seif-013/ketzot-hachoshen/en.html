The Gemara records a case where the possessor says, "I bought this house from you and occupied it for the years of possession," and the challenger answers, "I was living in the inner rooms." Rav Nachman tells the possessor to prove his occupation. Rava says the burden of proof is on the challenger. Rashbam explains the challenger's plea to mean that his principal residence was in the inner rooms and he continued passing through and using the outer house with the possessor. He therefore had no reason to protest. Ramban objects that continued passage should not necessarily defeat a sale of the outer house. People commonly sell an outer house while retaining an inner house and a right of passage. If the purchaser alone occupied the outer house, why should that not establish possession? Ramban also asks why Rava would disagree, since until the possessor proves he lived there alone he has not shown ordinary exclusive possession. Nimukei Yosef reports that later authorities rejected Rashbam's broader reading. If the possessor lived separately in the outer house, his possession can work even though the owner retained only a passage to the inner house. Otherwise, one who buys an outer parcel subject to a retained right of way could never establish possession. Bach says Nimukei Yosef accepts Ramban's first question but not the second, because mere passage is not residence and therefore Rava can place the burden on the challenger. I do not agree. Rava surely concedes that if the challenger proves a retained use sufficient to undermine exclusive possession, the possessor loses. The dispute between Rav Nachman and Rava is only who bears the burden of proof regarding whether such inner use existed. This is evident from the Gemara's comparison to the Bar Sisyn properties. Thus Ramban's second question stands independently. If Rashbam means the challenger merely passed through, then why should that passage defeat possession at all? If he means the challenger also used the outer house substantively, then the possessor did not have exclusive possession and Rav Nachman's demand for proof is understandable, but Ramban's first question is no longer pressing. The two objections form a dilemma against Rashbam's wording. Bach's attempt to remove the second objection therefore does not succeed. Rava agrees with the substantive rule and differs only over burden of proof. If the challenger later proves his continued right of passage or use, even Rava would remove the land from the possessor. This is clear.
Maharit discusses a courtyard that Reuven and his ancestors had used for many years, while Shimon retained only a route through it for bringing his animals to a cellar. Maharit held that once the cellar entrance remained closed for several years and Reuven used the courtyard without interruption, Reuven obtained a presumption, because Shimon's earlier passage was the only conduct that had functioned like a protest. I disagree in this case because Reuven does not claim that he himself bought from Shimon but comes by inheritance. Once the ancestor's possession had already been challenged by Shimon's continuing entry and exit, no new protest was required against the heir, just as one who protested against the original possessor need not protest anew against a purchaser who comes only through that possessor. There is also a second reason. Rosh explains that ordinarily one must repeat a protest every three years because, after three years of silence, the possessor may reasonably think the earlier protest was mere empty talk and stop guarding his document. But where the challenger performed an actual act demonstrating his claim, such as selling the property to another, the possessor cannot dismiss it as idle words. Shimon's actual exercise of passage through the courtyard is likewise an act rather than mere speech. Once the first presumption was defeated by that conduct, the possessor had to continue preserving his proof. The later cessation of the passage does not create a new three year presumption without more.
