If the document was lost, he is not believed. See the Bach, to whom the Shach alludes, who writes that this is difficult: what is a person whose document was lost supposed to do? Should he lose merely because the document was lost even though he possessed the land for three years? In his view, "he is not believed" means only that we do not automatically believe his assertion that the document was lost and allow the chazakah to suffice. Rather, he must swear that the document was lost, after which the case is adjudicated based on the chazakah. See there. Be'er HaGolah, Ateret Tzvi, and Ma'amar Kaddishin cite this briefly, and Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 3, siman 160, rules accordingly. Tumim, seif katan 3, however, disagrees with the Bach and writes that the poskim do not imply this. Rather, in the stated case he is not believed at all. The Bach's question is not difficult. If from the beginning the possessor came to beit din and said that his document was lost, he would plainly be believed, because that is the very basis of three year chazakah: after three years a purchaser is no longer careful to preserve his document. The problem is only where he first comes to beit din and says that the document is in his possession, and then, after being told to produce it, says that it was lost. That sequence creates a concrete defect in his claim. See there. Knesset HaGedolah, letter 27, also cites the Bach and writes that the Bach's rule appears limited to a case where the possessor went to search for the document and returned saying that he could not find it, or where surrounding circumstances demonstrate that the document was lost. If he initially comes to litigate on the basis of both document and chazakah, and only when told "bring the document" says that it was lost, he is not believed even with an oath. This, he writes, is the case addressed by Rabbeinu Chananel and Rav Hai Gaon. See there.
