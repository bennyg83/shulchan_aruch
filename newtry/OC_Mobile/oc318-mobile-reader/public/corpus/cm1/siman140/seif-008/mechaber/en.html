For houses intended as residences, the possessor must bring witnesses that he lived there or rented them out for three consecutive years. Once he brings such witnesses, he establishes presumptive ownership. If the protesting claimant says, 'I know he did not live there at night,' the possessor must bring witnesses who testify expressly concerning the nights as well. Neighbors may testify that they did not notice him leave at night, but saw him act in the ordinary manner, entering in the evening and leaving in the morning. If the claimant further says, 'I saw him leave at night after the neighbors were asleep,' presumptive ownership is not established unless the possessor brings tenants who testify, 'We rented the house from him and lived there by day and night.' If the protesting claimant is a traveling peddler who circulates among towns, we make this claim for him even if he did not state with certainty that the possessor failed to live there by day and night. According to the Rambam, even if any protesting claimant merely says, 'Perhaps he did not live there by day and night,' the possessor must bring witnesses that he did. If the possessor himself is a traveling peddler, even when the claimant does not raise the issue, we require the possessor to bring witnesses that he held it by day and night for three years. {Rama: Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh disagree with all this. They hold that whenever the neighbors testify that he lived there in the manner neighbors ordinarily know, presumptive ownership is established. This is so even though the neighbors sometimes left the city for business and did not know whether he remained in the house while they were away, provided they testify that they left him there when they departed and found him living there when they returned. If the possessor tells witnesses without qualification, 'Testify for me that I lived there for three years with the presumption of both days and nights,' then even if they do not know that he lived there every day and night but saw him there on some days and some nights, his claim is valid and their testimony suffices if they testify in accordance with it, even when the claimant says with certainty that he did not live there at night. Similarly, if those living there were traveling peddlers, although they sometimes left the house and did not live there for three uninterrupted years, they establish presumptive ownership when the house remained under their possession for three years, even though they traveled for many days and did not sleep there. The same applies to any possessor who sometimes leaves the house for business and returns immediately upon coming back: this is considered possession for three years. The same principle applies to synagogue seats. If witnesses testify that he sat in the place whenever he entered the synagogue, he establishes presumptive ownership even though he sometimes changed places because of mourning or did not enter the synagogue, since he used it in the customary manner. It appears proper to rule this way, although some disagree. In a synagogue where the custom is for each person to write his name upon his place, the inscription has the force of a document. The seat belongs to the person whose name is written upon it, and a protesting claimant has no claim. See Responsa of the Rosh, rule 5, responsum 5; Responsa Rashba 543; and notes 6 and 80.}
