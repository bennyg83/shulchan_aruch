And if he adds the claim. This ruling is from the Rosh. At first glance it is difficult to understand how the Rosh derives it from the Rashbam. If he reached the rule independently from logic, another difficulty arises. We find that Rabbeinu Tam disagrees with the Rashbam only in interpretation of the sugya. Rashbam explains Mar Zutra's admission as meaning that testimony of neighbors is unnecessary unless the challenger makes the specific claim, and he holds that neighbors suffice when they testify that the possessor lived there in the ordinary manner of the world. If the challenger does not make that claim, ordinary witnesses suffice. Rabbeinu Tam, by contrast, explains the phrase "if he claims" as referring to the possessor, and likewise holds that a general statement that one possessed the property by day and night is sufficient. Where, then, is there evidence that Rabbeinu Tam rejects the further rule that if the challenger claims with certainty that the possessor left after the neighbors had gone to sleep, concerning a matter the neighbors could not know, that such a claim cannot be made according to Rabbeinu Tam? The Shach in seif katan 11 similarly questions the Tur. It appears that the Rosh found a difficulty in Rashbam's interpretation. Rashbam reads the phrase "if he definitely claims" as referring to Abaye and Rava, meaning that neighbors are unnecessary unless the challenger claims. The Rosh therefore understood Rashbam to hold that if the challenger adds the specific claim, even the neighbors do not suffice. The statement still refers to Abaye, but it necessarily implies that an actual claim is required, because the Gemara says that if he claims it, his claim is effective, implying that without the claim it has no effect at all. According to Rabbeinu Tam, however, the phrase "if he claims" refers to the possessor and is lenient, namely that even a general assertion is sufficient. There is therefore no basis to impose upon the possessor a requirement greater than testimony of the neighbors merely because the challenger advances a more detailed claim. One should not object that according to the Rosh's explanation of Rashbam, Rava's statement is needed only to reject the questioner's assumption, leaving no room for Mar Zutra's novel ruling concerning a definite claim that the possessor left after the neighbors slept. That is incorrect, because this novelty is introduced by Mar Zutra; Rava's statement concerns only the interpretation of the Mishnah.
The challenger is a traveling peddler. According to this, it would seem that the same rule certainly applies to heirs or when the challenger is absent. Nevertheless, it appears to me that we plead only that perhaps the possessor did not live there at night, which is enough to require testimony of neighbors. We do not plead the far less common possibility that he left specifically after the neighbors went to sleep, which would require testimony from tenants themselves. The Shach in seif katan 11 treats this stringent view as primary. If so, every chazakah of a house established in the challenger's absence or against heirs would require tenant witnesses specifically, yet no posek has ever stated such a rule. Plainly we do not plead so remote a possibility. Even when a litigant actually raises it, we have not heard that practice follows the Shach; the primary ruling is like the Rama. Even according to the Mechaber, the matter appears to depend on the judgment of beit din. If the possessor lived there with his wife and children and kept many household articles there, it is unreasonable to assume that he moved his residence out every night and returned everything by day.
Perhaps he did not live in it. See Shach, seif katan 7. Even though the possessor can answer that the challenger did not find him because he sometimes left for business, this proves that even the Rambam and Rashbam agree that ordinary departure for business does not interrupt chazakah. Although Rashbam interprets the Gemara's reference to peddlers as describing the challenger, so that this rule is not expressly proven by the Gemara on his reading, it is nevertheless compelled by reason. Otherwise, chazakah of houses could virtually never exist, for a person cannot be required to remain inside his house without leaving for three entire years. One should not object from the Rambam and Mechaber, who according to their approach say that when the possessor himself is a peddler we plead for him. If going out for business does not interrupt chazakah, why is a special plea needed? The answer is that we plead that even during periods when he was home he might not have slept there at night. The challenger's failure to make this precise claim is explained by the fact that when he failed to find the possessor at night, he naturally attributed the absence to the possessor's business travel. The Shach further explains the Rambam's statement that if the challenger makes the claim, the tenants must bring proof, by saying that the case is one where they have already paid the rent. At first glance this is difficult, because then what is added by the Rambam's statement that witnesses must come and testify that they lived there by day and night? Their own testimony of three years would not suffice in any event. It therefore appears that the Rambam teaches that whenever there are no other witnesses that the tenants lived there by day and night and the tenants have already paid the rent, even if the neighbors know they lived there for three years, so long as the neighbors do not know specifically about day and night occupancy, the rent itself may be recovered from the tenants. Since the land returns, its produce also returns. Where there are witnesses that the tenants occupied it but no witnesses to a complete day and night chazakah, the challenger can recover the rent on this uncommon claim, even though ordinarily one might distinguish between using an uncommon claim to preserve the original owner's chazakah in the land and using it affirmatively to take money from the tenants. The Rambam teaches that recovery is nevertheless permitted. On this reading, Mar Zutra explains the earlier statement that the tenants are interested witnesses and cannot testify for the possessor, then adds that if the challenger actually makes this claim against the tenants in order to recover money from them, the claim is effective. If he does not raise it, however, we do not advance such an uncommon uncertain claim for him in order to extract money.
