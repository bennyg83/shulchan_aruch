According to Rambam, even if the protester makes only an uncertain claim, etc. In my humble opinion, this cannot mean a purely uncertain claim. The reason scattered residence does not establish chazakah is necessarily that the protester can say, 'When I came there I found my house empty, so I did not protest,' as Rashbam explains regarding a locale where fields are left fallow. What, then, can he claim merely on uncertainty? He should have gone and checked whether the possessor lived there. Further, if an uncertain claim sufficed, why would beit din not raise it for him even without traveling merchants? Beit din must always examine witnesses carefully lest property be taken unlawfully, for perhaps they do not know about the nights. Ribam posed this objection to Rashbam. Tosafot answer that testimony concerning the days presumptively includes the nights unless the protester claims with certainty that he did not reside there. That answer works only according to Rashbam, who requires a certain claim. According to Rambam, if even uncertainty obligates proof of nighttime residence, we evidently do not presume that daytime residence includes nighttime residence. The question therefore returns: why do we not raise this claim even without merchants? Rather, Rambam's uncertain claim is not pure uncertainty. He claims, 'I was there several nights and did not find him, and perhaps that was because he never lived there at night.' Although the possessor can answer that he happened to be away on business, once the protester says that he came repeatedly and did not find him, the possessor must prove nighttime residence, otherwise the protester can say this is why he did not protest. But if the protester does not claim that he came and failed to find him, beit din does not raise the claim for him, except in the case of traveling merchants. According to Rashbam, even repeated visits without finding him remain uncertain, because perhaps he was then away on business, until the protester claims, 'I know he did not live there at night,' such as that he was there every night and did not find him, or saw him leave after the neighbors had gone to sleep. According to Rambam, the lesser claim described above suffices. This appears correct, and Rambam's interpretation of the Gemara is primary; Ramban and the Maggid Mishneh also agree. Rambam further writes that even where tenants testify, 'He rented it to us and we lived there day and night,' if the protester demands that they bring witnesses to their day and night residence, they must do so. The Maggid Mishneh explains that this applies even while the rent remains in their hands, since the matter depends on their own testimony and they are interested witnesses. He concludes that the Raavad and others disagree, and he agrees with them. The Sema quotes this. In my humble opinion, Rambam too speaks only after they paid the rent. He did not need to state this because he relied on Hilchot Edut. The Raavad did not object, but merely explained Rambam. Rambam likewise understands Mar Zutra's statement in the Gemara as applying where the tenants themselves cannot testify, such as where they already paid. Therefore, if the protester demands that they bring two witnesses that they lived there day and night, his demand is valid. See there. This also appears to be the understanding of Torat Chaim.

Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh disagree, etc. The Sema writes that the authorities and the Rama, in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, refer specifically to a statement made to the neighbors. In my humble opinion this is incorrect, for there is no distinction between ordinary witnesses and neighbors. The plain language of the Rama and the authorities indicates that he makes the statement to other witnesses, and Tosafot's wording proves this. Further, if so, what did Mar Zutra add by saying, 'If he claims and says, etc.'? That would merely repeat Abaye. Had Mar Zutra intended to explain Abaye, he should have said, 'This applies only if he claims,' rather than 'If he claims.' Rather, Mar Zutra means that Abaye's requirement of neighbors, and Rava's requirement of ordinary witnesses who say, 'We rented from him,' applies only when no special claim is made. But if the protester demands, 'Let two witnesses come and testify for me,' his demand addresses ordinary witnesses as well.
Even if the protester claims with certainty, etc. It appears to me that if the protester claims with certainty, there is no chazakah. I have already written that Rambam's interpretation of the Gemara is primary even for an uncertain claim, and the Maggid Mishneh and Ramban agree. Moreover, on a certain claim Rabbeinu Chananel is a lone view. Ribam, Rabbeinu Yonah, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Rashbam, Raavan, and Rashba all disagree, and Hagahot Maimoniyot records that Ribam and Ritzba rejected Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam. Aviasaf likewise rejected Rabbeinu Tam. This is the correct ruling. Perhaps the Rama's statement that it is proper to rule accordingly refers only to synagogue seats and similar matters, where even Rambam, Rashbam, and their colleagues would agree. Rashba explicitly follows Rashbam but agrees concerning synagogue seats and the like. The Ir Shushan, however, understands the Rama as applying broadly. As a matter of law, the position stated here appears primary. See the Baal HaIttur at length. It further appears that Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam do not actually dispute Rashbam's practical ruling. Tosafot, the Rosh, and the other authorities never say that Rabbeinu Tam holds that even a protester's certain claim that he did not reside there at night is ineffective. They merely state that if the possessor asks witnesses to testify generally that he lived there for three years, including days and nights, this suffices. That concerns an ordinary case where the protester does not make a certain claim. The Rosh therefore also cites Rashbam's explanation at length, because they do not disagree in practice. I do not know the source for the Tur's statement that according to Rabbeinu Tam, even if the protester claims with certainty that he did not reside there at night, general testimony suffices. His words require study.
