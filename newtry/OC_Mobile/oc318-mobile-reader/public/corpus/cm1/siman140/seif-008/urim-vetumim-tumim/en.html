According to Rambam, even if the challenger claims only uncertainty. His intention is not that the challenger now merely says, "perhaps," with no further explanation. If that were enough, why would his uncertain statement matter more than complete silence, since beit din could always raise the same uncertainty? Rather, after the three years have already passed it is difficult to determine whether the possessor lived in the house continuously, both day and night. The challenger says that throughout the three years, while the possession was occurring, he repeatedly tried to discover whether the possessor was living there every night as well as every day so that he would know whether to protest, but at that time he could not determine the matter. He therefore refrained from protesting. Since the fact could then have been clarified relatively easily, his explanation reveals a real weakness in the chazakah. This is why the matter depends upon his claim. The Shach writes something similar, but my formulation fits Rambam's language better because the challenge remains entirely one of uncertainty rather than requiring the challenger to say definitely that he searched at night and did not find him. Rambam further writes that even the tenants themselves are not believed to testify that they lived there day and night. Raavad already objects. The Shach tries to explain Rambam as speaking after they already paid the rent, so they are interested witnesses. This is difficult. If so, why focus on the challenger's claim that perhaps they did not live there continuously? Their testimony would be disqualified in every respect because they have a monetary interest. It appears, though with some difficulty, that Rambam's reasoning is this. The possessor rented the house to them for continuous residence, day and night, and relied upon them to fulfill that use so that their occupation would establish his chazakah. If they deviated and repeatedly left the house empty, thereby causing him to lose his chazakah, he would have a serious grievance against them and perhaps even a claim in dina d'garmei because their conduct caused the loss of the house. They are therefore interested in testifying that they continuously lived there exactly as they had undertaken. Even a remote financial interest can disqualify testimony. Rambam may understand Mar Zutra's statement, "if he claims, bring two witnesses," as referring back to the tenants: even if they are holding the rent, when this specific claim is raised, the challenger is entitled to demand independent witnesses.
And even if the challenger claims definitely that he did not live there at night. The Shach, seif katan 11, asks how the Tur knew that according to Rabbeinu Tam even a definite challenge does not help, since Tosafos and Rosh do not say this explicitly. It appears the Tur inferred it from their explanation of Rava's statement, "we lived there by day and by night." Tosafos and Rosh say Rava spoke that way only to answer the questioner's assumption. But Rava was explaining the Mishnah, which teaches that possession of houses is established through three years. The Mishnah applies generally, including where the challenger makes a definite claim. Therefore, if a definite claim required direct proof of every night, Rava's formulation would have been essential, not merely a response to the questioner's wording. Tosafos and Rosh's statement that his words were not intrinsically necessary therefore implies that even a definite challenge does not impose that burden. This is straightforward.
And similarly if those residing there were traveling merchants. Rabbeinu Tam explains the statement that Mar Zutra concedes regarding merchants who travel among towns to mean that if the witnesses themselves are merchants, they can testify that they saw the possessor living in the house whenever they were in town, even though they were not continuously present. Rabbeinu Chananel, however, explains the statement as referring to the possessor or the tenants: if their occupation requires them to travel, they need not remain in the house continuously in order to establish possession. According to Rabbeinu Chananel's interpretation there is no Gemara source for Rabbeinu Tam's separate rule about traveling witnesses. It would therefore rest only on reasoning. The Rosh and Tur appear to follow Rabbeinu Chananel, because they derive from his interpretation the law concerning synagogue seats and discuss it at length. This interpretation is also more compelling because his teachings are received tradition. We should not combine two alternative explanations of the same Talmudic line and treat both as binding law. Therefore the Tur, in its abbreviated rulings, omits Rabbeinu Tam's law concerning traveling witnesses, and the Rama likewise correctly omitted it after codifying Rabbeinu Chananel's rule. The Sema's question about the omission is therefore not persuasive.
Synagogue seats where the custom is that each person writes his name upon his seat. The Rama abbreviated the underlying responsum of the Rosh. There, the custom was that people did not carefully sit only in the seat belonging to them. Members of the town sat in different places from time to time, and the primary public evidence of ownership was the name written on the seat. In that setting the Rosh rules that the written name functions like possession or a document, because that is the accepted proof of title. But in a town where the custom is that the true owner or members of his household are careful to sit in that place, while another person has in fact been sitting there, the mere written name may not prove ownership. Perhaps the former owner sold the seat and forgot to remove his name. The writing then carries its own refutation, because he no longer sits there while someone else does. The Rosh did not speak about such a case at all.
