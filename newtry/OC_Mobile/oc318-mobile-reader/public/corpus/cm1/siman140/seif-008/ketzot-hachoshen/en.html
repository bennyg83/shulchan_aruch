I later found explicit support for this distinction in a responsum of Bach. Shimon had long used Reuven's courtyard, drew water from the well, and used a privy in Reuven's courtyard. After Shimon died, his children continued these uses. Reuven protested, saying that until now he had not minded, but with more residents he now objected. The heirs claimed a presumption through inherited use. Bach ruled for Reuven. He cited the case of "I was living in the inner rooms," where Rashbam explains that the original owner continued to pass through and use the outer house, so the possessor's occupation was not exclusive. Bach concluded that even for rights of use, where the owner and possessor use the same facility equally, no presumption arises. Maharam Alshikh and Maharash Chashon likewise rule that shared use defeats presumption, citing the same inner rooms case. Maharashdam adds that one cannot say, "They did not establish exclusive ownership, but at least they established an equal right." The entire reason three year possession works is the owner's failure to protest. Where both parties are openly using the property equally, no protest is naturally called for, so the silence proves nothing. With great deference, I think equal shared use can indeed establish a partnership right. Rosh writes concerning one who established a right to place rafters on another's wall that after three years he can say he bought not merely a temporary use but half ownership in the wall itself. The Tur in siman 153 likewise records that three year use can establish half ownership of the wall stones, and Rama in siman 157 rules similarly. This is explicit proof that possession can establish an equal partnership even though both parties continue using the wall. The correct distinction is not simply whether the owner's hand remains present. In the inner rooms case the possessor claims ownership of the body of the outer land, yet the original owner continues physically passing over that very land. His continuing exercise of dominion over the exact body claimed undermines the possessor's assertion of exclusive ownership. In a true partnership, however, each partner is regarded as using his own share. Ran explains in Nedarim that according to Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov the law of bereirah means each partner, when using the courtyard, is using property presently regarded as his. Thus one partner's use does not constitute the other's dominion over the possessor's share. A presumption can therefore establish partnership. This also explains communal residential rights. Rama in siman 156 recognizes three year possession of a right of settlement. The original residents continue living in the town, yet the newcomer can establish a shared right to live there. That would be impossible if any continuing use by the original owners automatically defeated shared possession. Darkei Moshe cites Maharam as rejecting such settlement possession because the original residents remain there and compares it to the inner rooms case. But Maharam's own responsum shows additional reasons: the community is like a powerful authority before whom an individual may fear to protest, and the continued residence can be permissive only for a term or for the father but not his children. Thus the inner rooms comparison was only one support among several. The stronger law, reflected by Rama's practical ruling, is that possession can establish a shared right where the parties' uses are legally compatible as partnership.
Bach further writes that even a presumption of use requires a defined and exclusive location. I am surprised by the ordinary laws of windows overlooking another's courtyard, plaster or projections, drains and gutters, and similar use rights. The owner continues to use the courtyard and its air throughout. Yet the other person can establish a use right. The distinction again lies between a claim to the body of the land and a claim to a shared use. In the inner rooms case the possessor says the body of the outer house is entirely his, while the challenger continues to exercise a right of passage over that same body. That use directly undermines exclusive ownership. Where the possessor claims only a right of use, however, the owner's continuing use of the underlying land is not use of the possessor's right. The owner retains the land; the possessor claims an additional easement or use right. Their uses are legally compatible. The same is true of partnership. According to the accepted rule of bereirah, each partner uses his own legal share at the time of use. Therefore neither party's activity is exercise over the other's share. Three year possession can establish shared ownership or use without any contradiction from the continued use of the original owner. This also explains Rama's law of settlement possession in siman 156. If continued communal use itself defeated every presumption, settlement possession could never arise because the existing residents necessarily remain. Since Rama recognizes it, he must accept that possession can establish partnership despite concurrent use. Maharam of Rothenburg's responsum limiting settlement possession is not decisive against this, because his principal concern was the special strength of the community, like officials before whom individuals may fear to protest. He also gives other possibilities, such as permission only for a fixed time or only to the father. Thus his inner rooms comparison is not the sole foundation of the ruling. The better law is that shared use can create a presumption of partnership, for the reasons explained.
