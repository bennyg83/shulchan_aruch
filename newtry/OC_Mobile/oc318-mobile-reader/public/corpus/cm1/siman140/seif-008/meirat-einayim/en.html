If the challenger was a traveling merchant, etc., we advance the claim for him. The Rosh and Tur explain the reason: because he does not reside in the city, we assume that he may not know enough to make a definite claim himself. See the Perishah for the explanation.
According to the Rambam, even if the challenger did not claim, etc. The Rambam further writes that even if witnesses come and say, "He rented it to us and we lived there by day and by night," and the challenger responds, "Let them bring witnesses that they actually lived there continuously by day and night," those tenants must bring proof of continuous residence. Since that fact lies particularly within their own knowledge, it is not dependent merely upon the possessor's claim that they should testify for him. The Maggid Mishneh explains that this applies even if the rent is still in the tenants' hands, because the matter depends upon the tenants themselves and they are treated as interested witnesses. He then writes that the Raavad and others disagree, and that their view appears correct. It seems that this is why the Mechaber omitted this clause, and the Tur likewise cites the end of the Rambam's discussion but not this part. See Darkei Moshe and the Derishah.

They disagree with all of this, etc. They disagree with the Rashbam's interpretation, and all the more so with the Rambam's. According to the Rashbam, if the challenger definitely claims, or according to the Rambam perhaps even if he claims uncertainly, that the possessor left at night, the testimony specifically of the tenants is required to establish residence by day and night. Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh hold that even in such a case testimony of the neighbors is sufficient. The neighbors themselves need not testify that they literally saw the possessor enter and leave the house every morning and evening. Even if the challenger claims that they did not see him live there every day and night, it is enough for the possessor to say: Let the neighbors testify that whenever they were themselves at home they saw me regularly entering and leaving my house by day and night in the ordinary manner of neighbors. Although the neighbors were not home every single day, we disregard the challenger's claim that the possessor did not live there on the days when the neighbors were absent, and we maintain the possessor in the established pattern. However, if the possessor does not make this claim and merely says, "Let the neighbors testify for me," while the neighbors themselves do not volunteer that in their view he lived there throughout three years, beit din does not formulate the argument for him when the challenger definitely claims that he saw the possessor absent. But once the possessor makes the relevant claim, or the neighbors testify on their own in this manner, we accept it. If the neighbors are traveling merchants, then even if they do not themselves say that in their judgment the possessor lived there for three years and the possessor does not formulate the answer to the challenger, beit din advances it for him. We say that the merchant neighbors failed to describe continuous residence only because they themselves were home infrequently and assumed their testimony would not overcome the challenger's claim. Beit din therefore formulates the proper inference for them, because such traveling neighbors are stronger evidence than ordinary witnesses in the sense that whenever they were present they consistently saw the possessor entering and leaving his house. This is the view of Tosafot, the Rosh, and the Tur in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, based on their interpretation of the Gemara, as I explained in the Perishah and Derishah. Rama abbreviated and copied only the Tur's language, which is somewhat obscure, so I have explained it. Rama also omitted Rabbeinu Tam's rule that beit din advances the argument when the neighbors are traveling merchants, and I do not know why. In the Perishah and Derishah I clarified these points and proved that when the authorities and Rama write in Rabbeinu Tam's name that if the possessor says generally to the witnesses, "Testify for me," they mean specifically that he says this to the neighbors, not to unrelated witnesses. I also wrote that the phrase "testify for me generally" should be read, "testify for me, without qualification." See there.
