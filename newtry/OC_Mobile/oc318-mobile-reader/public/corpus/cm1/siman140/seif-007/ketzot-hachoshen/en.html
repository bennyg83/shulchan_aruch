The Gemara explains the three year land presumption by saying that a person preserves his deed for three years but ordinarily no longer does so after that. This still requires explanation. The fact that the possessor no longer has his deed merely removes a defect in his proof. It does not itself create evidence sufficient to remove land from the prior owner's presumption. Ramban explains that the true evidentiary basis is the prior owner's silence. Three years of undisturbed possession creates substantial indication that the possessor bought the land. During the first three years, however, we still tell him, "Show your deed," because people normally preserve documents during that time. After three years, the absence of a deed is no longer suspicious, and the owner's prolonged silence can therefore operate as proof. In my view the stronger structure is that the Sages created the three year presumption as an enactment. People in practice do not keep land deeds beyond three years because, after three peaceful years, they assume no challenger will appear. If the law required documents forever, possessors would routinely lose property simply because of normal human conduct. The Sages therefore enacted that after three years of possession, if the prior owner did not protest, the possessor is believed. This explains why one who was explicitly put into the land merely for fruits still obtains a presumption after three years unless the owner protests that the possession is only for fruits. If the whole matter were merely an inference from silence, an owner's act of allowing the possessor to take fruits would be stronger evidence against a sale than mere silence. Yet the law requires protest. That indicates a legal enactment rather than a pure inference. Tosafot's question in Perek Chazakat HaBatim also fits this. They say one cannot explain that Rav Ashi always believes the owner to say, "I placed you there for fruits," because then the possessor would have to preserve his deed forever and the three year rule would lose its purpose. This is difficult if the three year period merely reflects existing human behavior. But it is exact if the Sages established the presumption in order to protect people who no longer keep deeds after three years. If the owner could always defeat the possessor by saying, "You entered only for fruits," the enactment would accomplish nothing. Therefore, after three years, the owner's failure to protest causes him to lose even that claim. This also supports Rambam's formulation that the prior owner caused his own loss by failing to protest.

Tosafot asks against explaining Rav Ashi as always believing the owner to say, "I placed you there for fruits." If that were so, why did the Sages require three full years? Rava's explanation that people preserve documents only three years would not work, because the possessor would need to keep his deed forever lest the owner later claim the possession was only for fruits. At first sight this question is surprising. The fact that people do not preserve deeds after three years cannot itself be because they rely on the legal three year presumption, for then the presumption would be caused by the document practice and the document practice caused by the presumption. Rather, ordinary people stop preserving deeds after three peaceful years because they assume no challenger will arise. That social fact comes first and becomes the reason for the legal presumption. Even if the law did not recognize three year possession, people might still stop guarding old documents because they believe the dispute has ended. Why then do Tosafot say he would preserve the deed forever? The answer is that Tosafot understands the three year presumption as a rabbinic enactment protecting normal possessors. The Sages saw that people no longer preserve their deeds beyond three years because they expect no future challenge. They therefore enacted a legal presumption after three years, provided the original owner did not protest. If the owner could always answer, "I merely placed you there for fruits," the enactment would fail in its very purpose, for possessors would still need to keep deeds forever. Tosafot's question is therefore powerful precisely under the enactment theory. Ramban's approach, which grounds the presumption chiefly in the evidentiary force of the owner's silence, has a different difficulty. The Gemara says that if the owner actually handed the possessor a basket of fruits, immediate possession can be established, yet the owner can still claim he put him there for fruits. If an affirmative act of giving fruits can be explained that way, certainly mere silence should not necessarily prove a sale. This too supports the understanding that the three year rule is a legal enactment built around normal document preservation and protest, as Rambam formulates it. Even one originally put into the field for fruits must be protested after three years if the owner wishes to preserve that limited explanation. See also what I wrote in siman 154, seif katan 6.
