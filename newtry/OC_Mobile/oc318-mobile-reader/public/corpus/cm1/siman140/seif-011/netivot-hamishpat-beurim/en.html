For if they wished, they could have said, "We did not live there." Tumim asks that even if the challenger knows they lived there, if each tenant said, "I did not live there," he would not be obligated to swear because each would have the other as a supporting witness, unless they rented jointly and were mutually responsible, in which case neither could testify for the other. This is astonishing. The migo is only that each individual could say, "I did not live there." We do not construct a migo in which each both denies his own residence and falsely testifies that his fellow also did not live there. I already wrote in siman 121 that the reason we count as a migo the possibility that messengers could say, "We returned the money to the borrower," and therefore believe them when they say, "We paid the lender," although an ordinary migo requires a superior alternative claim and this alternative is not intrinsically stronger, is that by saying they returned it to the borrower they need not give false testimony. That reasoning cannot be extended to a supposed migo under which each person denies his own residence in order to exempt himself and then also testifies falsely for his fellow. Such a migo is certainly not recognized. Moreover, a joint migo for two people is used only to exempt themselves from monetary liability, not on the theory that each could testify for the other. There remains, however, a difficulty. The reason that when the challenger claims with certainty that the tenants lived there their migo of "we did not live there" is ineffective is that they are witnesses who require an oath. This must mean that even now, when they say they lived there for three years, they require an oath. Since they are believed only by virtue of the migo that they could have denied residence, and that alternative claim would have required an oath, their present claim likewise requires an oath. Otherwise it would be difficult to classify them as witnesses requiring an oath, since on their present version they would be believed by their bare statement. But if they now say that they lived there together for three years, each has the other as a supporting witness. According to the Rama in siman 84, seif 6, where a person is believed on his present claim only by a migo and has a supporting witness for that present claim, he nevertheless must swear because his credibility still derives from a migo whose alternative claim required an oath. On that view the law here is readily understood. According to the Shach there, seif katan 21, who exempts him from an oath in such a situation, why are these tenants considered witnesses who require an oath when their present three year claim is supported by another witness? There is no difficulty. A witness whose testimony depends upon an oath is not a valid witness even where another witness would exempt him from actually taking that oath. Testimonial credibility requires that he be believed by his statement alone. Where he is believed only through an oath, or where another witness merely relieves him of an oath that would otherwise be required, he is still not believed by his bare statement and cannot serve as the requisite witness. Beit Shmuel writes likewise in Even HaEzer, siman 38, seif katan 5.
