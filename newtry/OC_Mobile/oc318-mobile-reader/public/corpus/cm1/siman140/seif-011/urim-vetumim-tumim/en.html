If the challenger does not know that they lived there, etc. See Shach, seif katan 15, who writes that if he does know, they thereby become liable to a shevuat hesset, etc. This is straightforward, and Tosafot and Ramban likewise wrote so. See what I wrote above in siman 46 concerning a migo that they could have remained silent, which we say even with two persons. Maharshal explains in Sefer Keritut that this is because both can readily agree to remain silent. The Shach sought to explain that one need not care about his fellow's statement when he himself remains silent, and the Derishah writes similarly here. In truth, however, that reasoning does not apply here, for then there would still be no migo. Even if he remained silent, he would fear that his fellow would disclose the matter, making it known that he lived in the house, whereupon the challenger would sue him on the basis of one witness, bringing him close to liability for a Torah oath, and certainly for a shevuat hesset. We must therefore say, as Maharshal wrote, that both can readily agree to remain silent, so that nothing would become known through either of them. According to Tosafot in Kiddushin 43b, s.v. Vehashta, who write that where the witnesses themselves have a monetary interest, and there is monetary exposure for them, we do say a migo involving two persons, see there, we must explain that here the two were joint tenants, each guaranteeing the other's payment. Otherwise, if they merely rented the property and each discharged only his own rent, without responsibility for his fellow, then even if the challenger knows that they lived there, they would still have a migo. If each one's claim would subject him to an oath, as the Shach writes, his fellow could testify that his statement is true, since regarding a monetary matter we do say a migo involving two persons, and the fellow would then be a supporting witness exempting him from the oath, and conversely for the other tenant. We must therefore say that they were guarantors for one another, so that the fellow cannot serve as a supporting witness because he is himself a litigant. Consider this carefully. Alternatively, the case may be where they did not live there together, but one lived there in years one, two, and three, and the other in years four, five, and six, whose testimony can combine. One then has no connection with the other such that the other could testify for him, since it is possible that during those years he was not even in the country. The Tur disagrees with this and holds that there is no migo here, because they come to testify in order to establish the possessor's chazakah in the house, and that chazakah cannot be established unless they testify that they themselves lived there. Thus, there is no migo. Beit Yosef, Maharshal, and Bach objected strongly to the Tur, because ultimately they are not disqualified as interested witnesses by virtue of the benefit they receive, and therefore they retain the status of valid witnesses. One cannot explain the Tur to mean that although there is in fact a monetary interest, we believe them only because of the migo, and the Tur holds that this is a migo used to extract property, since the possessor seeks to establish his title to this field by these witnesses, who are interested witnesses and are believed only by virtue of the migo. That would indeed be a migo used to extract property. Although this reasoning is intrinsically plausible, it is difficult to fit into the Tur's language. One would have to say that once the migo establishes that they are not considered interested witnesses, it is no longer called a migo used to extract property, because a matter is established by the testimony of witnesses. Consider this. It is possible that this is what the Tur's wording means: had they said, "We did not live there," the possessor would still have remained in possession of the field, and therefore it would not be called a migo used to extract property. Whatever they said, he would remain in possession. In actuality, however, if they said that they did not live there, the possessor would not acquire a chazakah. He succeeds only by their statement that they lived there for three years, which we accept by virtue of a migo, and a migo used to extract property is not accepted. This is strained, however, because although the reasoning is sound, the wording is awkward. It appears more correct to explain that the Tur holds as follows. Suppose they actually lived there for two years. If they truthfully testify that they lived there for two years under the possessor, he need only establish by other witnesses residence for one additional year, which combines with their testimony and gives him a chazakah. If instead they say, "We never lived there at all," the possessor cannot establish three years through other witnesses, because during two of those years these tenants actually lived there and they now deny their own residence. The possessor would therefore lose his chazakah. They do not wish to cause him to lose his chazakah. On the other hand, they also do not wish to testify merely to two years, because perhaps he will fail to find witnesses for the third year, in which event they will lose the benefit concerning the rent. They therefore prefer to testify that they lived there for all three years, so that the possessor will have a chazakah and they will retain the benefit concerning their rent. By contrast, if they say that they never lived there, the possessor certainly loses his chazakah, since in truth they lived there for two years and are now denying their own residence. They do not wish to cause the possessor to lose his chazakah through falsehood. This explanation accords well with the Tur's language. Consider it carefully.
