This is another view. It is difficult that the Mechaber would state two contradictory rules without qualification. The Sema therefore distinguishes: in seif 1 the challenger has some advantage, for example witnesses that the land is his, or the possessor lacks the advantage of three years of chazakah, and therefore an oath is required. But where the possessor has three years of chazakah and the challenger has no corresponding advantage, such as witnesses, he is exempt from an oath. Even so, the Mechaber's wording remains difficult. Another explanation is that seif 1 teaches only that, according to everyone, possession is not removed from him, and at most one opinion requires an oath, while seif 2 rules that no oath is required. The oath in seif 4 is different because his chazakah was weakened by his initial claim that he possessed a document which he cannot validate. See Beurim for another distinction. The primary practical law is that he must swear, following the Shach and Taz. The Shach further writes that if one claims, "You sold me this land," or a purchaser holds a deed of sale and the seller claims that the purchaser later resold it to him, the challenger is exempt from hesset because he is in possession. See Beurim: this applies only where there is no separate monetary claim, such as a gift claim or where he admits he has not yet paid the purchase price. If he claims that he already paid the purchase money, the challenger must swear hesset. Practically, it appears that an oath is required in every case.
