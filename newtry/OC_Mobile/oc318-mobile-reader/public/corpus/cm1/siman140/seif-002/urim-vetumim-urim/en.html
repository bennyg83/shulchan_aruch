We do not submit him to an oath. See Tumim. I noted that this law appears twice in the Rashba's responsa, siman 916 and siman 1161, and neither uses the Mechaber's wording, 'we do not submit him to an oath.' In my opinion, the Mechaber intentionally changed the language to follow his own ruling below in siman 149 that beit din does not advance a claim for an heir merely in order to impose an oath. His intent here is that beit din itself does not submit the possessor to an oath when there is no litigant making a claim against the chazakah. This also somewhat resolves the Mechaber's apparent contradiction discussed by the Sema, seif katan 3. I further concluded that the Baal HaTerumot understands the Rambam's requirement of a hesset oath as applying specifically where the protester claims that the property was lent or rented. If he claims it was stolen, we do not presume a person to be a robber. This also resolves the Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilchot To'en: where one witness claims that the possessor consumed the produce by robbery for three years, the possessor takes a hesset oath and is exempt from paying for the produce. Specifically with one witness he must swear. With two witnesses, according to the foregoing reasoning, no oath is required. This resolves Gidulei Terumah's and Panim Meirot's difficulty on the Baal HaTerumot. See Tumim at length.
And there is a dissenting opinion, etc. The first opinion is primary, as is evident in many places. Nevertheless, Hagahot Ashri in chapter Chezkas HaBatim writes that if a person purchased land from another and has a deed, and within three years claims, 'You sold it to me again,' while the purchaser is in possession of the land, he need not take a hesset oath. Even if he has no deed, but one claims against another, 'You sold me this land,' while he is not in possession and has no deed, provided the defendant answers, 'It never happened,' etc., see there. The Mordechai in chapter Shevuat HaDayanim similarly cites a ruling of Ri. The matter requires study.
