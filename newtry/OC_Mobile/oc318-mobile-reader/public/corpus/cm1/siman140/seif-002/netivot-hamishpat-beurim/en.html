This is a different rationale. See Sema, seif katan 3, who attempts with difficulty to reconcile an apparent contradiction in Mechaber. In my view, the rulings can be reconciled as follows. Rashba rules that no oath is required because "chazakah is what its name implies." At first glance, why should chazakah of land differ from possession of movable property, where an oath is required? Taz asks this. We must distinguish between possession of movable property, which rests only on evidentiary possession, against which the heset oath was principally enacted, and chazakah of land, which also rests upon the owner's failure to protest. Against that latter type of chazakah, one can say that no heset oath was enacted, just as no oath is required against the chazakah that a debt is not paid within its term. With movable property, even where the claimant says, "You robbed or stole it from me," although we do not ordinarily presume theft, a heset oath was nevertheless enacted despite that chazakah. Accordingly, seif 1 can concern a claimant who alleges actual robbery. If that allegation were true, the owner's failure to protest would create no weakness, because one cannot safely protest against a robber, as explained in siman 151. The possessor is believed only because we do not presume people to be robbers, and against that chazakah a heset oath was enacted. Rashba's ruling cited in seif 2, by contrast, concerns a claim similar to rental or mortgage, as is clear from his words. In such a case there is genuine proof from the owner's failure to protest, and therefore he need not swear because the chazakah itself supports him. Mechaber did not need to state expressly in seif 2 that the claim was rental, because it is understood that he is discussing a claim different from the robbery claim in seif 1. Nevertheless, the primary ruling is that even in rental or mortgage claims one must swear, as Shach rules. See Shach, seif katan 5, who writes that if the claimant says, "You sold me the land," the defendant need not swear. In my view, if the demand concerns money, a heset oath is certainly required. If the claimant says only, "I gave you one hundred for the land that you promised to sell me, but no kinyan has yet been made. If you do not wish to sell it, return my money," his claim is purely monetary and an oath is plainly required. Even if he also claims the land, saying that he gave the money and acquired the land through a kinyan sudar, an oath is still required. Although he is not believed as to the land because of the lack of a deed and the prior owner's chazakah, and cannot even impose an oath on that land claim, he can still require an oath concerning the actual money. Who says he is lying about everything? The evidentiary weakness relates only to the acquisition of the land; his statement that he delivered the money may still be true. Proof appears in siman 223, where one claims, "I sold you a stolen field," or "I sold you an adult slave," and the other says it was a smaller one. A heset oath is required. There is no reason to distinguish, for heset purposes, between partial admission and total denial. Moreover, land and slaves are treated as immediately tendered property there, so it is effectively a total denial. Indeed, he has a migo that he could have denied everything, because the usual presumption that a person is not brazen does not apply where he is already denying the whole practical claim. Shach's ruling can therefore apply only where the claimant makes no monetary demand, but claims the land itself, such as a gift, or a sale for which the price has not yet been paid. Shach's underlying ruling is cited by Beit Yosef here, who notes that Mordechai requires a heset oath only where there is a genuine monetary uncertainty, whereas we do not rule that way. Shach apparently overlooked these words of Beit Yosef. It therefore appears that an oath is required in all cases.
