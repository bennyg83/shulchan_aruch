If he possessed it for three years, we do not subject him to an oath, etc. See Sema seif katan 3 for his resolution of the Mechaber's words. These statements of the Rashba, cited by the Beit Yosef, appear in responsa 916 and 1161. The Rashba does not use the exact phrase, We do not subject him to an oath. He says simply that after three years of chazakah there is no oath, for a chazakah means that the land is presumed his. His proof is Rabbah bar Rav Sharshum, who sought to avoid an oath through a migo that he could have claimed, I purchased it. This shows that the purchase claim itself ordinarily requires no oath. But according to the Mechaber's ruling below in siman 149 that although a possessor may normally have to swear, he is exempt against heirs because they come only with an uncertain claim, Rabbah bar Rav Sharshum provides no general proof, as the Rosh already notes. It therefore appears clear that the Mechaber deliberately wrote, We do not subject him to an oath, meaning that beit din itself does not formulate such an oath on behalf of heirs. Since we generally plead for heirs whatever their father could have pleaded, one might think beit din should also demand an oath for them. The Rashba teaches that we do not create such an oath against the chazakah. The heirs may still impose a general cherem. If the opposing owner himself makes a definite claim, however, the possessor may be required to swear. This is the correct understanding of the Mechaber, consistent with his explicit ruling below in siman 149 that we do not plead for heirs merely to impose an oath. That is exactly what the Rabbah bar Rav Sharshum incident proves. Examine it carefully. Baal HaTerumot also appears to understand Rambam as distinguishing the nature of the owner's counterclaim. Rambam expressly speaks of an owner who says the land was only borrowed or rented to the possessor. In such cases the possessor may be required to take a heset oath despite three years of chazakah. But if the owner says the possessor stole the land, there may be no oath at all, because we do not presume theft. This explains why Rambam carefully uses the examples of borrowing or rental. The Sages use a similar distinction concerning one seen hiding utensils under his garment. On this basis, another difficult passage becomes clear. Rambam in chapter 16 of Hilchot To'en rules that where one claims the other consumed stolen produce for three years and there is one witness that he consumed for three years, the possessor cannot establish title to the land through that witness, but the claimant must take a heset oath concerning the produce. Baal HaTerumot, gate 21, infers from this that a single supporting witness does not exempt from a heset oath. Gidulei Terumah, Panim Meirot, and other scholars question the inference: why should one supporting witness be better than two witnesses establishing three years of possession, yet the possessor still has to swear? According to what I wrote, there is no difficulty. Where two witnesses establish a full chazakah, the owner's claim that the possessor stole the land may not generate an oath at all. If a single witness who supports the possessor would ordinarily exempt from heset, then even in the one witness case the claimant should not be entitled to any oath. Thus Baal HaTerumot's inference is valid. This is clear.
