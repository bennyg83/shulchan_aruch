One can infer from here that even though the possessor admits he did not purchase the property from the recipient of the gift, nevertheless after three years of possession his migo is not categorized as a migo used to extract property. Otherwise, there would be no need to reject it specifically because it contradicts witnesses. It is possible, however, that the author of the cited responsum is the Rashba, who in general recognizes a migo to extract, and therefore he needed the special reason that this migo is against witnesses. The Mechaber copied his words even though the Mechaber himself does not ordinarily recognize a migo to extract, because the ruling still teaches for other cases that even where a person is presently in possession, a migo cannot sustain a claim that directly contradicts witnesses. There is no difficulty from above, siman 43 seif 9, where the Rashba says a recipient of a gift may claim that the witnesses made an error through a migo. There the document itself makes some mistake unavoidable: they wrote the year 56 when the circumstances required year 58. It is more plausible that they made an error than that they intentionally antedated the document through wrongdoing. Therefore the migo can support the claim of error. Here, however, there is no basis to suppose any error at all. The claimant merely says the witnesses never made the transaction they describe. His plea is therefore a direct contradiction of witnesses and has no force. Likewise, when witnesses themselves are believed to say, We erred, where their signatures are not independently authenticated, even concerning a matter in which error is unusual, they are believed through the migo that they could deny the signatures. That is not considered a migo against witnesses because until the signatures are authenticated there are not yet legally established witnesses. Tosafot say this explicitly in Ketubot 19b, s.v.
