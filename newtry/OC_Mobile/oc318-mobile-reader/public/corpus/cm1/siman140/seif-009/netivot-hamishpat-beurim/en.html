Only if the rent of the house is still in their hands. It appears that if, while the rent was still in their hands, the tenants testified that they had lived there only two years and beit din ordered them to pay the challenger, and the possessor afterward produced witnesses that he had possessed the property for an additional third year, the possessor cannot demand that the tenants pay him a second time. The original claim concerning the rent was adjudicated before beit din against the landlord. A proof can be drawn from Tosafot, Bava Batra 29, s.v. hanei noge'in. Tosafot ask why the tenants should not be believed by a migo that they could have said they already paid the possessor, and answer that they are afraid of the possessor. At first glance, if the Gemara's answer is that they still hold the rent, they would remain interested witnesses because they might fear that after paying the challenger, the possessor would later bring witnesses and collect the rent from them again. We must therefore say that payment made pursuant to beit din discharges them. They can pay before beit din in the possessor's presence and tell him, "Here is what belongs to you," since they rented from him; beit din then takes it by virtue of the possessor's own position. Indeed, even had they already paid the possessor, beit din could have taken the money from him. Conversely, if the possessor first brought witnesses and the tenants paid him pursuant to beit din, and the challenger later produced witnesses proving those witnesses false, the result depends on the circumstances. If, before the tenants paid the possessor, the challenger had already produced witnesses that the property belonged to him, so that the possessor was required to establish chazakah before beit din, their payment is treated as one made pursuant to beit din. Since judicial involvement was already necessary, we apply the rule at the end of siman 297 that one who delivers property pursuant to beit din is exempt. But if, when they paid the possessor, the challenger had not yet brought witnesses of ancestral ownership, the payment is not regarded as having been made pursuant to beit din, because at that point there was no need for beit din.

There is no need to establish the case as one where the house is not ordinarily rented and the borrowers are persons who ordinarily would rent a house. That wording is imprecise. Where the house itself is not ordinarily rented, the borrowers are exempt even if they are persons who would ordinarily rent, as explained in siman 363. See there, where it is implied that even one who rents a house from Reuven and later it is found to belong to Shimon is exempt from paying Shimon if Shimon or his agent had not sought to rent it out, for it is then treated as a house not ordinarily rented. The Gemara could have established its case in such circumstances, making the tenants disinterested witnesses. It merely chose one of two possible answers.
