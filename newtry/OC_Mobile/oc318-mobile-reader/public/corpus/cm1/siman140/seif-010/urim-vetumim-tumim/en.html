Even if they already paid him, etc. This ruling requires serious study. If the tenants already paid their rent, they are interested witnesses. How can they testify? The fact that no claimant is presently before us does not remove the interest, because the entire purpose of receiving their testimony now is to protect against a future claimant. If no one ever challenges the possessor, their testimony has no purpose. Thus their interest is precisely to ensure that if a claimant later appears they will not have to pay rent a second time. The Bach compares this to the rule that a witness is not considered interested merely because he may someday benefit if he later becomes wealthy, but the cases are entirely different. That possible future benefit is remote and uncommon. Here the tenants may already know that there is a real claimant who is likely to appear shortly. Moreover, there we say a person will not certainly lie now merely for a speculative future benefit. Here, by contrast, if no claimant ever appears, the testimony never produces any legal effect and the false statement causes no practical harm. If a claimant does appear, the testimony directly protects them from a second rent payment. Thus their interest is concrete in either practical scenario. We disqualify witnesses even for much more remote possible interests, such as a first purchaser testifying for a second purchaser and the other cases in siman 37. All the more so here, where the interest lies directly before us. Even if one could force a distinction, from where was this new rule derived? Perhaps Rabbenu Yonah reads Mar Zutra's statement, If he claims, Let two witnesses come and testify that he lived there three years by day and night, his claim is valid, as referring to a possessor who comes before any claimant appears and asks to record the tenants' testimony. But the Mechaber and Rama above in seif 8 interpret Mar Zutra differently and rule accordingly, so that interpretation supplies no proof for Rabbenu Yonah's rule here. In the Tur itself I would not explain the case as one where the tenants already paid the rent. Rather, they have not yet paid, and the possessor is presently coming to collect the rent from them. He therefore fears that if he waits to take their testimony until a claimant eventually appears, the tenants will by then necessarily have paid, because he cannot leave rent outstanding indefinitely when no one knows whether or when a claimant will come. Once they have paid, they will become interested witnesses. He therefore asks beit din now, before receiving the rent, to record their testimony. This fits the Tur's language: If the possessor claims in beit din before the claimant arrives, Let the tenants testify before you, because if someone later comes to challenge me the tenants will no longer be valid witnesses since they will already have paid me the rent, his claim is valid. This means precisely that if he waits until litigation arises, by then the rent will certainly have been paid. Beit din therefore records the testimony while they are still disinterested. The Tur's phrase, Since the claimant is not before us, can then be understood not as a reason why the tenants are disinterested, as the Bach reads it, but as a separate reason why their testimony may be received outside the claimant's presence. Ordinarily even sick witnesses or witnesses preparing to travel are not examined outside a litigant's presence unless the litigant was summoned and failed to appear. Here there is no one to summon, because the identity or location of the eventual claimant is unknown. Therefore beit din may receive the testimony now. Although this interpretation slightly strains the order of the Tur's words, it is far preferable to inventing a new rule that conflicts with the principles of interested testimony in siman 37. The Taz also questions this ruling from the first purchaser case and leaves it unresolved, though he did not note this alternative reading of the Tur. There is also a simpler reading of the Tur's phrase that no claimant is presently before us. One might say the tenants remain potentially interested even before they pay: they may fear that they cannot lawfully withhold rent from the person who rented the house to them merely because a future claimant may appear. To avoid the possibility of later paying again, they could be tempted to testify now that they lived there for three years, thereby blocking any future claimant. Since beit din cannot simply order them to deposit the rent when no adverse claimant exists, one might think this current concern itself makes them interested. The Tur teaches that we do not assume such speculative misconduct when no defect or claimant is presently visible and the rent has not yet been paid. This, in my view, is the correct understanding. But if they already paid, they are plainly interested, and the Mechaber's literal formulation requires serious study.
