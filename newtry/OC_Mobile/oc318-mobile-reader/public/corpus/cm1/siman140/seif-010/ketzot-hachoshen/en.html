Rabbeinu Yonah says that because the challenger is not presently before us, the tenants who testify about the possessor's three years are not yet disqualified as interested witnesses. Bach explains that a witness is considered interested only where the benefit is certain at the time of testimony. A merely possible future benefit, such as if the challenger later appears, does not disqualify him. Taz objects from siman 37, where a first purchaser cannot testify for a second purchaser because he worries that the land may have been stolen and wants to preserve his warranty. Here too, the tenants can see that the possessor is gathering evidence and therefore know a challenger likely exists. They have an interest in avoiding a second payment of rent. That comparison is not decisive. Tosafot explains that a lender or guarantor is not generally suspected of fearing that the property was stolen. The first purchaser's interest is rather that he bought specifically with warranty and wants that existing warranty preserved. Here the tenants have only a possible future exposure. Still, Bach's formulation that they do not even expect the challenger to appear seems too weak. If the possessor is actively gathering evidence, they surely sense a dispute. The better explanation follows Ramban's analysis of the sugya. The Gemara's question that the tenants are interested witnesses is understood after the enactment of hesset. Without that oath enactment, their migo would have allowed them to say they already paid the challenger. In Kiddushin Tosafot says that after hesset was enacted they cannot testify through such a migo because a witness who requires an oath is not a valid witness. Scripture requires testimony that stands by speech alone. Accordingly, while the challenger is absent, no hesset oath is presently due. At that moment the tenants are valid witnesses whose testimony stands on speech alone. If the challenger later comes, they may indeed have to swear concerning their own rent liability, but that later personal oath does not retroactively invalidate testimony they gave while legally disinterested. It is like a witness who was unrelated at the time of testimony and only later became related. Their testimony at the present time can establish the possessor's claim, though it does not necessarily exempt the tenants themselves from a later oath if the challenger appears. This explains Rabbeinu Yonah's ruling in a principled way.
Taz asks why the Tur needed the special reason permitting testimony here. Since the possessor is the defendant, and siman 28 sometimes allows defensive testimony to be received outside the claimant's presence, why not rely on that? Taz answers that land stands in the original owner's presumption. Therefore the current possessor is effectively the claimant who must prove his three year possession. I find this difficult. Siman 149 expressly says that if a person possessed land during the father's lifetime and, after the father died, the minor son challenged him, the minor's claim is not presently heard. If the possessor needs witnesses to establish his possession, the court receives them even though the challenger is a minor. A minor is surely equivalent to absence, yet because the possessor currently holds the land he is treated procedurally as defendant. The correct principle, as I wrote in siman 28 in the name of Maharit, is that the law of testimony outside a party's presence does not generally distinguish plaintiff from defendant. Scripture says testimony is to be given "in the presence of its owner," and loss of one's property through defensive testimony is also prejudice. Ran's statement permitting defensive testimony refers to a narrower case where others are presently trying to extract from the defendant while the claimant himself is absent. The defendant can produce enough evidence to repel the current extraction, but if the true claimant later appears, the testimony may need to be heard again in his presence. The minor land case is likewise special. Maharshal explains that either the minor's claim is treated seriously, in which case the possessor's answer and evidence must also be heard, or the minor is treated as not presently litigating, in which case there is no claim to disturb possession at all. The possessor can therefore maintain present possession, though when the minor grows up and litigates anew the witnesses may need to testify again. Here that solution is impossible. Once the challenger appears later, the tenants will then be interested witnesses because of possible rent liability. They could not repeat the testimony in his presence. Rabbeinu Yonah therefore gives the stronger reason that we receive their testimony now as final testimony because afterward it would become impossible, analogous to witnesses who are about to go overseas. This is why the special rationale is necessary.
