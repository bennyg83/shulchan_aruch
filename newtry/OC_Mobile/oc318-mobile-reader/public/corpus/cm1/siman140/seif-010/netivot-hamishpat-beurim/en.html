Since the claimant is not before us. Taz asks why this differs from siman 36, where a first purchaser may not testify for a second because perhaps the land is stolen and today or tomorrow the true owner may bring witnesses. All the more so here, where the tenants see the possessor collecting testimony, they necessarily know that some claimant exists. Tumim explains Tur to mean that the tenants have not yet paid the rent. They might nevertheless seem interested because, with no claimant presently before us, beit din will immediately order them to pay the possessor, and they fear that a claimant may later appear and force them to pay a second time. Tur teaches that we do not consider them interested because of that possibility. Tumim leaves Mechaber difficult. Ketzot HaChoshen answers that the disqualification here is not ordinary financial interest but the rule that a witness who himself requires an oath is disqualified by Scripture, as Ramban writes. Since at the time they testify no claimant is before us and they owe no oath, even if they will later become obligated to swear when the claimant arrives, this resembles a witness who was unrelated at the time of testimony and became related afterward, whose testimony remains valid. This is incorrect. If so, in siman 121, where an agent cannot become a witness because he too is a witness who would require an oath, he should be able to testify that he paid the creditor whenever the borrower is absent. Yet the Gemara and poskim state categorically that the agent does not become a witness. Rather, if the creditor or claimant later appears and contradicts them, their need for an oath reveals retroactively that their testimony was never sufficient by itself. The very point of their testimony was to operate against the possibility of the claimant's later contradiction. This is clear. It therefore appears to me that Mechaber deals with a case where there are no other witnesses to the tenants' occupancy. The only concern is that they are interested because they fear the claimant may later bring witnesses showing that they lived there for two years. We are taught that witnesses are not considered interested merely out of fear that some claimant may later appear, except in a case like siman 36, where their testimony cannot itself expose them to any disadvantage, while failure to testify can expose them to loss. There they have a present benefit from testifying and must testify immediately, because the present owner may immediately dispose of the field and their testimony will later be useless, and also because the first party may be easier for them to deal with. Here, by contrast, when they testify before the claimant appears, there is no reason to suspect that fear is inducing false testimony. Suppose in truth they lived there only two years but falsely testify to three because they fear witnesses or a claimant who knows of those two years but not the third. If they later had to pay for the two years or swear regarding them, false testimony to a third year might indeed save them. But the same testimony can just as readily harm them. Witnesses may later appear who know that they did not live there in the third year, or the claimant may know that fact while lacking knowledge of the first two years. Had they remained silent, they would owe neither payment nor oath because their occupancy would be unknown. By testifying that they lived there three years, however, and then being contradicted or disproved regarding the third year, the land would remain with its prior owner and they would have to pay for the first two years based on their own admission. Likewise, if the claimant knows they did not live there in the third year but does not know about the first two, their testimony may obligate them to swear, whereas silence would have required no oath. If they intended to lie, it would be more advantageous to wait until the claimant appeared, so that they could tailor their response to his actual knowledge. Since the possibility that testimony will help them and the possibility that it will harm them are balanced, their present testimony gives them no financial benefit and they are not regarded as interested. We therefore assume that they are telling the truth.
Even though testimony is not received outside the litigant's presence. Taz asks that testimony may be received for a defendant outside the other party's presence, as stated in siman 28. He answers that land remains in its owner's chazakah. At first glance, however, witnesses establishing a three-year chazakah are accepted even against minor orphans, as stated in siman 28 and siman 110. It appears that such testimony is accepted only when the possessor is actually in possession and living in the house. But where the possessor is not living there, even if a minor later entered with his servants and removed him, testimony is no longer received, as explained in siman 28, Sema seif katan 57. Here the house is not in the possessor's hands but in the tenants' hands. Until the tenants testify, it is as though the house remains in the chazakah of the prior owner. Their testimony is what would now establish the possessor's title. Such testimony is therefore not received outside the opposing party's presence, and Rama needed his stated rationale.
