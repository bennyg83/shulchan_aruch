To kill it in the injured party's domain. This is Rambam's wording in chapter 6 of Hilchot Chovel. It implies that if the victim's ox entered the tortfeasor's domain and the tortfeasor's ox climbed upon it, then even if the victim pulls his own ox out from underneath and the tortfeasor's ox falls and dies, the victim must pay. Rambam follows his own view that a tortfeasor is exempt for damage caused in his own domain even where the victim brought his property there with permission, as the Tur cites in Rambam's name above in siman 379. Since the tortfeasor would have been exempt for the original damage, the victim acted unlawfully by damaging his ox and must pay. The Beit Yosef explains this as well. The Tur omitted the phrase "in the injured party's domain" and above, at the end of siman 379, rules unlike Rambam. See there and the Derishah.
Whether the attacking ox was tam, etc. Meaning, it is not only where the attacking ox is tam, so that if the victim does not save his own ox he can collect only half-damages from the body of the attacking ox, that he is permitted to push the upper ox away. Even if the attacking ox is mu'ad and the victim could collect full damages from its owner from superior assets, he may nevertheless enforce his right directly and save his own property.
The correct text is: "and he pulled his own ox away to save it." So appears in the baraita and in Rambam.
If he could have removed it, etc. Meaning, if he could have gently moved the upper ox off the lower one, he should not have pushed it with force so that it died. But in the first clause, where he pulled away his own lower ox, he is fully permitted to save his property and need not carefully arrange matters even if he could have moved the upper ox gently.
Likewise, if Reuven took Shimon's utensil, etc. The word "likewise" means that just as in the ox case, if he has a way to save the situation by gently removing the upper ox and fails to use it, he is liable, so too here, where he could support the object with something else, he is liable if he simply removes the support. This differs from the first clause, where he is entitled to withdraw his own lower ox without concern that the upper ox may fall and die, because he is acting to save his property from being killed and a person is alarmed over his property. Here, by contrast, the barrel itself is not damaging his utensil. This is straightforward.
He is liable, for he should have supported it with another object. Responsa Rosh concludes that if he did support it with another object, he is exempt even if the substitute stone was not as large as the first. Darkei Moshe cites this.
