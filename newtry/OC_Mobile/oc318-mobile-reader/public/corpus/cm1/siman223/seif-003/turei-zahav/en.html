(See there.) Even if the buyer makes a certain claim. To resolve the words of the Rambam and Maggid Mishneh, which the Beit Yosef left requiring study, it appears to me that the Rif holds as follows. Although at the beginning of the chapter HaParah we rule like the Sages who disagree with Sumchus and say, “This is a great principle in monetary law: the burden of proof rests on one who seeks to extract,” even where the injured party claims certainty and the tortfeasor claims uncertainty, here we rule like Sumchus. There the claimant seeks to remove property from the tortfeasor's possession. Here, by contrast, the offspring stands in a meadow or side alley and is not in either party's possession. This factor suffices to prevent us from placing it in the prior owner's presumptive possession. Although the Sages there still disagree with Sumchus and apply the burden of proof even in that uncertainty, in this case we rule like Sumchus because no one is presently extracting from the other's possession. This, in my humble opinion, is the Rif's intent. On this basis the Rambam's words, copied here by the Mechaber, are also clear. When he says, “Even if the seller says, ‘I do not know,’ the buyer must bring proof,” he means that if the buyer claims certainty and the seller uncertainty, and the buyer seeks the entire offspring, the buyer must bring proof. If he does not bring proof, the law differs according to the seller's claim. If the seller claims certainty, he takes a severe oath concerning the offspring and receives it entirely. If the seller claims uncertainty, then even if the buyer also claims uncertainty, they divide it. This is the Mechaber's ruling in seif 2, “This one says, ‘I do not know,’” etc., where he cites a view that they divide, namely the Rambam's view. There the seller does not swear that he does not know, because such an oath is found only where the buyer seeks to extract from the seller but lacks power to do so, so the seller takes everything, as in seif 5. Here, by contrast, they divide in any event, and the seller need not swear that he does not know merely to take his half. This is precisely the Maggid Mishneh's intent when he writes: “The Rambam's statement, ‘even if the seller says, I do not know,’ does not mean that where the buyer claims certainty but brings no proof, and the seller says, ‘I do not know,’ the seller swears that he does not know and the offspring is his, for it will be explained below,” etc. The Beit Yosef wrote that he did not understand what this explanation had accomplished, because in any event the offspring remains with the seller until the buyer brings proof, so the original difficulty returns. With due respect, the Beit Yosef did not examine the matter sufficiently. He also copied the Maggid Mishneh inaccurately. The four words “the seller swears that he does not know” are a scribal error and do not appear in the Kesef Mishneh's printed text. The Beit Yosef understood “the buyer must bring proof” to mean that if he fails, the seller receives the entire offspring. He therefore asked why the Maggid Mishneh's later rule of division does not contradict it. In my humble opinion, however, the Rambam means only that if the buyer wishes to take the entire offspring he must bring proof. Without proof, the concluding clause explains the law: where both say “I do not know,” they divide. Everything is thereby resolved, with Heaven's help. The Sema attempts to answer the Maggid Mishneh's question by saying that the opening clause, where the buyer bears the burden of proof even though the seller is uncertain, concerns the offspring being in the seller's possession. He then understands the Rambam to begin a new law with the case where the cow stands in a meadow, though even that is treated like the seller's possession, and where the seller also claims certainty. The Rambam's conclusion, that if proof is not brought the seller swears, applies to both clauses, in the first because the offspring is in his possession and in the second because he claims certainty. The final rule, where both say “I do not know” and divide, applies only to the second clause. This is exceedingly difficult. If the words “standing in the meadow” do not apply to the opening clause, the Rambam never identifies in whose possession the offspring stands there. The Gemara itself asks, “Let us see in whose possession it is, and let the other bear the burden of proof.” Why then would the Rambam state categorically in the first clause that the buyer must bring proof? He should have said that whoever does not possess the offspring must bring proof. Apart from this, the language of the Mechaber cannot bear the Sema's construction.
