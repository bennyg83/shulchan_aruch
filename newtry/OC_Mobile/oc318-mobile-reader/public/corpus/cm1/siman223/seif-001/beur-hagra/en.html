One who exchanges a cow, etc.; this one says, “I do not know,” etc.; some say they divide. This is the Rambam’s view. He rules like Sumchus because the anonymous Mishnah follows him, and the Gemara establishes the case specifically where the animal stands in a meadow and both sides make uncertain claims. Without those conditions Sumchus agrees with the Sages, as shown by the baraisa there concerning “this one says I do not know, etc.” The Rif’s lengthy explanation also indicates that he rules like Sumchus. The Rambam’s general rule that the burden of proof rests on the claimant applies where one of those conditions is absent. Although Bava Kamma chapter 3 initially says that Sumchus divides even where both parties make definite claims, that statement does not remain according to the conclusion there. The Rambam’s other rulings that the burden of proof rests upon the claimant fit this distinction.
And he received the money, etc. See Beer HaGolah and the explanation of Rashi and the commentators there.
With an article in his hand. This refers to a formal oath with an article held in hand, not merely a shevuas heses. The oaths discussed in the baraisa are not heses oaths, for the Sages state that there is no oath concerning slaves and the other excluded classes. Sumchus likewise agrees where a Torah oath applies. See Rashi, Sema, and Perishah.
And concerning the offspring, etc. This follows the Sages in the baraisa. See Bava Kamma 96b, Shevuos 42b, and HaZahav. The entire baraisa is consistent with Sumchus: where both claims are uncertain he rules that they divide, but in these cases he agrees with the Sages. The Rambam accordingly records all three rulings.
He takes a shevuas heses. A shevuas heses applies even to a claim concerning land, as Rav Hai Gaon derives from the chapter HaKosev.
And if the purchaser, etc. The Gemara says to examine in whose possession the property stands. This applies even according to Sumchus.
