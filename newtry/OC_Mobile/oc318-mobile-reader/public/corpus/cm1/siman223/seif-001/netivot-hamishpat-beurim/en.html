One who exchanges a cow for a donkey. See Taz, who writes that the case can also arise in an ordinary sale where the two parties contradict one another with definite claims. This is difficult. In such a case there is no derara de mamona, because derara de mamona exists only where the doubt arises independently through witnesses, as Tosafot write in Bava Metzia 100, s.v. Demei Eved Gadol. Here the doubt would arise only from the litigants' own claims, and Sumchus did not state his rule in such a case. The Mechaber here wishes both to teach that the halachah does not follow Sumchus generally and to state the special law that if the buyer is in possession he can prevail, a law relevant specifically to a derara de mamona. He therefore had to use the case of exchanging a cow for a donkey.
And the owner of the cow claims. See Taz, who questions this from siman 220. He did not take account of the Sema there, who writes that where the sale concerns "this cow," everyone agrees that the fetus was included. This is straightforward law.
Even if the buyer makes a definite claim. See Taz, who writes that Rambam rules like Sumchus, and that our ruling like the Sages regarding the pursuing ox in siman 400 applies only where one party is actually in possession, whereas if the property stands in a marsh they would divide. In my humble opinion this is very difficult. First, Rambam should then have distinguished there and said that if the property stands in a marsh they divide. Second, siman 400 says that seizure can help. According to the Rif and Rambam, who in the case of one claiming wheat and admitting barley require seizure before judgment, as explained above in siman 88, such seizure is no weaker than property standing in a marsh. Yet they still rule that the injured party recovers only according to what the tortfeasor admits. According to the Taz they should instead divide the disputed amount. Third, the Mechaber in siman 399:3, regarding an ox that gored a cow, expressly rules that even if the animal stands in a marsh the claimant bears the burden of proof, and does not note that Rambam would require division. Rambam likewise states broadly throughout the laws depending on the dispute of Sumchus and the Sages that the claimant bears the burden, following the Sages, without distinguishing a marsh case. It therefore appears to me that Rambam should be understood differently. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 100, s.v. Hani Milei Sumchus Hi, write that in the case of a cow that gored an ox, where one eighth is collected from the fetus even though the owner of the fetus is in possession, the reason is that the presumption that the cow remained pregnant stands against that possession. Tosafot's use of this presumption is necessary to explain the view of Ri, who holds, according to Maharshal's interpretation there, that the buyer's possession can help even when he has only an uncertain claim. I explained in my novellae that Maharshal's reading is the principal one, unlike Maharsha. The reason is that with an object there can be both the presumption of the prior owner and present monetary possession, which together can help even against an uncertain claim. Yet the presumption that the cow remained pregnant counteracts them. See Pnei Yehoshua in Ketubot 76 on Rashi, s.v. Kallah BeBeit Chamiha. He explains that in ordinary present monetary possession the prior ownership presumption does not independently operate, and the possessor of the fetus who caused the damage is analogous to a seller who seized the object. It follows that even where present possession and prior ownership would ordinarily help even according to Sumchus, the presumption of continued pregnancy can neutralize them. Tosafot state this, however, only where both parties make uncertain claims. With definite claims on both sides, the pregnancy presumption does not override ordinary possession. The Gemara says that Sumchus does not apply in a definite versus definite case, implying that the claimant bears the burden, and the authorities rule accordingly even though the buyer has the pregnancy presumption. This shows that in a definite versus definite case, even the prior ownership presumption by itself can outweigh the pregnancy presumption. Accordingly Rambam's ruling can be understood without saying that he follows Sumchus generally. As long as it is possible, we do not reject an anonymous Mishnah from halachah unless the Gemara expressly says the halachah is otherwise. Tosafot ask why the Mishnah cannot be established even according to the Sages, because the pregnancy presumption stands against the prior owner's presumption. They answer that the prior ownership presumption is stronger. That proves superiority of prior ownership over the pregnancy presumption only according to the Gemara's first answer, which establishes the Mishnah as definite versus definite. In such a case, as explained, the pregnancy presumption does not overcome the Sages' prior ownership presumption, just as it does not overcome the combination of prior ownership and possession where Sumchus himself recognizes them. According to Rav Ashi, however, who establishes the Mishnah as uncertain versus uncertain, one can say that it indeed accords even with the Sages. In an uncertain versus uncertain case, the pregnancy presumption can stand against the prior ownership presumption. Just as it cancels prior ownership together with possession according to Sumchus, it can cancel the Sages' prior ownership presumption. Once the prior ownership presumption is neutralized, the parties divide, even according to the Sages, as Tosafot write in Bava Kamma 34 where no controlling prior presumption exists. There is even a partial a fortiori argument. In the case of a cow that gored an ox, the owner of the fetus has both prior ownership and possession, which can help even under Sumchus, yet the pregnancy presumption overrides them. All the more so might it override prior ownership alone, which is effective only according to the Sages. One can reject the inference by saying that in a true derara de mamona Sumchus weakens possession and prior ownership more than the Sages do. Still, Rambam can hold that since the Gemara never proves in an uncertain versus uncertain case that the Sages' prior ownership presumption defeats the pregnancy presumption, we should not reject the anonymous Mishnah when it can be read consistently with the Sages. Minimizing dispute is preferable. Present monetary possession according to the Sages is stronger than prior ownership, and the pregnancy presumption certainly cannot cancel it. Even the stronger bodily presumption of virginity does not override present monetary possession except in a definite versus uncertain case. In definite versus definite or uncertain versus uncertain cases, present possession prevails, as Ketubot 36 teaches, even though that case also contains a derara de mamona. All the more so present possession is stronger than the pregnancy presumption. Sumchus, by contrast, gives reduced weight to present possession where there is a derara de mamona, allowing the pregnancy presumption to stand against it. Rambam therefore does not rule division in every dispute between Sumchus and the Sages, but only here, because the buyer possesses the special pregnancy presumption against the prior owner. One might ask that in the case of an ox that gored a cow and the dead fetus is found beside her, Rambam and the Mechaber in siman 399 rule that even where the animal stands in a marsh the claimant bears the burden, though the cow's owner seemingly has the pregnancy presumption. This is also a difficulty for Tosafot, who at the beginning of chapter HaParah say that Sumchus's division in that case is only where the animal stands in a marsh, because if it stood in the defendant's domain even Sumchus would concede. Why should that differ from a cow that gored an ox, where Tosafot say that even possession is countered by the pregnancy presumption? The answer appears to be that the case of the ox goring the cow is different because the fetus is now dead. There is not only a doubt whether the miscarriage occurred before or after the goring; even if it occurred afterward, perhaps the fetus had already died in the womb before the goring. To support the claim one would need a presumption of continued life, since the fetus had previously been developing alive. But such a presumption was never established at the relevant time. Tosafot in Chullin 11 explain that a presumption not established at the time is not treated as a valid chazakah even in prohibitions, all the more so in monetary law, where the original status can be known only through majority and monetary law does not ordinarily follow majority. If it is definitely known that the cow miscarried after the goring, the tortfeasor is liable, as the Mishnah's wording "we do not know whether before he gored or after he gored" implies. The reason is that once we see that she did not miscarry before the goring but only afterward, we can presume that the goring caused the miscarriage. Otherwise, Bava Batra 93, which challenges the view that monetary law follows majority from this Mishnah, should equally have challenged the opposite view by saying that even where birth after the goring is known, perhaps the fetus had already died. Evidently the post goring miscarriage supplies a direct causal presumption. Where, however, there is both a doubt whether the miscarriage preceded the goring and a further doubt whether the fetus was already nonviable, the case becomes money in doubt with two independent uncertainties, somewhat like the Gemara's formulation that it may have come from before or from another direction. There is therefore no operative pregnancy presumption. This explains why the Mechaber rules in the ox goring cow case that even in a marsh the claimant bears the burden. In the converse case, a cow that gored an ox, Rambam states generally that the claimant cannot collect from the fetus. It is somewhat difficult that he does not add that if the fetus stands in a marsh they should divide according to the foregoing analysis. Perhaps Rambam's practice is not to record a law that is not expressly stated in the Gemara, and the concealed case can be learned from the explicit one.
The seller shall swear. See Sema, seif katan 3, who struggles considerably to explain the oath. In my humble opinion the reason is straightforward. The Rama rules in siman 224 that if the seller cannot bring proof, he swears that he did not know. Sema there, seif katan 4, writes that the same applies that he swears he did not know of the death. Mordechai explains the reason: even one who is in possession may need to swear that he does not know whether he became obligated, all the more so one who seeks to take money from another's possession. Although the other party's claim is uncertain, we find many cases where a person who is especially expected to know must swear, such as the seller of his utensils. There, despite the seller's bodily presumption and the presumption of "here it was found," he must swear because the event occurred while the matter was within his sphere and he is expected to know of the death. According to the Sema, the seller is similarly expected to know more about the birth because the cow was his. Here too, although he has prior ownership, when the property stands in a marsh it is as though both parties are in possession, while the seller is the party more likely to know whether the birth occurred before or after the transaction. This resembles siman 91, where when one party knows and the other does not, the knowledgeable party swears. Here the seller must take a stringent oath resembling an oath to collect, because standing in a marsh makes the parties analogous to equal possessors. If the property is in the seller's own domain, he need swear only a hesset oath. According to this reasoning, even if the buyer's claim is uncertain the seller must swear, since the seller is the party expected to know, just as in siman 224.
And if the buyer is in possession. The language implies that this is so even if the buyer makes only an uncertain claim, contrary to Tosafot in Bava Metzia 100, s.v. Velichzi. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that this follows only according to Rambam's supposed view that seizure helps in the case of the disputed firstborn lamb, where if the kohen seized we do not remove it. According to him this is difficult for the Rama, who should have glossed according to our ruling that such property is removed from the kohen. It therefore appears that Rambam follows the position of Ri in Tosafot, s.v. Hani Milei Sumchus, as interpreted by Maharshal. When the buyer seizes the property, even an uncertain claim can prevail because he then has both present monetary possession and the pregnancy presumption. I explained in my novellae that Maharshal's reading is the principal one and Maharsha's discussion there is difficult. Ri indeed holds, according to Maharshal, that a seller's possession does not help according to Sumchus. But according to the Taz's understanding of the Rif and Rambam, they accept Sumchus only to the limited extent that Sumchus weakens possession in a derara de mamona, while on the core rule they follow the Sages. If the buyer has both present possession and the pregnancy presumption, a combination strong enough even according to Sumchus, then it certainly helps according to the Sages, who give greater force to monetary possession. The same follows from my own explanation of Rambam's division rule: where the buyer has the pregnancy presumption against the seller's prior ownership, and in addition the buyer is presently in possession, his combined presumptions prevail.
