Even if the purchaser claims with certainty. In the Perishah I explained the Rambam's words in chapter 1 of Hilchot Mekhirah, which are similar to the Mechaber's ruling here. The Rambam writes that in one who exchanges a cow for a donkey, even if the seller says, I do not know, the purchaser bears the burden of proof. Although the cow stands in a marsh and the maidservant in a side alley, they remain under the seller's prior presumption until the purchaser proves his claim. If he proves nothing, the seller takes an oath. He later says that if both parties say, I do not know, and the property stands in neither one's domain, they divide. The Maggid Mishneh asks: if the Rambam holds that in a case of mutual uncertainty they divide, why did he first rule that where the purchaser is certain and seller uncertain the burden of proof remains on the purchaser? I answered that the first case, where the purchaser claims certainly and the seller uncertainly, speaks where the offspring still stands in the seller's domain. The later rule of division speaks where it stands in a neutral alley. Proof is that the Rambam twice writes that the burden of proof lies upon the claimant, and in the middle clause says, 'although it stands in a marsh,' without a connective word. The first clause therefore speaks where it stands in the seller's domain, so even if the seller is uncertain, the purchaser bears the burden. The second clause speaks where it stands in a marsh and the seller claims certainly, and therefore he again says the purchaser bears the burden. In both cases, if the purchaser brings no proof, the seller swears, in the first because he possesses it, in the second because he makes a definite claim. In the Perishah and Derishah I wrote that this is, in my view, the correct explanation, not that of the Maggid Mishneh. The Mechaber, however, changed the Rambam's wording and did not write twice that the burden of proof lies upon the claimant. He also begins the following seif with the case where both parties say they do not know and the animal stands in a marsh, ruling that the burden of proof lies upon the purchaser, in accordance with the Tur and not the Rambam, who rules division. This might imply that the Mechaber's first seif also follows the Tur and is one continuous case: even if the seller is uncertain and the animal stands in a marsh, the purchaser bears the burden, and if he proves nothing the seller swears, according to the Sages who reject Symmachus and maintain the prior owner's presumption even where both are uncertain. But if so, the following seif would be unnecessary, because it would follow all the more so from the preceding one. Rama's gloss there, that some say this applies even if the claimant is certain and the defendant uncertain, would likewise merely repeat the first seif. Therefore it appears that the Mechaber intended the first seif as I explained the Rambam: when the purchaser is certain and seller uncertain, the offspring remains in the seller's domain. The concluding phrase, that even if the cow and maidservant are not in the seller's domain but stand in a marsh, speaks where the seller too claims certainly. Therefore he writes 'even if' without a connective. In this seif the Mechaber states laws accepted by all. In the following seif he states the dispute between Rambam and Tur where the object stands in neutral ground and the seller does not claim certainly. He begins with the Tur's view that where both say, I do not know, and neither possesses it, the purchaser bears the burden because the Sages reject Symmachus and leave property under its former owner's presumption. The Tur expressly holds the same even if the seller is uncertain and the purchaser certain, and this is Rama's gloss. The Mechaber did not state that novelty in the Tur's name because he preferred first to state the Rambam's stronger novelty, that if both are uncertain they divide, following his usual tendency to adopt the Rambam. This resolves everything.
The seller shall swear, etc. In the Derishah I gave the source of this oath from the Gemara. One explanation for the Torah oath is that the fetus is regarded as part of its mother's body. The purchaser claims a pregnant cow as it stood at the time of meshichah, while the seller admits the cow but denies the offspring. This resembles partial admission, without a complete case of 'here it is,' because he does not return the cow exactly as claimed. Although the Gemara calls it 'here it is,' it is not a complete instance. I also wrote in the Perishah that the oath was enacted similarly to the oath of two people holding one garment, so that people should not simply seize property belonging to others.
And regarding the offspring of the maidservant he takes an oath of hesset. The reason is that no Torah oath is imposed regarding slaves, as the Tur and Mechaber write in siman 95.
And if the purchaser is in possession, etc. His present possession is stronger than the former owner's prior presumption, and we therefore apply the rule that the burden of proof lies upon the claimant.
