Some say that even if the claimant is certain and the defendant uncertain. Sema, seif katan 6, leaves Rama’s wording difficult. The intent of the gloss, in my view, is that the buyer bears the burden of proof even where the claimant is certain and the defendant uncertain. Certainly, where the buyer is claimant and the seller is in possession, even if the seller says “I do not know,” the buyer must prove his case; Mechaber himself states that even a definite buyer claim does not overcome possession. Rama adds the converse: even where the buyer is defendant because he presently holds the property, if the buyer’s own claim is uncertain his possession does not automatically prevail. He must bring proof. Although possession is ordinarily strengthened by pregnancy chazakah, in a definite-versus-uncertain case the definite claim is stronger than ordinary chazakot other than true present possession. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 100 writes that a definite claim can even overcome prior-owner chazakah according to some approaches, while Ramban disagrees. But against secondary chazakot such as pregnancy chazakah, all agree the definite claim is stronger. Taz’s view above that the seller’s oath is d'oraisa also creates no problem of “one who cannot swear must pay” where the buyer says “I do not know.” That doctrine does not apply when the defendant could not reasonably have been expected to know the fact. Here it is entirely possible that even Shach in siman 72 agrees the buyer may genuinely have no way to know, while the seller can know.
