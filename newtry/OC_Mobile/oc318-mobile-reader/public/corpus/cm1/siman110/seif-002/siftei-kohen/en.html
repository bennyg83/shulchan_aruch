Therefore, if he seized property belonging to them, etc. The Tur writes that, since the concern is that the father may have left money or security for payment, one who knows with certainty that the debt was not paid may retain property of the heirs when he could have claimed that it was purchased and now belongs to him. The Bach explains that this applies even where there are witnesses and the property was seen in his possession, contrary to Baal HaTerumot. His words are astonishing. The Tur expressly limits the ruling to a case in which the possessor could claim, 'It was purchased and is mine,' precisely as Baal HaTerumot rules and as the Sema explains. This is also explicit in the Rosh's treatment of Rava bar Sharshom, where even one who possessed a written claim against the estate could retain the property only by virtue of a migo. The Beit Yosef asks why the Tur says that it is permitted for him to retain the property, rather than saying that his seizure is legally effective, which would imply that beit din itself rules in his favor. He also asks why Baal HaTerumot speaks specifically of movable property seized during the father's lifetime, since seizure after death should likewise be effective. The Mechaber therefore combined the Tur's language in the first clause with Baal HaTerumot's language in the latter clause. In my view there is no difficulty and no dispute. The Rosh explains that Rava bar Sharshom followed Rav Huna, who attributes the rule against collecting from minor heirs to the concern that the father may have left security. Since Rava knew that no such security existed, he was permitted to obtain payment from the estate. Had he followed Rav Pappa, who bases the rule upon the mitzvah to repay a debt, minors are not obligated in that mitzvah and retaining their land would be theft. We rule like Rav Huna. Thus the Tur says only that the creditor may retain the property without violating theft. If he comes before beit din and admits that he seized movable property after the father's death for a debt owed by the father, he must return it, or it is placed with a third party if the heirs dispute the claim, because beit din cannot collect from minor heirs at that time. He should therefore either refrain from coming to court or claim that he seized it during the father's lifetime, as in the case of Rava bar Sharshom. Baal HaTerumot consequently speaks specifically of seizure during the father's lifetime when determining what beit din will uphold. Seizure after death may be morally permitted, but if the possessor admits it in court, the seizure is ineffective while the heirs are minors. The later rule that seizure after death is effective applies to adult heirs, from whom the creditor can collect immediately upon taking an oath, not to minors. The Bach's attempt to distinguish between the period before and after the Geonic enactment is incorrect. Even after that enactment, known seizure of identifiable movable property after death is ineffective, even where the possessor has a migo. Rabbenu Yerucham explicitly distinguishes these cases. He writes that when a person presently seizes identifiable movable property for a debt owed by the deceased father, the property is placed with a third party until the heirs mature, unless he claims that the father had pledged it to him. This necessarily refers to a seizure made without witnesses, where a migo exists, yet current seizure still requires deposit with a third party. It must also refer to the period after the Geonic enactment, for before it movable property of heirs was not subject to the debt at all. The Mechaber's broad formulation is therefore not the law, because it implies that even known seizure after death is effective. The Sema's application of Rabbenu Yerucham to the Mechaber is also inaccurate. Money is different. Rabbenu Yerucham writes that if the creditor seized money and holds a document, even where the seizure was witnessed, the money is not removed from him, but he must take the required oath. The Tur can agree, because one who has already seized money is treated as having been paid. The rule against resorting to the estate of minor heirs governs collection still to be made, not payment already received. Identifiable movable property, by contrast, remains the heirs' property and retention of it constitutes present collection from them. Baal HaTerumot can also agree regarding money, contrary to the Bach. The Bach himself accepts Rabbenu Yerucham's rulings as settled law. I later found this distinction stated in Emunat Shmuel, folio 85a. Baal HaTerumot refers to seizure during the father's lifetime for purposes of a judicial ruling, while the Tur speaks of the permission to conceal a seizure made after death. Emunat Shmuel also disputes the Beit Yosef on this point, though he does not bring the proof from Rabbenu Yerucham given above. Although a claim that he returned it would be audacious, a migo is nevertheless accepted in such a case, as explained above in siman 81, seif 23, and in siman 93 regarding the Sema, seif katan 9. Mahari likewise rules that one who seized money of heirs may say that their father owed him by virtue of a migo. Although the migo is not ideal, since he prefers the claim that their father owed him because the opposing party cannot know that it is false, whereas a denial or claim of payment could be recognized as false, Rabbi Yitzchak proves from the second chapter of Ketubot, concerning one who says, 'This field belonged to your father, and I purchased it from him,' that a migo applies even in such circumstances.
