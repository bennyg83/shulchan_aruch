Therefore, etc. The Tur uses this expression only in the place where the Mechaber writes that "his seizure is effective." The Tur writes instead that "he is permitted to seize them for his debt." The Beit Yosef writes that the term "permitted" is not exact, and therefore the Shulchan Aruch writes that the seizure is effective. The Beit Yosef also writes that the Baal HaTerumot states that seizure during the debtor's lifetime is effective, whereas the Tur writes that it is effective even after death. The authorities explain that the Baal HaTerumot discusses the Talmudic law, under which the movables of orphans are not subject to a creditor's lien, and therefore seizure must take place during the debtor's lifetime. The Tur speaks after the Geonic enactment, under which after death is equivalent to during life, without distinction. The words of the Shulchan Aruch here refer to that enactment. The Shach, however, disputes this and holds that during the debtor's lifetime seizure is effective even if the creditor comes to beit din and says that he seized the property. After death, by contrast, he is permitted to seize, but if he comes to beit din and says that he seized it, beit din removes it from him, because one does not collect from the property of minor orphans. If he does not come to beit din, he may retain it on his own in order to discharge his obligation toward Heaven. Accordingly, the Tur deliberately wrote "he is permitted to seize them" rather than "his seizure is effective." The Shach's principal proof is from Rabbenu Yerucham, Netiv 26, part 3, cited by the Sema in seif katan 6, who explicitly writes that if one seized movables, they are removed from his possession and placed with a third party until the orphans become adults. See there at length. I do not understand his words at all. As for his explanation of the Tur that one may seize merely in order to discharge his obligation toward Heaven, what novelty is there? The creditor knows personally that he has not been paid and did not receive any bundle of valuables in satisfaction. It is obvious that he may seize, and who can prevent him, since he knows that he has not been paid and the truth is revealed before Heaven. As for the Shach's substantive ruling that if one seized movables after death and comes to beit din, beit din removes them from him because one does not collect from the property of minor orphans, this does not appear to be the Tur's view at all. What difference is there, in our times, between movables and land? The entire source that seizure is effective is the case of Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom. Had it not become publicly known that the land belonged to the orphans, his migo would have made his seizure effective. There, the seizure was after death. Although the land had been in his possession during the debtor's lifetime, it was not held by way of seizure for this debt, but as a mortgage. After death, the mortgage term expired, as the Baal HaTerumot and all the authorities explicitly write, and only then did he retain it for the debt. Thus, there was no seizure during the debtor's lifetime for this debt at all. Even where an object is in one's possession as a deposit during the debtor's lifetime, it is not called seizure until he declares that he is holding it for his debt, as is clear in Perek HaKotev from the incident involving the grandmother and the bag of documents. All the more so land, which remains in the presumptive ownership of its owner, where during the debtor's lifetime he did not hold it for this debt at all, but only as a mortgage whose term had already ended. Moreover, the seizure of Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom concerned the produce, as the Rosh and Baal HaTerumot write. How can seizure during the debtor's lifetime apply to produce that grows year by year after his death, so as to grant him the right to deduct the produce each year? Clearly, this is considered seizure after death. As for the proof from Rabbenu Yerucham, I am astonished. Are all authorities in agreement on this law? This matter is explicitly disputed. The Ramban, in his novellae to Bava Batra regarding Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom, asks why Rabbah concealed the mortgage document rather than coming to beit din after the mortgage term ended and stating the truth: "I have such and such a debt, and I have a migo." He should have been believed. The Ramban answers that even so, beit din would have removed the property from his possession, because the heirs were minors. A migo is no better than witnesses, and even with witnesses the property would be removed from him. Rabbi Yosef HaLevi, however, writes that this is in fact what happened. Rabbah came to beit din and stated the matter as above and was believed by virtue of his migo. His point was only that had he first returned the land and afterward brought his claim, he would not have collected because one does not collect from minor orphans. Therefore he came to beit din while still holding it, possessed a migo, and the property was not removed from him. See his words at length. Thus the matter is explicitly disputed. The Baal HaTerumot, however, writes expressly that if one consumed the produce for the years of chazakah during the father's lifetime, then because he could have said, "I purchased it," since he possessed it for the years of chazakah, he is believed to say, "I have a debt against their father and will collect it from the produce," and he collects without an oath. The Rambam likewise rules this way. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en also writes that the Rambam's view follows that of his teacher, the Ri Migash. The same is clear below in Shulchan Aruch siman 150, and the Shach himself writes there in seif katan 3 that we do not rule like the Ramban, but like the Ri Migash. The law that seizure is effective is therefore clear. Rabbenu Yerucham, by contrast, follows the Ramban. In the cited Netiv he writes explicitly that it is proved from the case of Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom in Bava Batra that even if one held the land during the father's lifetime and claims, "Their father owed me such and such," he returns the land to the orphans, and when they become adults he litigates with them. If he claims that the land came to him as a mortgage, then if the circumstances are such that he could have claimed that he purchased it, he is believed. He further writes that if one seized movables and said, "They are collateral in my possession," he is believed. This is specifically where he claims they were collateral, but ordinary seizure is ineffective according to his view, because he follows the Ramban and his school. This is clear in Rabbenu Yerucham. The Shach therefore sought to bring a proof from Rabbenu Yerucham against the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur, but these views cannot be joined, since they disagree on the very law in question. It is therefore clear that seizure is effective. There is nevertheless a difficulty. According to the Baal HaTerumot, under the Talmudic law seizure of movables after death is ineffective. How then could Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom seize the produce that grew on the land after the debtor's death? Those fruits are movables. Even if one says that he seized them while still attached, so that they were considered land, is this any better than an increase in value produced by the property? A creditor likewise has no right to an increase in value that arose after the father's death, because it is property that came only afterward. What difference is there between that and movables of the orphans, which are not subject to the lien? I had thought to say that the Baal HaTerumot follows his own view that an automatic increase in value is given to the creditor, and therefore he distinguishes between seizure during life and after death. But according to the Tur, who holds that even such an increase is not collected by the creditor, it would follow that seizure after death must nevertheless be effective, and therefore he indeed rules that it is effective even after death. Yet this is strained, because it is difficult to classify produce growing year after year after death merely as an automatic increase, and to say that it is not included among the movables of the orphans. It is therefore clear that the principle is different. Even though the movables themselves are not subject to the lien, the creditor has a migo, and we rule that a migo may operate from one item of property to another. A person may seize one object by reason of a claim to another object. Likewise here, he can seize movables of the orphans because they also possess land to which his debt does attach. The Baal HaTerumot's reference to seizure during the debtor's lifetime is, as the Beit Yosef writes, stated for the sake of precision in a case where there are witnesses, in which seizure after death would not help. Otherwise, what distinction is there between seizure during life and after death? See Mishneh LaMelekh, chapter 11 of Hilkhot Malveh, who likewise writes that there is no basis to doubt that seizure after death without witnesses is effective even under the Talmudic law. He brings a sound proof from the statement in the second chapter of Ketubot, "Sometimes she seized two hundred," which Tosafot explain to mean that she would then have a migo and could say, "You married me as a virgin," and would be believed. This proves that although movables were not subject to a ketubah even during the husband's lifetime, seizure nevertheless helps. The same should therefore be true for a creditor against orphans. The Mishneh LaMelekh presses himself to distinguish between a husband with respect to a ketubah and orphans with respect to a creditor, but the distinction is weak and strained. One could reject the proof by saying that sometimes she seized actual money, and regarding money the Ran at the beginning of Perek Almanah Nizonet distinguishes that everyone agrees seizure is effective because it is equivalent to payment. See there. But this is only a possible rejection, and there is no reason to force such an explanation. It appears to me, however, that there is a proof in the opposite direction concerning the assumption here that seizure during the debtor's lifetime is always effective even where one does not collect from minor orphans. At the end of Ketubot, in the discussion of "this one collects and this one collects," concerning two people who lent money to one another, the Gemara labors to find a case where both collections have practical effect and are not merely an exchange of baskets. See there. The Ramban, as cited by the Baal HaTerumot in sha'ar 37, and the Ran ask that the case could simply involve minor orphans: they can collect from him, while he cannot collect from them until they become adults. They answer that since he had already seized during the father's lifetime, the Sages did not say in such a case that one does not collect from minor orphans so as to remove the property from his possession. This law is ruled straightforwardly above in Shulchan Aruch siman 85, seif 5. See the Sema there. Yet there the seizure was in the presence of witnesses, for the orphans have his promissory note in their possession, and nevertheless the property is not removed from him. One cannot say that this is because he seized money, since the debt in his possession is itself a monetary obligation. That is not correct. On the contrary, under the Talmudic law, if he has no land from which to pay the orphans, but only money or movables, he must pay the heirs while he himself collects nothing, because the movables of orphans are not subject to a creditor's lien. This demonstrates that his situation is not regarded as a full seizure. Were it considered seizure during the debtor's lifetime, he could collect from the orphans' movables. All the more so, if it were deemed seizure of actual money, it would certainly be effective against orphans, since money is equivalent to payment, as the Ran writes at the beginning of Perek Almanah regarding a ketubah. See Gidulei Terumah in the cited sha'ar. Indeed, the fact that the orphans can collect from him there is because he has land to give them. This itself creates a difficulty: once he gives them land, it is as though that land had been in their father's possession, as Tosafot write. See what I wrote above at the end of siman 85 concerning how seizure during life can help. One must therefore say that when he seized during the father's lifetime, even in the presence of witnesses, beit din does not remove the property from him. The Ramban's words, and especially the Ran's words at the end of Ketubot, indicate this. The matter requires study and careful consideration. In any event, the Shach correctly objected to the Sema's citation of Rabbenu Yerucham, because Rabbenu Yerucham does not follow the law adopted by the Shulchan Aruch here; the issue is subject to dispute.
