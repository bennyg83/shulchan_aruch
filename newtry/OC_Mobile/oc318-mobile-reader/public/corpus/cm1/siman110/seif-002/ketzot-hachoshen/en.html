The Shulchan Aruch rules that if a creditor seized property from minor heirs after their father's death, the seizure can sometimes be effective. Several great authorities, however, held that a migo based seizure after death does not help against minors, because a migo is no stronger than witnesses and testimony is not accepted outside the litigant's presence. This view was attacked by later authorities who said that testimony offered merely to maintain possession can be received. Mahari Tzalon correctly explains that the distinction is not simply between plaintiff and defendant. Testimony cannot be used outside the other party's presence to destroy his property rights. The Ran's statement that defensive testimony may be received means that where a claimant is not present and the court is considering acting on his behalf, the defendant can show enough evidence to prevent the court from presently removing property from him. When the actual claimant later appears, the witnesses must testify again in his presence. Rivah and Maharshal apply this to a minor. If a person is already living in a house and says he has witnesses of a valid possession, the court can hear enough of those witnesses to avoid removing him merely on the minor's present claim. When the minor becomes an adult, the testimony must be given again in his presence. But if the possessor has no evidence that will still be usable when the minor reaches majority, the court should not leave the property with him merely because of a temporary procedural advantage. A migo is weaker in this respect. Even if the migo exists now, once the minor becomes an adult the possessor may no longer have it, because he has already admitted the factual predicate in court. The court's refusal to decide now on the migo is like the case where a person reveals an object before witnesses and says it is pledged to him for its value. If the migo depended on a later judicial oath, the prior admission destroys it. Therefore a migo that exists only now, outside the minor's litigational presence, does not provide the enduring proof required to maintain the seizure. This vindicates Maharam Mintz, Maharshal, and Mahari Shach that a migo after the father's death normally does not help against minor heirs. There is, however, a distinction where the seizure is of money and the migo does not require any judicial oath. In such a case the migo can be fully established by the possessor's statement before witnesses, just as certain possessory claims can be established outside beit din when no judicial oath is required. The migo then remains meaningful even when the minor later becomes an adult. This explains why Rabbeinu Yerucham can permit seizure of money after death while other authorities reject seizure of ordinary property based on migo. It also explains Rava bar Sharshom's case: he could not simply assert his debt against minor heirs on the strength of a present migo over the land, but he could consume fruits to the amount of his claim, turning his position into possession of money, where the migo is effective.
The Shach writes that if the creditor seized money after the father's death, even before witnesses and while holding a debt instrument, we do not remove it. He attributes this to Rabbeinu Yerucham and says even the Tur can agree. The language of the Tur in siman 85 seems difficult, because it says that where each party is creditor and debtor against the other, even if the heirs are minors the surviving party can offset only because he had already seized during their father's lifetime. This suggests that seizure after death does not help even with money. The episode of Rava bar Sharshom is also difficult. If he had already eaten fruits equal to his claim, he had effectively seized money. Why then was he required to wait until the minors grew up rather than swear now on his document? This depends on how the episode is read. Rabbeinu Yerucham can interpret it as concerning future fruits not yet consumed. Most commentators, however, understand that he had already eaten the fruits. According to them, seizure of money without a current migo may still be removed from a person when taken after the father's death. Thus the Shach's broad rule remains subject to substantial dispute.
