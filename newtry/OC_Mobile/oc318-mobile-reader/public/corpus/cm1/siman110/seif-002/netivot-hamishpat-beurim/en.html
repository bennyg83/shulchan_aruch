Ketzot HaChoshen objects that the authorities explain Rava's case as concerning fruit already consumed, which is like seized money, and nevertheless Rava says that he will wait until the orphans grow up. There is no difficulty. Under Talmudic law, the heirs' movable property was not subject to their father's creditor, and even appreciation accruing to the property after the father's death was considered merely prospective. The creditor is therefore seizing movable property in exchange for land left by the father that was subject to him. It can be said that this is not considered actual payment already completed, and therefore the seizure is ineffective. Even according to the Ran at the beginning of Perek Almanah Nizonet, who holds that seizure of money is effective for a ketubah, he can agree that it is ineffective for an ordinary creditor, as is evident in Ketubot 92 regarding Reuven who sold a field to Shimon and converted the purchase price into a loan. Only where the creditor first gives land to the heirs does his claim work; seizure of money does not. The same is evident in Ketubot 110. In our time, by contrast, the heirs' movable property is subject to their father's creditor. If the creditor seizes money, it is as though he has already been paid, because a creditor's ordinary collection is from money. As to the principal rule whether seizure after death is effective, the Tumim's view appears primary, that it is effective, contrary to the Shach. The authorities who disagree reason that a migo is no stronger than witnesses, and since testimony is not accepted outside the presence of a litigant, a migo is likewise not accepted. In my opinion the comparison is not valid. Witnesses are different because the Torah did not grant credibility to testimony given outside the litigant's presence, and the witnesses can retract and testify again. It is comparable to testimony from a written statement or testimony that cannot be made subject to refutation, forms the Torah did not validate. Such testimony gives no proof that the matter is true. A migo, by contrast, is evidence that the claimant is telling the truth because he did not make the alternative, stronger claim. What difference should it make whether the other litigant is present? The authorities' statement that testimony may be accepted merely to preserve the status quo also has a clear rationale. Ketzot HaChoshen cites Maharit, who holds that testimony accepted outside the claimant's presence in order to preserve possession must later be repeated before the claimant when he sues. Ketzot argues that with a migo, when the minor grows up the claimant will no longer possess the migo, while the judges who heard it are then merely witnesses and the object is already seen in his possession. This is not persuasive. Maharit's view is that testimony outside the claimant's presence helps only to save the defendant from a claim being advanced by beit din when the claimant is absent. When the claimant later appears and sues, the witnesses must testify again because beit din is then adjudicating his claim in his presence. Were beit din not acting as beit din, the defendant would not have to answer them at all. Here, however, beit din can adjudicate against the absent claim and certainly require the holder of the pledge to swear, because even to retain the pledge until the orphans grow up he requires an oath. The main distinction is that of the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 91. With a claim of purchase, a migo can work outside beit din and outside the claimant's presence, because in the end no oath is required. With a pledge, however, an oath is required, and witnesses cannot adjudicate the law of an oath. Therefore the migo does not work outside beit din. Here the claimant has his migo before an actual beit din capable of administering the oath. It is therefore a complete migo. When the orphans grow up, the judges themselves can testify that he had a valid migo, which should work. Ketzot's approach would also imply that seizure during the father's lifetime should not help, and his distinction there is very strained. It further appears that when the authorities say that just as testimony is not accepted, a migo is not accepted, although the two are not truly comparable, their intent is as follows. We see that even where testimony ordinarily can be accepted outside a litigant's presence, such as where the witnesses are ill, it is nevertheless not accepted against minor orphans, because they are unable to search for arguments in their favor, as Rashba writes and Tumim cites in siman 28. For the same reason beit din does not address the property of minor heirs. Accordingly, the authorities correctly say that all the more so beit din should not extract money from minors based only on a migo. Yet once the migo is used merely to preserve possession, the problem disappears. It is not correct to say that because the claimant already argued before beit din and the migo did not then help, once the heirs grow up it becomes only a retroactive migo in the presence of witnesses and observation. If he had a valid migo before beit din, it certainly remains effective. The Shach in siman 72, seif katan 91, rules that even where witnesses saw the object, if the claimant then had a migo it is effective, except with a pledge because an oath is required. See there. The remaining distinctions offered by Ketzot are therefore unnecessary. There is also a scribal error in Shach, seif katan 5. The text should read: And so it appears from Baal HaTerumot, cited by Beit Yosef, that when they now saw it in his possession, this implies that he is believed to say, Your father owes me, through a migo.
