Give a field, etc. Although this is essentially an admitted debt, we nevertheless appoint a guardian to argue for the minors, perhaps there is some uncertainty or qualification in the language.
Likewise, if he said, etc. The Gemara states that Rava generally does not proceed against minor heirs' property. If the deceased said, 'Give,' we do give. If he specified 'this field' or 'this maneh,' we proceed without appointing a guardian. If he said only 'a field' or 'a maneh,' we proceed but appoint a guardian. The scholars of Nehardea say that in all these cases we proceed and appoint a guardian, except where a field is found not to belong to the estate, because we do not presume witnesses to have testified falsely. Rav Ashi rules that ordinarily we do not proceed, following Rava, but where we do proceed we appoint a guardian, following Nehardea, except where the field is clearly not the estate's. Rashba explains Rambam as follows. Rav Ashi's statement that we do not proceed refers to ordinary creditor claims. His statement that where a court does proceed it should appoint a guardian means that even a court following a view other than Rava should not act without a guardian, since even Nehardea requires one except in the clear case of a field that never belonged to the estate. We ourselves, however, rule like Rava. Therefore, with an unspecified field or maneh, and with a field proven not to belong to the estate, a guardian is appointed. But where the deceased specified this field or this maneh, Rava considered the matter even clearer than the case of a field belonging to another, and no guardian is needed. This is Rambam's intent. There is no need for an alternate textual reading, as Sema suggests. See Maggid Mishneh.
That he gave, etc. See Tumim for the reason. Since payment was personally incumbent upon him and he transferred the property to a minor, this resembles a debtor who goes overseas, from whose property we collect in his absence without concern for hidden repayment, so that people should not take others' money and flee overseas. Here too, payment was incumbent upon him and he attempted to remove the creditor's right by transferring the assets to a minor. It is therefore treated like a debtor who went overseas. This explanation is correct and Sema and Shach's reasons are unnecessary.
We place the creditor into the property. The Mechaber states this without qualification, even if the note was not authenticated during the deceased's lifetime or while the property was still held by an adult heir. Rosh and Tur, however, require prior authentication. If the note was not authenticated, they do not authenticate it now in the minor's absence. The authorities ask that documents can ordinarily be authenticated outside the presence of the opposing party. The later authorities answer that we do not combine two exceptional procedures, both collecting from minor heirs and authenticating the note in their absence. Knesset HaGedolah answers that before authentication there was not yet an established payment obligation upon the adult heir, so transferring the property to the minor did not remove a vested creditor right. Once the note had been authenticated, the payment obligation had taken effect and the adult heir should not have impaired the creditor by transferring the property away. Therefore, if it was not authenticated, we do not descend to the minor's property. See Tumim.
