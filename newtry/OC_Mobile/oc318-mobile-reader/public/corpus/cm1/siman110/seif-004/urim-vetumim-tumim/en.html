This is strained, as the Shach writes. The Shach answers that the case is within the time fixed for payment, but that too is untenable. What novelty would the Rosh and Tur thereby teach, and what difference would there be between an adult heir who gave the property to a minor and a case where the heir was a minor from beginning to end? In truth, none of this is necessary. The responsum itself explains why, when a creditor collects in the debtor's absence, we do not suspect that he received valuables in satisfaction, whereas with minor orphans we do entertain that concern. The answer is as the Gemara states: because of tikkun olam, so that a person should not take another's money and go overseas. The Sages therefore enacted that the creditor may collect upon taking an oath. This rationale does not apply to minor orphans. Accordingly, in the present case, where an adult heir transfers the property to a minor heir, the matter is comparable to one who goes overseas. Otherwise every person could destroy his creditor's ability to collect simply by transferring his assets to a minor. It is therefore like one who went overseas, from whose property collection is made in his absence. This is straightforward.
Now, in the responsum of the Rosh, he stipulated that this applies only where the promissory note was already authenticated. If it was not authenticated, the note is not authenticated while the heirs are minors, because a document is not authenticated in the absence of the opposing litigant. All have wondered at this, for we rule that a document may be authenticated in the absence of the opposing party. The Sema answers that here there are two disadvantages: collection from the property of a minor and acceptance of testimony authenticating the document in his absence. We do not combine both. The author of Knesset HaGedolah takes essentially the same approach. So long as the document was already authenticated, the obligation of payment had already attached to the adult heir, and he cannot weaken that lien merely by transferring his property to a minor. But as long as the document had not yet been authenticated, even though it could be authenticated, the obligation of payment had not yet attached to the adult heir until authentication. He therefore validly transferred the property to the minor, and the case is then like ordinary minor orphans, whose property we do not collect from. See there. Maharit, his teacher, already preceded him with this reasoning in Choshen Mishpat, responsum siman 7. Yet according to them, why does the Rosh base his reason on the statement that a document is not authenticated in the absence of the opposing litigant? That proposition is in truth not the law. He should simply have said that one does not collect from the property of minors because the document had not yet been authenticated. It is therefore preferable, despite some strain, to say that the actual case before the Rosh involved no other witnesses who recognized the signatures. The witnesses to the document themselves were the ones identifying and authenticating their own signatures. The Rosh may hold like the Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma, who explains that a document may ordinarily be authenticated in the absence of the opposing party because the witnesses testify merely about the handwriting of the witnesses. What need is there for the holder of the document to be present? That applies when other witnesses identify the signatures. But when the witnesses to the document themselves testify, we rule like the Sages that they testify concerning the debt recorded in the document itself. According to all opinions, they must then remember the substance of the loan and their testimony, as stated above in siman 46. The Rashba's rationale therefore no longer applies, and such testimony must be accepted in the presence of the opposing litigant, because it is full substantive testimony and they must remember the actual event. This may be a sound rule, even though it is somewhat strained to read the Rosh's unqualified language as referring to that particular factual circumstance. It is nevertheless preferable to creating new laws with no root or branch in the words of the Sages. It also appears that the Rosh has a proof from the incident of Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom in Bava Batra. The Ramban's question above, seif katan 1, applies: why did he need to conceal the document? He should have stated the facts in beit din and been believed by virtue of his migo. One must answer, as the Ramban does in one answer according to the Geonim, that although he had a migo he would nevertheless have had to take an oath, because this was a case of one who swears and takes payment. Therefore he concealed the document; the Geonim did not impose their oath in a case of actual extraction by continued possession. See there. But there is a difficulty. Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom had a promissory note, as is evident from the Gemara, and the Geonic oath does not apply to a creditor holding a promissory note, as the Shach writes below in siman 149, seif katan 24. See there and what I wrote above. The Rosh therefore may hold that he did indeed have a promissory note. Otherwise, how could he ever collect from the orphans by oath? The document, however, had not been authenticated. Were it not for the oath problem, he could have waited until they became adults, authenticated the document, and collected upon oath. To avoid the oath he reasoned that if he came while they were minors and stated the facts, the Geonic oath would still be required, while the document could not help because it was unauthenticated and could not be authenticated in the presence of minors. According to what I wrote, perhaps the form of authentication in Rabbah bar Rav Sharshom's case was precisely one where the witnesses themselves testified. He therefore continued consuming the produce, and the Geonic oath no longer applied, as the Ramban explains. This is precise.
