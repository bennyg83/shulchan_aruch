One does not collect with it. Responsa Mahara ben Chaim writes that if the document expressly states that collection may be made from minor heirs, one may indeed collect even from minor orphans. I found proof for his ruling from Tosafos in Perek Get Pashut, Bava Batra 174, who explains according to Rav Papa, who says that we ordinarily do not collect from minors because they are not subject to the mitzvah of repayment, that if the borrower wrote an acknowledgment that effectively gave the creditor power to collect from minor heirs, collection is valid. The purpose of such a clause is precisely to strengthen the creditor's right against minor heirs. All the more so according to Rav Huna, whose concern is that the father may have deposited payment with the creditor, can the borrower stipulate and waive any such concern. This is why the Gemara does not present a stipulation regarding minor heirs as a practical distinction between Rav Papa and Rav Huna. Such a stipulation works according to both. This is correct and clear. Although Rambam writes that conditions do not help, he may not have intended this specific condition. Common practice is indeed that beit din collects even from minor heirs where documents contain the customary clause authorizing such collection, and this must be the basis upon which the courts rely.
From a non Jew who accepted, etc. Even though we rule above in siman 92 that a person suspected of false oaths who comes to collect from orphans falls under the rule that a person cannot transmit to his heirs a claim that required his own oath, and this non Jew is certainly treated as suspect for such purposes, as Beit Yosef writes in siman 28, the case must concern a document containing a credibility clause. Even so, the ordinary rule remains that we do not collect from the minor heirs. According to the authorities who hold that such credibility can overcome the oath problem even in this context, as Baal HaTerumot indicates, they necessarily disagree with the ruling cited above in siman 92.
And he did not accept upon himself not to take interest. The Gemara itself gives this answer. One might ask why it did not establish the case more simply as a non Jew who accepted the seven Noahide commandments, a resident alien who is morally observant. According to the Rama in responsum 10, the Noahide commandment of courts requires them to follow the laws of Israel without alteration. If so, we should judge him under our monetary law. Yet interest remains permitted to him because Scripture explicitly permitted it, as Tosafos writes in Bava Metzia 70 concerning the verse dealing with interest. One cannot answer that rabbinic enactments are outside the Noahide obligation of civil law, and that waiting rather than collecting from minor heirs is only a rabbinic enactment. The Gemara afterward challenges from the baraita that collection from orphan property is only from inferior land, and there too it establishes a non Jew who accepted Jewish civil law. It then asks how he can take interest. Collection from inferior land is itself a Torah rule for creditors. At first sight this would support those who disagree with the Rama and hold that Noahides are commanded only to establish courts and justice, but may follow their own reasonable legal system. It appears, however, that the Gemara wished to formulate an answer that works even according to Rav Papa. Rav Papa's reason that minor heirs are not obligated in the mitzvah of repayment rests upon his position that property liens are not of Torah law. As Ramban explains, and as discussed above in siman 39, the obligation of the heirs after they mature is itself rabbinic. Even if a non Jew who accepted the Noahide commandments is generally required to conform to Torah civil law, repayment by heirs in this case is not a Torah obligation but a rabbinic institution. The Sages may have made no distinction between Jewish and non Jewish creditors concerning the basic debt, but with respect to interest, which has no parallel obligation between Jews at all, they did not institute payment. Thus the Gemara properly asks, "How can he take interest?" not because the non Jew is prohibited from receiving it, but because the heirs were never obligated by the rabbinic repayment institution to pay such interest. Examine carefully.
