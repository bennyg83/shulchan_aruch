And likewise if their predecessor robbed, etc. The Shach writes in siman 118 that if the father admitted this in recognizable handwriting, the rule applies. He attributes this to the Maharshal, but notes that the Baal HaTerumot appears to hold that testimony is accepted in such a case even after the robber's death. The straightforward language of the Tur also implies this, and the Ir Shushan states it explicitly. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. He has combined distinct views. It is obvious that the Baal HaTerumot follows Tosafot and the Rambam, who understand the statement that where a field is discovered not to belong to the possessor we address the claim because witnesses are not presumed false, and testimony is therefore accepted even in the presence of minor orphans. Accordingly, in the baraita stating that when the stolen object remains extant, according to the Sages both adult and minor heirs must return it, there is no need for an admission by the father. Testimony may be accepted after the robber's death without hesitation. This is also the Shulchan Aruch's view, and therefore the Ir Shushan correctly wrote as he did according to the Shulchan Aruch. The Tur, however, follows his father the Rosh. The Rosh expressly explains that the statement "except for a field that is not his," etc., refers to testimony that had already been accepted during the father's lifetime. In Perek HaGozel he also writes that the dispute between the Sages and Symmachus concerns a stolen object that remains extant and whose status is known. The Yam Shel Shlomo explains that this means it is publicly known that the object belongs to the victim, so testimony is unnecessary, and the matter is equivalent to testimony accepted during the father's lifetime. Accordingly, where the stolen object no longer remains extant, that consideration does not apply. Indeed, the Rosh labored specifically to establish the baraita as involving an extant stolen object. Thus the Maharshal is plainly correct that according to the Rosh the relevant testimony must have been accepted during the father's lifetime. The views of the Baal HaTerumot and the Tur therefore differ. In truth, however, my understanding of the Rosh differs from the Maharshal's explanation that when the Rosh says the object remains extant and its status is known, he means it is so public that no testimony is needed and is therefore equivalent to testimony accepted during the father's lifetime. If so, what proof did Rabbi Yirmiyah bring against Rabbi Avin's rule that testimony is not accepted outside the presence of the litigant from the Sages, who say that both adult and minor heirs are liable? There no testimony would be needed at all, as though it had already been accepted during the father's lifetime. Why then did the Gemara initially raise a difficulty from Symmachus and the Sages? The matters would not be comparable. Rather, it is clear that when the Rosh establishes the case of a field "found not to be his" as one where testimony was accepted during the father's lifetime, he does not explain it as a case where the orphans lack ancestral possession of that field. The Gemara implies that the field is removed from an orphan, as in Rabbi Yirmiyah's incident had the orphan lacked ancestral possession. The Rosh understood the phrase "it is found not to be his" to mean that only now it became known that the field was not his, whereas until then it stood in the father's possession. That is an ancestral chazakah. He therefore had to explain that the testimony was accepted during the father's lifetime. Accordingly, the dispute of Symmachus and the Sages concerning robbery can be a case where during the father's lifetime the matter became public and it was known that the object in his possession was stolen. That publicity removes the ancestral chazakah from the particular object. Testimony may then be accepted outside the litigant's presence after the father's death. This does not mean testimony is unnecessary. It is still needed. Rather, the prior publicity and knowledge remove the father's chazakah from the object. On this basis there is no difficulty with the flow of the Gemara. Before the Gemara introduced the distinction between a case with ancestral chazakah and one without it, the dispute of Symmachus and the Sages appeared to prove that testimony may be accepted after death. One could not say the testimony had already been accepted during the father's lifetime, because if so, why would Symmachus disagree? Once the Gemara teaches the distinction based on ancestral chazakah, there is no difficulty. In the robbery case, where there is no ancestral chazakah regarding the known stolen object, testimony may be accepted, and therefore Symmachus's view is rejected. It is admittedly somewhat strained why the Rosh brought proof from the case in Eruvin concerning a field found not to belong to the possessor, since the cases are not precisely the same, one involving testimony accepted during life and the other involving absence of ancestral chazakah. One can say, however, that there too the reason we do not plead for the orphans that perhaps the father later bought the field back from the victim, and that when the orphans grow they may find the deed, just as elsewhere we are concerned they may find a receipt, is that once testimony was accepted during the father's lifetime the ancestral chazakah was impaired. We no longer search for possible arguments on behalf of the orphans. The same applies to a publicly known stolen object. There is another difficulty. Even if witnesses testified during the father's lifetime that he stole a known object, how can it be recovered from the orphans? Perhaps their father returned it. We rule that one who robbed in the presence of witnesses need not return in the presence of witnesses. Although we do not plead that he purchased it by virtue of a migo that he returned it, because that is an uncommon claim, as the Rosh writes, nevertheless perhaps he actually did return it. All the more so since the rule requires specifically that the stolen object still exist. Perhaps the object now before us is no longer the stolen one. One must therefore say that after the father's death the witnesses are shown the object and identify it by distinctive visual recognition or number as the very object that was stolen. See below siman 296, where this matter is discussed at length regarding a deposit. If so, testimony is still required after the father's death. The original difficulty returns: may testimony be accepted outside the presence of the litigant? The answer must be that once testimony had already been accepted during the father's lifetime that a particular object in his possession was stolen, the father no longer has ancestral chazakah in that object. Additional testimony after death identifying the object may therefore be accepted before minor heirs, because ancestral chazakah is absent and the object remains in the victim's legal domain. The Rosh thus appropriately compared it to a field that is not his. Once testimony was given during his lifetime, we address the matter even against the minors, because it already passed outside the heirs' presumptive ownership and is not considered ordinary collection from the property of minor orphans. Accordingly, Rabbi Yirmiyah, before he had conceived the distinction based on ancestral chazakah, properly proved that testimony can be accepted after death. In the robbery case it is necessarily accepted after death for the purpose of identifying that the object remains extant. Those witnesses are witnesses of visual identification. Once the Gemara introduces the distinction based on ancestral chazakah, however, there is no difficulty. Because testimony concerning the robbery itself was accepted during the father's lifetime, subsequent identification testimony may be accepted after death. This is precise. The Rosh may also have had support from Tosafot's question in Bava Kamma 112, s.v. ketanim. The Gemara asks, if the heirs consumed the stolen property, why are the minors liable, but does not ask why the adults are liable as well. According to the Rosh, as stated, the father left property subject to liability. The question is specifically against the minors because testimony is not accepted against them. Tosafot's response that according to the Sages testimony is accepted is unnecessary, because that is so only when the stolen object remains extant, as the Rosh explains, where the item remains in the victim's presumptive ownership. Where it is no longer extant, all agree that testimony is not accepted against the minors, because the money representing the stolen object is certainly in the orphans' presumptive possession. The Gemara therefore properly asks why they should be liable. This is precise.
