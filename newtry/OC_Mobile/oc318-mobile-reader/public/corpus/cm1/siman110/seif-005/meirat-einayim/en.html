We act on their behalf if the property is found stolen. The Gemara, Rosh, and Tur add that this applies specifically where the testimony was received during the father's lifetime, or the claimant produces an authenticated deed showing he bought the property from the father and the father did not possess it for three years after the date of that deed. If the testimony was not received during the father's lifetime, according to these authorities it is not received afterward. The Mechaber follows Rambam, who holds that when the property is shown to have been stolen or the deceased expressly instructed about it, testimony may be received even in the minors' absence. The rule against receiving testimony outside the defendant's presence applies where the property was in the father's established possession, he gave no instruction, and another now seeks to remove it from the heirs by claiming purchase.
According to Rambam, a guardian must be appointed for them. Other authorities do not require a guardian. The Tur explains that we do not appoint one merely to search for some way to disqualify the dispossessed owner's witnesses, because we do not presume witnesses to be false. Rambam apparently had a different reading of the Gemara.
Likewise, a minor who forcibly entered, etc., land that had been in the minors' ancestral possession, etc. The underlying sugya is the case of Rabbi Yirmiyah, who claimed a house from his young brother-in-law, asserting that he bought it from the boy's father and had witnesses to three years' possession during the father's lifetime. The minor closed the house against him. Rabbi Avin ruled for the minor and refused to receive Rabbi Yirmiyah's testimony until the boy matured. The Gemara asks from Rabbi Oshaya's statement that a child who, assisted by his servants, forcibly enters another's field and claims it as his own is removed immediately; we do not wait until he matures. It answers that in Rabbi Oshaya's case the child lacked ancestral possession, whereas Rabbi Yirmiyah's opponent had inherited possession from his father.
Tosafos prove that protection of the minor requires two elements: ancestral possession and present possession. If either is lacking, the adult occupant's testimony may be received. Thus, if the minor lacks ancestral possession, he may be removed even though he currently occupies the land. Conversely, if he has ancestral possession but is not presently in possession and the adult occupant claims purchase from the father plus three years' possession during the father's life, the adult's evidence is received and he is not removed merely because the claimant is a minor. Rambam and the Mechaber include these distinctions across the two seifim. The phrase "likewise, a minor who forcibly entered" teaches that even where the adult concedes the property once belonged to the minor's father and only claims a later purchase, if the minor lacks the combination of ancestral and present possession his status does not prevent receipt of the adult's evidence.
Rama's gloss, that this applies specifically where the minor lacked a chazakah, refers to this adjacent ruling. If the minor has ancestral possession and is presently holding the land, he is not removed even if the adult had previously occupied it and the minor forcibly expelled him. This is a further novelty not explicit in the Mechaber's next seif. Because they damage him directly. In such a case the minors are removed from the damaging activity, but they do not pay for damage they caused while minors, as explained in siman 96. Whether there is substance to his claims, etc. Raavad writes that a guardian should generally be appointed to argue for the heirs, whether his own arguments appear substantial or not, perhaps to arrange a compromise for their benefit, except in a case of property established as stolen. Rama's words refer to the guardian's arguments, not the opposing claimant's. And "always" is not literal, since Raavad himself excludes the stolen-property case.
