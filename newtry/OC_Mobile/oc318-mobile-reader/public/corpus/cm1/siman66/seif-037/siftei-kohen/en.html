A convert, etc., his right lapses, etc. Baal HaTerumot, Gate 51, at the end of part 6, writes that the decisors agreed that this resembles a Jew's pledge held by a gentile: the holder does not acquire it because the gentile's lien lapses upon the convert's death. I searched the early and later authorities and found no one who ruled so, and the comparison is entirely incorrect. There, the gentile has only a lien on the pledge and has not acquired the pledge itself, for a gentile does not acquire a Jew's pledge according to any opinion, certainly when it was taken at the time of the loan. When the gentile dies, his lien therefore lapses. Indeed, had the Jew repaid the money, the gentile would have had to return the pledge. Here, however, the purchaser acquired the note by a complete acquisition through writing and delivery. If the seller later returned the purchase money, the sale could not be rescinded against the purchaser's will. Although the seller retains the power to forgive the debt, until he actually does so the note belongs entirely to the purchaser, since he acquired it by a complete acquisition. It is therefore unreasonable that the note should lapse upon the convert's death, especially according to our ruling that the sale of notes is effective by Torah law. This requires study. I later found that the Ritva in Kiddushin explicitly writes as I do, and I rejoiced. He explains that although even an heir can forgive the debt, the sale remains valid until the heir actually forgives it. We may say that at the time the money was given the borrower effectively undertook liability to anyone deriving title from the lender, so long as the lender did not forgive the debt. Therefore, when a convert sells a promissory note and afterward dies, even though the personal lien has lapsed, the purchaser may collect from the borrower, because the borrower is already obligated to him until the lender or his heirs release him. This is correct, in the name of his teacher.
