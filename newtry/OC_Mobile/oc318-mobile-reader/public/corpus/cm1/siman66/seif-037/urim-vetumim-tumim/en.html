But if he sold it to two people, and so forth. Shach, seif katan 71, questions Baal HaTerumot and Tur. If the concern is a genuine suspicion that a purchaser who also serves as witness is biased, then even where only one of the two purchasers is a witness, how can we rely on him? Remove the interested purchaser from his own matter and only one valid witness remains. How can money be extracted on the basis of one witness? This objection is not enough to reject the Geonim. Yevamot 25b itself proves the point. The Gemara says the disqualification applies specifically to a beit din member, implying that two ordinary witnesses do not suffer the same suspicion. It asks from the rule that witnesses who signed a transaction may afterward purchase the property. But in some of the related cases, if a suspect witness marries or otherwise benefits, the marriage may even be dissolved. If the Gemara's question worked only according to the lenient view that a completed marriage is not dissolved, it should have said so explicitly. The unqualified challenge implies that once there are two witnesses, the suspicion disappears altogether. Tosafot Ketubot 22b similarly ask from the rule that one who testifies "we killed him" may not marry the widow. Their question must involve the stringent view that even after marriage she may have to leave, or else there would be little difficulty. Tosafot answer that where there are two witnesses, the suspicion is absent. But one could ask exactly Shach's question: if one of the two is personally interested, remove him and only one witness remains. The answer is that the second witness does more than merely add another independent unit of testimony. His presence removes the suspicion from the first. Once two people jointly give consistent testimony, Chazal do not suspect that each fabricated the story for his own later benefit. The first witness therefore returns to full testimonial validity. This is especially clear because the present concern is only rabbinic suspicion, not a Torah disqualification of an actual interested party in the core subject matter. Shach himself cites Tosafot Gittin regarding a Samaritan witness. Because by Torah law Samaritans were considered true converts, a single Jewish witness alongside the Samaritan can validate the act despite rabbinic suspicion. Rama in responsum derives that in a purely rabbinic concern we can rely on a second witness to remove the suspicion. Our case is no stronger. The suspicion that a purchaser testified in order to acquire the note is rabbinic. The presence of the second witness removes it. Accordingly, Baal HaTerumot and Tur are correct.
