A convert. Baal HaTerumot records that the authorities ruled that when a convert sold a debt instrument and afterward died without heirs, the purchaser loses the debt. The Shach instead follows Ritva. Ritva explains that the reason a sold debt can be waived is Rabbeinu Tam's distinction between the debtor's personal obligation and the property lien. The purchaser acquires the property lien, but the personal obligation remains with the seller. When the seller waives the personal obligation, the property lien falls away, just as a guarantor's obligation falls away when the principal debt is forgiven. Ritva asks that if the purchaser collects only through an authorization embedded in the property lien, the seller's death should terminate the authorization. He answers that at the time of the loan the debtor is treated as saying, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you, so long as you have not waived." This explanation is difficult because in the first chapter of Gittin the Gemara asks, regarding a three party transfer explained in that language, what happens if the right is transferred to a person not yet born. The same question should arise in every sale of a debt instrument. In my view the simpler explanation is that the sale transfers only the property lien. The debtor's personal obligation remains with the seller and continues to exist so long as the seller has not waived it. The property lien is like a guarantor for that personal debt. Ordinarily one may not collect from a guarantor before demanding payment from the principal debtor, but that is only when the principal debtor can actually be sued. If he is absent or beyond reach, the guarantor may be pursued. For the purchaser of a debt instrument, the debtor's personal obligation is legally outside his reach because he never acquired it. He may therefore proceed directly against the property lien, just as one proceeds against a guarantor when the principal cannot be reached. This avoids any need for an implied obligation to all future holders. Accordingly, when the original creditor is a convert who dies without heirs, the personal obligation disappears because there is no one who holds it, and the property lien then falls away as well.
It falls away. This applies specifically to a sold debt instrument. If the convert sold actual collateral and then died, the purchaser's right does not disappear. The seller could not have waived the collateral once its body was transferred, because a creditor acquires movable collateral under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule. The purchaser therefore acquired the body of the pledge while the convert was alive. The Gemara at the end of Perek HaParah, concerning a Jew's pledge held by a convert who then dies and another person seizes it, is different. There the second person took the pledge only after the convert's death, when the debt had already fallen away. He therefore acquired nothing and the borrower takes back the pledge. A purchaser who acquired the pledge during the convert's lifetime retains it.
