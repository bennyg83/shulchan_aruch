And if the borrower is unable to pay, and so forth. Terumat HaDeshen infers from Rosh's language that the seller returns and collects from the borrower, not from the purchaser. This implies the seller has no direct claim against the purchaser. Shach and Gidulei Terumah find this difficult. If we treat the purchaser's ineffective acquisition like forgiveness, why may the seller recover from the borrower at all? If we do not treat it like forgiveness, why can he not also recover from the purchaser? And once the seller collects from the borrower, the borrower will surely turn to the purchaser and say, "Return what I paid you improperly." If the borrower has no assets, why should the seller not reach the purchaser through R. Natan's lien? Shach labors to answer. According to what I wrote in my kuntres on possession, there is a coherent explanation in Rosh's own system. Rosh rules concerning a worker and similar cases that where the original debt is certain and only the alleged payment is doubtful, seizure cannot create certainty. The debtor stands in the position of one who says, "I do not know whether I paid," and remains liable. Rosh also writes in responsum, cited by Tur at the end of siman 248, that although Rashbam holds debt documents are acquired by delivery alone, Rif, Rabbenu Chananel, and Rabbenu Tam require writing and delivery. Since major authorities disagree, money already possessed is not removed from the holder. Thus Rosh may regard the need for writing and delivery as a genuine unresolved dispute for purposes of possession. The purchaser can therefore say: "I follow Rashbam. I acquired the note validly by delivery." As possessor, he cannot be forced to return what he collected. But the borrower cannot use the same argument. The borrower certainly owed the seller originally. Whether his payment to the purchaser discharged him depends on the disputed transfer law. He therefore stands in the position of one who is certain he owed and uncertain whether he paid the proper person. Such doubt does not discharge him. And because the mainstream halachah follows Rif and those requiring writing and delivery, the borrower's kim li argument is particularly weak. This also answers the point that the borrower may later sue the purchaser. The purchaser says: "I possessed the money under a valid legal view, that delivery alone acquired the document. The fact that your own defensive claim against the seller fails does not make my possession wrongful." This resembles the rule discussed above in siman 58: if a person lawfully seized property under a defensible claim and another party later suffers because he cannot use that same defense, the seizer need not reimburse merely because the other party lost. This is a strong defense of Terumat HaDeshen. Nevertheless, for practical halachah today, the consensus of Shulchan Aruch and later authorities is that ordinary debt documents require writing and delivery. If that is treated as settled law rather than a balanced dispute, the purchaser cannot simply say kim li like Rashbam. Then the seller should indeed have a claim against the purchaser as well, because the purchaser never acquired the debt properly. Even so, Rosh and Tur's wording that the seller "returns and collects from the borrower" teaches an additional point. When collecting from the borrower, the seller can demand the full face amount. The borrower cannot say, "At least deduct the price you received from the purchaser." The seller can answer: "That purchase money is a separate deposit or obligation between me and the purchaser. You owe me the full debt on your note. If the purchaser later claims his money from me, I will answer him separately." R. Natan's rule does not automatically permit the borrower to set off the purchaser's claim, because R. Natan has many limitations, including where the intermediary has other assets. By contrast, if the seller recovers directly from the purchaser, he obviously must account for the purchase price the purchaser paid. Thus Rosh's emphasis on collecting from the borrower may primarily teach that the borrower owes the full note amount without deduction. That seems the best practical reading.
