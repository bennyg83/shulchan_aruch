The same applies to one who gives it as a gift, etc. The Sema, seif katan 84, writes that even according to those who disagree above in seif 32, the giver must here pay the entire value of the note, because the recipient can say that he had conferred benefits upon the giver corresponding to the full value of the note. There is a textual error in his wording; it should read that the recipient can say, 'I conferred benefits upon the giver in return,' in accordance with the statement at the beginning of Bava Metzia that, had the recipient not exerted himself to please the giver, the giver would not have given him the gift. In my humble opinion, however, the Sema's ruling is not correct. According to the dissenting authorities the giver is exempt here as well. This is indicated by the Ritva cited below and by Shiltei Giborim at the end of Ketubot in the name of Riaz. When one studies Baal HaTerumot, Gate 51, part 6, one sees that this ruling appears within the Ramban's lengthy responsum on another matter, and the Ramban explicitly follows the Mechaber's view above in seif 32. I likewise found that Maharshal, chapter Merubah, siman 14, writes that if an agent later seeks payment from the principal because the principal forgave the agent's assigned share, the principal is exempt, for one who gives a promissory note as a gift and afterward forgives it is exempt according to all opinions. Thus Maharshal regarded the exemption as obvious. Nevertheless, Maharshal appears to follow Tosafot and their associates that, even according to the rule of liability for indirect damage, the seller need pay only the money actually received. His statement 'according to all opinions' therefore means even according to those who impose liability for indirect damage. According to what I wrote above, seif katan 109, that the seller must pay the entire value of the note, the same follows here: one who gave the note as a gift and then forgave it must pay the full value of the note. This is also written by the Ritva in Ketubot: 'He collects from him the proper value of the note,' meaning the value of the debt at the time of forgiveness, whether much or little, because the giver's liability is that of one who causes damage, and we rule like Rabbi Meir that indirect damage is actionable. Therefore, this applies even if the recipient received it as a gift. The Maggid Mishneh cited above in seif 11, seif katan 36, and Shiltei Giborim cited above in seif 32, seif katan 109, also indicate this. This is the principal ruling.
