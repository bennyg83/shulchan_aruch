There is an opinion that he is not required to accept Mi Shepara. Shach, in seif katan 8, leaves this difficult. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 51, part 4, explains that the reason no Mi Shepara applies is that the sale took effect to some extent: the buyer can say that he acquired the physical paper to use for wrapping a small vessel. If, as our halachah holds, he did not acquire even the paper, Shach argues that we should return to the ordinary rule and require Mi Shepara. The stated reason does appear in Baal HaTerumot, but it was necessary only because Baal HaTerumot assumes that here there are both money and a verbal agreement, which can generate Mi Shepara. This depends on the dispute in Perek HaZahav. The Gemara asks according to Reish Lakish, who holds that money does not acquire movables, how the Mishnah can say, "But they said: He Who punished..." According to R. Yochanan, money acquires by Torah law, so it is understandable that the rabbinic undoing of the acquisition leaves Mi Shepara. But according to Reish Lakish, if money never acquires at all, why should anything more than the rule "the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with one who retracts from words alone" apply? The Gemara answers that mere words bring only that disapproval, whereas money together with words brings Mi Shepara. The authorities disagree whether that answer survives according to R. Yochanan. Ri Migash, as cited in Shitah Mekubetzet, holds that it does. Even where there is no Torah acquisition at all, such as buying with a preexisting debt or buying something not presently in the seller's possession, payment together with agreement can still generate Mi Shepara. Ran and Nimukei Yosef disagree. They hold the answer was needed only according to Reish Lakish. Since our halachah follows R. Yochanan, Mi Shepara exists only where there was an underlying Torah acquisition that Chazal suspended. Where the transaction has no Torah acquisition at all, there is no Mi Shepara. Shitah Mekubetzet indicates that other authorities, including Rashba, follow Ran. Accordingly, Baal HaTerumot either follows Reish Lakish as a matter of halachah, like some major authorities, or follows Ri Migash even according to R. Yochanan. He therefore needed another reason why Mi Shepara does not apply here, and said that the buyer at least acquired the paper. But Mechaber and Rama below in siman 204 rule that where one buys with a preexisting debt or through collateral, there is no Mi Shepara. Likewise, below in siman 209, seif 6, when one sells something not presently in his possession, Mi Shepara applies only where the item is common and expected to come into his possession, so that it resembles an available transaction. These rulings follow Ran and his school: where there is no Torah acquisition, money and words do not create Mi Shepara. Therefore, in our case, once the sale of the debt note fails because the required transfer was not performed, there is no underlying Torah acquisition. Mi Shepara does not apply regardless of whether the physical paper itself was acquired. Shach's difficulty therefore falls away.
