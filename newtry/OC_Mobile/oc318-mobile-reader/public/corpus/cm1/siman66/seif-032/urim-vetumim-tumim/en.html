Even with an oath. Rosh in Perek HaKotev rules that if a woman sold her ketubah and later says, "My husband paid me before I sold it," she is not believed through a migo that she could forgive. If she openly forgives now, she may have to reimburse the purchaser for the full value. By saying she was paid before the sale, she seeks to pay only the smaller consideration she received. These are not equivalent alternatives. Even according to Rabbenu Tam, who holds that a forgiver pays only the purchase price, Rosh says the migo is weak because not everyone knows the precise law. She may fear that a court will require the full value and therefore prefer the prior-payment claim. Tur and Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer siman 105 codify this without dissent. Shach, seif katan 56, attacks the ruling and says it contradicts the Gemara in Bava Metzia, where a woman is believed regarding a receipt through a migo that she could forgive her ketubah. His objection should not lead us to reject Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber. First, Rosh himself explains the found-receipt Gemara in a case where there is no established sale creating the same financial exposure. He also cites an explanation that ketubah sales are uncommon because everyone knows the wife can forgive to her husband. The Talmudic concern for a sold ketubah is therefore already weaker in that context. Here the sale is actually before us. The purchaser exists and the financial consequences of forgiveness are concrete. The migo can therefore fail. Second, Rosh's statement that "not everyone knows the law" concerns his own time, when major authorities disagreed whether the forgiver pays the full value or only the price received. Even if the particular dayan rules like Rabbenu Tam, the woman may fear that another court will follow the opposing authorities. She does not want to enter a serious dispute. In the Talmudic era, by contrast, the operative legal rule for that sugya would have been understood uniformly. According to Rabbenu Tam's reading, a litigant there could know that forgiveness would not expose her to more than the price. The migo can therefore work. This also answers Tosafot's question in Kiddushin. They ask according to the view that forgiving a sold note causes full garmi damages: why would a woman distrust kiddushin through a purchased note because the seller might forgive, if forgiveness will make him pay the full amount anyway? The answer cannot be that she merely fears another legal opinion. In the Gemara's own setting the halachah being analyzed is assumed definite. Rosh's later uncertainty analysis belongs to a later period of disputed psak. Third, Tosafot Ketubot 19b expressly say that even a weak migo of forgiveness can be enough when an actual receipt is present. The receipt itself weakens the note. This is exactly the found-receipt case. Shach calls that answer strained, but it is Tosafot's explicit position and cannot simply be ignored. Fourth, there is an additional strong chazakah in our present case. A woman is not presumed to have received her ketubah and then knowingly sold a worthless document. That would make her a thief. The claim of prior payment is therefore inherently suspect. A weak forgiveness migo cannot overcome that chazakah. In the found-receipt case, however, we are not starting from an established sale and asking whether she knowingly sold a paid note. We are merely considering a possible hidden sale as one remote explanation for the receipt. The actual receipt before us supports her statement that payment occurred, and the migo assists it. Thus the cases are materially different. There remains an internal question in Rosh. Elsewhere he writes that a creditor who says "paid" is not believed through forgiveness because he prefers to retain a future claim against the borrower. Why does he not use that answer here too? Probably because once the ketubah has been sold, the seller cannot easily preserve an independent future claim against the husband by merely saying "paid." The purchaser can treat the transaction as rescinded and recover the price. Rosh therefore needed the stronger damages-based distinction. Tosafot may disagree on that detail, but it does not undermine the practical ruling. Accordingly, Mechaber's Even HaEzer ruling remains binding. What follows for an ordinary sold loan note is less certain. One might infer from the present siman that the seller is believed to say payment occurred even before the sale. Shach reads it that way. But the language can easily refer to payment after the sale, or to a case where the seller disclosed at the time of sale that the note had already been paid and the purchaser accepted the risk. There is no necessary proof that a post-sale claim of prior payment is believed. On the other hand, an ordinary creditor today may have the migo of writing the debtor an undated receipt, which can support a prior-payment claim more strongly than in the ketubah case. Even that is uncertain. The creditor may fear that the receipt witnesses will later disclose when it was written, making the migo weak. Therefore the law of a seller who, after the sale, claims payment occurred before the sale remains doubtful in ordinary debt notes. We should not derive a broad leniency against the explicit Even HaEzer ruling.
