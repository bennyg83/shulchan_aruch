The Tur writes: This is not comparable to an ordinary sale where, if a creditor seizes the property from the purchaser, the purchaser is entitled to reimbursement including the enhanced value. Here, since the seller himself can waive the debt, and even his heir can waive it, the sale was defective from the outset. The purchaser should have caused the document to be rewritten in his own name. Since he did not do so, it is proper that he receive only the money he paid for the document. This applies specifically here, because through the waiver the document becomes mere paper and the sale was defective from the outset. But if the seller's creditor comes and seizes the document from the purchaser, or another person establishes that it was his, and in the other cases mentioned by the Tur and Mechaber below in seif 34, everyone agrees that the seller must pay the full value of the document. There the instrument remains a valid promissory note and the borrower must pay the other claimant. Moreover, the purchaser had no reason to anticipate that the seller would sell something that was not his. Therefore the sale was not inherently defective. See the Perishah and Derishah, contrary to the Beit Yosef.
