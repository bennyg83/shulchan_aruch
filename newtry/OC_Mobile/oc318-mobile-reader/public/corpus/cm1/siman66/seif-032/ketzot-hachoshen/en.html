When the seller waives. I was uncertain whether, if the seller does not waive the whole debt but merely releases one parcel of the debtor's land from its lien, he must reimburse the purchaser for the resulting loss. Mishneh LaMelech cites a responsum of Rosh that appears to distinguish complete waiver from release of one parcel. Yet if a person physically digs up land encumbered to another, he can be liable for direct causation even though the entire debt has not yet been lost. More fundamentally, however, according to Rabbeinu Tam's explanation of waiver, the seller can waive only the debtor's personal obligation, which remained with him. The property lien itself was sold to the purchaser. Therefore releasing a particular parcel, without waiving the personal obligation, should be ineffective. Ritva, however, cites Raah that if the seller grants the debtor an extension of time, waives a guarantor, or releases a specific parcel from lien, the purchaser can claim the loss thereby caused. This implies that such partial waiver is legally effective. One must say that Raah follows Rambam, who explains the power of waiver by saying that sale of debt instruments is rabbinic. According to that approach the seller can still affect both personal and property liens. According to Rabbeinu Tam, whom we follow, only the personal obligation remains with the seller and the property lien itself cannot be waived independently. This may explain why the Shulchan Aruch mentions reimbursement for extending the time but not for releasing one parcel. Rosh's responsum, though Rosh usually follows Rabbeinu Tam, may have been written according to Rif and Rambam for the sake of that particular analysis. There is also a distinction in tort liability. Complete waiver destroys the debt by speech and is direct causation. Releasing one parcel by speech only removes one layer of security. Even physically damaging encumbered land is itself only causative damage. A verbal release of that security is therefore a cause of a cause and may be exempt. This also helps explain the sugya of a found receipt in Bava Metzia. If the wife's admission merely releases the specifically designated ketubah land, that partial release can provide a migo without creating full damages liability to the purchaser. The exact result depends upon which theory of sold debt waiver is followed.
