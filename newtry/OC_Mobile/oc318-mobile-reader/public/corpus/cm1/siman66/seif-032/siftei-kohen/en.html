He must pay him even the interest, etc. The Rama's ruling requires study. Although the Beit Yosef, section 22, cites a responsum of the Rashba, and Rabbeinu Yerucham likewise cites the Rashba, that the person who forgave must pay the purchaser the interest as well, the Rashba himself expressed doubt in another responsum whether accrued interest can be sold at all. That responsum appears among the Ramban's responsa, number 223, and the Beit Yosef cites it briefly at the beginning of this siman. If the interest was never validly sold, then even though the creditor could have collected it, he is not liable to compensate the purchaser for it, because prevention of expected profit is not considered compensable damage. The Rashba there plainly addresses interest that had already accrued, and he derives his doubt from the Gemara in Bava Batra, which speaks of accrued interest. Indeed, the Rashba's proof is sound. Bava Batra 124b indicates that interest, until actually collected, is not yet the creditor's property. Therefore, even the Sages who hold that a firstborn receives a double portion in an outstanding loan agree that he does not receive a double portion of accrued interest. We must therefore explain that the Rashba and Rabbeinu Yerucham, who require payment of the interest, refer to a case where the accrued interest was capitalized and entered into a new debt document. It is then treated as collected, as is evident from Bava Metzia 72a and as explained in Yoreh Deah, siman 171. The Rama's broad language, however, that he must pay even the interest that accrued, does not imply this limitation. His words therefore require study. See responsum Maharam Mintz, number 66.

But many disagree, etc. The Levush omitted the many dissenting authorities and recorded only the Mechaber's ruling. In my view, the Mechaber's ruling is the principal halachah in the Gemara, and it is also the position of most authorities, as I shall explain. The proofs of the dissenters can be answered as I wrote above in seif 11, seif katan 34 and 35, and seif 15, seif katan 52 and 53. See also the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, the Ritva and Ran in Ketubot, and the other authorities. Rashi in Ketubot states expressly and more clearly that the person who forgave must pay everything written in the document, even if the document stated one thousand and he sold it for one hundred. The Beit Yosef says that the Ran follows Rashi, and the Ran indeed explains the rule even more explicitly. The Ritva rules likewise, as does the Maggid Mishneh. This also appears to be the view of the Nimukei Yosef, who reports that Rabbeinu Chananel, Ramban, and Rashba agree. The Baal HaIttur, under the entry Forgiveness, cites Rashi's position as his conclusion and rules accordingly. Maharam Recanati, ruling 538, writes that Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam agree with Rashi. Maharam of Rothenburg likewise rules so in responsum 128. The Rashbam in Bava Batra 147b is generally understood as disagreeing, and the Baal HaTerumot also cites him that way. Yet this is not compelled by his words. He says only that the seller must return the purchaser's money, and this may refer to a case where the purchaser paid the document's full value. Tosafot there likewise show that the Rashbam's wording does not necessarily establish the narrower view. The language of the Gemara, that the purchaser is awarded the value of a sound document, supports Rashi. There is also a decisive conceptual proof. According to the opinion that does not impose liability for indirectly caused damage, the seller must at least return the purchase price. Otherwise he would retain another's money without justification, and every seller could collude with the debtor and forgive a debt document after selling it. Thus, even without the law of directly caused damage, restitution of the price is required. Once we rule that directly caused damage is compensable, liability must extend to the full amount written in the document. Raavan writes this expressly: even the opinion that does not adjudicate directly caused damage requires return of the purchase price. The Gemara's statement that such an authority awards only the paper's value concerns the excess of the debt over the price paid. The authority who adjudicates directly caused damage, however, requires payment of the entire debt recorded in the document. The same follows from the explanation cited by the Rif and by the Ran in the name of Rabbeinu Chananel. They explain that the seller can forgive because he may say, 'I reviewed the accounts and found that the debtor owed me nothing,' or, 'The debt had already been paid.' In such a case he plainly must return the purchase money even according to the view that does not impose liability for indirectly caused damage. Therefore, according to our ruling that such damage is compensable, he must pay the full debt. Otherwise Amemar's distinction would have no effect, since the seller would already have to return the purchase price under either view. Although the Rif and Ran disagree with Rabbeinu Chananel's explanation of why forgiveness is effective, they do not disagree with this consequence. Rav Hai Gaon's Sefer HaMekach VeHaMimkar also supports this. He first rules that one who sells a debt document and then forgives it has effected forgiveness but must return the money received, and that the same applies where an heir forgives. He then states that if the document was written in the purchaser's own name, forgiveness is impossible, and cites Amemar's ruling concerning the value of a sound document. It follows that the duty to return the purchase price exists even before invoking Amemar, while Amemar adds liability for the full amount written in the document. Although the Rosh in his halachic rulings wrote that Tosafot's narrower view appears reasonable, he did not rule so in his responsa, as the Beit Yosef records. The Shiltei HaGibborim, citing Riaz, rules explicitly that a seller or donor who forgives the debt, admits that it was paid, or refuses to take an oath required for collection, must pay the purchaser or recipient the entire value of the document. The document was transferred with the presumption that it was unpaid, and the transferor now causes the loss through his own act or assertion. If witnesses establish that the document was already paid when sold, the sale was mistaken. The seller then returns only the purchase price, and if it was a gift, the gift is void but the donor owes nothing. This is the primary halachah.
