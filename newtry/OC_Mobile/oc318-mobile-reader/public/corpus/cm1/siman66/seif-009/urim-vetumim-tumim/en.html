Through chalipin, and so forth. Shach also challenges Terumat HaDeshen here. He understands Rosh as being uncertain whether chalipin can stand in place of delivery, and concludes that Rosh ultimately holds that a kinyan sudar does not work for debt documents. Therefore, Shach says, even a bearer note written simply "to whoever produces it" cannot be transferred through chalipin. I have already explained at the beginning of this siman that once this special bearer document is removed from the ordinary category of debt-note transfer, which normally requires writing and delivery, we cannot invent a new hybrid rule for it. Either it remains within the general laws of debt documents, in which case written transfer should also be required, or its special language removes it from that category, in which case we should apply the ordinary acquisition rules of movables unless Chazal said otherwise. Where is there any source for a new intermediate form saying: writing is unnecessary, but physical delivery alone is indispensable? This is especially so according to what I explained above. The borrower's property lien is already created in favor of whoever produces the note. The transferee does not need a new acquisition to obtain the lien itself. He needs only to acquire the physical paper. Chalipin can accomplish that just like other modes of acquiring movables. There is therefore no basis to demand actual hand-to-hand delivery. And even the general rule that chalipin does not substitute for delivery in ordinary debt notes is heavily disputed, as explained above in seif 4. We should not extend that stringency to a bearer note whose very structure is different. Responsa Rosh, klal 85, siman 2, states explicitly that according to Rashbam, who holds that documents are acquired by delivery alone, a kinyan sudar also works. This proves the principle: where the acquisition requirement is reduced to mere delivery, chalipin can stand in its place. The matter is therefore clear, and Shach's alarm here is unnecessary.
