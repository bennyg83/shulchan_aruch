(Netivot HaMishpat, seif katan 23) It is as though he waived the lien, and the lien falls away automatically. Note: Tosafot establish only that the Sages uprooted the lien of an oral loan. With a written loan, since the document creates publicity and purchasers suffer no hidden loss, the Torah lien remains in force. This is clear from Tosafot, Gittin 20b, passage beginning "and he writes," and from the novellae of the Ramban, Rashba, and Ran there. The Rashbam in Bava Batra 172a, passage beginning "ruled," requires study, for he says that the lien arises from the loan while the document is only evidence. According to authorities who hold that an earlier oral loan takes priority over a later written loan with respect to unencumbered property, the Torah lien likewise remains on unencumbered property even for an oral loan. Tosafot in Bava Metzia, passage beginning "we do not administer an oath," also imply that a Torah lien remains on unencumbered property, which is why they explain Rabbi Yochanan's case of denying money supported by witnesses as one where the debtor has no unencumbered property. It therefore appears that even if the Sages uprooted the lien as against purchasers, that applies only to an oral loan. Where a document creates publicity, the Torah lien remains by virtue of the loan itself, not by virtue of the document. According to those who hold that even if liens are Torah law they apply only to property owned by the borrower at the time of the loan and not to after-acquired property, a loan document may itself function as a Torah conveyancing instrument. For example, if the loan was in Nisan, the borrower bought land in Iyar, and in Sivan gave the lender a debt document, the newly acquired land could become encumbered through the document from the time it was written.
