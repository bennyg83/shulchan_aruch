Even hand to hand delivery is not required. It appears that the seller must nevertheless remove the proofs from his own possession. Once he does so, the acquisition is effective for the reason Ramban gives: when the seller relinquishes control of the evidentiary documents, he demonstrates final intent to transfer them. What is unnecessary is only actual hand to hand delivery. Other modes of acquisition, however, do not work while the seller continues to retain the evidence. Exchange itself is ineffective. There is no reason that exchange should be inferior to other modes, except that the authorities treat acquisition together with land as uniquely powerful, as the Gemara indicates. Tumim questions why exchange should be weaker than other forms of acquisition, and according to what I have written the distinction is understood. The Shach also seems to imply that even a document payable to whoever produces it requires actual delivery and cannot be acquired by other modes. I do not agree. Terumat HaDeshen, cited by the Shach in seif katan 10, indicates that such a document is comparable to ordinary movables.
One who acquires an ownerless debt instrument. See Shach, seif katan 25, who asks that in any event there is no written assignment, and further that the debtor should have the first right to acquire it, as with the property of a convert who dies in siman 72, seif 37. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The language of abandonment cannot operate on the debt itself, because just as one cannot consecrate a debt that is outside one's possession, one cannot render it ownerless. Consecration and abandonment are equivalent in this respect. Even as against the debtor, what is effective is only express forgiveness of the personal obligation. The physical debt instrument is different. Tosafot in Ketubot, s.v. HaMokher, write that the paper itself can be sold, and the purchaser can resell it to the debtor. As I explained above in seif katan 1, he can likewise sell it to the creditor. Just as one can sell this possessory interest by Torah law through delivery alone, one can also abandon the proprietary right he has in the document, so that the finder will be entitled to hold it until the debt is paid or to sell it to either creditor or debtor. Indeed, even despair can transfer this limited proprietary interest, as Tosafot in Bava Batra 76 indicate. The Mechaber, however, holds that even this limited acquisition requires another person intentionally transferring it. Therefore abandonment and despair do not suffice according to the Mechaber. Tosafot necessarily disagree and hold that this type of proprietary right can be acquired by simple physical acquisition, since they write that despair is effective. Tumim also cites those Tosafot to answer the Shach's question, though by a more circuitous route.
