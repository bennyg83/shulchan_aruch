It requires lifting. Tosafot and Rosh hold that debt instruments require an ordinary form of physical acquisition such as lifting or meshichah in addition to the written assignment. Ramban disagrees. He writes that ordinary delivery is itself sufficient for debt instruments because neither meshichah nor lifting meaningfully moves the evidentiary right embodied in the paper. The Sages therefore instituted acquisition when the creditor removes the evidence from his own control and delivers it to the purchaser. This raises a question whether, according to Ramban, delivery of a document can acquire in a side alley. Ordinary delivery works only in a public domain or in a courtyard belonging to neither party, because in a side alley meshichah is available and is superior. According to Tosafot's reason, the limitation is that where meshichah can be used, mere delivery is insufficient. Here, however, Ramban holds that meshichah has no function for the legal right embodied in a document. It would therefore follow that delivery should work even in a side alley. According to Rabbeinu Chaim Kohen, however, the reason delivery does not work in a side alley is that the side alley becomes the temporary domain of the first possessor and resembles the seller's domain. On that rationale even a debt instrument cannot be acquired there by mere delivery. Ramban himself indicates that documents are not governed by ordinary distinctions of physical domain because their legal content is not moved by ordinary meshichah. Therefore his position most naturally supports delivery in any place where the seller intentionally places the document into the purchaser's control.
Therefore the one who acquires it. Maharam Lublin asks why the acquirer of an ownerless debt instrument does not fail simply because no written assignment was made. He answers that the requirement of writing operates from the seller's side: while the creditor retains some right, a written transfer is needed to show that he fully transferred it. One who makes the instrument ownerless has already emptied himself of all rights. The Shach objects that if a seller saying, "Acquire it and all its liens," still needs writing, why should ownerless designation be stronger? The answer is that it is indeed stronger in one respect. A gift or sale to a particular person does not itself remove the property from the transferor until the recipient acquires. A declaration of ownerless property immediately removes the property from the owner and cannot ordinarily be retracted. Therefore ownerless designation can serve the function ordinarily supplied by writing from the transferor's side. A remaining difficulty is how the finder acquires the lien itself. Although the owner has abandoned it, the recipient ordinarily needs some act of acquisition. He can acquire the paper, but what acquires the lien embodied in it? Maharam Lublin therefore says that writing is needed only to effect the transferor's removal of rights. Once the lien itself has become ownerless, the recipient's acquisition of the paper can carry the lien with it. In sale, by contrast, the seller's rights do not leave him until writing and delivery complete the transaction.
An ownerless document. The Shach, following his earlier conclusion that documents can be acquired by meshichah or lifting, writes that a finder of an ownerless debt instrument acquires it through those acts. If he merely takes hold of it without the seller's intentional delivery, he does not acquire through the special law of delivery, because delivery requires another person intentionally transferring. I have already written that even according to Ramban the Shach's conclusion does not follow. Ramban's reason that delivery works is precisely that meshichah and lifting have no true function for the evidentiary right embodied in the paper. If so, where there is no other person's intent, ordinary meshichah and lifting likewise cannot create the right. The Gemara concerning documents of a deceased convert strongly supports this. It asks whether one who takes possession of the convert's documents acquires the land or the document. The case necessarily involves physical taking of the paper. Yet acquisition of the underlying right remains doubtful, showing that physical taking alone is not enough. Therefore ownerless debt instruments and documents of a deceased convert present a special problem. Without another person's intentional transfer, the ordinary acquisition of the paper does not automatically acquire the underlying lien.
His fellow made the debt instrument ownerless. The Shach asks that once the creditor abandons the debt, the debtor himself should acquire the released obligation. He therefore limits the case to a debt owed by a gentile. Even there, however, the same basic problem exists: what exactly does the finder acquire? In my view the earlier analysis resolves it. Abandonment can function in place of the written transfer by removing the creditor's rights. Yet the property lien remains bound to the paper and is not acquired by the debtor unless he also comes into possession of the document. During the first three days after an ownerless declaration, so long as no one has yet acquired it, rabbinic law treats the abandonment as not fully effective for some purposes and the original owner can still retract. If another person acquires the paper during that period, he can acquire the lien with it. After three days, when the ownerless status is fully established, the debtor may acquire the released lien upon his own property because the debt relationship has become ownerless. The Shach's assumption that the debtor automatically acquires immediately overlooks the role of the paper as the vehicle through which the lien leaves and is taken. The exact details depend upon the laws of abandonment during the first three days.
Because there is no other person's intent to transfer. Semag uses the case of a deceased convert rather than an ownerless document, perhaps because he follows Rashi and Rav Yehudai Gaon that deliberate ownerless designation can itself count as another person's intentional transfer. Nimukei Yosef understands their view this way. Tosafot disagrees and treats ownerless property as lacking such intent. According to Rashi, therefore, the ownerless case is not a clear example of the defect, while the property of a deceased convert certainly lacks any transferor. If so, where one acquires an ownerless debt instrument and refuses to return it, there is substantial reason not to remove it from him, because Rashi and the Gaon may regard the original owner's abandonment as sufficient transferor intent. Still, Semag's use of the convert case creates a difficulty. According to Tosafot in Bava Batra, the Sages did not institute transfer of debt instruments for consecration because the need was less pressing than in commercial sale. If even consecration does not receive the special enactment, why should ordinary ownerless acquisition receive it? Perhaps Tosafot's statement is limited to the special commercial mode of transferring liens. A deliberate ownerless declaration has its own removal mechanism and can be stronger than consecration in this context, especially according to Rashi's model of intentional transfer.
Transfers. Even in our bearer notes, which do not require a written assignment, delivery is still required. Therefore one who merely finds a bearer note that was made ownerless does not acquire the debt without another person's intentional transfer.
