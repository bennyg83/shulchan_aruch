This delivery, etc. Ramban rejects the view that ‘delivery’ means delivery accompanied by pulling. Delivery and pulling are distinct modes elsewhere in the Gemara. With debt documents, however, delivery, pulling, and lifting are equivalent because the paper itself is not the true subject of sale and the evidentiary lien is neither pulled nor lifted. The Sages therefore ordained acquisition once the creditor removes the evidence from his own control and definitively transfers it. This is the primary view. The Gemara’s contrast between a ship acquired by pulling and documents by delivery also indicates simple delivery. Tosafot and the Rosh object that ordinary movables other than ships and animals are not acquired by delivery, but documents are different precisely because the lien cannot physically be pulled or lifted. The Mishnah in Kiddushin mentions pulling as the common acquisition of movables and does not exclude delivery or lifting. Nor does the requirement of writing prove that a more significant physical act is needed. Writing is necessary because possession of the paper does not itself prove acquisition of the lien; the seller must additionally write, ‘acquire it and all its liens.’ Baal HaTerumot, Rambam, Mordechai, Semag, and Nimukei Yosef support Ramban’s position. Ordinary delivery at the seller’s direction is therefore sufficient, and pulling or lifting certainly suffices.

Therefore, one who takes possession of an ownerless document, etc. The stated reason is that no other person conveys it. One might ask that acquisition should already fail for lack of writing. Maharam of Lublin answers that writing is needed only to remove the seller’s retained right, whereas abandonment removes all such right. This is unpersuasive, for even an express oral formula transferring the document and all its liens does not replace writing. A further difficulty is that when the creditor abandons the document, the debtor immediately acquires the release of his own obligation, as the Gemara teaches concerning the document of a convert who dies. The case must therefore involve a bearer document obligating the debtor to whoever produces it. Regarding the convert’s document, it may concern a debt owed by a non-Jew and likewise drafted to whoever produces it. In that setting, absent the requirement of another person’s conveyance, the finder who pulls the document would acquire it and no other Jew could collect the debt. According to the conclusion above that debt documents may be acquired by delivery, pulling, or lifting, one who pulls such an abandoned bearer document against a non-Jew acquires it. Merely holding it without pulling does not suffice, because delivery works only at the transferor’s direction.
