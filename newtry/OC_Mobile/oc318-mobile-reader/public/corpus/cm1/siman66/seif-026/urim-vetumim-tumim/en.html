Even if the document was held by a third party, etc. The Shach asks that according to authorities who apply the rule "one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear must pay" even where the defendant could not have known, the borrower should be liable. The third party is one witness that the transferee had a written assignment. Since the borrower cannot contradict that witness, he should pay. The Shach answers that the third party may be a relative or otherwise disqualified. This is unnecessary. First, the claim concerns a document and therefore a lien on land, for which there is no Torah oath and hence no rule of payment for inability to swear. Second, where the person was genuinely not expected to know, the ordinary rule of payment for inability to swear does not apply in the manner assumed. More fundamentally, the entire premise is mistaken. The borrower's position is simply: "I have no concern with your dispute. I will place the money in beit din and they may give it to whomever is legally entitled." Why should he be required to swear concerning whether the creditor sold the document? He has no personal knowledge or legal relationship to that dispute. Consider Reuven who deposited an object with Shimon. Levi appears with one witness and says that Reuven sold the deposit to him. Is Shimon required to hand it to Levi merely because he cannot contradict the witness? Certainly not. That would amount to extracting property through one witness. Shimon simply says: "I am not responsible to believe your assertion. I received the object from Reuven and will return it only according to law." There is no oath obligation upon him concerning a transaction between Reuven and Levi. The same is true here. The real difficulty is different. Why does the Rama follow the Nimukei Yosef in believing the third party against the creditor? A third party has no more credibility than he would have had if he had already delivered the document to the person he claims is entitled to it, as explained above in siman 56. If he had delivered it to the alleged purchaser, that purchaser would not be believed against the creditor without proving the written assignment, according to the view we follow. Why should the third party's present statement have greater force? Nimukei Yosef follows his own position above in siman 56 that a third party can possess greater credibility than the litigant himself. An unauthenticated receipt in the borrower's hand is worthless, but if held by a third party whom the creditor admits he trusted, the third party can be believed. According to our ruling there, however, which rejects that special credibility, the present rule appears difficult. Perhaps the Rama follows his own responsum, siman 86. There he cites Rambam and those who hold that against the seller the purchaser is believed to say, "I had a written assignment and lost it," and concludes that even according to the many authorities who disagree, in his case the claimant is believed because one witness supports him. The wording indicates that the supporting witness helps against the creditor, together with the claimant's possession or chazakah, but not against the borrower, because one witness alone cannot extract money. That fits our case. The third party functions as one supporting witness against the creditor, and this is enough to establish the matter as between them. Against the borrower, however, that one witness cannot compel payment. This may supply the Rama with support, though one should still investigate whether other authorities expressly agree.
