I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you. If the purchaser notified the debtor that the holder sold him the document, some authorities, especially Raavad, hold that the original creditor can no longer forgive it because the debtor originally obligated himself to every successor of the creditor. The purchaser is then treated as coming through the original creditor. Other authorities dispute this. Even according to Raavad, the purchaser must first acquire the document from the seller by the legally required writing and delivery; the clause does not itself transfer ownership from the seller.
He can no longer forgive it. The Sema cites the Ran as treating this as the principal view, and notes support from later authorities. The clause makes the purchaser resemble the creditor himself as against the debtor. Nevertheless, it does not dispense with a valid acquisition from the seller. Once the purchaser has properly acquired the document and the debtor’s original undertaking extends to successors, the seller’s later waiver is ineffective according to this view.
Some say that even so he may forgive it. The Sema infers that these authorities nonetheless allow acquisition by mere delivery because of the clause. That inference is incorrect. Even Raavad, who denies the power of waiver, still requires the purchaser to acquire the document through writing and delivery, or another valid mode. The words ‘to anyone who comes through you’ concern the debtor’s obligation, not the transfer of ownership from seller to purchaser. Until a valid transfer occurs, the document remains the seller’s and he may forgive it. Rashba, Ramban, Ran, and Baal HaTerumot support this distinction. The clause may remove the need for a separate authorization to litigate against the debtor, but it does not remove the requirement that the seller write the transfer formula and deliver the document.
But if he owed him money and had no means to pay, etc. This applies specifically where the seller already owed the purchaser an independent debt and lacked other assets. Then the debtor’s obligation may be subjected directly to the purchaser under Rabbi Natan’s rule, even without the sale, and the seller cannot forgive it. If the seller did not previously owe the purchaser, then even if he lacks assets to compensate for the loss caused by forgiveness, he may still forgive the sold document. The Beit Yosef’s suggestion that inability to pay alone removes the power of waiver is not supported by the principal authorities. The Gemara concerning a woman who may forgive her ketubah despite lacking assets, and Tosafot, Ran, Ritva, Ramban, Raavad, and Baal HaTerumot, indicate that insolvency by itself does not prevent waiver. The difficult language of certain Tosafot does not justify a contrary general rule.
He cannot forgive it. This is the primary law, contrary to the dissenting opinion, where the underlying debt is already subjected to the purchaser under Rabbi Natan’s rule. Rama himself rules similarly in siman 86. This applies while the seller still independently owes the purchaser. If he gave the debt document in payment of that obligation, however, the original debt may be considered discharged, and the seller may then retain the power to forgive the transferred document, as explained at length in siman 86.
