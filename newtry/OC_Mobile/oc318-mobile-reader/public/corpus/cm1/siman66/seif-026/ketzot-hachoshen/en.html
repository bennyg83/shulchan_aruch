And to anyone who comes through you. This is Raavad's view in Hilchot Mechirah 6. Rosh disagrees. He explains that even according to those who hold that in a three party transfer the original creditor can no longer waive, a sale of a debt instrument is different. The purchaser does not acquire the debtor's personal obligation by virtue of the creditor's transfer. The creditor therefore retains the personal lien, while the purchaser acquires only the property lien. Even if the debtor wrote, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," we should not assume that he intended to subject his person to two independent creditors. Rather, the phrase refers to the property lien. Raavad, however, follows his own explanation of why a sold debt can later be waived. He writes that the reason is not that sale of debt instruments is rabbinic, but that the debtor may say to the purchaser, "I never obligated myself to you." Accordingly, if the debtor expressly wrote, "I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," he can no longer say that. From the creditor's side the sale is fully effective, and from the debtor's side his own undertaking extends to the later holder. See what I wrote above in seif katan 25. Ran, in the first chapter of Gittin, cites Ramban that even such language does not prevent waiver because on the Torah level it cannot create a present obligation to an unidentified future person, since there is no bererah. Ran rejects that objection. Bererah is a problem where one possibility excludes the other. Here the debtor can be obligated successively to everyone who comes through the original creditor. There is therefore no true bererah problem.
And he has no means with which to pay. Beit Yosef infers from a responsum of Rosh that if one sells a debt instrument and afterward wishes to waive it, but has no property from which to reimburse the purchaser, he cannot waive. At first this is astonishing. Why should the debtor lose the benefit of the creditor's power of waiver merely because the seller cannot reimburse the purchaser? It appears that Rosh's reasoning is Rabbi Natan's lien. At the moment the seller waives, he becomes liable to the purchaser under the law of direct causation. If he has no assets, the debtor's obligation to the seller becomes subject to Rabbi Natan's lien in favor of the purchaser at that very moment. Although before waiver the seller was not yet liable to the purchaser, the waiver and the Rabbi Natan lien arise simultaneously. Once that lien attaches, the seller can no longer waive the debt. This resembles cases where two legal consequences arise together even though one could not follow the other sequentially. This also explains the Gemara's statement that a woman will certainly waive her ketubah to her husband even though she lacks assets to reimburse the purchaser. A ketubah is different because Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply to a right that may never mature into collection, as Ran explains in Ketubot. Therefore the waiver can still take effect there.
It is not acquired incidentally with land. I was uncertain regarding an iska, half of which is loan and half deposit. The deposit half certainly transfers incidentally with land like any other deposit. What of the loan half? Perhaps it resembles every other debt and can be transferred only by writing and delivery. Or perhaps, because the recipient may not spend the principal freely and because on his death it does not become ordinary movables in the hands of his heirs, the loan half is also sufficiently like a deposit to transfer incidentally with land. I found Nimukei Yosef at the end of the first chapter of Bava Kamma discussing Rav Papa, who was owed thirteen thousand zuz by the people of Bei Chozaei and transferred the right to Rav Shmuel bar Acha incidentally with land. Raah asks that if this were an ordinary oral loan, it could not be transferred in that manner. He answers in the name of his teacher that the money was an iska for profit sharing. Since it was not given for unrestricted expenditure, it has the law of a deposit and can be transferred incidentally with land. It follows that even the loan half of an iska is transferred that way. It cannot, however, be transferred through exchange because money is not acquired through exchange. Once the body of the iska is acquired incidentally with land, waiver no longer helps, just as waiver does not defeat a deposit whose body was transferred. Nevertheless, the loan half can also be transferred by writing and delivery, because unlike a pure deposit it carries a personal obligation for unavoidable loss and therefore has a true lien that can be transferred with the formula, "it and all its liens." The restriction against spending the funds makes it resemble collateral in this respect. Therefore the loan half of an iska has two possible modes: acquisition of the body incidentally with land, in which case waiver no longer helps, or transfer of the debt by writing and delivery, in which case waiver can still be effective.
