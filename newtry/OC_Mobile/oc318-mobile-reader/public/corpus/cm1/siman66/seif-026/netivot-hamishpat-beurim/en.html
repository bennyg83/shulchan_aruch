And to anyone who comes by virtue of you. See Shach, seif katan 86, who writes that the borrower must specifically have consented. Tumim objects that if the borrower consented, even Rosh agrees, as is evident from Rosh's question against Raavad, the author of this opinion. Raavad proves his position from maamad sheloshtan, and Rosh distinguishes that maamad sheloshtan specifically requires the debtor's consent. This indicates that where there is consent, Rosh agrees. The same point is also evident from Rosh's question concerning a found receipt in Bava Metzia 20, where the Gemara answers by invoking the rule of Shmuel. Even then, one must still be concerned that the document may have contained a written clause binding the debtor “to anyone who comes by virtue of you.” If Shach were correct that the debtor's consent is required, there would be no question. If the purchaser cannot produce witnesses that the debtor consented, the original creditor's waiver would be effective. If the purchaser can produce such witnesses, then the receipt would not help him, because the debtor's acknowledgement at the time of sale is itself equivalent to consent, as Tosafot explain. Rosh himself gives this answer regarding the question from maamad sheloshtan. In my humble view, however, Shach's position is that the two matters depend upon one another. If maamad sheloshtan takes effect even against the debtor's will, then the phrase “to anyone who comes by virtue of you” likewise means even without the debtor's later consent. If, however, maamad sheloshtan requires the debtor's consent, then this clause also requires consent. Since Shach proves in siman 126 that Rashba, in Chiddushei HaRashba to the third chapter of Gittin, holds that maamad sheloshtan requires the debtor's consent, Shach accordingly counts Rashba here among the authorities who require consent. Rosh's question against Raavad from Bava Metzia is consistent with Rosh's own view, which agrees with Raavad that maamad sheloshtan is effective even if the debtor did not consent. That is why Rosh could challenge Raavad. The Mechaber likewise states the rule here without qualification because in siman 126 he rules that maamad sheloshtan is effective even without the debtor's consent.
But if he owed him. See Shach, who questions the words of Tosafot in the chapter Mi Shemet. In my humble view, Tosafot should be understood as follows. Their words should be read: “This is difficult according to the one who imposes liability for direct causative damage. How can she enable the husband to collect unlawfully? If she waives the claim in favor of her husband, the purchasers will return against him and collect from him the entire amount.” Their meaning is that how can the receipt be returned to the husband merely on the basis of her admission, when doing so creates a defect in the purchasers' position? If the receipt is not returned and she later waives the debt, or if she writes a new receipt after the date of sale, the woman will have to return all the money as one who caused damage. But if the earlier receipt is returned, bearing a date before the sale, we will say that a document is presumed to have been delivered on the day it was written, and the woman will then have to return only the money she received. Returning the receipt will therefore cause loss to the purchasers with respect to their ability to collect the additional amount from the woman. From this Tosafot prove that even when she waives the debt after the sale, she returns only the money she received, so returning the receipt causes no additional defect or loss. Tosafot then ask that the husband will nevertheless collect from the purchasers unlawfully, because they will at least return against him for the amount they paid her. Their intention is that Tosafot hold that when she has no money to pay, she cannot waive the claim against her husband to the extent of the money the purchasers gave her, although she may waive the excess, as Tosafot conclude: “For she cannot waive that which she caused them to lose.” Tumim likewise writes explicitly that, according to Tosafot, she can waive only the amount beyond the purchase money, but not the purchase money itself. Accordingly, by returning the receipt the husband may be enabled to collect unlawfully if she later has no assets. Without the receipt, if she waived the debt, the husband would himself have to return the purchase money. With the receipt returned, the purchasers can proceed only against the woman. Tosafot answer that one need not be concerned about a small amount. Since she is presently married, the concern is only hypothetical, namely, perhaps after divorce the woman will lack money while the husband has it. The amount they paid her is small, and it is uncommon that after divorce she would lack even that small amount. Therefore the Sages did not refrain from returning the receipt, since in any event the purchasers retain a claim against her. Tosafot then return and ask from the case of a divorced woman who sells her ketubah for a substantial sum and has no assets while her former husband does. In that case, returning the receipt could indeed cause the purchasers a loss. As for the underlying distinction, why she may waive the excess but cannot waive the amount she received, one might initially explain as follows. A purchaser who buys a promissory note does so intending to obtain both the personal obligation and the lien upon property, as is evident from the fact that the purchaser may sue the borrower. The problem is only that the personal obligation itself is not transferred by sale, so the original creditor can retract and waive that personal obligation. This resembles one who sells in a single price both an item not yet in existence and an item already in existence. The purchaser may retract from the transaction and say, “I intended to purchase only the entire package,” as Taz writes in siman 277, seif 5. If the purchaser cancels the sale, the case is comparable to the Rama's ruling in the gloss to seif 1 that where the document was not acquired by writing and delivery and the seller lacks assets, the purchaser may recover from the borrower under the rule of Rabbi Natan. By contrast, if the purchaser chooses to uphold the sale and the seller becomes liable only because he waived the debt and thereby caused damage, the seller's liability arises only after the waiver. At that later point the borrower no longer owes the seller, and the rule of Rabbi Natan is therefore unavailable. This would explain why the seller cannot waive the portion corresponding to the money received while she may waive the excess. However, what Tosafot conclude regarding the authority who does not impose liability for causative damage remains difficult according to this explanation.
If it was transferred together with land, he cannot waive it. The commentators ask that if so, the question raised in seif katan 69 should have been answered simply: let her sell her ketubah together with land, in which case she would no longer be able to waive it. Tumim answers well that when she sells the ketubah in a manner that leaves her with no power of waiver and no remaining right whatsoever in the ketubah, it is as though the ketubah had been written in another person's name, and the husband may not continue living with her in such a state, as Tosafot explain there. Regarding a business document, Maharit, part 2, siman 101, writes that the deposit component is not acquired through writing and delivery. According to this, it appears that the deposit component is acquired together with land like any other deposit, while the loan component is acquired through writing and delivery, as Maharit writes there. Nevertheless, even the loan component can be transferred together with land, as Nimukei Yosef writes at the end of the first chapter of Bava Kamma, because money that may not be used for one's own purposes is treated as a deposit. Still, since the loan component includes a personal obligation, it may also be transferred through writing and delivery. Therefore, if the entire business document is transferred together with land, its transfer is based on the law governing a deposit and the original creditor cannot waive it. If it is transferred through writing and delivery, the deposit itself is not acquired through that method, but the loan component is acquired by virtue of its personal debt obligation and therefore remains subject to waiver. Ketzot HaChoshen writes similarly.
