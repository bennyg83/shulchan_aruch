To pay. This means the full value of the document, as the Maggid Mishneh writes. The Shach explains that this does not contradict the Tur and Mechaber's ruling in seif 34 that if the borrower produces a receipt showing that the note was already paid, the sale is a mistaken transaction and the purchase money is returned. There the receipt independently proves, even without the seller's admission, that he sold a paid document. The sale is therefore void and only the purchase price is returned. Here, by contrast, the matter is established only through Levi's own admission. Had Levi wished to swear, Reuven could have collected despite Shimon. Levi's present admission therefore prevents Reuven from collecting and is equivalent to a present waiver that causes him loss. He must therefore pay the entire value of the note. Thus the Shach. The Sema writes that this applies only if Reuven has proof that the document was sold to him by writing and delivery. If he has no such proof, Reuven is not believed against Levi when seeking to extract money from him.

A third party. The reason is that the third party is no better than the purchaser. If the document were in the purchaser's own hands, he would not be believed without proof of writing and delivery. Thus the Sema. The Shach asks why the borrower should not be considered one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear, since he contradicts the third party, who is a witness. According to the authorities who say that this rule does not apply where the defendant could not have known the fact, one may answer that here too the borrower could not have known whether the note was sold. According to the other authorities, however, the difficulty remains. It therefore appears that the third party here is a relative or otherwise disqualified as a witness. Indeed, the Nimukei Yosef, the source of this law, speaks of a woman serving as the third party. Thus the Shach.
