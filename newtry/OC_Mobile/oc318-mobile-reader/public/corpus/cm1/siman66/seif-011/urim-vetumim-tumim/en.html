He did not acquire them, and so forth. Shach cites a responsum of Ritva holding that when one says, "I give you my property," the word "property" includes his debt rights as well. It is therefore as though he had said, "Acquire the note and all its liens." Perhaps Ritva follows his own view that sale of debt documents is of Torah force. On that understanding, the lender has a genuine proprietary interest in the borrower's lien and assets that can be treated as part of "my property." But according to our halachah that sale of ordinary debt documents is not of Torah force, there is no tangible asset in the lender's possession upon which an ordinary proprietary transfer can operate. A creditor collects only prospectively. The debt lien is therefore not naturally included in the word "my property." This issue appears to depend on the dispute below in siman 253 concerning a deathbed person who says, "My documents to so-and-so." Must he also say, "Acquire them and all the liens contained in them"? If one requires the additional language, Ritva's position seems almost compelled by the Gemara's statement that when a deathbed person says, "My property to so-and-so," his debt documents are included. If "property" did not include the debt lien, what would the Gemara gain by saying the papers themselves are included? The beneficiary still would not acquire the debts. One could say that the deathbed person explicitly said "the documents and all their liens," but then there would be no need for the Gemara's proof that documents are included in "property"; his express words already identify them. Thus, under the view requiring the special phrase when documents are given specifically, the broader word "my property" must itself include the liens. This is likely Ritva's proof. Rashba, however, complicates matters. He holds that when a deathbed person specifically gives his documents, he must say "them and all their liens," yet elsewhere he appears to hold that a general gift of "my property" does not automatically include the debt liens. One possible answer is that the Gemara's proof that documents are included in "property" is needed only for special bearer notes that require no separate phrase "and all their liens." If the note says "to whoever produces it," the borrower's lien already follows the paper. Then the only question is whether the physical note is included in the word "property." The Gemara teaches that it is. This also supports what I wrote above that bearer notes require no additional transfer formula. Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaSefinah suggests another distinction within deathbed gifts themselves: if the dying person says specifically, "my documents," he must add the lien language, but if he gives "all my property," Chazal treated the broader gift more leniently. That is difficult and lacks a clear source. Perhaps Rashba instead regards the meaning of "property" as context dependent. In an ordinary healthy person's transaction, where precise drafting is expected, if he intended debt liens he should have said so. A deathbed declaration is normally brief and informal, and we therefore interpret "my property" broadly enough to include the liens. This too is somewhat strained. At any rate, outside the special deathbed context there is no clear proof that the general word "property" transfers ordinary debt liens. Especially under the accepted view that sale of documents is rabbinic, we should not assume such an unusual proprietary meaning without express language. Therefore Ritva's broad ruling should not be extended beyond the contexts in which the authorities clearly adopted it.
