(Collected note) Even if... It should read "and if," because according to Rambam this law is clearer. Nimukei Yosef distinguishes only where the possessor asserts the claim for himself. According to Rambam he too is believed, but a third party is believed even according to the second opinion. The language "even though he is believed..." points to this. As a matter of law, however, the point requires study: if the purchaser holding the document would not be believed, why should we believe a third party where he never had a migo? See siman 56, seif 4. One cannot answer that he has a migo because he could destroy the document, because then the purchaser should have the same migo. Nimukei Yosef follows his own view at the end of Zeh Borer that the rule that a third party is believed is based on such a migo, or on the migo that he could have returned it to the borrower. Although such a migo is generally not accepted, since the third party does not seek to establish rights for himself but for the borrower, even an imperfect migo can suffice. We follow the first formulation of Rav Nachman's rule there. The same applies to writing and delivery. There the Gemara asks, "According to whom? Rabbi..." and answers, "A third party is different..." Although it is not a full migo, it is effective. Nimukei Yosef in Bava Batra therefore distinguishes between one claiming for himself and one claiming for others. But others explain that the third party's credibility comes from the fact that the parties originally trusted him, meaning that he once possessed a genuine migo. According to that approach, the Rama's words here are difficult. (End of note.)
