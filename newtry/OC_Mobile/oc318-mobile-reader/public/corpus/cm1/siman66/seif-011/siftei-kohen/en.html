He does not require witnesses for purposes of his acquisition. Many authorities disagree, and the practical halachah follows them, that witnesses are required even to establish the transfer itself, as explained below. Therefore, if the debtor pays the purchaser without the seller's knowledge, he may recover that payment from him, as will be explained below in seif 17. So too writes the Bach. However, if the debtor claims with certainty, "I know that the purchaser had a written transfer document and it was lost," he takes a heset oath and is exempt.
Or he claimed that it was transferred to him together with land. This follows the Rambam and Mechaber above in seif 10, that transfer by agav does not require writing. According to what I wrote there in seif katan 26, however, that agav substitutes only for delivery and written transfer is still required, this argument does not apply. See Responsa Maharit, siman 91.
And if there is proof that he sold it. So writes the Maggid Mishneh. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 51, end of part 4, folio 312, asks why the Maggid Mishneh requires proof. Even without proof, if the seller himself admits the sale, that should suffice, and migo has no role here, since migo is used to establish one claim because the litigant could have made another, or as a means to avoid an oath. There is no difficulty. A litigant's admission is certainly equivalent to one hundred witnesses. Further, it appears that the Maggid Mishneh deliberately says, "if there is proof," because he means that the seller must pay Reuven the full value of the document. If there is no proof of sale, then even if the seller admits that he sold it and refuses to swear, his refusal does not prove that he was paid only after the sale. Perhaps he had already been paid beforehand. If he were to say explicitly that he had been paid before the sale, in which case he would owe only the money he received for the sale, he would be believed through a migo that he could have denied ever selling it. Therefore, even now, although he refuses to swear, perhaps he refuses because he had been paid before the sale. But where there is proof of sale and he has no migo, he is not believed to say that payment preceded the sale, as I write below in seif katan 35 in the name of the Maggid Mishneh. Consequently, because he refuses to swear, we do not accept the claim that he was paid before the sale, and since the purchaser now suffers loss through his conduct, he must pay the full value of the debt document. This appears correct to me.
He must pay Reuven. This is so even though he did not admit that the debtor paid him, provided that Shimon says, "let him swear to me," as I write in the next seif katan. He must pay the full value of the debt document; returning only the purchase price he received is insufficient. This is implied by the Maggid Mishneh there and is stated explicitly by the Sema, seif katan 31. Although the Maggid Mishneh explicitly writes payment of the full value only where the seller admitted he had been paid, it clearly follows there that the same applies here. Since the seller refuses to swear, he is treated as admitting that he was paid, because the document becomes uncollectible through his conduct. Shiltei Giborim in the name of Ri'az states the same, as I cite below in seif 32, seif katan 109. This is the primary ruling.
And likewise if he admitted, etc. The Mechaber's word "likewise" is somewhat awkward. He should have written: "All the more so if Levi admitted that the debtor paid him." Nevertheless, the rule that if the seller refuses to swear he must pay Reuven applies only when Shimon says, "let him swear to me," as the Rambam and Mechaber state. If Shimon does not demand an oath, even if he says he paid the seller, the seller is not obligated to swear, as below in siman 82, seif 2, and Reuven may collect. Similarly, the later statement that the seller's heirs swear applies only when the debtor says to them, "swear to me," as below in siman 108, seif 5.
If Levi admitted that the debtor paid him, etc. Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 1, writes regarding this: it is reasonable that this applies only where the debt document does not contain a credibility clause. But if it contains a credibility clause, the purchaser collects from the debtor against his will, and the creditor's admission has no effect, according to those who rule that one is believed to say, "I had a transfer document and it was lost," because we suspect collusion. This does not appear correct to me. Once the creditor himself says that the debt was paid, what difference does a credibility clause make? He himself is declaring that the debt no longer exists, as I wrote above in siman 77, seif katan 4. Further, the reason he is believed to say that it was paid is either his migo that he could have forgiven the debt, or because his statement itself is treated as a present waiver, as I explain in the next seif katan. Both reasons apply even where the debt document contains a credibility clause. This is also implied by the Rambam, Tur, Mechaber, and other poskim, who make no such distinction. This is the primary ruling. A practical difference arises even according to those who disagree and do not believe the purchaser's claim that he had a transfer document and lost it, where the purchaser does possess a written transfer.
Levi admitted that the debtor paid him, etc. This law applies even where Reuven has witnesses to the transfer, because Levi could have forgiven the debt. Through the migo that he could have forgiven it, he is believed when he says that the debt document was paid. Some explain that when he says the debt document is paid, he is effectively forgiving it at that very moment, and there is no greater waiver than this. These are the words of the Maggid Mishneh. I also cite below in seif 16, seif katan 61, in the name of the French authorities and Rabbi Meir son of Rabbi Yehudah that a declaration of payment itself constitutes waiver. Accordingly, not only a claim of payment but any statement that nullifies the document, such as that it was an amanah document, that it was subject to an unfulfilled condition, or the like, is within the seller's power. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes the same at the end of Nesiv 14, cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman, seif 14, and again at the end of this siman, that the seller is believed to say the document was an amanah or was paid. He explains it by the migo that the seller could have forgiven the debt. According to what I have written, no migo is needed. A declaration of amanah, condition, or payment has the same effect as waiver. Admittedly, the Rosh in the second chapter of Kesubos, concerning a person who owes money to others and says a debt owed to him was paid, writes that he is not believed through a migo that he could forgive it because he does not intend to forgive and lose his own debt. Rabbeinu Yerucham, Nesiv 6, part 6, writes similarly. That does not appear consistent with my explanation, because according to them he can in fact forgive even where he owes money to others, and saying that it is paid should itself be the waiver. Nevertheless, the primary ruling appears to me as I have written. It is a strong principle: what difference is there between expressly saying, "I forgive it," and saying, "it has been paid"? According to his own statement the debt document is canceled and extinguished; there is no greater waiver. I reach the same conclusion below in siman 86, seif 5, seif katan 11. According to this, the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Metzia 20a, which says, "this proves that Shmuel's law applies," need not be explained, as some authorities do, that if the woman had intended collusion she could simply have forgiven the ketubah. Rather, the plain explanation is that when she now gives the husband a receipt and says that her ketubah was paid, this itself is the greatest possible waiver. Thus Shmuel's law applies, that one who sells a debt document and later forgives the debt has effected a valid waiver. Shmuel states the greater novelty, that even a gratuitous waiver is effective; all the more so where the seller states that the debt was paid or was merely an amanah and therefore never collectible. Nimukei Yosef there, at the end of Bava Metzia, likewise writes that the case is not based on migo at all. Since she admits that it is paid and the receipt is valid, that itself is her waiver, equivalent to saying, "it is forgiven to you." It appears there that Nimukei Yosef takes this from the Rashba and Ran. Rashi's explanation also appears this way. He writes that since we are not concerned for the possibility that she sold the ketubah and is now producing a receipt, even if that had occurred the husband would properly acquire through the receipt written for her, because Shmuel's law applies. Thus if she sold her ketubah in Iyar and later forgave it to the husband in Tishrei, the waiver is effective and the husband properly defeats the purchaser. Rashi does not mean specifically that she physically wrote a receipt. The same applies if she says the debt was paid, and the receipt merely serves as evidence that the ketubah was paid. Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, beginning of part 2, cites a responsum of the Raavad that a seller of a debt document is not believed to say it was paid through a migo that he could have forgiven it, and claims that the Bava Metzia sugya proves this, because the Mishnah speaks of finding a receipt in the marketplace. If mere speech could defeat the purchaser, why discuss a receipt? This does not appear correct to me. In addition, the Ramban, Rosh, Ran at the end of Perek Kol HaNishba'in, the other poskim, the Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, Mechaber here, Baal HaItur, Baal HaTerumos, Tur, and Mechaber below in seif 15 plainly disagree, because they explicitly rule that the seller of a debt document is believed to say that it was paid. Rather, as I explained, the Gemara mentions a receipt because the receipt provides evidence, or because of the continuation of the Mishnah, that where the woman does not admit its validity the receipt is returned to neither party. In truth she is believed by her oral statement alone that the ketubah was paid, either by a migo that she could forgive it or, as I have written, because her very statement that it was paid is the greatest possible waiver. This is contrary to the Raavad's responsum and is the primary ruling.
Levi shall pay Reuven, etc. The Maggid Mishneh writes regarding the Rambam's later phrase, "Levi claims that he did not sell and did not give, etc.," that from the Rambam's inclusion of "did not give" it appears that when Levi says the debt was paid he must compensate Reuven like one who forgives an assigned debt, as explained below in seif 32, who must pay the full amount of the document. Thus, even if he sold it for one hundred while it was worth one thousand, the person who forgave it pays one thousand. So too here, when Levi says it was paid, he pays the full value of the debt document. We do not believe him to say that it had already been paid before the sale. It appears to me that this assumes there are witnesses that he sold it. Otherwise, he would be believed that payment preceded the sale through a migo that he could deny the sale. Even though denying a sale would be a brazen claim while his present claim is not, a migo of that type is accepted, as I write below in siman 81, seif 23, seif katan 58, and siman 93, seif 2, and as I prove below at the end of siman 82 in the laws of migo, letter 6. If payment preceded the sale, he would owe only the purchase price he received because the sale was a transaction in something that did not exist. Although we believe him with respect to the debtor because he could forgive the debt, he is certainly liable in damages to the purchaser just as he would be if he actually forgave it. Otherwise, even if Levi admitted that he gave the document as a gift, he would pay nothing if we believed him that payment occurred before the gift, because he would have given something with no value. One could answer that when the Rambam says, "and did not give," he means that he gave it without responsibility, or alternatively that he did not give it and only afterward received payment. Nevertheless, the primary law appears as the Maggid Mishneh explained. His words are correct, the Rambam likewise appears to hold this way, and the attempted alternative is strained. I also cite below in seif 32, seif katan 109, Shiltei Giborim in the name of Ri'az to the same effect. The Mechaber likewise appears to rule this way, and it is primary. It further appears that even if the debtor did not pay the seller the full face value, but the seller claims he accepted only an amount equal to what he had received for the sale and thereby agreed that the debt should be treated as paid, he must nevertheless pay the purchaser the full value of the document, because the document was lost through his conduct. There is no difficulty from Baal HaTerumos, Tur, and Mechaber below in seif 34, who rule that if the debtor produces a receipt showing that this debt document was paid, the transaction is a mistaken sale and the purchase money is returned, implying that the seller need return only what he received. There, the debtor produces an independent receipt, so even without the seller's admission it is established that he sold a paid document. The sale was therefore void from inception and the purchase price is returned. Here, by contrast, there is no proof that the debt was paid other than Levi's admission. Had Levi wished to swear, Reuven could have collected from Shimon against Shimon's will. It is therefore Levi's admission, or his refusal to swear, which is equivalent to an admission, that prevents Reuven from collecting. Levi is effectively forgiving the debt now and causing the purchaser's loss now. He must therefore pay the full value of the debt document. Shiltei Giborim in the name of Ri'az, cited below in seif 32, seif katan 109, appears to make this very distinction, and it is primary. This also answers, in my humble opinion, the Rosh's question at the end of Perek Shnayim Ochazin on the Gemara's statement, "this proves that Shmuel's law applies": if the woman forgives the ketubah she will have to pay the purchaser the full value of the ketubah. The Rosh cites those who infer from this that one who forgives an assigned debt need repay only the amount the purchaser paid. In my humble opinion, the one who forgives must pay the full value of the debt document, as I write below in seif 32, seif katan 109. The Gemara's statement is nevertheless correct, because in its present case too she must compensate the purchaser for the full value. The receipt is returned only because of her admission. Without her admission, the receipt would not be returned. Thus the ketubah is lost through her conduct, and she is equivalent to one who presently forgives the ketubah. This appears clear to me. Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia also plainly implies this.
And if they do not wish to swear, they pay the purchaser. This implies that they pay him the full value of the document, and the same is implied by the Maggid Mishneh. Further, according to the Sema's explanation in seif katan 31, the Mechaber's earlier phrase, "he must pay Reuven," means the full value of the debt document. Presumably, therefore, the Mechaber's phrase here, "they pay the purchaser," likewise means the full value. All liabilities their father bore are presumptively inherited by them. The two clauses also presumptively concern the same type of liability; otherwise, the Mechaber should have distinguished them expressly. There is no reason to distinguish between the father and the heirs. It is not reasonable to say that because the father was the seller he owes more. He owes more than the purchase money he received, and even if he gave the document as a gift he must pay. Rather, the basis of liability is that by refusing to swear he causes the purchaser a loss and is therefore liable under the law of garmi for the damage he causes. That reasoning applies equally to the heirs. One might say that the heirs can claim, "we do not intend to harm the purchaser; we know the debt was paid and do not wish to swear falsely." But the seller or donor could make the identical claim. Necessarily, because they cannot substantiate their assertion they are liable under the law of garmi, as I write below in seif 32, seif katan 105, in the name of Shiltei Giborim citing Ri'az. The same therefore applies to heirs. This is clear. It also contradicts the Sema's words below in seif 15, seif katan 41, as I explain there in seif katan 53.
Levi claimed that he did not sell, etc. Meaning, Levi makes this claim against Reuven, and Levi then swears. The Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh, however, explain that Levi makes this claim against Shimon and that Shimon swears. This is not satisfactory. The Sema, seif katan 33, writes that they did not explain the Rambam according to its plain meaning because they hold that if Levi admits the debt was paid but says to Reuven, "I never sold it to you," Levi would not even need to swear, since the burden of proof is upon the claimant. This does not appear correct. Why should Levi not swear? According to Reuven's certain claim, Levi owes him money. The plain meaning of the Rambam is therefore as I have explained. The Maggid Mishneh explicitly explains it this way, and the Beit Yosef cites him. The Rama also writes in responsum 95, question 2, that Levi swears, and says that the Beit Yosef agrees, merely adding on his own that Shimon also takes a heset oath. The Bach likewise writes that even the Beit Yosef agrees to Levi's oath as a matter of law, and the Ir Shushan says the same. See there.
That even with respect to its acquisition, etc. See the Ir Shushan, who writes that the physical paper of the document must be returned to him. The same is stated by Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 8, in the name of Rabbi Nasan son of Rabbi Meir, and by the Ran in Perek HaKosev concerning the incident of the bundle of documents, as I wrote above in siman 64, seif katan 4.
He is not believed. It follows that the same applies if he claims, "it was given to me as a deathly ill person's gift," and he is not believed, contrary to what the Beit Yosef writes in Even HaEzer, siman 86. He cites Responsa Ramban, siman 30, concerning debt documents claimed by the guardians. If the woman claims that her husband gave them to her, the guardians prevail, because a debt document is acquired only through writing and delivery. This remains so even where there are no witnesses and no present sight of the documents, because migo does not apply to documents in this context, as the Rif writes in Perek HaKosev concerning the bundle of documents. The Beit Yosef then adds that if she says they were given to her as a gift of a shechiv mera, she would apparently be believed according to the Rambam and Rosh, who hold that in a shechiv mera gift the giver need not say, "Acquire it and all the lien contained in it," as explained in Tur Choshen Mishpat. This does not appear correct to me. The issue does not depend on that dispute. Even according to the Rambam and Rosh, who hold that the special transfer formula is unnecessary in a shechiv mera gift, that applies where the gift itself is known. It does not establish that the possessor is believed to say that such a gift occurred. Proof is the incident of the bundle of documents in Perek HaKosev, Kesubos 85a, from which this very point is derived. That case also proves that she is not believed even to say that they were given to her as a shechiv mera gift, for otherwise the woman who seized them during the deceased's lifetime would have been believed through that migo. The Beit Yosef in this siman, seif 12, likewise cites a responsum of the Rashba that one always needs proof of the written transfer, even according to Rava, and that one is likewise not believed to say that the documents were given as a shechiv mera gift, as proved by the incident of the bundle of documents in Perek HaKosev. He also cites Nimukei Yosef that the commentators clarified that possession is no proof either of written transfer or of acquisition through agav, and the same applies to a claim that the documents were given as a shechiv mera gift, which requires proof. One cannot answer that the Rashba follows his own view, cited by me at the end of this siman, seif katan 122, that even a shechiv mera must say, "Acquire it and all its liens," because that has no bearing on the evidentiary question. Further, the Ran in Perek HaKosev also writes that she is not believed to say, "he gave it to me as a shechiv mera gift," while the Beit Yosef himself writes at the end of this siman that in Perek Mi SheMes the Ran follows the Rambam that a shechiv mera need not use the special transfer formula. The Ramban in his novellae to Perek Get Pashut, folio 108b, and the Ritva in Perek HaKosev likewise say so. Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, beginning of part 8, writes the same, and at the end of that part cites Rabbi Nasan in the name of Rabbi Meir, who proves that one is not believed on any such claim, whether he says, "he gave them to me together with land," or, "he gave them to me as a shechiv mera gift." He there challenges the Raavad. This is the primary ruling, and the Rama in the gloss also appears to follow it.
And even if it was, etc. It appears to me that the text should read, "And if it was," as in the Ir Shushan.
He is not believed, etc., and the purchaser does not, etc. One can ask why the debtor should not be obligated to take an oath contradicting the third party, who is a single witness, and then be required to pay because he cannot swear, since he does not know. According to those poskim who hold that we do not say, "one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay," where the matter was not something he should have known, one can answer that here too the debtor had no reason to know that the document had been sold. But according to the other authorities the question remains. It appears that the case concerns a third party who is a relative or otherwise disqualified as a witness. Nimukei Yosef, which is the source of this ruling, cites the case as discussed in Sanhedrin, and there, at the end of Perek Zeh Borer, the third party was a woman. See there carefully.
