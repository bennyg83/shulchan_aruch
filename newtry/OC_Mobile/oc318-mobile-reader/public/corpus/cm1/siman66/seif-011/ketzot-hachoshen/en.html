He is not believed. Ramban explains that movables are different from debt instruments. Since movables are acquired through delivery or lifting, the very possession of the claimant supports his assertion, "I purchased them," and money is not removed from a possessor without proof. A debt instrument is different. Mere possession of the paper does not acquire the underlying lien. The holder is therefore not considered in possession of the debt merely because he holds the paper. According to the view that letters are acquired by delivery alone, Rava can treat the holder like the possessor of ordinary movables. But according to the view requiring writing and delivery, one who says, "It was assigned to me incidentally with land," or, "It was given to me as a deathbed gift," bears the burden of proof. The lien remains under the presumption of the creditor named in the instrument. Ramban therefore expressly concludes that even after the original creditor dies, the holder is not believed merely to say that the debt was given to him as a deathbed gift. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer suggested that according to Rambam and Rosh, who do not require the precise formula, "Acquire it and all its liens," a person might be believed to say it was given as a deathbed gift. Ramban's words show otherwise. The issue is not the wording of assignment, but the fact that mere possession of the paper is not possession of the debt.
He is not believed. The Shach asks why this is not a case of one obligated to swear but unable to swear, who must pay. Even if sale of debt instruments is only rabbinic, I have written elsewhere that where the oath itself is a Torah oath, inability to swear can require payment even when the payment obligation is rabbinic. Here, however, there is no Torah oath. If the shalish testifies that this debt instrument and all its liens were sold to one of the claimants, the dispute concerns a lien on land, and there is no Torah oath on denial of a land lien. Even where the instrument is only the debtor's handwriting and carries no land lien, debt instruments themselves are excluded from the Torah laws of custodial oath because their bodies are not money. There is also another reason. Where one person borrowed from two, one hundred from one and two hundred from the other, and each claims two hundred, the borrower places one hundred between them and withdraws. He is not treated as saying, "fifty I know and fifty I do not," because he knows certainly that he does not owe more than the total amount. The same applies here. The possessor makes a certain claim about the total legal ownership even if he does not know which claimant is entitled. Therefore the rule of inability to swear does not apply.
