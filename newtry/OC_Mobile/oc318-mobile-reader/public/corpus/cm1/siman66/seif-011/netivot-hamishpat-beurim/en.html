Witnesses are not required for purposes of acquiring it. Maggid Mishneh labors to explain why the possessor is believed against the creditor but not against the debtor. See there. One may explain that he understands Tosafot in Yevamot 115, s.v. Mai amrat, to hold that Rava is not concerned that the note fell or was deposited, but only that perhaps it was delivered to him. Thus it is certain that the creditor sold it to him, and the only doubt is whether there was a written assignment. If there was no writing, the seller can retract. The gloss in seif 1 states that even though he can retract, the purchaser may nevertheless retain the paper until his purchase money is returned. Since the note is therefore held by him like collateral, it appears that if both parties admitted to a sale by delivery without writing but disputed the purchase price, the purchaser would be believed to retain the paper up to the amount he claims. This resembles siman 72, Shach seif katan 94, regarding collateral known to have been given for a loan where the amount of the loan is unknown, in which case the creditor is believed. So too here. Since, had he claimed that the note was sold to him for the entire amount written in it, he would be believed to retain it to that amount, he is likewise believed through a migo when he claims that he purchased the note by writing and delivery and is entitled to the full value of the note. He may retain it until he receives that amount. This is also why he is believed if he claims that it was sold to him merely for use as paper, as Ran writes in Ketubot. As for Baal HaTerumot's challenge to Rambam from the end of Zeh Borer, where the Gemara says that the ruling follows Rabbi, who holds that documentary obligations are acquired by delivery, and the holder is believed through a migo that he purchased it, which implies that according to the view that documentary obligations are not acquired by delivery he could not claim purchase, see what I wrote above in siman 56.
And if there is proof that he sold it. See Shach, seif katan 31. His words are difficult to me. Since the purchaser has no migo, why should he be believed? We must say that there is a chazakah that one does not sell a paid note, as Tumim writes, except that when the purchaser has a migo he is believed because we find many instances in which a migo is accepted even against a chazakah, since not all chazakot are equal, as Tosafot write. Accordingly, if he does not wish to swear and says nothing as to when the note was paid, so that there is no migo, we certainly apply the above chazakah and assume that it was to be paid thereafter. The primary ruling is therefore Shach's first answer, that he must pay the full value of the note.
He takes a hesset oath. See Sema, seif katan 33, who writes that if Shimon claims, "I already paid, the note fell from me, and you found it," Shimon swears and is exempt both from Levi and from Reuven. Sema compares this to siman 41, where a note is found in the possession of another person and the creditor is believed with an oath, and Rama likewise rules in responsum 94, seif 2. At first glance this is difficult. Earlier in this seif it is stated that if Shimon claims that he paid Levi, Levi swears to Shimon and Reuven collects, which implies that even if Shimon says he paid Levi and the note then fell from Levi, Shimon is not believed. Moreover, in siman 72, in the case of Chanani and Chanan, it appears that even when the note is in Chanan's possession, Chanani collects, and it is not treated like a note found in another person's hand. It therefore appears as follows. Shach later, in siman 96, writes regarding a transfer in maamad sheloshtan that only the debt, not the document itself, is acquired. So long as the transferee has not received writing and delivery, because the document itself has not been acquired, the debtor is believed to claim payment. Once writing and delivery are subsequently given, it again becomes a debt evidenced by a note. Shach similarly writes in seif katan 65 that until the document itself has been acquired, the debt has the status of an oral loan, until writing and delivery are given. Accordingly, a distinction should be made. When Levi litigates with Shimon, since according to Rambam Reuven is believed regarding his acquisition and has acquired the note, Levi is left as one asserting an oral claim because the note is not in his possession, and Shimon is therefore believed against him. But when Levi later gives writing and delivery to Reuven, the debt again becomes a loan evidenced by a note in Reuven's possession, and Shimon can no longer claim payment against him. In any event the note belongs to Reuven: if Levi already gave him writing and delivery it is his, and if he did not do so earlier, he gives it to him now. According to Rama's gloss, who holds that Reuven is not believed regarding acquisition, the note is legally regarded as though it were still in Levi's hand, since Reuven is obligated to return it to him. It is therefore a debt evidenced by a note in Levi's possession. This also explains siman 42: whenever the law requires the document to be returned to the creditor, the claim is a full debt evidenced by a note; only where the document is not legally to be returned to him is it treated as an oral debt. See also what I wrote in siman 65, Biurim seif katan 6. Tumim likewise agrees that the debtor is not believed to claim payment, although he gives no reason, and he contradicts what he himself wrote at the end of siman 41, that even where beit din returned the note into the custodian's hand, the debtor is believed because the creditor has no migo, all the more so here. I have already rejected his position there as well.
Also with respect to acquiring it. The reason is stated by Ran in Perek HaKotev regarding a bundle of notes. Ramban, in Sefer HaZechut, writes that specifically with movable property, or with documents according to the view that documentary obligations are acquired by delivery, since possession itself effects acquisition, the possessor is also considered a muchzak by virtue of that possession. A promissory note that requires both writing and delivery is different. Since delivery alone is not an act of acquisition, possession of the paper does not make him a muchzak. For the same reason he is not believed to say that he acquired it through agav or by a gift of a shechiv mera, because his physical possession does not give him the legal status of a muchzak.
It is held by the third party, even though he is believed against the seller. At first glance this is difficult. A third party should not be more credible than the litigant himself. If the note were in the purchaser's own hand he would not be believed regarding acquisition, so on what basis is the third party believed? Tumim likewise raises this difficulty. Upon examination, however, there is no difficulty. Sema in siman 64, seif katan 20, explains why there the holder is believed through a migo that he could deny the transaction entirely and may therefore retain the note, whereas here he is not believed even merely to retain it. There he claims only that the note is collateral and has a migo. Here he claims that he bought it and seeks to collect through it. His alternative claim of complete denial would not enable him to collect, and therefore it is not a valid migo even for retaining the note. It is a migo for only half of the asserted claim. This, I believe, is Sema's intent. A third party is different. The law is that a third party is believed even if he has no present migo, provided that at some earlier time he had the ability to act accordingly, as explained in siman 56. Tosafot in Gittin 64 explain that whenever the depositor placed him in a position where at some time he could have brought about a particular result, the depositor empowered him so that the legal result follows the third party's statement even if that statement is false. Thus, regarding acquisition in the limited sense that the purchaser may retain the paper, the third party at some point had the ability to accomplish that result. He could have delivered the note to the purchaser, and the purchaser could then have claimed that he acquired the paper merely for its material value, a claim on which he would be believed, as Ran writes in Ketubot. Such a claim would allow him to retain the note until all the money he claims is paid, even up to the entire amount of the note, as explained above. Alternatively, the third party could have enabled him to say that the note was pledged to him for the full amount. The depositor therefore trusted the third party to establish that result by his statement, even falsely, because whatever result the third party could once have produced was entrusted to his word. But as to collecting the debt, the third party could never have effected that result merely by his own act, and therefore his statement does not suffice. Although his statement is ineffective for collection, it is effective for possession, because we divide the statement. There are two distinct acquisitions in the sale of a note. Acquisition of the paper for its physical use is a Torah acquisition, while acquisition of the right to collect is rabbinic. Although the third party is not believed to permit collection, he is believed as to the paper itself, because the depositor entrusted him as to that which he could have effected even if he were to speak falsely. By contrast, when the note is in the purchaser's own possession, the purchaser can be believed only through a present migo. Since he seeks to collect through his asserted purchase, the alternative claim would support only part of his position, and there is no migo for half a claim. He is therefore not believed at all. This appears clear.
And the purchaser cannot collect. See Shach, seif katan 42, who asks why this is not a case of one who is obligated to take an oath but cannot swear, and therefore must pay, in light of the third party's testimony. At first glance this is very difficult, as Tumim also notes. First, the claim involves a lien on land, for which there is no Torah oath. Second, the debtor can say, "What concern is this oath to me? I will deposit the money with beit din, and they can do with it whatever the law requires." If Reuven had a deposit in Shimon's possession and Levi came with one witness saying that Reuven sold him the deposit, would Shimon be required to hand it over because he is unable to take an oath? That would amount to extracting money on the testimony of one witness, which is certainly not the law. There is no oath imposed on the debtor or custodian in such a case. Upon examination, however, the second objection is not difficult. In the custodial case described by Tumim, there is no oath because the custodian has no personal benefit in denying Levi. He wishes to return the property but does not know to whom, so he deposits it with beit din and withdraws from the dispute. Here, by contrast, the document is held by a third party whom Rama rules is believed against the seller. Consequently, if the debtor does not plead against the purchaser, "You are not my litigant," the money is paid to the purchaser, because the third party is believed regarding the purchaser's acquisition. This follows the Mechaber's presentation of Rambam's view that the possessor is believed as to acquisition, so that if the debtor does not raise that defense, payment goes to the purchaser. Therefore, even if the debtor deposits the money with beit din here, beit din will deliver it to the purchaser. The debtor is exempt only when he affirmatively pleads to the purchaser, "You are not my litigant." That denial serves solely the debtor's own interest by preventing the purchaser from receiving the money, since without it the money would be delivered to the purchaser. It is therefore properly treated as a case of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. This is straightforward. This analysis, however, follows Shach's view that the third party is fully believed. According to what I wrote above, seif katan 19, that the third party is believed only to permit retention of the note while the debtor may still pay the original creditor, the rule of one who cannot swear does not apply. The first objection, that this is a lien on land, is also not difficult, because Shach's question applies at least to a note without property liability or to a handwritten obligation. Since the Mechaber speaks without qualification, his ruling includes such cases. As for Ketzot HaChoshen's statement that documents are excluded from the law of oaths, I do not understand it. The claim here concerns the money represented by the document, not the document itself. Moreover, even a note containing property liability may be regarded here as having lost its effective lien, because according to Sema, seif katan 33, the debtor can discharge himself, and here likewise the holder is believed regarding acquisition but not regarding the underlying claim while the note is in a third party's possession. Purchasers of the debtor's land can also say to the note purchaser, "You are not our litigant," and the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so does not apply to purchasers. Just as we expound that the oath of Hashem is "between the two of them," excluding heirs, so too it excludes purchasers. Since the note's force against encumbered property has thereby been impaired, denial is not considered denial of a land lien, as explained at the end of siman 62 where the debtor claims that half has been paid and witnesses testify that all was paid. Nor can one challenge the present ruling from Shach in siman 98, seif katan 12, who writes that when in any event the money will leave the defendant's possession, a definite claim prevails over an uncertain one. Here too, it might be argued, the money will go either to the creditor or to the purchaser, so the purchaser's definite claim that he bought the note should prevail over the debtor's uncertainty. This is no difficulty. There, at least, the defendant is a litigant vis a vis the shopkeeper because he instructed the shopkeeper to incur the expense. Here the purchaser is not the debtor's litigant at all, and the debtor need not answer his claim. The purchaser therefore cannot prevail by a forced either way argument. This is simple.
