The seller cannot recover from the purchaser, since the purchaser has already taken possession. See Shach, seif katan 7, who questions this ruling. With some difficulty, it may be reconciled as follows. Perishah writes concerning a seller that even if he did not know that he could waive the debt, his waiver is effective. The reason, in my view, is that the Gemara in Bava Metzia 67 states that the underlying transaction here was a sale. Tosafot explain that the seller should not retract because he wishes to preserve his reliability. Therefore, we assume that even had he known that he could retract, he would not have done so. Thus, even if he did not actually know, there is a compelling presumption that had he known, he still would have waived the debt. This is comparable to abandonment without knowledge. Although we rule like Rava that such abandonment is ineffective, nevertheless with respect to a gift it is effective, as Shach writes in siman 358, seif katan 1. This opinion understands the statement that a person prefers to preserve his reliability to mean that he does not wish to sue the person to whom he sold the right and demand return of what that purchaser received under their transaction. By contrast, where he would not need to sue his own purchaser but only some third party, that rationale does not apply. Accordingly, when the borrower has no assets, the seller cannot proceed against the purchaser through the rule of Rabbi Natan, because so long as the seller must sue the purchaser, the presumption just described stands against him. But when the borrower has assets, the seller need only sue the borrower, and he may indeed do so. The borrower may then proceed against the purchaser afterward.
