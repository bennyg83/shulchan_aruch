And some say that a document is not acquired through agav. Responsa Rosh, klal 75, siman 3, makes clear that even according to this view debt documents can nevertheless become encumbered through agav. Rosh explains that the power of a lien is stronger than the power of a sale: one cannot sell property that has not yet come into existence, yet one can create a lien on after-acquired property. Shach objects that the comparison is not sound. A debt document has no intrinsic monetary substance upon which a lien can attach. Even in an outright sale, everyone agrees the transferor must say, "Acquire it and all its liens." Rashba, responsum 914, states the opposite of Rosh: even if debt documents can be sold through agav, they cannot themselves be subjected to a lien through agav, especially where the language "acquire it and all its liens" was not used. Rashba's words require careful explanation. He says debt documents cannot become encumbered because they have no intrinsic monetary body. As for the borrower's assets represented by the note, even if those assets are eventually collected, until collection they are only assets subject to the creditor's lien, and we rule like Rava that a creditor acquires only from the time of actual collection. Therefore the lender cannot presently encumber them. But why should Rava's rule matter to a lien? It certainly limits outright sale of the future assets, and Tosafot and other authorities use it to explain why the lender cannot simply transfer the land he may later collect. Yet a person can create a lien on property that has not yet come into existence. If the lender writes an after-acquired-property clause, why can he not encumber whatever assets he will later collect from his borrower? Rashba himself holds that an after-acquired-property clause is ordinarily implied. Moreover, his wording says the result is so even if the lien is expressly stated. The answer is that Rashba's responsum concerns priority after a later creditor has already collected from the debt notes. The earlier creditor says: "Those note rights were already encumbered to me through agav." Rashba answers that the lien cannot attach to the borrower's property until the original lender actually acquires it through collection. Until then, under Rava, the assets are future-acquired property. Once they are collected, an after-acquired-property lien can indeed attach. But then the later creditor may already have his own lien on the same after-acquired property. If the later creditor collected first, the usual rules governing two creditors and after-acquired property apply, and the earlier creditor cannot automatically displace him. Indeed, even if the later creditor did not independently have an after-acquired-property clause, Rashba holds below in siman 112 that an obligor may retract from an after-acquired-property lien before the property comes into existence. Transferring or delivering the property to the later creditor can itself constitute such a retraction. Thus Rashba's ruling is understandable in his own system. According to the authorities who disagree with Rashba below and hold that once a debtor has accepted an after-acquired-property lien he cannot retract before acquiring the property, the result should be different. Once the lender eventually acquires the borrower's assets, the earlier lien attaches automatically. The concern that Rava says collection is only prospective does not prevent a previously created lien from attaching to after-acquired property. Accordingly, for those authorities there is strong reason to follow Rosh: debt rights can be encumbered through agav even where outright agav sale is disputed. There is, however, a broader conceptual difficulty in Tosafot's explanation that ordinary debt-note sale cannot transfer the future property because we rule "from now and onward he collects." According to R. Meir, a person can sell or consecrate property not yet in existence. Why then would the future character of the borrower's property prevent a Torah sale? And Rebbi in Kiddushin follows R. Meir regarding future property. Why would Rebbi nevertheless formulate a special document-transfer rule requiring delivery rather than simply relying on ordinary Torah transfer of the future property? One could say Chazal required physical delivery so that the seller could not retract before the future property came into existence. Even R. Meir may permit retraction before the future object actually exists. Then delivery serves to strengthen the transfer. But this is not entirely satisfactory, especially in a gift where ordinary money acquisition is not available and where a document dated "from now" can itself indicate present effect. Rashba also holds that an explicit "from now" can prevent retraction even according to R. Meir. Since the date of a document often proves present intent, one would still expect a Torah-level sale to be possible without the special rabbinic form. The better explanation is that even according to R. Meir, a sale of future property must identify the future property itself. A creditor who says generally, "Acquire this note and all its liens," is not selling any particular field that he will later collect. The borrower may pay money, may give movables, or may satisfy the debt from any of various assets. Until collection, no defined property belongs to the creditor. Thus the purchaser acquires the debt right itself only through the special document-transfer mechanism. The ordinary rule allowing sale of future property does not automatically transfer an unidentified future collection. This also resolves the problem according to Abaye, who holds that a creditor can sometimes be treated as collecting retroactively. That doctrine is strongest in a mortgage or apotiki, where a particular asset is already connected to the debt. Baal HaIttur and Baal HaTerumot cite Rabbenu Chananel that the dispute of Abaye and Rava in Pesachim concerns precisely mortgages and apotiki, while in an ordinary unsecured debt even Abaye agrees that collection is only prospective. On that view all of the difficulties disappear. The ordinary debt-note cases here involve no mortgage or designated property. For authorities who apply Abaye more broadly, one can still say that retroactive collection does not make the purchaser of "all the liens" the present owner of an unidentified future asset. The special document transfer remains necessary. Accordingly, Rosh's principle that a lien can reach farther than a sale is coherent, and Rashba's contrary ruling follows his special approach to after-acquired property and retraction. The matter therefore depends substantially on the dispute below in siman 112. I have discussed the related question whether debt notes themselves become encumbered through agav, or whether R. Natan's lien supplies the result independently, at length in simanim 85 and 111, where the issue more properly belongs.
