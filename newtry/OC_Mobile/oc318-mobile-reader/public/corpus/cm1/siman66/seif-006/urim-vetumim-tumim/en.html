And even the paper, and so forth. This rule is from Tur. Yet earlier Tur cites Baal HaTerumot that if the seller transferred a debt note without writing "acquire it and all its liens," the purchaser does not acquire the lien, but may use the physical paper to wrap a small vessel. If the debtor later pays the original creditor, beit din compels the purchaser to return the note to the debtor. Beit Yosef asks that Tur appears inconsistent. Bach answers that here Tur speaks of a purchaser who does not claim to have acquired the paper itself. Since one cannot acquire half of an integrated transaction, he holds the paper only as collateral until the seller returns his money. Shach objects that the language "he uses the paper to wrap a small vessel" does not sound like mere collateral. Knesset HaGedolah raises a parallel contradiction in Rashba. In responsum 918 Rashba appears to say the purchaser acquired the paper, while Rabbenu Yerucham cites him as saying he did not. Knesset HaGedolah therefore distinguishes between mere delivery without any writing, where not even the paper is acquired, and a case where there was writing but the transfer omitted the phrase "it and all its liens," where at least the physical paper is acquired. He says this distinction appears in Ritva Kiddushin and Ramban Bava Batra. I do not understand the conceptual basis. Either an integrated transaction can be partially acquired or it cannot. If it can, delivery alone should acquire the paper, because physical paper is movable property and its ordinary mode of acquisition is delivery. If it cannot, why should an ineffective writing suddenly allow acquisition of the paper when it fails to transfer the debt? Ritva Kiddushin does not clearly state such a distinction. He compares the matter to one who says, "I sell you a kor for thirty," where either party may retract until the measure is complete. Ramban's wording in Bava Batra can perhaps be forced into the proposed distinction, but it is not compelling. The proper distinction, in my opinion, is between two different claims by the buyer. Suppose he paid one hundred for the debt note and then says: "The transfer of the debt failed. Return my one hundred. At most I should retain the paper itself for the few pennies it is worth." Here Ramban is correct that there is no acquisition by halves. The buyer cannot both rescind the substantive purchase and simultaneously say that one tiny physical component remains acquired. Since the intended integrated sale failed, the paper also returns. But suppose the buyer says: "Although I did not acquire the debt lien, I do not rescind. I am willing to keep the physical paper as the entire subject of the sale for the full price I paid. It is worth that much to me, and I do not demand any money back." That case is different. According to Rif and Rambam, where the buyer is already in possession and wishes to maintain the transaction, the seller may not necessarily cancel it. Even according to Rosh and Ri, who allow the seller to retract in certain defective sales, debt documents are excluded from the law of ona'ah. The buyer can say, "To me this paper is worth a great deal." Its market valuation is indeterminate. Tosafot Ketubot 85b make this point. They ask how documents can be excluded from ona'ah if sale of documents is only rabbinic. They answer that the paper itself can be worth a great deal to the purchaser because he can sell it to the debtor for a high price. Thus even on a Torah level, where the debt itself was not acquired, the buyer can purchase the physical paper at a high value and there is no ona'ah. So too here. If the buyer insists on maintaining the transaction as a purchase of the paper for the entire price, the seller cannot simply say the paper is worth only a perutah. The buyer may value it at the whole price. There is no problem of "partial acquisition," because he is not trying to keep part of the original transaction while rescinding the rest. He is saying the sale stands entirely, with the physical paper as its subject. This also explains Tosafot's discussion of a migo that the documents were purchased. At first glance, who would ever purchase valuable collectible notes merely for their paper value? Such a claim would be extraordinarily uncommon, and Tosafot generally do not accept an extremely uncommon migo. According to what I have written, the case is not that someone paid a trivial amount for scrap paper. He may have paid a substantial price precisely because possession of the paper has strategic value: the debtor cannot collect or litigate easily without it. This resembles saying, "I seized it as collateral." Such a transaction is not implausible.
And with this, Tur's later statement is also resolved. Tur says that if the purchaser ultimately returns the paper to the debtor, the debtor must pay him the value of the paper. Why does the purchaser not simply recover his full purchase money from the seller? If the sale failed, then even the paper value should ultimately be reimbursed by the seller. And if the seller has no assets, then under R. Natan the purchaser should be able to collect the entire amount from the debtor, since the price became a debt owed by the seller and R. Natan's lien cannot be forgiven. According to the distinction above, the answer is simple. The buyer is not rescinding against the seller. He says, "The paper itself is worth to me the entire price of the debt." By electing to retain the transaction on that basis, he has no remaining claim against the seller. His consideration is the paper. If beit din later requires him to return the paper to the debtor because the debt has been paid, the debtor must compensate him for the paper value that is actually being taken from him. This also harmonizes the authorities. Rabbenu Chananel, as cited by Rosh, Maggid Mishneh, and Rashba's responsum can all be discussing a purchaser who retains the paper for the full purchase price and says it is worth that much to him. They therefore say he acquired it. Ramban and Ritva, who say there is no partial acquisition, can be speaking where the purchaser seeks rescission and return of his price while claiming only the nominal paper value as acquired. Everyone can agree that this cannot be split. This also explains Ritva's distinction between a gift and a sale. In a gift there is no purchase price to rescind. Why should the recipient not say, "Even if the debt lien did not pass, I accept the paper itself as the entire gift"? The donor suffers no loss of consideration and the recipient need not return anything. Ritva elsewhere in Ketubot appears to say the opposite in the case of an elderly woman who seized bundles of documents. We do not know which statement is later. But according to the reasoning above, the Kiddushin position is primary and can be reconciled with the general principles. Finally, this does not contradict Rama below in siman 203, who rules that if one performs a kinyan effective for one item together with another item for which it is ineffective, he can acquire the valid half under the rule "you and the donkey acquire half." There, the parties agree the items are independent. For example, one transfers a house and coins in one chalipin. Even if the chalipin fails for the coins, everyone agrees the house was intended to be sold independently. But where the seller or buyer says, "I intended the paper and its debt lien as one integrated object. I would not sell or buy one without the other," the failure of the lien prevents completion of the entire transaction. That is the meaning of "there is no acquisition by halves" here. Thus Tur, Rashba, Ramban, and Ritva can all be reconciled without creating unnecessary disputes.
