A gentile who sold. According to the Gaon cited by the Tur in siman 154, certain rules concerning transfer from a gentile are applied only to the gentile's disadvantage and not to his advantage. If so, the gentile seller might retain the power of waiver. Yet if payment itself removes the property from the gentile's domain and leaves it ownerless, as explained in siman 194, one might say the debtor automatically acquires his own personal obligation once it becomes ownerless. The Shach's approach to a deceased convert says that the debt can be treated as though the creditor had obligated the debtor also toward anyone coming through the creditor until express waiver. Under that model mere ownerless status may not suffice. But if abandonment of the debt did work, then after abandonment the creditor could no longer waive because the debt would already have left his ownership. Upon further analysis, however, personal obligation cannot be made ownerless in the ordinary way. Just as it cannot be sold as a tangible object because it has no substance, it likewise cannot be abandoned like property. Therefore the personal lien remains governed by its own rules and does not disappear merely because the creditor's other property becomes ownerless.
And a Jew who sold to a gentile. Ir Shushan writes that sale of debt instruments is biblical, implying that if it were only rabbinic a gentile purchaser would not acquire. Ritva's responsum likewise suggests this. The Shach disagrees and says that even a rabbinic sale can benefit a gentile because every enactment of the Sages was fashioned along Torah patterns. There is support for Ir Shushan and Ritva from Bava Kamma. When the Gemara asks whether a gentile who improved stolen property receives the rabbinic improvement enactment, Rav Acha asks whether we should rise and make a special enactment for a gentile. This indicates that the principle that rabbinic enactments mirror Torah law does not automatically extend benefits instituted for Jews to gentiles. Accordingly, if sale of debt instruments were purely a rabbinic commercial enactment, there is substantial reason that it would not automatically transfer the debt to a gentile purchaser unless the Sages specifically included him.
