If he later waived it, the waiver is ineffective. The Rama concludes in the gloss that even without writing and delivery, the purchaser acquires it under their law. Tumim questions this. The rule that a Jew who derives title through a non-Jew has the same legal standing as the non-Jew is generally said only where the Jew can answer, “What loss have I caused you?” Here, however, had the document remained in the non-Jew's hands, the debt would have been subject to waiver once the original creditor waived it. Why, then, should the Jew who purchases from the non-Jew obtain a stronger right? Tumim likewise questions the Rama's conclusion that writing and delivery are unnecessary. Maharam of Lublin already raised this difficulty in his responsa. The rule that one who derives title through a non-Jew stands in the non-Jew's position applies only after the property has first been acquired by the Jew, granting him all the rights the non-Jew possessed. It does not mean that the Jew can acquire from the non-Jew through a form of transfer that is itself legally ineffective, such as a promissory note that, under Jewish law, is not acquired by delivery alone. A further difficulty is that this should seemingly be comparable to siman 194. Property of a non-Jew becomes ownerless in a case where the non-Jew acquired land by payment, received the money, and before the Jewish purchaser took possession another Jew took possession and acquired it. The second Jew acquires because once the non-Jew receives the money he withdraws his ownership, while the first Jew has not yet acquired the property. The same should seemingly apply here. Once the non-Jew receives the money, he withdraws his ownership, but the Jewish purchaser has not yet acquired the note according to Jewish law, so the right should be ownerless and the borrower should be first to acquire the money, as Shach writes in siman 9. This argument is even stronger where the purchaser paid the non-Jew before the promissory note was delivered, for then the right should become ownerless just as movable property does in siman 194. One cannot answer that in siman 194 the Jew had not acquired even according to non-Jewish law, whereas here he does acquire under their law without writing and delivery. That is not so, because in siman 194, with respect to renting movable property, the entire discussion turns upon whether pulling or payment effects acquisition under Torah law, and the authorities do not consider the non-Jewish rule at all. By contrast, in the present law of promissory notes, they do consider the rule under non-Jewish law. Taz in siman 194 also compares the two cases and gives a strained resolution, as does Sema in these passages. In my humble view, however, the matter is very straightforward. If the borrower accepted in the promissory note that he subjects himself to every rule recognized under non-Jewish law, a Jewish beit din may enforce every right that non-Jewish law gives the creditor beyond ordinary Jewish law, because the borrower expressly obligated himself in that manner. Sema writes this in siman 61, seif katan 14, and refers there to the end of siman 369 in the gloss. There it is explicit that if a Jew owed money to a non-Jew and the non-Jew sold the document to another Jew, the Jewish purchaser receives whatever greater amount the non-Jew would have been entitled to recover under their law. The reason must be that since the debtor obligated himself to the non-Jew, who would certainly enforce his rights according to their law, it is as though the debtor expressly stipulated that he obligates himself in every matter recognized under their law. Therefore the Jewish purchaser receives every right that their law confers. Accordingly, our case is also clear. Since under their law writing is unnecessary, the clause is equivalent to an express undertaking, “I obligate myself to you and to anyone who comes by virtue of you.” Such an obligation does not require writing and delivery. It is as though the debtor explicitly bound himself to everything for which he would be liable under their law. Under the Jewish law of a person who obligates himself to anyone who may later come, the obligation may pass without a separate writing. It is therefore precisely comparable to one who obligates himself and writes, “I am bound to you and to anyone who comes by virtue of you,” where writing and delivery are unnecessary and the original creditor cannot later waive the debt, as explained immediately below. Even according to the authority who disagrees there, the point here remains. Shach explains there that where the document says only “to anyone who comes by virtue of you,” a person who has not yet acquired it is not yet considered one who comes by virtue of the creditor. Shach would certainly agree that if the document expressly said, “whether he comes by virtue of me or not by virtue of me,” no further writing would be required, as explained in siman 50. Here, since the debtor obligated himself to the non-Jew, it is as though he expressly bound himself to anyone whom their law would recognize as entitled, whether that person comes through the original creditor or otherwise. The resulting obligation therefore has the same status as a negotiable instrument, which needs no writing and delivery and cannot be waived by the original creditor, as Shach explains above. This differs from siman 194, where the debtor never undertook to follow non-Jewish law. There, the dispute whether the Jew takes the non-Jew's status for advantage as well as disadvantage remains relevant. Here, by contrast, the debtor expressly undertook the obligation, and that undertaking governs.
And a Jew who sold it to a non-Jew. See Shach, who writes that although the sale of promissory notes is effective only by rabbinic enactment, they may nevertheless be sold to a non-Jew. Tumim questions why this differs from maamad sheloshtan, which the Sages did not enact for a non-Jew, and resolves the issue only with difficulty. In my humble view, the matter is straightforward. It is explained in Bava Kamma 113 that when a non-Jew and a Jew come before a beit din, if one can vindicate the Jew under Jewish law, the court says, “This is our law,” and if one can vindicate him under non-Jewish law, the court says, “This is your law.” Accordingly, wherever an acquisition created by rabbinic enactment is not recognized as an acquisition under their law, the Sages did not extend that acquisition to dealings with a non-Jew in a manner that would weaken the Jew's position, since the Jew can say, “This is your law.” Therefore, maamad sheloshtan, which under their law is merely an oral statement and may be retracted, was not enacted to the Jew's disadvantage in a transaction with a non-Jew. By contrast, with the sale of promissory notes, their law recognizes acquisition even without writing, and certainly through delivery, as explained at the beginning of this seif. Jewish law requires writing and delivery. Once writing and delivery are present, however, the transaction is valid both under our law and under theirs. The non-Jew can therefore no longer be met with either the claim “This is your law” or the claim “This is our law,” and the sale remains effective. In my humble view, this is a broad rule in this area. Whenever an act constitutes acquisition under non-Jewish law and is also recognized under Jewish law by rabbinic enactment, it also effects acquisition for a non-Jew. But where their law does not recognize the act as an acquisition, even though among Jews it is effective by rabbinic enactment, the Sages did not extend that enactment to a non-Jew, since doing so would give the non-Jew more than his own law gives him.
