He can forgive it to himself. Shach, seif katan 80, writes that if a minor heir forgives the sold debt, the forgiveness is effective and the minor need not pay damages even after reaching adulthood. This requires substantial qualification. Shach himself later, seif katan 109, writes that according to most authorities, even those who do not impose full dinei de-garmi damages for forgiveness still require the seller to return at least the purchase price he received. That obligation arises from ordinary monetary law, not from the law of a damager. If so, even where a minor is exempt from tort liability, why should he be exempt from returning the purchase money that came from the original transaction and is now retained after he destroys the purchaser's right? To the extent the inherited estate contains the purchase funds or their substitute, ordinary restitution can still apply. This point explains Tosafot Bava Batra 147b. They ask, according to the view that one who forgives a sold note pays the entire value, what benefit R. Nachman's young relative gained by forgiving her mother's ketubah to her father. They answer that she was a minor. Then Tosafot ask a second question: how could R. Nachman advise her to forgive at all? Even if she is exempt from damages, knowingly causing another's loss is prohibited. They answer that no real loss remained because she would return to the purchasers the money they had actually paid. This passage at first seems internally difficult. If Tosafot hold a minor may be advised to forgive because she has no garmi liability, why do they then worry about the prohibition of causing loss? And if they do not allow advising a minor to damage others, how does her minority solve the first problem? According to what I have written, everything is clear. Even a minor who is exempt from the additional garmi damages can still be obligated, through the estate or ordinary restitution principles, to return the actual purchase money received. That amount is not a tort penalty. Therefore, when R. Nachman advised the minor to forgive, the purchaser would still receive back what he had paid. The only amount lost was the speculative additional value of the debt, the portion imposed only under dinei de-garmi. Because the minor was exempt from that added liability, the advice created no uncompensated basic loss. This is why Tosafot say there was no prohibition in the advice: the purchasers would receive their purchase money back. It also shows that Shach's broad statement, that a minor who forgives need never pay anything even after adulthood, cannot be correct without qualification. At minimum, the purchase price must be restored where ordinary monetary law requires it. I previously offered another pilpul-based explanation of Tosafot, but the language itself strongly supports the distinction just stated. Shach also cites Shiltei HaGibborim that one may advise an heir to forgive where the heir gains a benefit, provided the adviser does not improperly act as an advocate. This too requires caution. Rosh in the first chapter of Bava Metzia says such advice is permitted specifically for a minor. Regarding an adult, under the view that a forgiver pays only the purchase price, the act still causes the purchaser loss beyond that price. Even if full garmi damages are not imposed, actively advising the destruction of another person's right can be prohibited. Tosafot's discussion likewise suggests that permissibility depends on the purchaser not suffering uncompensated loss. Therefore one cannot state generally that advice to forgive is allowed whenever the heir benefits. The correct practical rule depends on age, the measure of damages due under the adopted view, whether the purchaser will at least recover the purchase price, and whether the adviser's conduct constitutes improper litigation assistance. Shach's unqualified adoption of Shiltei HaGibborim overlooks the explicit concerns of Rosh and Tosafot.
