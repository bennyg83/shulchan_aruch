Seif 23. One who sells a debt document in the proper manner, etc. See the Shach, who cites Mabit that the borrower may say that he will pay the money to the original lender even after the debt has been sold, because the sale of debt documents is only rabbinic. The Shach rejects this because we rule that sale of debt documents is biblical, and he says there is no proof from siman 310, since there the house itself belongs to the lessor and the lessor can say, “I rented to you and not to another.” In my opinion the Shach’s rejection is difficult and Mabit’s position is correct. According to the view that sale of debt documents is biblical, the original creditor can nevertheless forgive the debt because there are two liens: the personal lien on the debtor and the lien on the debtor’s property. The sale primarily transfers only the lien on the property. If so, the purchaser’s principal acquisition is in the property lien, and the debtor can make the same argument: the property itself is his, and he can say, “I became obligated to you, not to another.” There can also be a meaningful difference between one creditor and another, for the first may be easy for him to deal with and the second more difficult. That concern applies to a borrower no less than to a tenant. Thus the case is truly parallel to siman 310: the underlying property is his, and he may say that his obligation was undertaken toward the original party and not another. Mabit’s words are therefore well founded, contrary to the Shach.
