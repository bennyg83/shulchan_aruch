Sema, seif katan 4, writes in the name of Darkei Moshe and Mahari Weil that the money presently held by the seller is only a deposit for which he has the status of a paid custodian. Therefore he can say, "I repaid you," through a migo that he could have said the money was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Shach, seif katan 8, says this is an error. He points to the case of the orchard in Perek Eizehu Neshekh and to Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 47, where in similar failed-sale situations the purchase money is treated as a full loan even though the sale is undone. I say the opposite: Shach erred in accusing them, while their ruling is correct. Tosafot Bava Metzia 43b ask the following. According to R. Yochanan, money acquires movables by Torah law. If so, the seller should be permitted to use the purchase money before meshichah. Then why does the Gemara say that if one gives money to a barber, there is no misuse of hekdesh until he performs meshichah? According to Reish Lakish too, if the seller may use the money, there should already be misuse. Tosafot first answer that the seller is in fact not permitted to use the money. Nevertheless, the rationale "your money was burned in the attic" does not apply because money does not require that sort of physical guarding. They then give a second answer: according to R. Yochanan, because there was a Torah acquisition and Chazal only removed it rabbinically, they left the seller with the right to use the money. He therefore has the status of a borrower. According to Reish Lakish, where no Torah acquisition existed, Chazal never gave him permission to use it. Now consider the sesame case in Perek HaZahav. A man paid for sesame, the seller retracted, told him to take his money, and the buyer did not take it. The money was then lost. Rava first said the seller was not even a paid custodian, perhaps not even an unpaid custodian. The Gemara answers that the seller must first accept Mi Shepara. Rif and Rambam rule that until he receives Mi Shepara, the seller remains liable even for unavoidable loss. See below siman 198, seif 15. Maggid Mishneh says this differs from Tosafot. Beit Yosef says it accords with Tosafot's second answer. At first this is difficult. Even according to the second Tosafot answer, the seller is a borrower only according to Rav Huna, who says benefit from permission to use deposited money creates borrower liability even for unavoidable loss. We rule like Rav Nachman that such permission creates only paid-custodian liability. Thus the second answer should still impose no unavoidable-loss liability. Rosh confirms this. In the sesame case he cites both Tosafot answers and says that for us, where everyone is treated like a moneychanger with respect to loose money, there is no practical difference. Under either answer the seller is only a paid custodian. The solution is that Tosafot and Rosh were discussing a case where Mi Shepara does not apply, such as a price change or another circumstance in which there was no continuing enforceable transaction. Where Mi Shepara does apply, however, the situation is different. Until the seller accepts Mi Shepara, the transaction remains standing at the rabbinic level. He continues to hold the funds on the basis of the intended sale, and they are treated as a loan in his hands. This is clear from Rosh himself. After citing both Tosafot answers and saying they make no practical difference, he later cites Rif's rule that until the seller accepts Mi Shepara the money is considered a loan, without presenting it as a disagreement. Tur likewise codifies Rif's rule without treating it as inconsistent with the earlier Tosafot analysis. Therefore, Tosafot and Rosh agree with Rif in a case where Mi Shepara is actually required. Beit Yosef's statement that Rif follows Tosafot's second answer means that only the second answer provides the conceptual distinction between a transaction with an underlying Torah acquisition and one with none. According to the first answer, which never uses that distinction, it would be harder to explain why Mi Shepara should transform the money into a loan. This is why Maggid Mishneh thought Tosafot disagreed. The practical rule is therefore: Where Mi Shepara does not apply, all the authorities can agree that the seller holds the money only as a deposit. Under our halachah he is at most a paid custodian, not a borrower. Where Mi Shepara does apply and the seller has not yet accepted it, the transaction retains enough force that the money is treated as a loan and he is liable accordingly. In our present case there is no Torah acquisition and, as explained in the preceding seif katan, no Mi Shepara. Therefore the money in the seller's hands is merely a deposit and he is a paid custodian. Sema, Darkei Moshe, and Mahari Weil are correct. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 47, might appear to disagree because he says that where one knowingly bought property that did not belong to the seller, or bought "what I will inherit from my father," the money is treated as a loan. But Baal HaTerumot may be alone against Tosafot, Rosh, and the many authorities cited in Shitah Mekubetzet. And even Baal HaTerumot can be distinguished. In his cases the buyer already entered into possession of the land and enjoyed it until an adverse claimant appeared. As the Gemara says, a sale for a day is still a sale. Because the purchaser actually used the property and received its fruits, the seller's receipt of the price resembles a loan. Here the buyer never entered into the substantive purchased right at all. Without written transfer and delivery of the note, what did he actually possess beyond the paper itself? The failed transaction never produced practical enjoyment. In such a case even Baal HaTerumot can agree that the money remains a deposit. The fact that Mahari Weil's case involved a written acknowledgment of the money does not change this. Baal HaTerumot himself does not distinguish between a documented and undocumented payment, and his proofs include cases involving writing. The legal character depends on the transaction, not on whether the receipt of money was documented. Accordingly, the practical ruling follows Sema and Rosh: where no Mi Shepara applies and the buyer never entered into the purchased right, the seller holds the money as a paid custodian, not as a borrower.
