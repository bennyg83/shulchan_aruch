And if there is proof that he sold, and so forth. Gidulei Terumah asks: the case itself says the seller admits that he sold the note. A litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Why, then, does Maggid Mishneh distinguish a case where there are witnesses to the sale? Shach answers that if there are witnesses of the sale, the seller is believed to say the note had previously been paid only through a migo that he could have forgiven it. Since forgiveness would obligate him to reimburse the purchaser for the full value, he must likewise pay the full value here. If there are no witnesses of sale, however, he can say the note was already paid before the sale through a migo that he could deny the sale entirely, and then he owes only the price he actually received. This is strained, and Maggid Mishneh's essential point is missing from the text. It also conflicts with the principle I have mentioned repeatedly: the seller can create an undated receipt before witnesses. Even where witnesses prove the sale, he can say the debt was already paid before the sale through the migo that he could have written such a receipt. If there is a strong chazakah that no one sells a paid note, then this migo is against that chazakah and should not help. But then the migo of denying the sale should not help either. The issue should be understood differently. Why does a seller who refuses to swear have to pay the purchaser at all? His refusal to swear is not direct causation of damage. At most, he withholds helpful testimony or proof. One who withholds testimony is exempt from liability in human court. The reason for liability must therefore be evidentiary. The borrower says, "I paid." The seller refuses to swear that he did not receive payment. We infer that the borrower is probably telling the truth. If the debt truly remained unpaid, why would the seller refuse a truthful oath and allow the purchaser to lose? His refusal indicates that the seller did in fact receive payment after the sale. That is why he must compensate the purchaser. If, however, it is established by a valid migo that the seller had not been paid, then even if he refuses the formal oath, his refusal is only a failure to assist the purchaser. That is analogous to withholding testimony and does not itself generate damages. This is where proof of sale matters. If the sale is not independently established, the seller can say, "I never sold it to you," and that defense creates a migo supporting his assertion that he was not paid. Once the factual conclusion that no payment occurred is sustained, mere refusal to swear does not impose the full value. If the sale is independently established, that migo is gone. His refusal to swear can then be treated as evidence that the borrower's claim of payment is true, and the seller becomes liable. One should not explain the liability merely as a mistaken sale. If it were ordinary mekach ta'ut, the seller would return only the purchase price, not the full value of the debt. Yet Maggid Mishneh and Shiltei HaGibborim in the name of Riaz imply full compensation. Nor does the rule concern only a sale. Shulchan Aruch's language extends to a gift as well. There is no purchase price to return in a gift, yet wrongful post-transfer payment can still create liability. Thus the evidentiary explanation is necessary. There remains a difficulty even on this reading: if the seller says, "I never sold it," he must take a hesset oath against the purchaser. What migo is there if the whole point is that he refuses to swear? One could force a distinction that he refuses the more severe oath required regarding collection of the debt but is willing to take hesset concerning the sale. That is possible but not the natural reading. For this reason, the cleanest conclusion may simply be that the mention of witnesses is not exclusive. An express admission of sale is equivalent to witnesses for this purpose. The halachah is the same either way. This is indeed one of Shach's own alternative answers and appears preferable.
