Through maamad sheloshtan. See Shach, who concludes that only the underlying debt is acquired through maamad sheloshtan, whereas the document itself is not. Consequently, as against the purchaser, the debt has the status of an oral loan. This is a reasonable approach, because we find no mode of acquisition for a promissory note itself other than writing and delivery, while maamad sheloshtan does not acquire the document. However, Shach further writes that even if the creditor sold the promissory note through writing and delivery and also transferred the debt through maamad sheloshtan, if the seller subsequently waives the debt it reverts to the status of an oral loan. This does not appear reasonable to me at all. The entire reason the original creditor can ordinarily waive a sold debt is that the personal obligation remains with him. Once he waives the personal obligation, the lien upon property falls away automatically. Here, however, he transferred the debt through maamad sheloshtan, so the personal obligation itself passed to the purchaser and can no longer be waived by the seller. The document contains only the lien upon property, and since he sold the document through writing and delivery, a later waiver should certainly be ineffective. There is no proof to the contrary from the Gemara. The Gemara states that the practical remedy is to induce the borrower with money so that he will write a new document in the purchaser's name. It does not suggest maamad sheloshtan. It appears that the Gemara there seeks a remedy after the original creditor has already sold the document and the purchaser acquired it by writing and delivery. At that point maamad sheloshtan is no longer effective, because the original creditor no longer possesses the lien upon property and his power over this loan has already ceased. Maamad sheloshtan is a legal rule without stated rationale, so one must not extend it beyond its established scope. It therefore applies at the outset of the sale, but not after the creditor has already transferred the document and lost his power over the secured claim. Tumim's question from Tosafot in Bava Batra 20 is indeed strong. Tosafot ask that perhaps she sold the ketubah through maamad sheloshtan after the divorce. After divorce, the debt is no longer within its due date, so the husband would ordinarily be believed to say that he paid it. According to what I have written, however, there is no difficulty. The concern is that perhaps she sold it through both maamad sheloshtan and writing and delivery. In that case, even if she later waives it, the purchaser still holds the rights of a documented loan, as explained above.
