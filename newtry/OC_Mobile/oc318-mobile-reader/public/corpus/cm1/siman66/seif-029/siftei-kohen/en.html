One who gives, etc. The Mechaber rules that when a debt was assigned in the presence of all three parties, the original creditor cannot forgive it. This follows the Beit Yosef, who rejected the Nimukei Yosef's statement that most authorities follow the Ramban. Yet below, siman 126, seif katan 10, I disputed the Beit Yosef and cited many authorities who hold like the Ramban, that even after an assignment in the presence of all three parties the original creditor may forgive the debt. Moreover, in my view the essential ruling in the present case is that he may forgive it. The Tosafists and the leading authorities divided into two camps and wrote at length concerning the basis of their dispute. It appears to me that a new resolution, which Heaven enabled me to perceive, establishes neither camp's position in full. Under that resolution, the debt in the present case may indeed be forgiven, contrary to the Tur and the Mechaber. I must therefore explain the matter at length. Tosafot, Gittin 13b, write in the name of Rabbeinu Tam that although one who sells a debt document may subsequently forgive the debt, if he sold or gave it as a gift through an assignment in the presence of all three parties, he cannot forgive it. They infer this from Kiddushin. There the Gemara explains that, regarding a debt evidenced by a document, the dispute may concern Shmuel's rule that a seller of a debt document may forgive it. According to the opinion that the woman is betrothed, Shmuel's rule is rejected; according to the opinion that she is not betrothed, Shmuel's rule is accepted. Alternatively, all accept Shmuel's rule and dispute whether the woman relies upon the transfer, one opinion reasoning that she relies upon it because the creditor would not abandon her and forgive the debt to another. When the Gemara then discusses an oral loan, it says that they dispute Rav Huna's rule concerning acquisition in the presence of all three parties. This implies that if they accepted Rav Huna's rule, all would agree that she is betrothed even through an oral loan. Were the original creditor still able to forgive the debt, why would she be betrothed according to the opinion that, with a documented debt, she does not rely upon the transfer? Why did the Gemara not conclude that all agree an oral loan is acquired in the presence of all three parties, and that they dispute only whether the woman relies upon it, as it concludes regarding Shmuel's rule and a documented loan? It therefore appears that a debt assigned in the presence of all three parties cannot be forgiven. Tosafot ask from Bava Kamma, in the case of a married woman who injured another. The Gemara asks why she should not sell her ketubah for its present value, and answers that she would certainly forgive it for her husband's benefit. Why not require her to sell it in the presence of all three parties, after which she could not forgive it? Likewise, at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, concerning a receipt that was found, the Gemara asks why, when the wife admits its validity, the receipt should be returned to the husband. We should suspect that she wrote it in Nisan but did not deliver it until Tishrei, after having sold the ketubah in the interim. The Gemara answers that this proves Shmuel's rule. But we should still suspect that she sold it in the presence of all three parties, in which case she could not forgive it. Tosafot answer that the enactment of assignment in the presence of all three parties was not made for a ketubah, which may never become collectible. They add that in the case of the receipt there is no concern. If there are no witnesses to the sale, husband and wife can deny it. If there are witnesses and the husband consented, or even remained silent, the receipt is plainly false; if he protested, that itself demonstrates that the receipt is genuine. The Rosh, Ran, Rashba in the opening of his comments there, and the Ran in responsum 69 rule similarly. So too do Tosafot in Bava Kamma 89, Bava Metzia 20, and Kiddushin 48, as well as the Rosh, Nimukei Yosef, and other authorities. The Ramban, in his comments to the chapter Mi Shemet, disagrees and holds that even after assignment in the presence of all three parties the original creditor may forgive the debt. The Rashba cites him as explaining that Kiddushin did not offer the alternative that all agree both loans and deposits are transferable and that they dispute Shmuel's rule, because the Gemara wished to formulate its answer consistently with the earlier explanations, under which the Sages dispute the validity of the acquisition itself. According to the final explanation, however, their dispute over an oral loan may also concern Shmuel's rule. A documented loan was mentioned to show the full force of the Sages' view, that even a documented debt can be forgiven, while an oral loan was mentioned to show the full force of Rabbi Meir's view. Both agree that even a loan is acquired in the presence of all three parties, as the law is established. The Ramban also cites the baraita that betrothal with collateral is valid, which the Gemara explains as collateral belonging to another. This indicates that betrothal through another person's loan is effective only when the creditor transfers the collateral as well, for when a loan secured by collateral is transferred together with the collateral, subsequent forgiveness is ineffective. The Baal HaIttur, Nimukei Yosef, and other authorities agree, as I cited below in siman 126, seif katan 10. If Tosafot's view were correct, however, it is difficult that the Gemara repeatedly states without qualification that one who sells a debt document and then forgives it has effected forgiveness, yet nowhere distinguishes a sale made in the presence of all three parties. The Ramban himself makes this point: Shmuel's formulation is unqualified, and Rabbeinu Chananel likewise understood it so; had such a distinction existed, the Geonim would not have omitted it. The Nimukei Yosef writes similarly that most commentators understand Shmuel's statement to include even a sale in the presence of all three parties. There is a further difficulty. In Ketubot, Rav Huna bar Yehoshua says that a prudent purchaser should induce the debtor with money to write a new document in the purchaser's own name. This implies that there is no other effective safeguard. If assignment in the presence of all three parties prevented forgiveness, the Gemara should have advised the purchaser to acquire it that way before witnesses and have the witnesses record the assignment. One cannot identify this with writing a new document in the purchaser's name, because a new document collects only from its later date, while an assignment of the earlier document would preserve the earlier lien. Even if this can be forced into the Gemara's words, the better remedy should have been stated expressly. Moreover, the Gemara says that Shmuel concedes that a woman who brings a debt document into the marriage cannot forgive it, and elsewhere that he concedes the same concerning a deathbed gift. This suggests that only those exceptions exist. Had assignment in the presence of all three parties been another exception, it too should have been stated. Conversely, the Ramban's view is also difficult. The Gemara repeatedly states without qualification that acquisition in the presence of all three parties is effective, yet nowhere says that the original creditor may afterward forgive the debt. Tosafot's proof from Kiddushin is persuasive. The explanations of the Ramban, Rashba, and Ritva are strained, because the Gemara should have expressly formulated the alternative that all agree regarding both a loan and a deposit and dispute Shmuel's rule. The fact that the Gemara places the Shmuel explanation earlier, regarding a documented debt, rather than in its final conclusion, indicates that Shmuel's rule applies specifically there and not to an assignment in the presence of all three parties. It therefore appears clear to me that the entire difficulty rests on an incorrect premise. A debt evidenced by a document is not acquired through the presence of all three parties at all, not even as to the underlying debt, and in this respect it is inferior to an oral loan. There is a strong reason. In an oral debt the debtor is wholly released from his relationship with the first creditor. The Sages treated the transaction as though the debtor paid the first creditor, the first creditor gave the money to the transferee, and the transferee lent it anew to the debtor. The debtor thus becomes directly obligated to the transferee and has no further dealings with the first creditor. He may therefore plead payment directly against the transferee. A documented loan is different. So long as the document remains in the hands of the first creditor or one claiming through him, the debtor is not detached from the first creditor for purposes of a plea of payment and similar defenses. Even if the first creditor transfers the document by writing and delivery, the document and its lien are not themselves acquired through the presence of all three parties. The transferee must still enforce the document through the first creditor's title. If the debtor pleads payment, the transferee answers by invoking the first creditor's document. Assignment in the presence of all three parties is therefore inapplicable. If one says that the parties' presence treats the transaction as payment to the first creditor followed by a new loan from the transferee, the old document has necessarily been extinguished. If the document was not extinguished, the debtor has not paid the first creditor and remains bound to him. There is no coherent basis for saying that the debtor is released only partially, so that he owes the money orally to the transferee while remaining bound under the document to the first creditor. Furthermore, assignment in the presence of all three parties is a rule without an articulated rationale. We must not expand it beyond its stated scope. Nothing in the Gemara extends that enactment to a document. It can apply to a documented loan only after the document is torn up and the debt thereby becomes oral. Then the debtor can be detached completely from the original creditor. So long as the document remains valid and the debtor is still bound to the first creditor under it, the assignment cannot operate even upon the underlying debt. This is also the straightforward implication of Kiddushin, which asks, regarding an oral loan, whether the dispute concerns Rav Huna's rule. It implies that with a documented loan there is no possibility that the dispute concerns that rule. The Gemara instead says that a documented loan involves the dispute between Rabbi Meir and the Sages. Why not say that it too concerns Rav Huna? The answer is that assignment in the presence of all three parties has no place in a documented loan unless the document is first destroyed and the debt becomes oral. The Gemara therefore mentions Rav Huna only with respect to an oral loan. Accordingly, the questions from Bava Kamma and Bava Metzia disappear. Assignment in the presence of all three parties has no application to a ketubah or any other document. There is plainly no concern that the woman would tear up her ketubah and then sell the underlying oral debt, apart from the separate prohibition against remaining married without a ketubah. Even were we to grant the Tosafists' premise that assignment in the presence of all three parties can affect a documented loan, it could affect only the monetary debt represented by the document, not make the transferee the holder of a new documented loan. Nowhere in the Gemara or the Geonim is it said that the document itself is acquired through this procedure. Tosafot's conclusion, that forgiveness is impossible, would require the document and its lien themselves to pass through the assignment, rendering written transfer and delivery unnecessary. The Ramban and Rashba likewise concede that an acquisition occurs until forgiveness, which raises the same question: why would written transfer and delivery still be required? One might answer that they hold the document and its lien are acquired through the three-party assignment, even against purchasers, while physical delivery is needed only as evidence. But this is not persuasive. The Gemara always says that documentary rights are acquired by writing and delivery. It nowhere says that they are acquired by assignment in the presence of all three parties together with delivery but without written transfer. The proper explanation is that the procedure can at most create an oral obligation for the principal debt, as though the transferee had lent the debtor the money. It cannot create a documented loan. Such a loan exists only when the debtor authorizes witnesses to write a document. Here he writes no new document. The earlier document was written for the original creditor, not for the transferee. Even if one imagines actual payment to the first creditor followed by a new advance from the transferee, that new advance would plainly be oral. The first document was extinguished by payment and in any event belongs to the first creditor. Written transfer and delivery therefore remain necessary for the document, and even when the debt was sold in the presence of all three parties together with writing and delivery, the first creditor may still forgive the documented obligation. This appears clear. It is possible that Tosafot, the Rosh, Rashba, Ran, and the other authorities intended the same distinction. Their statement that one who sells or gives a debt in the presence of all three parties cannot forgive it may refer only to the underlying debt, not to the continuing force of the document. When Tosafot say that Shmuel's rule applies only where the debtor was not present at the sale, they may mean that only then can forgiveness release the debtor even from the underlying debt. Their questions from the ketubah cases can also be understood accordingly. Even if forgiveness after a three-party assignment would reduce the purchaser's right to an oral claim, he could still collect from the husband because a ketubah is not collectible during the marriage and the husband therefore cannot credibly plead payment before its due time. Nor can he raise other defenses without a credible claim of payment. On this understanding, all the authorities agree that assignment in the presence of all three parties does not transfer the document and its lien. The dispute concerns only the underlying obligation. This is supported by Tosafot's proof from Kiddushin, which addresses whether the woman is betrothed through the debt itself. It does not establish that she acquires a documentary lien. The same is true of the Gemara's general statements that acquisition in the presence of all three parties is effective. They establish a direct oral obligation to the transferee, not transfer of the written instrument. The Ramban's proof from betrothal with collateral is also no proof. That case teaches that betrothal is valid even without a three-party assignment when the collateral is delivered. The Ran likewise explains this in responsum 69. The apparent contradiction is therefore resolved. With an oral loan, assignment in the presence of all three parties creates a direct obligation to the transferee and the original creditor cannot forgive it. With a documented loan, the procedure does not transfer the document or its lien. The original creditor may still forgive the documentary debt, although the transferee may retain an oral claim if the debtor expressly accepted that obligation in the three-party proceeding. This also accords with the Rambam. He rules generally that acquisition in the presence of all three parties is effective and that a woman betrothed through such a debt is betrothed, which shows that an oral debt so assigned cannot be forgiven. He later rules without qualification that the seller of a debt document may forgive it. There is no contradiction, because the latter rule applies to a document in every case. The same may be the Rif's position. Although the Ritva and Ran inferred from the Rif's omission of the relevant explanation that he permits forgiveness, that inference is not compelling. Once the Gemara states that the dispute concerns Rav Huna's rule, and we rule in accordance with Rav Huna, it follows directly that the betrothal is effective and forgiveness is impossible. The Kesef Mishneh likewise understands the Rif this way. The conclusion is as follows. The authorities commonly appear to take two absolute positions: Tosafot and the Rosh, that assignment in the presence of all three parties always prevents forgiveness, and the Ramban and Rashba, that it never prevents forgiveness. In my view, neither absolute formulation is correct. An oral loan assigned in the presence of all three parties cannot afterward be forgiven, because the debtor becomes directly obligated to the transferee. A documented loan, however, is not transferable through that procedure as a document, and in my principal view the procedure does not apply to it at all unless the document is first destroyed and the debt becomes oral. I am nevertheless hesitant to rule broadly against Tosafot, the Rosh, Ramban, Rashba, and the other great authorities. Yet one point is clear for practical halachah: the document and its lien are not acquired through the presence of all three parties. This can be reconciled even with Tosafot as explained above. Therefore, where a debt document is sold or given in the presence of all three parties and is also transferred by writing and delivery, the original creditor's later forgiveness is effective and the document is void. The Ramban, Rashba, and those who agree with them concur in this practical result. Only an oral obligation remains owing from the debtor to the purchaser, provided the debtor agreed in the presence of all three parties to pay the purchaser, as explained below in siman 126, seif katan 28. This is contrary to the Tur and the Mechaber.
