But if he says, "I reviewed my accounts and discovered that nothing was ever owed," then unless his claimed mistake is independently established by witnesses, he is not believed. Shach strongly objects. The seller has a migo that he could forgive the debt. He also challenges Baal HaTerumot. In Shaar 51, part 4, Baal HaTerumot says that a seller cannot simply claim, "I made an accounting error," while elsewhere in Shaar 28 he follows Ramban that one who has a migo to say "I paid" can be believed to say "I erred." Shach sees a contradiction. There is no contradiction. Baal HaTerumot consistently follows the view that a seller of a debt note is not ordinarily believed to say the note was already paid where a strong credibility clause exists. He relies on the presumption that a person does not knowingly sell a worthless paid instrument and make himself a thief. The migo of forgiveness is too weak against that chazakah. The same applies all the more to a claim that the original note itself was created through an accounting error. We must distinguish two kinds of "mistake." If the seller says, "The debt was originally valid, but afterward the borrower paid me, and on reviewing my account I now see that payment," this is simply the ordinary claim "paid." The fact that he explains how he realized it was paid changes nothing. But the authorities here mean a different claim: "The loan document was mistaken from the beginning. We believed the borrower owed me money, wrote the note on that basis, and I have now discovered that our original calculation was wrong. He never owed the debt at all." That is a much stronger attack on the instrument. Rama below in siman 126 rules like Tosafot and Rosh that one who made a deliberate admission cannot later say, "I was mistaken," even with a migo, because we presume a person carefully checks before creating a formal obligation. If that is true of an oral admission, all the more so of a signed loan document. A borrower does not normally execute a debt note against himself without checking the underlying account. The claim of original mistake therefore contradicts a powerful chazakah. A migo cannot overcome it. This is also why the cases cited by Rif do not prove otherwise. They concern mistakes discovered after a valid obligation had already arisen, not a claim that the solemn note itself was false from its inception. Accordingly, the ruling of Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema is correct: the seller is not believed merely by migo to say the note was originally created through an accounting error. Such a claim requires independent proof.
