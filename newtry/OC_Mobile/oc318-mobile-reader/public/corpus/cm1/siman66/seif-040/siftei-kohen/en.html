And the first view is primary, etc. It is true that this is the view of very many poskim, especially the Rosh in a responsum, who writes that one should not rule like the Rambam but like Tosafos, who brought well known proofs for their position. The Beit Yosef himself also rules below in siman 301 against the Rambam, and so rules the Shulchan Aruch below in siman 95 without qualification. Nevertheless, what can I do when in my humble opinion the Rambam's position is primary and compelled by the Gemara, while the proofs of Tosafos and the poskim who challenged him are not proofs, as I will explain. It is obvious that we do not simply follow a majority of poskim where the Gemara itself appears clearly to support the minority, especially where the poskim ruled as they did only because of their proofs. Once those proofs are rejected, the structure built upon them falls. Tosafos and the Rosh in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim, the Ran there, and other poskim write that the matter is explicitly proved by the Gemara at the end of Perek HaChovel, Bava Kamma 93a. There, a charity purse came to Pumbedisa and Rav Yosef deposited it with a certain man. The man was negligent and thieves stole it. Rav Yosef held him liable. Abaye said to him: was it not taught, "to guard," excluding funds intended for distribution to the poor? Rav Yosef answered: the poor of Pumbedisa had fixed allotments, and therefore the money was considered entrusted "to guard." They infer that whatever is excluded by this verse is excluded even from liability for negligence. In my humble opinion nothing is proved. In that case, once the property is excluded by the phrase, "to guard," but not "to distribute to the poor," it follows that distribution to the poor is not a custodial relationship at all. Since the person has no legal status of a shomer, he is like an ordinary stranger and is obviously exempt even for negligence. The Rambam himself therefore writes at the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchos She'eilah U'Pikadon that one who receives money belonging to the poor and negligently allows it to be stolen is exempt, based on the verse, "to guard," excluding money to be distributed to the poor. Here, however, the person does have the legal status of a shomer. He is merely excluded from the Torah's special oath or payment rules, while negligence itself is not excluded, as will be explained. Tosafos in Perek HaZahav, Bava Metzia 57b, also write that a negligent shomer appears exempt because the Gemara later asks from one who hires a worker to guard and does not answer that the case concerns negligence. In my humble opinion this is not a proof either. The baraita there states without qualification that liability for Shabbos rests upon him, which implies the ordinary liability of a paid shomer even where he was not negligent. Further, the case cannot be specifically negligence, because the first clause says that where no Shabbos wage is paid, he has no Shabbos liability. What does the absence of Shabbos wages matter if he is at least an unpaid shomer who should be liable for negligence? One could respond that because no Shabbos wage was paid, it is as though he accepted no Shabbos guardianship at all and is therefore like an ordinary person on Shabbos. But the Gemara can readily understand that since he accepted the object generally and it remained in his possession on Shabbos, he is at least an unpaid shomer on Shabbos. The Raavad, in his glosses at the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchos Sechirus, writes: if it is true that he is an unpaid shomer, why does he not swear concerning his claim that he was not negligent, since if he were negligent he would pay? There is no difficulty. A defendant who admits part of a claim and a defendant contradicted by one witness prove that a person can be obligated in payment upon admission without being obligated in the corresponding Torah oath. The Raavad himself continues by saying, "if you wish to say that, one who admits part of the claim proves it, for if he admits he pays, and nevertheless he is not sworn on these excluded categories." That response is certainly correct. What the Raavad then writes, that the Torah used a general and specific formulation both for an unpaid shomer and a paid shomer, and therefore just as a paid shomer's excluded categories are excluded from the basic monetary liability and not merely from the oath, so too the unpaid shomer's excluded categories must be excluded from his basic liability and not merely from the oath, is an independent argument that does not depend upon his first objection. He should have presented it directly. In truth, in my humble opinion this argument too is not compelling. Whatever the Torah affirmatively imposed in the four shomer passages, such as an unpaid shomer's oath and a paid shomer's monetary liability, is excluded by the general and specific derivation from the particular Torah obligation imposed upon that category. Negligence by an unpaid shomer, however, is not a liability created by a special verse at all. Even without a verse, logic would obligate him, because one who is negligent is akin to one who causes damage. It appears to me that this is the Rambam's reasoning. He writes at the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchos Sechirus: "It appears to me that if the shomer was negligent with slaves and similar excluded categories, he must pay. He is exempt regarding slaves, documents, and land only from the laws of theft, loss, death, and the like. If he was an unpaid shomer of movables and they were stolen or lost, he swears; with slaves, land, and documents he is exempt from the oath. Likewise, if he was a paid shomer and would ordinarily pay for theft and loss of movables, he is exempt from payment regarding these categories. But if he was negligent he must pay, because every negligent shomer is like one who causes damage. There is no distinction between the law of one who damages land and one who damages movables. This is a true ruling for those who understand, and it is proper to judge accordingly." These words are worthy of their author and of understanding. When he says, "every negligent shomer is like one who causes damage," he means, as I explained, that the negligent shomer is liable by logic even without a verse. Therefore, a paid shomer is excluded from the special Torah monetary liability, but an unpaid shomer is not excluded from negligence liability that exists independently by logic. The Raavad challenges that a negligent shomer cannot literally be a damager, because negligence in a case where the owner is with the shomer is exempt, whereas an actual damager would not be. The Rosh and Ran in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim and other poskim repeat this. In my humble opinion it is no proof. The Rambam obviously does not mean that a negligent shomer is literally classified as a tortfeasor in every respect. He means only that he is comparable to a damager insofar as logic itself imposes liability even without a verse. One might still ask how we know to exclude slaves, documents, and land only from the Torah payment rules but not from negligence, while in a case where the owner is with the shomer we exclude even negligence. The answer appears as follows. Slaves, documents, and land are excluded by the Gemara's derivation from general and specific terms: the specified example is movable and intrinsically monetary, excluding land because it is not movable, documents because their bodies are not money, slaves because they are compared to land, and consecrated property because the verse says, "his fellow's," excluding consecrated property. Since these things are outside the class to which the Torah attached its special liabilities, they are simply not mentioned by the Torah at all. There is then no source to exempt them from the independent logical liability for negligence, so we leave them to that external principle: exempt from the special Torah oath, liable for negligence because negligence is akin to damage. By contrast, the Torah expressly writes that where the owner is with the shomer, "he shall not pay." That exemption therefore applies to every liability mentioned in the shomer passages, including negligence, theft, and the other cases. The reason of the verse is that where the owner is with him, the custodian does not have ordinary shomer status at all and is more like an ordinary person. Once I have shown that the proofs of Tosafos and the poskim are not compelling, I will bring proofs for the Rambam. First, the plain language of the Mishnah, which says, "an unpaid shomer does not swear, and a paid shomer does not pay," implies that specifically the unpaid shomer does not swear, but if he admits negligence he pays. Further, the Mishnah deliberately says concerning slaves, documents, land, and consecrated property that they have no double payment and no fourfold or fivefold payment, then says, "an unpaid shomer does not swear and a paid shomer does not pay." This formulation is repeated twice, in Perek HaZahav and Perek Shevuas HaDayanim. If these categories had no law of shomrim at all, the Mishnah could simply have said that they have no law of custodians. Further, Perek Shevuas HaDayanim states, "These are the things concerning which one does not swear: slaves, documents, land, etc.," which again implies specifically that no oath is taken. Further, the Mishnah chose to teach this in tractate Shevuos in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim. If the real rule were that slaves, documents, and land have no shomer law at all, it would have belonged with the chapter discussing the four shomrim. Rather, the Mishnah means that they are excluded from the Torah oath and from the other special payments the Torah imposed. Because it was already teaching in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim that an unpaid shomer does not swear, it also mentioned that a paid shomer does not pay. There is also a strong proof from Perek HaZahav, Bava Metzia 58a. The Gemara cites the Mishnah that an unpaid shomer does not swear and a paid shomer does not pay, then raises a contradiction from townspeople who sent their shekalim and the money was stolen or lost, where the messengers swear to the treasurers. Rabbi Elazar concludes that this oath is a rabbinic enactment so that people will not treat consecrated property lightly. This implies that according to law the person is liable in payment where negligence is known, but exempt from the special oath. It is not reasonable to say that the Sages imposed monetary liability itself in order to prevent people from treating consecrated property lightly. Would they take money from one person and give it to another merely as a deterrent, even though actual negligence would be exempt? If so, in every case involving consecrated property where the Torah exempts the person they could impose payment lest people treat consecrated property lightly. Moreover, the phrase, "this oath is a rabbinic enactment," implies that only the oath was enacted by the Sages. If even the underlying payment liability were rabbinic, the Gemara should have said simply, "it is a rabbinic enactment." The specific wording indicates that if negligence is known or admitted, payment is certainly due, while the oath itself is not a Torah oath regarding consecrated property and similar excluded categories. Rashi's words also support this. On the Mishnah in Perek HaZahav he explains, "an unpaid shomer does not swear that he was not negligent, because the Torah did not obligate him to swear regarding these things; a paid shomer does not pay if they were stolen." In the Gemara's discussion of consecrated property he writes that the case concerns a paid shomer and explains that this oath is not an oath to avoid payment, because an unpaid shomer does not take an oath concerning negligence and a paid shomer does not pay for theft and loss regarding the excluded categories, as our Mishnah teaches. Rather, the oath is imposed to collect his wage. The Maggid Mishneh at the beginning of chapter 2 of Hilchos Sechirus and Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaZahav write that this is Rashi's view and that other commentators agree. The Tur below in siman 95 cites the Ramah in this direction. The Rosh in Perek Shevuas HaDayanim cites this view in the name of the Ramban and Raavad. The Bach at the end of this siman writes that the names Ramban and Raavad in the Rosh are scribal errors and should read Rambam and Ramah, because in a responsum the Rosh cites the Raavad for the opposite view and the Ritva and Maggid Mishneh cite the Ramban against the Rambam. In truth the Raavad in his glosses does disagree, but I do not think the Rosh's text is necessarily corrupt. Nimukei Yosef in Perek HaZahav and Rabbeinu Yerucham, Nesiv 14, also cite the Raavad as agreeing with the Rambam. It is therefore likely that there were two scholars known as Raavad, as is well known, or that in his commentary the Raavad agreed with the Rambam and later retracted in his glosses. As for the Ramban, although the Maggid Mishneh and Ritva cite him as disagreeing with the Rambam, in his work on garmi he writes that although one who damages such property must pay, one who was merely negligent in guarding it is exempt, because we have not found garmi liability except where one actively causes damage, while some say that a negligent shomer is considered like a damager and must pay; in any event no Torah oath is taken concerning these categories. Thus the Ramban himself did not decisively reject the Rambam's view. Semag, folio 171a, and Mordechai in Perek HaDayanim also conclude by noting that the Rambam disagrees. In any event, the primary ruling is like the Rambam for the reasons stated, and there was room left for me to clarify this point. I later found in Baal HaItur, laws of oral debts, folio 67b, the following: "These are the things concerning which one does not swear: slaves, documents, and land. This applies specifically to all obligations written in the Torah, even the oath created by one witness, although it is not written explicitly in the shomer passage. But negligence and damage are all subject to payment; if the item broke or was lost through unavoidable circumstances, he is exempt from the Torah oath." This is exactly the Rambam's position and accords with the explanation I gave above. His phrase concerning a one witness oath means that although that oath is not explicitly written in the shomer passage, it is nevertheless a Torah created oath that would not exist without a verse. Negligence and damage, however, are subject to payment by logic even without a verse, as I explained. See carefully.

And if they made a kinyan from him, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that this clause appears to refer only to a paid shomer, who can obligate himself through a kinyan to make payment. But how can an unpaid shomer become obligated through a kinyan to take an oath? The Gemara too discusses the case of a paid shomer. Darkei Moshe writes that he does not understand the distinction between paid and unpaid shomrim, because even an unpaid shomer can accept by kinyan a condition that he will swear or pay. The Sema, seif katan 90, likewise applies the rule to an unpaid shomer and rejects the Beit Yosef. In my humble opinion they did not understand the Beit Yosef's intent. Certainly, if an unpaid shomer stipulates that he will become liable to pay, the kinyan can attach to the monetary obligation. But if he merely stipulates that he will swear, to what substantive object can the kinyan attach? That is only a kinyan on words. The Beit Yosef states this explicitly in Bedek HaBayis and Kesef Mishneh: if a kinyan was made from an unpaid shomer that he will swear, it is ineffective because it is a kinyan on words. But if a kinyan was made obligating the unpaid shomer to pay like a paid shomer or borrower, he is liable. His words are correct and are compelled by the Gemara at the beginning of Bava Basra. There the Gemara asks concerning parties who made a kinyan to divide property: what does the kinyan accomplish? It is merely a kinyan on words. The Gemara answers that the kinyan was made concerning the physical directions of the property, or that each went and took possession of the other's share. All poskim agree, as explained below at the beginning of siman 157. The same follows from the Ramah and Tur below in siman 157, seif 20, who write that if a kinyan was made obligating one to construct a building, he cannot retract, because that is not merely a kinyan on words. The Ramah distinguishes where the kinyan says, "I obligate myself to give such and such," which is either an admission or a personal monetary obligation, from a kinyan merely "to give" or "to build." Where the kinyan does not attach to the obligation or the physical money but only to the act of giving, some authorities call it a kinyan on words, while others hold that "to give" or "to build" is substantive enough. But all agree that a kinyan merely to join a partnership or to walk somewhere, where there is no substantive object, is a kinyan on words. The same applies here to a kinyan merely to swear. It appears that even if the person says in the kinyan that he subjects himself to the obligation to swear, it is ineffective. The Ramah's distinction regarding "to give" between a mere act and one who subjects himself to the monetary obligation implies that where the contemplated act itself has no substance, such as dividing, joining, or walking, even language of personal subjection cannot make the kinyan effective. The Gemara at the beginning of Bava Basra likewise could have answered that they subjected themselves to divide, yet does not. The same therefore applies to an oath. This is also supported by Raavan, whom I cited above in siman 22, seif katan 4. Even according to Nimukei Yosef and Rashi there, that case is different because once a kinyan was made that one would not retract, it is as though he made a kinyan to perform whatever the adjudicator would rule. That reasoning does not apply here. The same principle also appears below in siman 245, seifim 1 and 2. The Ir Shushan here writes correctly that only a monetary payment obligation can be assumed by kinyan, such as where witnesses establish negligence by an unpaid shomer, or theft or loss by a paid shomer. But a kinyan to become obligated in an oath where there are no witnesses is ineffective, because a kinyan can create a monetary lien but not a mere obligation to perform an oath. This is the Beit Yosef's position and is primary.
They made a kinyan from him, etc. Although an unpaid shomer can verbally stipulate to assume the liability of a borrower without a kinyan, as below in siman 291, seif 27, that case is different. There he already has shomer status, and because he is within the laws of custodians he can verbally strengthen his obligation and accept the law of a borrower. Here, however, the excluded property is not subject to the ordinary Torah shomer rules at all with respect to these liabilities. No additional obligation can attach unless a kinyan is made. The Rashba and Ran reach this conclusion, as Nimukei Yosef writes in Perek HaZahav, cited by the Beit Yosef below at the beginning of siman 301. Tosafos in Perek HaZahav, Bava Metzia 58a, also write this. The Sema here, seif katan 90, and below in siman 301, seif katan 7, attributes this to the Maggid Mishneh, but it is not found there. He did not examine the Beit Yosef carefully. The reason appears to be that in siman 291, when he says, "I will be like a borrower," that legal category itself carries the Torah liability there. It resembles a statement such as, "I consider it as though I received it," in the laws of vows, explained in Yoreh Deah at the end of siman 232. Here, however, even a borrower would be exempt from the Torah's ordinary shomer liabilities regarding these excluded categories. Therefore, there is no existing legal shomer category into which his words can place him, and he cannot obligate himself without a kinyan.
