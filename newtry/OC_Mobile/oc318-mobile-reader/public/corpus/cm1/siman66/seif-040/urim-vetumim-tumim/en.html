The Gemara Gittin 53 asks: one who performs work with the red-heifer waters is exempt in human court. If damage that is not externally visible is legally considered damage, why should he not be liable in human court? In that case he intended the act for his own benefit, not in order to harm. If self-benefit exempts from garmi, why is the Gemara's question valid? There is no difficulty. The distinction concerning intent for one's own benefit applies to indirect causation, garmi. But if invisible damage is legally treated as actual damage, then the person who physically performed work with the water directly damaged it with his own hands. A direct damager is liable whether his motive was to harm or to benefit himself. Thus the Gemara asks correctly. Indeed, this sugya may support the distinction. Maharsha asks according to the opinion that invisible damage is not legally damage: for those who derive one rabbinic penalty from another, why is the person still exempt in human court? If Chazal penalize intentional invisible damage, perhaps they should impose payment here too. According to the present distinction there is an answer. Once invisible damage is not true direct damage and liability would only be a rabbinic extension comparable to garmi, one who acted for his own benefit can remain exempt. If invisible damage is true damage, however, he is a direct damager and no such distinction exists. Tosafot Bava Kamma offer another answer to the Gemara's question. They note that one who creates a blemish in an animal can sometimes be exempt because ordinary animals are not presumed destined for the altar. They therefore say the Gemara could have used the case of a blemish instead of work with red-heifer water. According to that approach, Maharsha's question need not arise in the same way. This also explains Rambam, Hilchot Chovel 7, who rules that one who performs work with the red-heifer cow can be liable, while Raavad challenges him from the view that invisible damage is not legally damage. Rambam follows Rava's principle that creating a disqualifying blemish can constitute damage where the particular animal is in fact designated for the sacred use. In the red-heifer case, once the circumstances show the animal truly stands for that purpose, the physical act is direct damage. Therefore Rambam's ruling is consistent. The supposed proof against the self-benefit exception in garmi does not stand.
