Some obligate for negligence. Raavad objects to Rambam's exemption from liability for negligence concerning land, slaves, and debt instruments. If an unpaid custodian is liable for negligence, why should he not at least swear that he did not act negligently? One cannot answer from partial admission, where admitting part creates liability yet these items are still excluded from oath. Scripture used a general and specific formulation for an unpaid custodian and likewise for a paid custodian. In a paid custodian, the exclusion concerns the principal payment obligations. The same should be true for an unpaid custodian, meaning the items were excluded from liability itself rather than merely from oath. The reasoning can be clarified through the oath that the item is not in his possession. According to Maggid Mishneh, that is the principal oath and the remaining custodial oaths are attached through oath extension. If Scripture had excluded debt instruments only from the principal oath, but not from liability for negligence, then where an oath that the item is not in his possession still applied by reason, the oath against negligence should be extended along with it. The fact that Scripture excludes even that structure shows that the exclusion reaches the substantive liability. Conversely, once we accept that even the rational oath of nonpossession is excluded, we see that the Torah's exclusion is not limited only to liabilities generated by a special verse. It can also remove duties that appear reasonable on their own. Therefore the argument that negligence liability should survive merely because it arises by reason rather than by explicit inclusion is not decisive.
Even from negligence. The Shach rejects Tosafot's proof from the baraita concerning one who hires a worker by days. The baraita says that he receives no Shabbat wage and therefore bears no Shabbat responsibility. The Shach argues that if the case involved negligence, why would there be no Shabbat responsibility? Even an unpaid custodian is liable for negligence. Tumim proposes a type of negligence applying only to a paid custodian, based on the rule that if one could have saved property by hiring help but did not, a paid custodian is liable. My brother Rabbi Yehudah correctly answered that such conduct is not true negligence. It is only analogous to theft or loss, and therefore a paid custodian is liable while an unpaid custodian is exempt. The Shulchan Aruch itself sometimes calls this "negligence" only relative to the paid custodian. The same is true of a paid shepherd who moves animals together over a bridge and one pushes another off. Tosafot explicitly treats this as akin to theft, not true negligence. Independently, Tumim's proposal cannot fit the Shabbat case. Where rescue could be obtained for free, even an unpaid custodian is liable for failing to do so. Where rescue requires hiring workers, one cannot normally hire them on Shabbat for a stated wage. Therefore the baraita's rule concerning Shabbat responsibility still could not be established through this category.
If they made a kinyan from him. Beit Yosef writes that the Gemara in Perek HaZahav says a custodian can accept liability for items otherwise excluded from the laws of custodians through a kinyan. Rambam records this in Hilchot Sechirut. Beit Yosef says this can refer only to a paid custodian's monetary liability. With an unpaid custodian, how can a kinyan obligate him to swear? Darkei Moshe asks why there should be any difference. An unpaid custodian can stipulate that he will swear or pay. The Shach correctly responds that a kinyan can create a monetary obligation to pay, but an agreement merely to swear is only a kinyan on words and is ineffective. Yet Beit Yosef's wording still needs explanation because the same objection would apply to a paid custodian if the agreement concerned only an oath. The correct reading is that Beit Yosef is commenting on Rambam. Rambam holds that an unpaid custodian is always liable for negligence in these items because negligence is like direct damage. A kinyan therefore adds nothing to his payment liability for negligence. It also cannot create an oath because that is only a kinyan on words. A paid custodian, by contrast, can use the kinyan to accept additional monetary liability for theft or loss. This explains why Beit Yosef says Rambam's ruling applies particularly to a paid custodian. Responsa of Mahara ibn Sasson derives from Beit Yosef that if an unpaid custodian verbally accepts the liability of a borrower, the acceptance can obligate him to pay for unavoidable accident through the benefit of having been trusted, even without a formal kinyan. He suggests, however, that if no witnesses establish the accident and the depositor wants him to swear the borrower's oath while the custodian says he will swear only the unpaid custodian's oath, the agreement may not create the new oath because a kinyan itself does not create an oath obligation. In my view, once the monetary undertaking to be liable as a borrower takes effect, the corresponding oath follows automatically. If witnesses establish a qualifying unavoidable accident, he would have to pay as a borrower. Therefore when he claims that the item died through use rather than through an ordinary accident, he must swear the oath appropriate to that asserted exemption. No separate kinyan on the oath is needed. This differs from land and debt instruments, which are excluded not only from ordinary custodial liability but also from the Torah structures of oath. There a kinyan on payment does not by itself create an oath where the law contains an independent exclusion from oath. Tumim writes that with consecrated property no sudar kinyan can be made because the kinyan requires the acquirer's utensil, and whose utensil can consecrated property use? If townspeople give their sudar to consecrated property, it already belongs to consecration and ordinary exchange cannot operate. As I wrote in siman 195, however, one person can supply his own utensil so that another acquires through it, just as one person may give money for another's acquisition. Kiddushin says, "Here is a maneh for you, and become betrothed to so and so," which works through the law of a guarantor. The same structure can work for consecrated property. See siman 301.
