And as to practical halachah, and so forth. Shach, seif katan 26, argues at length for the view that agav is only equivalent to physical delivery and does not replace the required writing. His arguments are not decisive. In my opinion Rosh and Tur follow their own general position. They accept Abaye's doctrine that the signing witnesses acquire rights for the recipient. Therefore the Gemara's statement, "From where do we know this? From what we learned: one may write a deed for the seller even if the purchaser is absent," can be understood of ordinary deeds, because the signing itself already creates the relevant right. Tosafot and Rosh then ask: what proof is there from that case that agav can transfer ordinary debt documents? The deed there was written directly in the purchaser's name. Obviously no additional writing should be necessary. They therefore explain that the Gemara's novelty is that agav substitutes for delivery. But if we do not generally rule like Abaye's signing doctrine, Rashba's question returns. How could the deed have been validly written in the purchaser's absence without a kinyan? The more natural interpretation is therefore Ritva's: the seller possessed an older deed from his own seller, and the new purchaser acquires that existing deed together with the land. This is an ordinary third-party document, not a deed originally written in the new purchaser's name. On that reading the Gemara plainly proves that agav can transfer an ordinary document even though it was not written in the transferee's name. The answer "agav is different" means that agav substitutes for the full ordinary transfer mechanism. Therefore, according to our halachah which does not broadly accept Abaye's signing rule, there is substantial support for Mechaber's view that agav can replace writing and delivery. At minimum, the matter is a genuine dispute, and one certainly should not rule categorically against Mechaber as Shach does. The possessor can retain property through the principle that the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Shach also asks that Rosh appears self-contradictory. At the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia, Rosh cites Rabbenu Chananel that agav does not even substitute for delivery. His proof is from Abaye's answer in Ketubot: if a ketubah is in the woman's hand, we do not fear she previously transferred it through agav. Yet in Perek HaSefinah Rosh says agav without writing does not work, which sounds as though with writing it at least substitutes for delivery. The resolution is that the two statements address different Talmudic frameworks. According to the view that ordinary debt documents require both writing and delivery, the Gemara's answer "agav is different" can elevate agav to the level of delivery. Once writing is also present, agav is enough. But according to Abaye, who holds that documents can be acquired by delivery alone, the Gemara's initial assumption can remain: agav is weaker than delivery in ordinary note transfers. Therefore Abaye can say in Ketubot that there is no concern for agav. Rabbenu Chananel's statement is thus specifically within Abaye's delivery-only framework. Rava, who holds that ordinary note transfer requires writing and delivery, can say agav replaces delivery when accompanied by writing. His challenge to Abaye about two ketubot is made even according to Abaye's own assumptions and does not prove otherwise. There is also another reason the ketubah case may differ. A woman's right to ketubah payment is not yet fully collectible during the marriage. Since it is uncertain whether she or her husband will die first, an agav lien over the future debt may not attach in the ordinary way. Rosh can agree with Rashba that the uncertainty weakens the transfer. Thus Rosh's rulings are reconcilable. The practical conclusion remains that agav, when combined with the necessary language transferring the lien, has substantial authority as a replacement for physical delivery, and Mechaber's ruling cannot be rejected.
