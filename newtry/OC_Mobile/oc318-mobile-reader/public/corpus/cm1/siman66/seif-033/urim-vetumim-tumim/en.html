The purchaser then recovers from him. All the commentators explain that the purchaser recovers from the seller. The borrower is discharged. Bach, however, introduces a new rule. If beit din has already ruled that the borrower must pay the purchaser, then the purchaser's claim against the borrower becomes independently fixed by the judicial decision. The original seller can no longer forgive. Therefore, if the borrower afterward pays the seller, the borrower must pay the purchaser a second time. Shach strongly rejects this. He says R. Natan's lien is irrelevant. The purchaser's entire right arises from the sale of the original debt. What new proprietary power can a court judgment add? As a matter of general principle, Shach's objection is strong. Nevertheless, Raavad appears to support Bach's result from another direction. Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 51, part 4, cites Raavad that even if the purchaser received a proper writing transferring the note and all its liens, he cannot compel the borrower to pay unless the writing also authorizes him expressly to "litigate and collect for yourself." One of Raavad's proofs is the Gemara's advice: if the purchaser is clever, he should pay the borrower a small amount and have the borrower write a new note directly in the purchaser's name. Gidulei Terumah asks: the Gemara's reason for obtaining a new note is to prevent the original seller from forgiving. How does this prove the borrower otherwise could say, "You are not my litigant"? The answer can be exactly Bach's insight. If the purchaser could simply sue the borrower and obtain a final judgment ordering payment, and if that judgment itself made the debt independent and immune from forgiveness, then there would be no need to pay the borrower for a new note. The purchaser could achieve the same security merely by litigating to judgment. Raavad therefore infers that before a proper direct authorization, the borrower can say, "You are not my litigant." Beit din will not enter a final personal judgment in the purchaser's favor. That is why the purchaser must obtain the new document. This suggests that if a valid final judgment were somehow entered directly obligating the borrower to the purchaser, forgiveness by the original seller might indeed cease to matter. Bach's result would then follow in that limited setting. There is no difficulty from Bava Kamma, where a woman's injury claim is problematic because she can sell her ketubah to the injured party and then forgive it to her husband. Why not simply have beit din order the husband to pay the injured party, thereby preventing forgiveness? A ketubah obligation is conditional. If the wife dies before her husband, the ketubah may never become payable. The court cannot presently enter an unconditional final money judgment against the husband. The purchaser's right remains dependent on the original ketubah structure. There may also be the additional concern that a direct present lien on the husband while the marriage continues would effectively leave the wife without a ketubah or distort its protective function. Thus that case does not refute Bach. Still, outside Raavad's framework I do not find other authorities expressly adopting Bach's doctrine. A court judgment ordinarily determines an existing right rather than creating a wholly new debt independent of its source. Therefore, for practical halachah I would follow Shach's caution unless the judgment was expressly structured as a new independent obligation. Bach has respectable support in Raavad's reasoning, but not enough to establish a general rule.
