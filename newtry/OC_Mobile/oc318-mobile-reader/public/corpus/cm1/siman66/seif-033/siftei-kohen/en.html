The person who forgives is exempt by reason of the forgiveness itself, for his act is no worse than one who burns another's document, as explained below in siman 386, seif 2. The Beit Yosef cites here a responsum of the Rashba stating that where a purchaser of a debt document is met by the debtor's claim, 'I paid the original creditor, let him come and swear that I did not pay him,' the debtor's claim is ineffective. Since the document remains in the purchaser's possession, there is a presumption that it was not paid. Even if the seller comes and admits that the debtor paid him, he is not believed. If the seller has money, he should return what he received; if he lacks money, we suspect collusion. A person is not believed where his statement prejudices others, as explained in Ketubot. At first glance this responsum is difficult. It would mean that the Rashba disputes the Rambam, Tur, and Mechaber above in seif 11; the Baal HaIttur, Ramban, Rosh, Ran, Tur, and Mechaber above in seif 16; and many other authorities, all of whom hold that the seller is believed to say the debt was paid because he could instead forgive it. Maharam ibn Chaviv likewise understood the responsum as disagreeing with the authorities who rule that if the seller refuses to swear, the purchaser loses, and as holding that the original creditor is not compelled to swear. It is implausible, however, that the Rashba would dispute all those authorities, especially when their reasoning is clear. The Gemara at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia also implies that the seller is believed to say the debt was paid because he could forgive it, and the Nimukei Yosef there indicates that the Rashba agrees. The responsum must therefore concern a case where the seller was independently indebted to the purchaser, so that the debt document was subject to the purchaser's claim under Rabbi Natan's rule. In such a case the seller cannot forgive the document and cannot assert that it was paid, as I explained above in seif 26, seif katan 87. This is why the Rashba concludes that a person is not believed where his statement prejudices others, citing the chapter HaIshah Shenitarmelah, which discusses Rabbi Natan's lien. This appears correct.
