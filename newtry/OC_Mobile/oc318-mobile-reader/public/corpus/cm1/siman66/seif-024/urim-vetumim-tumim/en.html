The simple meaning, as Maggid Mishneh and most authorities explain, is that if the possessor says, "It is true the borrower paid, but I never sold or gave you this note," he must take a hesset oath. There is an ordinary monetary dispute between him and the claimant, so hesset plainly applies. Beit Yosef, however, sought to derive a different law, cited in Rama's responsum siman 55. Suppose Levi sues Shimon on a debt note. Shimon says, "I already paid," and claims that the note now in Reuven's hand somehow fell from us. Beit Yosef says Shimon must take hesset. I do not understand this. The classic case where a note is found in another person's hand and the debtor is believed concerns a third party who does not know the note's status. Kesef Mishneh says this explicitly in explaining Rambam, Hilchot Malveh chapter 16. Here, by contrast, both competing parties claim ownership of the note. According to each side's version, the note is still a live unpaid instrument in someone's hands. Why should the debtor overcome a written note with only hesset? I found Tiferet Shmuel to Maharshal raising essentially the same objection. The Mishnah can be read far more simply. The clause continues the preceding law that Reuven, the possessor of the note, is believed against Levi, the alleged transferor, and can collect from the borrower if the borrower is willing to pay him. Levi then says to Reuven: "You yourself claim I never sold or gave this note to you. You are holding my document without right." Reuven must take a hesset oath that his claim of ownership is true, and is thereby discharged from Levi's personal demand. Thus the phrase "he takes a hesset oath" refers to Reuven's dispute with Levi, not to the borrower's ability to defeat the written note. This is both doctrinally correct and the natural reading.
