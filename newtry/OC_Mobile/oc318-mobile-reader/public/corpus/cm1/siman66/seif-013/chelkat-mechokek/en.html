Specifically if she wrote to him, "I have received it." The Rama added the word "specifically" based on Mordechai, chapter HaChovel, who says that the rule "with respect to her husband she certainly waived" applies specifically where she writes, "I have received it." A mere oral waiver is not effective while she remains married. Although regarding an ordinary debt document Mordechai in Sanhedrin rules that an oral waiver is effective even if the creditor retains the document, and brings a clear proof from the rule that one who sells a debt document may later waive it, one can distinguish a ketubah held by a woman living with her husband. So long as she has not written "I have received it," she may say, "I was merely joking with you and spoke idle words." After the husband's death, Mordechai implies that even a simple waiver of a ketubah is effective, for he brings proof from a woman who remained silent for twenty-five years without claiming her ketubah. Still, Mordechai's source for distinguishing a married woman's ketubah from other debts requires precision. Ketubot 56a states Rabbi Yehudah's view that if he wishes he may write a virgin a document for two hundred and she writes, "I have received one hundred from you." The Gemara infers that writing is required, whereas an oral condition is ineffective because it contradicts a Torah entitlement. But immediately afterward the Gemara cites Rabbi Yose, who holds that he may stipulate, and Rashi explains that even orally the condition is valid. Ketubot 57a discusses whether their dispute concerns the beginning or the end of the arrangement. Rambam, Hilchot Ishut chapter 16, rules that one who waives her ketubah needs neither a kinyan nor witnesses, just like other waivers, and mere words suffice. See below, siman 105. The Bach attempts a reconciliation, but his words are not clear.
