Through a kinyan of chalipin, and so forth. Many were surprised that Tur ultimately cites Ramban while Mechaber states the law as though there were no dispute. Ramban himself, in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 51, part 3, writes that Raavad's view that chalipin can substitute for delivery appears reasonable, and Ri Migash's position also tends that way. Tosafot in Bava Kamma remain uncertain. Regarding Tur, however, the difficulty is not so severe. It is true that Rosh in Perek HaSefinah writes that debt documents are not acquired through land-based acquisition without the necessary writing, "and likewise not through a kinyan without writing the document." This sounds as though a kinyan sudar can stand in place of delivery if the writing is present. Rosh also writes in responsum klal 75 that Rashbam's view, under which documents are acquired by delivery alone, implies that chalipin works as well. Why then would Tur omit his father's apparent view? Ramban himself explains that the issue depends on what "delivery" means. If delivery of documents requires not merely ordinary meshichah or hagbahah, but an additional hand-to-hand transfer from the seller to the buyer, then chalipin cannot substitute. Why should a symbolic kinyan be stronger than physical meshichah or hagbahah, which are themselves insufficient unless accompanied by that special delivery? This is Ramban's argument against Raavad. Rosh rules that small documents require hagbahah and large bundles require meshichah, together with the special document-delivery element. If so, his own position is difficult to reconcile with broad use of chalipin. Tur can therefore read Rosh consistently with Ramban and reject chalipin. There is another proof from the Tosafot and Rosh interpretation of the Gemara's question: "Once he acquires the field, he acquires the deed wherever it is." Tosafot ask that the deed there was written in the purchaser's own name, so ordinary writing should already be satisfied. They understand the Gemara initially to reason that land-based acquisition is a weak kinyan, yet it works in this special case; therefore ordinary delivery, a stronger acquisition, should certainly work in other debt-note transfers. The Gemara answers that acquisition through land is itself especially strong. This discussion assumes that chalipin does not simply equal delivery. If chalipin always substituted for delivery, there would have been little reason to focus on whether land-based acquisition was weaker or stronger than delivery. Symbolic acquisition would already provide an obvious parallel. Thus the Gemara's final answer can preserve the initial assumption that chalipin is not enough even though agav is a special strong mode. Ramban says explicitly that Raavad, who treats chalipin like delivery, must interpret the Gemara's answer differently, more like Rif: agav replaces both writing and delivery, not merely delivery. Tosafot and Rosh follow Rabbenu Chananel's approach, and that points away from Raavad. Rosh's phrase that "a kinyan without writing does not help" can therefore refer not specifically to kinyan sudar but to other ordinary acquisitions such as meshichah or hagbahah. His point is that no acquisition of the physical paper alone transfers the lien without the required writing. According to Rabbenu Tam, however, who holds writing and delivery are of Torah force, another question appears. We rule like R. Yochanan that Torah law recognizes money rather than meshichah as the basic acquisition of movables. Why should physical delivery of the paper have Torah significance at all? One could answer that the relevant Torah acquisition is chalipin, which does work by Torah law. Chazal later recognized meshichah for movable property, and therefore the special delivery of documents can operate within their enactment. This is not decisive. Mechaber is more difficult. Below he appears to rule that agav alone can substitute for writing and delivery, and in seif 9 he entertains a view that document delivery need not be literally hand to hand. If actual special delivery is not indispensable, why should chalipin be worse than other valid acquisitions? Ramban's opposition to chalipin seems tied to the stricter view that delivery must be hand to hand in addition to meshichah or hagbahah. If Mechaber does not accept that premise, Raavad's position becomes considerably stronger. Tosafot's uncertainty can also be understood conceptually. Ordinary meshichah certainly acquires the physical paper, but the difficult part is transfer of the land lien. A special hand-to-hand act may symbolize transfer of that lien. Chalipin, however, is a kinyan that can acquire land itself. It may therefore be even more suited than meshichah to transfer a document carrying a land lien. That gives real force to Raavad's view. For this reason the matter must be treated as a genuine dispute among major authorities. The possessor can therefore say kim li in accordance with the view that chalipin is effective.
