A seller or donor now holds a deed of sale or gift and says: "This deed was transferred back to me with proper writing and delivery, but the transfer document was lost," or, "It was transferred to me through agav." Is he believed? At first glance, perhaps everyone should believe him. Bava Batra 168b discusses Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel, who says a replacement deed of sale should not be written because documents can be acquired by delivery alone. Perhaps the purchaser returned the original deed to the seller and thereby transferred the field back. If a replacement is now issued to the purchaser, the seller will be harmed. Tosafot ask according to Abaye, who generally requires a holder to prove acquisition: what harm is there? If the seller later holds the original deed, he will still have to prove that the purchaser transferred it back to him. Without proof he gains nothing. And if he has proof, then a replacement deed held by the purchaser should not defeat that proof. Tosafot answer that in this special setting even Abaye does not require proof. Why not? We do not suspect that the deed merely fell from the purchaser, and the purchaser would not normally deposit his deed with the seller, since the seller could suppress it. Therefore possession of the deed by the seller itself strongly proves a retransfer. From Abaye we can infer the same principle under our halachah. Even though ordinary note transfer requires writing and delivery, where the seller now holds the very sale deed he had previously delivered, the normal explanations of possession are absent. We do not suspect loss, and a purchaser would not deposit such a deed with the seller. Therefore the seller's possession can be strong evidence that the deed was transferred back. One might object: according to our system, perhaps the purchaser merely delivered the deed without the required transfer writing. Everyone knows writing and delivery are required, so an ineffective bare delivery is unlikely to represent a completed transfer. But Tosafot in Perek HaSefinah already say that where the public knows a transfer requires formal writing, people ordinarily do not intend an ineffective delivery. This supports rather than weakens the inference that, if the deed truly returned to the seller, a proper transfer probably accompanied it. Could the seller instead say the field was transferred to him through agav? That claim is more difficult because agav is less common and normally leaves independent evidence through the land transaction. We generally do not build possession presumptions on uncommon agav transfers. The matter therefore depends on the exact claim. Ramban in his chiddushim to Bava Batra appears not to accept Tosafot's special answer and may require proof even in this setting. If so, the practical law remains doubtful. I have not found the case expressly resolved by the later authorities. It should therefore be treated as a genuine safek.
