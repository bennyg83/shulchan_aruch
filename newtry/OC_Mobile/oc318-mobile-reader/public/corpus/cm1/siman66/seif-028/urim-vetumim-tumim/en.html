Rosh in Perek Get Pashut, preceded by Ri Migash, writes that if land itself was already acquired through a kinyan, and the written deed serves only as evidence, the deed cannot afterward be transferred through the ordinary writing-and-delivery formula. The underlying acquisition already occurred independently, and the paper is merely proof. Ramban in his chiddushim to Bava Batra expressly disagrees. He says he sees no reason for the distinction. Even if the field itself was acquired by kinyan, the document contains legal rights and evidentiary force that can themselves be transferred. Tosafot also appear to disagree with Rosh. They explain Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's refusal to write a replacement sale deed by saying that the purchaser may have returned the original deed to the seller and thereby retransferred the field. If the purchaser now receives a second deed, he could falsely say that the first deed was merely one of several deeds originally written by the seller. What kind of sale deed is involved? If there was no kinyan in the underlying sale, then according to R. Elazar witnesses should not write the deed for the seller until the purchaser is present and it is delivered. How then could the seller plausibly have had several original deeds in his possession? The case therefore appears to involve a sale completed by kinyan, with the deed written as evidence. Yet Tosafot still assume that delivery of that evidentiary deed can later transfer the field back. That contradicts Rosh's distinction. Rosh may understand the sugya differently. He can follow Tosafot's alternate explanation: even if the seller has the original deed, the purchaser may later claim, "I never transferred the field back. That paper was merely another copy originally written for you." On that reading the case need not prove that an evidentiary deed itself retransfers ownership. Ramban rejects that explanation at length. Another possibility is based on Rosh's responsum, klal 94. There he says the baraita can concern a purchaser who has witnesses that the original deed was lost, but lacks witnesses that it was destroyed by fire. If a replacement is issued and the original later reappears, the purchaser could possess two deeds and use one improperly. On this approach, Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel's concern does not prove an evidentiary deed is itself transferable. Thus Rosh can be internally consistent. Still, Ramban explicitly disagrees, Tosafot appear to support him, and Tur cites R. Yeshayah as disagreeing as well. The dispute is therefore balanced among major authorities. There is also a conceptual difficulty with Rosh if, as Sema explains in several places, a standard kinyan is "destined for writing" only if a deed is eventually written. In a loan made with kinyan, the land lien may still depend practically on the later document. Why should a sale deed written after a land kinyan be treated as purely evidentiary while a loan document under kinyan retains substantive transfer value? And according to our halachah, a kinyan can support collection even if no deed is ever written. Then the later deed is indeed evidentiary in both cases. If an evidentiary debt note can nevertheless be transferred, why should a sale deed be different? For these reasons, Mechaber should not be read as eliminating the dispute. The matter remains a safek din among major authorities.
