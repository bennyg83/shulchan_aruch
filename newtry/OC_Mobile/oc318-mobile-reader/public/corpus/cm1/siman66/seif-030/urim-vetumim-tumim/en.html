The purchaser may recover from him. Sema explains that this means from the assets inherited from the seller's estate. If the seller died leaving no assets, what property of the heir would create liability? Shach disagrees and says the heir himself is liable because he is a tortfeasor under dinei de-garmi. He elaborates below in siman 86. But Shach contradicts his own position above in siman 47. There he says that if the purchaser has no recourse against the seller, the seller is not even believed to say the note was paid and there is no oath. Why impose an oath where, even if the seller openly admitted payment, his words would have no legal consequence? If the seller dies leaving nothing, the same should apply to the heir. An admission by the heir that his father had received payment cannot create personal liability merely because the heir speaks truthfully. More fundamentally, the heir is not a tortfeasor at all. At most he withholds helpful testimony. That is like one who suppresses testimony, who is exempt in human court. What obligation does the son have to his father's purchaser if he inherited nothing? If he says, "I know the borrower paid my father in my presence," he certainly should not have to lie merely to protect the purchaser. Since he inherited no estate, his truthful testimony creates no personal debt. Why then should refusing to swear make him pay personally? The proper rule is Sema's. Where the father left assets, the seller's estate can be liable because the refusal to swear supports the conclusion that the borrower's claim is true, meaning the father received payment and thereby harmed the purchaser. The estate therefore answers for that loss. If the borrower claims instead that he paid the heir personally after the father's death, and the heir refuses to swear, then Shach's reasoning can have force. The inference is that the heir himself received the money. Having accepted payment on a debt whose collection right belonged to the purchaser, he can be personally liable. Absent such a personal receipt, however, the heir should not pay from his own property. Indeed, were it not that the authorities do not state the matter explicitly, I would say that where the seller left no estate, the heir should not even need to take the special oath. He is no longer interested in whether the note was paid, since no estate is exposed. He resembles an ordinary witness who can simply testify what he knows without an oath. Perhaps one can answer that the heir retains some residual personal connection to the debt because the original creditor's personal lien descends to him, which is why he may have the power to forgive. That may prevent treating him as a completely detached witness. This point requires study. But the main law remains as Sema states: ordinary liability of the heir is from the father's estate, not from the heir's personal assets.
