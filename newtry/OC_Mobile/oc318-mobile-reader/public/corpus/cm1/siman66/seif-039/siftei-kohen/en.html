One does not take an oath concerning notes, etc. The Maggid Mishneh, in the second chapter of the laws of hiring, writes in the name of the commentators that the same exemption applies to the oath that the entrusted item is not in the custodian's possession. The Ran in Shevuot cites the Rashba in the name of some French teachers that an unpaid custodian is exempt even from the oaths that he did not misuse the item and that it is not in his possession, because Scripture exempted these items from all oaths. The Ran, however, does not accept that this proves exemption from the rabbinic oath that the item is not in his possession, since that oath was instituted lest the custodian desire the item for himself. In my opinion, the Rashba referred only to the Torah oath that the item is not in his possession. The Gemara enumerates three Torah oaths imposed upon a custodian: that he was not negligent, that he did not misuse the item, and that it is not in his possession. When exemption from payment depends upon his claim that it was stolen or lost, he must swear by Torah law that it is no longer in his possession. Concerning notes and the other excluded items, the Rashba teaches that Scripture exempted him from all such Torah oaths. His language, 'Scripture exempted him from all oaths,' plainly addresses Torah oaths, and he couples this oath with the oath against misuse, which is certainly a Torah oath. But the separate oath instituted by Rav Huna in Bava Metzia, lest the custodian have set his eyes upon the object, remains applicable even according to the Rashba. As a matter of law, although Maimonides appears to exempt him from every oath, including the rabbinic oath, the principal ruling is like the Ran, as the Bach also rules below in siman 95, that he must swear rabbinically that the item is not in his possession. The Tur's language may likewise concern only the Torah obligation, in opposition to the Raavad, who appears to hold that the oath is required even by Torah law. The Raavad's reasoning is that although Scripture created the oaths against negligence and misuse, common reason itself requires an oath that the object is not still in the custodian's possession, for otherwise no depositor could safely entrust property. Even if the Tur rejects a Torah obligation, he may agree that the rabbinic oath remains. Indeed, since rabbinic oaths may be imposed even concerning slaves, notes, and land, and since the Sages required such an oath even when the custodian pays, lest he desire the object, it is all the more reasonable to require it when he does not pay. Otherwise, anyone entrusted with a note could simply claim that it was lost and escape entirely.
If there was a definite claim. The Beit Yosef cites a responsum of the Ritva concerning one who claims that he returned entrusted notes. The question is whether he must swear, since he is believed through a migo that he could have claimed that they were lost through unavoidable accident, and on such a claim no oath would be imposed because the claimant has no definite knowledge. The Ritva records a dispute among his teachers, but his words indicate that the principal view requires an oath, and this is also my conclusion. Maimonides likewise rules that if one says, 'You gave me a note,' and the defendant denies it, the defendant takes a rabbinic oath, whereas if he admits receiving it but says it was lost, he is exempt even from that oath. Although the denier has a migo that he could have claimed loss, he must nevertheless swear. The Mechaber's unqualified ruling that a rabbinic oath is imposed when there is a definite claim also indicates this. According to what I wrote in the preceding seif katan, however, one who claims that he returned the note must certainly take at least a rabbinic oath, because even had he claimed unavoidable loss he would have had to swear rabbinically that the note is not in his possession. The broader practical conclusion is that where a definite claim ordinarily generates a rabbinic oath, the defendant must swear even if he has a migo to a claim that, because it would face only a doubtful allegation, would not independently require such an oath.
