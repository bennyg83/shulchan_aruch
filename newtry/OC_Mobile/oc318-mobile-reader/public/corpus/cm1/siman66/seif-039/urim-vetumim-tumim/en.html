Rashba rules that if the original creditor forgives the sold debt, the purchaser does not have to return the physical paper itself without receiving its paper value. The paper remains acquired to him to that extent. Rashba proves this from Kiddushin. One baraita says that if a man betroths a woman with a debt note owed by another person, R. Meir says she is betrothed and the Sages say she is not. The Gemara offers several explanations: whether documents require writing and delivery, whether one must say "acquire it and all its liens," whether the seller can forgive, or whether the woman relies on the note despite the possibility of forgiveness. Another baraita says that if he betroths her with a debt note, R. Meir says she is not betrothed, while the Sages say the paper is appraised and if it is worth a perutah she is betrothed. The Gemara asks that R. Meir contradicts himself. Rashba observes that the Sages also appear to contradict themselves. If in the first baraita they say she is not betrothed because the seller can forgive and she therefore does not rely on the note, then why in the second baraita do they say the paper value itself can create kiddushin? If forgiveness required immediate return of the paper for no value, she would not be able to rely even on the paper. Therefore forgiveness must leave the purchaser with a valid right in the physical paper itself until its value is paid. Shach attempts to reject this proof. He says the first baraita can be explained through one of the other Talmudic approaches, such as a case where the document was delivered without the required writing. Under that explanation, everyone can agree the woman trusts the transaction and the issue is only whether she acquired the debt. The paper itself may still be acquired, as Ritva and Bach say in some failed-transfer cases. Then the second baraita's paper-value kiddushin creates no contradiction. But Shach did not see Rashba's chiddushim to Kiddushin. Rashba himself asks why the Gemara does not resolve R. Meir's contradiction by saying the first baraita involved proper writing and delivery while the second involved delivery without writing. He answers that the Gemara prefers to interpret the second baraita in a way compatible with every explanation of the first. Once that is accepted, Rashba's proof from the Sages is strong. Under the explanation that the first baraita turns on Shmuel's forgiveness rule, the second baraita must still be consistent. That requires the paper itself to remain with the purchaser. One cannot answer that the contradiction is resolved only whichever way one chooses: if the first baraita turns on forgiveness, then R. Meir is easy but the Sages are difficult; if it turns on defective transfer, the Sages are easy but R. Meir is difficult. The Gemara specifically states only that R. Meir contradicts himself. Rashba is therefore right that the solution must fit the Sages as well. There is also a conceptual point. If the physical paper in the creditor's hand functions as collateral for the debt, then transfer of the note gives the purchaser at least the creditor's possessory interest in that collateral. Even if forgiveness extinguishes the principal debt, the purchaser's possessory right in the paper need not vanish without compensation for its value. Rashba expressly describes the paper as a type of collateral. This also answers Shach's question on Tur in Even HaEzer siman 28. Tur rules that where a woman is betrothed with a properly transferred debt note, the value of the paper itself can matter. Shach asks: the paper ultimately belongs to the borrower, so how can its value create kiddushin? Tur earlier rules that if the purchaser must return the paper after the debt is discharged, the borrower must pay him the paper value. That right gives the purchaser a genuine monetary interest, analogous to collateral. It can support kiddushin. There is admittedly a further issue because Tur holds that collateral given at the time of a loan does not always support kiddushin in the same way as later collateral. Perhaps this paper-collateral is different because the very structure of a written loan is that the creditor is entitled to retain the paper until satisfaction. The borrower effectively gives him that possessory interest as part of the document's creation. The authorities who say the purchaser acquires only the right to use the paper, not its physical substance, may explain the Gemara differently. But Rashba's position is strong and should not be rejected. Shach also cites Rosh's mention of the Provencal scholars who required return of the paper after forgiveness. But Rosh does not necessarily endorse their position merely by quoting it. And if the forgiver pays only the purchase price rather than the full debt value, Rosh's separate discussion of paper-value damages can relate to a person who destroys another's note rather than to forgiveness itself. Thus the practical halachah remains at least doubtful, and Rashba and Tur provide substantial authority that the purchaser retains the paper until its value is paid.
