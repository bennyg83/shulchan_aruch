One does not swear. Debt instruments are exempt even from the Torah oath that an item is not in the custodian's possession. Raavad, however, in Hilchot To'en chapter 5, holds that this particular oath remains. The Shach explains that an oath denying present possession is required by reason even without a special verse. Scripture therefore excluded debt instruments only from custodial oaths that arise solely by Torah decree, such as the oath that there was no negligence. This explains Raavad's language: "This is astonishing, for perhaps it is in his possession and he is denying it." Mishneh LaMelech cites Rashba, responsum 980, who rules like Raavad and adds that the custodian must also swear that he did not commit misappropriation. I find that difficult. Even if he did misuse the instrument, why should he be liable? Debt instruments are excluded from the law of robbery, just as land is, and one who robs land and a river later washes it away is exempt. Perhaps misappropriation by a custodian is more stringent than ordinary robbery because the item first came to him for safekeeping. This requires study. Ran in Perek Shevuat HaDayanim cites French authorities who hold that an unpaid custodian of documents is exempt even from the oath that the item is not in his possession and from the oath against misappropriation, because Scripture excluded these items from all custodial oaths.
