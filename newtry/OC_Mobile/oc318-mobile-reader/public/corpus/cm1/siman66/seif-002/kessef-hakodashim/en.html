See Shach, seif katan 18. In siman 66 the Shach inclines to the view that partners who divide promissory notes between themselves do not need formal writing and delivery. This may be because of bereirah, as though it is retroactively clarified that each note always belonged to the partner who ultimately received it. Although the general rule is that there is no bereirah in Torah law, in the first chapter of Nedarim we rule like Rabbi Eliezer ben Yaakov that partners have bereirah in jointly owned property, each saying that he enters his own portion. The commentators explain this principle, and perhaps the same reasoning applies to notes. This would also explain why the other partner cannot later forgive the debt: the division is effective by Torah law even with respect to the personal lien of the debtor, retroactively. If, however, one partner originally sold on credit and the debt entered the partnership only because the other partner later remained silent or consented, and that note was eventually allocated to the other partner, retroactive bereirah may not apply because at the beginning the debt belonged only to the partner who made the sale. On this basis the same may apply even to an oral debt. In a written debt, mere agreement of the partners appears sufficient and neither can retract even while the note is held by a third person; neither can later forgive it. Urim VeTumim and Netivot HaMishpat in siman 101 similarly write that an oral debt can be assigned through beit din collection without formal writing and delivery. Although one might distinguish written debts because handing a note to beit din can itself make it resemble collected property, while an oral debt needs a prosbul for the law of "do not press," they nevertheless make no distinction. The Shach's statement in siman 66, seif katan 45, that our customary notes can be acquired by writing and delivery because they are instruments of transfer, means only where no other acquisition was made. Where it is known that a kinyan sudar was made, as is customary, or money was given as the principal mode of acquisition, the past-tense language in the instrument confirms what was already effected. Language mentioning both instrument and money can indicate that both are intended where the transaction itself so provides. The same analysis applies in transfers involving non-Jews.
