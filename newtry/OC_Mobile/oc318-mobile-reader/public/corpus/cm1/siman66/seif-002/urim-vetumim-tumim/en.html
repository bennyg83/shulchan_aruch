Only by writing and delivery. Shach cites Ritva in Kiddushin that a dedication to the poor is treated as though written and delivered. Therefore, if one dedicates a debt document to the poor and says, "Acquire it and all its liens," the poor acquire it as though writing and delivery had been performed. Ritva indeed says this repeatedly, and R. Betzalel Ashkenazi cites Raah similarly. However, Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah siman 258, seif 6, rules in the name of Rashba that one who dedicates a debt document to the poor still needs writing and delivery just as in an ordinary private transfer. Rashba explains that the rule "a statement to the sacred is like delivery to an ordinary person" applies to true hekdesh, not to ordinary tzedakah. Shach himself there adds that the original creditor can forgive the note, exactly as in an ordinary sale. I am surprised he did not mention that explicit ruling here. In truth, though, the issue is a major dispute. Beit Yosef generally follows Rashba as though the point were settled. Therefore below in siman 225, where Tur says a pledge to the poor is like a declaration to hekdesh and operates as delivery, Beit Yosef explains that Tur means only that the person is obligated by neder. It is not literally a complete proprietary acquisition by mere words. Rav Hai Gaon, Rabbenu Chananel, and Baal HaMaor in Bava Kamma likewise distinguish ordinary charity from full hekdesh. Raah and Ritva disagree, and Rosh in responsum klal 58, siman 5, also appears to hold that a declaration to the poor can itself effect acquisition: whatever would require a kinyan between private people can be acquired for tzedakah by words alone. Ran in Kiddushin and Nedarim says this explicitly as well. Even Rashba himself is not entirely consistent. R. Betzalel Ashkenazi cites a manuscript responsum of Rashba taking the opposite position, and in Rashba's chiddushim to Nedarim 29 he writes explicitly that one who dedicates property to the poor for a later time cannot retract because a statement to the sacred is like delivery to an ordinary person. He applies the same rule to a coin or field dedicated to charity. The reconciliation, in my opinion, is that Rashba's restrictive rule applies specifically to debt rights. The Gemara leaves unresolved whether the legal category of hekdesh fully applies to tzedakah. The question is connected to whether there is yad for tzedakah. Because it is unresolved, we rule stringently in a Torah-level prohibition. Therefore, where the underlying private acquisition is of Torah force, a declaration to charity may be treated stringently as a binding acquisition. But ordinary sale of a debt document is itself only rabbinic. Even if we say that declaring it to charity is equivalent to writing and delivery, the resulting acquisition remains rabbinic. Since whether tzedakah receives the full hekdesh rule is unresolved, we have a doubt about a rabbinic acquisition, and a rabbinic doubt is treated leniently. Thus the poor do not acquire the debt merely through the declaration. This removes the contradiction within Rashba. It can also reconcile Rosh. In klal 58 he speaks of ordinary property where the relevant acquisition is of Torah force. In klal 12 he writes that even a true vow to hekdesh can be released by a sage and all the more so a vow to the poor, which sounds as though tzedakah is not fully equal to hekdesh. There is no contradiction if tzedakah is treated as an unresolved extension of hekdesh. Ritva, who says expressly that even a debt note is acquired by declaration to the poor, follows his own view that sale of documents is of Torah force, or else he may hold that the Talmudic doubt about tzedakah was effectively resolved through an "if you say" structure. But according to the majority view that document sale is rabbinic and the tzedakah question remains unresolved, Rashba's ruling is primary.
Therefore, the practical halachah should follow Shulchan Aruch and Rashba: a debt document is not acquired by the poor through mere declaration; writing and delivery or another effective mode of transfer is required. A further proof appears from the law of asmachta. Rama below in siman 207 rules like Mordechai that asmachta does not apply to a tzedakah undertaking, because declaration to charity is treated like delivery in an effective manner. At first this seems inconsistent with Rama's failure to disagree in Yoreh Deah siman 258 regarding debt documents. The distinction is exactly as stated. In an ordinary tzedakah undertaking, the underlying private acquisition can be of Torah force, so the possible hekdesh-equivalent rule is applied stringently. In transfer of a debt right, the entire private acquisition is rabbinic, and the unresolved extension to tzedakah is therefore not enough to create the transfer. This is the proper practical distinction.
