Where the seller said nothing to him. This implies that if the seller said that the debt had been paid, he would be believed even after the sale to say that it had been paid before the sale. I reach this conclusion at length below in seif katan 55. See there.
He too, etc. The Sema explains that both the purchaser and the seller swear, because on behalf of heirs we advance every claim their father could have made. Had their father been alive, he could have claimed that he paid either the seller or the purchaser. Therefore both must swear because of the uncertainty. If the debtor himself is alive, however, only the person against whom he specifically claims with certainty that he paid must swear. The Bach likewise explains the phrase, "he too must swear," to mean that just as there is a doubt whether he paid the purchaser, so there is a doubt whether he paid the seller. In my humble opinion, the plain explanation is different. It refers back to the preceding oath imposed on the purchaser, that the purchaser must swear that he does not know that the debtor paid the seller, because perhaps he knows that the seller was paid. The seller must also swear that he was not paid. The seller's oath does not exempt the purchaser, because perhaps the seller swears falsely while the purchaser knows that the debt was paid. Since the purchaser now comes to collect from the orphans, he too must swear. When the debtor himself is alive, by contrast, the purchaser need not swear, because the debtor does not claim that the purchaser knows that he paid the seller. Nevertheless, it appears that the purchaser's oath should also include that he himself was not paid by the debtor. According to this, even if the orphans admit that the purchaser was not paid, for example they know that he never left their presence from the time he acquired the document, he must still swear as I have explained.
He must swear. Nevertheless, if the seller dies, the purchaser may collect, because he acquired the document while the debtor was alive, and at the time of sale the seller had not necessarily become obligated in an oath, since perhaps the debtor would never claim that he had paid. This is not included in the rule that one does not bequeath an oath. These are the words of the Sema. His wording is not precise. Even if the debtor had loudly claimed before the sale that he had paid the seller, the law would be the same, because the rule that one does not bequeath an oath applies only to an oath required for collection from orphans, not to an oath against the debtor himself. We do not extend that special rule beyond its stated case, as explained below in siman 108, seif 14. The reason here is as the Rosh and Ran explain: once the seller sold the debt document, he no longer bears the principal collection claim. He is made to swear only because he remains believed to say that the debt was paid through the migo that he could forgive it.
And he claims that it was paid. Meaning, the debtor claims that he paid the seller and says, "let the seller swear to me." Therefore the seller must swear. This is the meaning of the Rambam and Mechaber above in seif 11: if Shimon claims that he paid Levi and says, "let him swear to me, etc." But if he does not demand an oath, then even if he claims that he paid, the seller need not swear, as below in siman 82, seif 2. If the debtor has died, however, the seller must swear against the heirs even if they do not say, "swear to us," because collection is not made from the property of orphans without an oath, as below in siman 108, seif 3.
And it contains no credibility clause. If it does contain a credibility clause, the seller need not swear. Therefore, even if the seller refuses to swear, the purchaser may still collect. Where there is no credibility clause and the seller is obligated to swear, then if he refuses to swear, even without affirmatively saying that the debt was paid, the debtor need not pay. But if the seller admits that the debt was paid, then even where the document contains a credibility clause the debtor is exempt, as I wrote above in seif 11, seif katan 34, and the seller must compensate the purchaser for the loss caused by his statement. The Rosh, Ran, Maggid Mishneh, and other poskim likewise write that the seller is believed to say that it was paid because he has a migo that he could forgive it. The Beit Yosef writes in seif 14 that this applies specifically where the seller admits that the debt was paid. But if he says, "I reviewed my accounting and discovered that nothing remains due to me," then unless the matter is established by witnesses, the seller has no power to say this, like the incident involving the gardeners at the end of the first chapter of Gittin. He says that Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, rules the same. Later, in his new material 13, the Beit Yosef again cites Baal HaTerumos that the seller cannot say, "I reviewed my accounts and was mistaken," unless the matter is established by witnesses. Darkei Moshe and the Sema, seif katan 32, follow him and distinguish between saying, "it was paid," and saying, "I reviewed the accounts and was mistaken." In my humble opinion this distinction is incorrect. Just as the seller can say, "it was paid," because he has the migo that he could forgive it, so too he is believed to say, "I reviewed my accounts and was mistaken." The case of the gardeners at the end of the first chapter of Gittin is different. There, the person had affirmatively admitted that he owed the debt, and a person is presumed not to make such an admission unless he has first checked carefully, as Tosafos and the Rosh explain there. Here, by contrast, the seller never made any personal admission of indebtedness. There is a clear proof. Tosafos and the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Gittin write that according to Rabbeinu Chananel and the Rif, in the incident of the gardeners one cannot say, "I was mistaken," through a migo that he could say, "I paid," and the Ran records that some explain the Rif this way. Yet in Perek HaKosev, regarding one who sells a debt document, the Rif explicitly writes that the seller can claim, "I reviewed my accounts and was mistaken." The Rif writes there that some earlier authorities explain the rule that one who sells a debt document may later forgive it by saying that the seller can claim, "I reviewed my accounts and found that nothing remains owing to me." The Rif rejects that explanation only because Shmuel's wording, "and afterward he forgave it," implies that even if the debt in fact remains due, the seller may simply forgive it. Thus it is explicit in the Rif that the seller can say he reviewed his accounts and was mistaken. The Ran likewise writes in Perek HaKosev that this was Rabbeinu Chananel's view, that the reason a seller may waive the debt is that he can say, "I reviewed my accounts and nothing remains owing," and the Rif challenged him. The Ran further notes an objection that where the seller owes money to others, he is not believed to say that the document is an amanah, as in Perek HaIshah SheNisarmelah, and the same would apply to a claim of payment. On the contrary, the end of the first chapter of Bava Basra proves that he is believed to say that it was paid only because he can forgive it. Thus, just as he can say "paid," he can say, "I reviewed my accounts." Accordingly, the Baal HaTerumos himself requires study. How can he write that the matter must be established by witnesses and then conclude that this is the Rif's reasoning in the first chapter of Gittin, when the Rif explicitly distinguishes the case in Perek HaKosev as I have explained? Necessarily, Baal HaTerumos speaks where the seller has no migo that he could forgive the debt, such as where it was transferred by maamad sheloshtan or a similar method. In such a case he could not say "paid" either. Proof is that Baal HaTerumos himself, Shaar 28, cites the Ramban in Sefer HaZechus that even the Rif agrees in the gardeners case that a person can say, "I reviewed my accounts and was mistaken," when he has a migo that he could say, "I paid." The Rif meant only that the claim of mistake has no independent credibility. Here in Shaar 51, however, Baal HaTerumos writes according to the Rif that the matter must be known by witnesses. Moreover, when one examines Shaar 51, after the words cited by the Beit Yosef he writes that the Rif explained the reason at the end of the first chapter of Gittin concerning the two gardeners, and that this matter had already been written in Shaar 28. In Shaar 28 he concludes in the name of the Ramban that the Rif holds that one is believed to say, "I was mistaken," through a migo that he could say, "I paid." Baal HaTerumos himself appears to follow that view there, as the Tur and Beit Yosef cite him below in siman 126, seif 14. Therefore, his words here in Shaar 51 can only mean that the claim, "I was mistaken," is intrinsically weak and requires witnesses where the litigant has no migo. But the Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema cannot be read this way, because they expressly distinguish between "paid" and "I reviewed my accounts," meaning a case where he does have the migo, yet still say he is not believed to claim mistake. That is certainly incorrect, as I have shown. Moreover, according to what I wrote above in seif katan 35, that every statement nullifying the document has the same law as saying "paid," the distinction of the Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Sema is independently untenable.

And if he does not wish to swear, the purchaser has recourse against him. The Sema, seif katan 41, writes that this means the purchaser may collect from property the heirs inherited from their father, but not from their personal property, even if there is no inherited property. Although they cause loss by refusing to swear, they do not pay the damage from their own pocket. He compares this to the Tur below in siman 107, seif 28, that if heirs sold their father's movables, even where the proceeds remain in their hands, the creditor cannot collect from them personally, even after the rabbinic enactment making movables available to creditors. I discussed this proof at length in the Sema in siman 86, seif katan 12. I have already refuted the Sema's position at length in siman 86, seif 7, seif katan 18, and proved that the heirs must pay from their own property. It is not comparable to siman 107 at all. There is also further proof from what I established above in seif 11, seif katan 36, from the Maggid Mishneh, the Mechaber, and the Sema himself in seif katan 31, that where the heirs refuse to swear they must pay the entire value of the debt document and cannot discharge themselves by returning only the amount their father received from the purchaser. This necessarily rests on the law of garmi: through their present refusal to swear they now cause the purchaser damage and render the document worthless. Otherwise, it would suffice to return the money the purchaser gave their father. Once their liability is based on the present damage they themselves cause, it is obvious that they must pay from their own property, as below in seif 32. This is clear. Even according to the authorities cited in the gloss below in seif 32, that the seller need repay only the purchase price, the damage still occurs now. They merely hold that because the purchaser originally bought a defective claim, compensation is limited to what he paid. Therefore, even according to them the heirs must pay from their personal assets. In addition, the Sema's words are written on the Mechaber's ruling, while the Mechaber himself holds below in seif 32 that the full value of the debt document must be paid, which I also rule as primary there in seif katan 109.

The Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer, siman 105, seif 2, rule that a woman who sold her ketubah and was later divorced, if she says that she was paid after the sale, is believed through a migo that she could have forgiven the ketubah. But if she says that she had been paid before the sale, she is not believed. Their source is the Rosh in Perek HaKosev, who writes that if she says she was paid before the sale, the migo that she could forgive now is not a good migo. If she were to forgive the ketubah now, she would have to pay the purchaser the ketubah's full present value, whereas if she had already been paid before the sale she would owe only the purchase money, because the sale would have been a mistaken transaction. Even according to those who hold that any seller who forgives an assigned debt need repay only the purchase price, not everyone knows the law, and she might fear forgiving because she thinks she could be charged the full value of the debt document. By contrast, if she says that payment occurred before the sale, everyone knows that the sale was mistaken and the purchase money merely returns. These words do not appear correct to me, and I have several strong objections. They contradict logic, the Rosh's own words elsewhere, many poskim, and the Gemara. First, the suggestion that not everyone knows the law and she may fear forgiving is difficult. We find many migo arguments far more legally complex, requiring substantial Talmudic analysis, through which a woman is believed, and neither the Gemara nor the poskim say that perhaps she does not know the law. When the Gemara occasionally says, "do all women know the law?" that is specifically in the few places where the Gemara itself says so and where there is an independent reason. Otherwise, this approach would abolish most of the migo arguments in the Gemara and poskim. It also contradicts the Rosh's own words. At the end of Perek Shnayim Ochazin, regarding the Gemara's statement that Shmuel's law applies, that one who sells a debt document and later forgives it has effected a valid waiver, the Rosh asks why the woman would prefer producing a receipt to forgiving the ketubah. If she forgave it now, she would have to pay the purchasers the ketubah's full present value because she is now divorced, whereas when she sold it she was married and received only a small price. If the receipt shows she had already been paid before the sale, the sale was no sale and she returns only the small amount she received. Because of this question, some authorities infer that even one who forgives an assigned debt pays only what he received. Others hold that he pays the debt's full current value and answer that the Gemara's case can be established where she is still married. End of the Rosh's words. It is explicit that according to the authorities who require repayment only of the purchase price, she is believed that she delivered the receipt before the sale and was paid before the sale through a migo that she could forgive now. The Rosh does not say that perhaps she does not know the law and fears she will be charged the full value. One cannot answer that the receipt itself changes the matter, because it is returned only based on her own statement. The Rosh's position also conflicts with other poskim. Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 6, writes that some say one who forgives must pay the entire face value, and this is Rabbi Meir's view. Rabbi Shmuel, however, writes that he need repay only what the purchaser paid, and Baal HaTerumos finds that view reasonable, citing proof from Perek Shnayim Ochazin. Nimukei Yosef likewise writes at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia that according to Tosafos, who hold that one who forgives repays only what he received, the migo works well. The Semag at the end of the positive commandment of returning lost property says the same. Thus Baal HaTerumos, Nimukei Yosef, and Semag explicitly hold that according to those authorities the migo is valid and do not say that perhaps she fears a mistaken understanding of the law. I will cite additional poskim below who disagree with the Rosh's rule. Most importantly, the rule is inconsistent with the Gemara even according to those who hold that one who forgives must pay the full value of the debt document at the time of waiver. I rule below in seif 32, seif katan 109, that this is indeed the primary law. The Gemara's statement that Shmuel's law applies would therefore raise the Rosh's own difficulty. The Rosh's final answer, that the Gemara can be established where she is still married, does not solve the problem. That answer would address only his particular question based on the increase in the ketubah's value after divorce. But an even stronger question remains whether she is married, widowed, or divorced: we do not know to whom she sold the ketubah or at what price. Perhaps she sold it for a low price, even a very low price, and documents are not subject to ona'ah. If she forgives now, she must pay the full present value. If she had been paid before the sale, she need return only the small sum she received. This appears to be the intent of Baal HaTerumos, Nimukei Yosef, and Semag, who ask the question without mentioning that she is now divorced. Necessarily, according to the Rif and the authorities who explain the Gemara's "this proves that Shmuel's law applies" through migo, we believe her that the ketubah is paid because she could forgive it, but as against the purchaser himself we do not believe her assertion that payment occurred before the sale. Since through her present statement and conduct she now causes the purchaser to lose the entire ketubah, she must compensate him for its full value. The Maggid Mishneh in chapter 15 of Hilchos Malveh, halachah 7, writes this explicitly, as I cited above in seif 11, seif katan 35, and I wrote there that other poskim likewise state it. Thus the Maggid Mishneh and those authorities clearly disagree with the Rosh in Perek HaKosev. There is also a more fundamental explanation, which appears primary and follows Rashi and the Nimukei Yosef. Nimukei Yosef writes that according to Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that the one who forgives pays the full value of the debt document rather than merely what he received, what migo is there? Surely she prefers producing the receipt. He answers that the Gemara is not relying on migo at all. Once she admits that the ketubah was paid and the receipt is valid, that very admission is her waiver, equivalent to saying, "it is forgiven to you." He indicates that this is from the Rashba and Ran. According to Rashi, Rashba, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef, therefore, the Rosh's rule in Perek HaKosev is certainly incorrect. I also cited above in seif 11, seif katan 35, the Maggid Mishneh in the name of some authorities, the French sages, and Rabbi Meir son of Rabbi Yehudah, that when she says "paid" she thereby presently forgives the debt, and there is no greater waiver than that. I prove in siman 86, seif 5, seif katan 18, that this is the primary law. It follows plainly that the Rosh's rule above in Perek HaKosev cannot stand. Therefore, contrary to the Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer 105, seif 2, who simply cite the Rosh without noting disagreement, the woman is believed in every case. This is clear.
