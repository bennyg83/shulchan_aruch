The Sema explains that if the seller or his heirs refuse to take the required oath, the purchaser recovers from assets inherited from the seller but not from the heirs' own property. The Shach argues that refusal to swear is equivalent to saying the debt was paid. Since the seller or heir has a migo that he could waive the debt, the refusal is actual damaging conduct and creates liability from their own assets. Tumim objects that the Shach elsewhere says that if the purchaser has no recourse against the seller, the seller is not believed to say the debt was paid and therefore has no oath obligation at all. That objection is misplaced where ordinary waiver remains available. The more fundamental point is that refusal to swear is not a tort under the law of direct causation. Witnesses who withhold testimony are exempt in human court. Ramban explains that their duty to testify is essentially the duty to return another's lost property, and one who merely fails to return a lost object is not a tortfeasor. Direct causation generally requires an affirmative damaging act or statement. One who simply refuses to swear, while not asserting that payment occurred, is even further removed from direct damage. Therefore the seller's obligation to reimburse the purchaser when he refuses the oath arises from sale warranty. A debt instrument on which the seller will not take the necessary oath is practically worthless, like an oral claim that cannot be collected. Since the purchaser bought a collectible instrument and did not receive one, he returns against the seller under the warranty of the sale. That warranty reaches the seller's estate but does not create a new tort claim against heirs from their own property merely because they decline to swear.
