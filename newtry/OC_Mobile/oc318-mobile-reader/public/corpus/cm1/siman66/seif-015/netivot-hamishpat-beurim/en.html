The purchaser swears that the seller did not tell him. But if the seller is alive, he too must swear. I am greatly puzzled. In the first case, where the debtor and seller have both died, the Mechaber mentions only the purchaser's oath, implying that the seller's heirs need not swear. In the latter case, where the debtor is alive, the Mechaber mentions only the heir's oath and not the purchaser's, implying that the purchaser may collect on the strength of the heir's oath alone and need not swear at all. The matter may be resolved as follows. Ran in the chapter HaKotev discusses the authorities' explanation that one who sells a debt instrument and then forgives the debt can effect forgiveness because the debtor's personal obligation remains with the seller. When the seller forgives the personal obligation, the lien on the debtor's property falls away automatically. Tosafot ask that if so, when the debtor dies the personal obligation also ends, and the property lien should likewise disappear. Ran answers that the very nature of property serving as security is that when the debtor cannot be found, such as because he died or traveled overseas, collection may be made from his property. Sema in seif katan 58 gives the same answer, writing that the problem is specific to forgiveness, where the obligation ends through an act of the creditor. Tumim in seif katan 50 gives another answer: when the debtor dies, the heir who inherits the property becomes personally bound to the creditor in place of the debtor. Accordingly, where the debtor is alive but the creditor dies, the creditor's heirs inherit the personal obligation. Since they can forgive it, they are believed to say that it was paid through the migo that they could forgive. They therefore must swear, for if they made that assertion they would be believed through the migo, as Ran and Rosh write at the end of Kol HaNishba'in and as the Shach cites in seif katan 50. In the first case, however, the debtor died during the creditor's lifetime. There the rule is that one cannot transmit to one's children a monetary claim that the creditor himself could collect only by taking an oath. The creditor therefore could not transmit even the debtor's personal obligation to his heirs, because collection on that personal obligation itself required an oath. A person cannot transmit to his children a debt lien burdened by an oath. The heirs thus inherit neither the personal obligation nor the property lien. With respect to this money they are outsiders. They cannot forgive the debt and are not believed to say it was paid, because they have no migo of forgiveness. There is therefore no reason for them to take this oath at all. The purchaser, however, may collect upon taking his own oath, because he acquired the lien on the debtor's property during the debtor's lifetime. The rule that one cannot transmit an oath burdened claim to one's children does not apply to the purchaser, as Ran writes and the Shach cites in seif katan 3. Nor do we say that because the personal obligation has ceased the property lien automatically ceases, because here the personal obligation ended only through death, not through forgiveness. In the latter case, where the debtor remains alive, the seller's heir swears because he inherited the personal obligation, can forgive it, and is therefore believed to say that it was paid. The purchaser need not swear. He can collect upon the heir's oath by saying to the debtor, "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, I will return the document to its owner," meaning the heir, who could then collect on the strength of his own oath. See Ran in the chapter HaCholetz regarding this argument. Its basis is the rule that the first seller transfers to the purchaser every right that may come into his own hands. As a general principle, the purchaser's right can never be weaker than the right of the person from whom he purchased. Thus, if the purchaser returned the document to the heir after the heir swore, the debtor would have to pay the heir even if the purchaser himself refused to swear. Indeed, when the first purchaser sells the document back to the original seller, the original seller now stands as a second purchaser. Even if the first purchaser, who had been the seller, refuses to swear, the present holder does not lose. The entire reason the seller must swear is that, as Ran and Rosh explain, he is believed to say the debt was paid through a migo that he could forgive it. The Beit Yosef cites this explanation in the Tur. Accordingly, in seif 31, once the purchaser resells the document and can no longer forgive the debt, he is no longer believed to say it was paid and need not swear. The Mechaber can therefore properly rule that the debtor must pay the purchaser upon the heir's oath even though the purchaser does not swear, because the purchaser can invoke the argument that he can return the instrument to the heir. One might ask that in the first clause, where the seller is alive, the Mechaber requires both seller and purchaser to swear. Why can the purchaser not collect on the seller's oath alone through the same argument? The answer is that the latter case concerns an assertion that payment was made to the seller. There the purchaser can say, "Even if you paid the seller, I did not injure you by purchasing the debt. Had I not purchased it, you would still have owed payment to him. Since you were obligated to pay him, I acquired every right he could have exercised." That is the setting in which the argument of returning the document applies. In the first clause, however, the debtor claims that he paid the purchaser himself. The purchaser certainly must swear that he did not receive that payment. The argument that the purchaser did no harm by buying the debt has no place there, because if he had never acquired the debt the debtor would never have paid him. The suspicious circumstance arises specifically from the purchaser's own conduct in acquiring the debt and then allegedly receiving payment. Thus the purchaser must swear regarding his own alleged receipt. Accordingly, the Sema's view is primary, contrary to the Shach in seif katan 49, who holds that the purchaser must also swear even when the debtor claims payment to the seller. Regarding alleged payment to the seller, the purchaser need not swear. The seller's oath alone is sufficient because the purchaser can invoke the argument stated above.
The purchaser may recover from him. See Sema, who explains that the purchaser recovers only from property inherited by the heir. The Shach holds that he may recover even from the heir's own property. The Sema is correct, as Tumim writes. The heir's conduct is no more than withholding testimony. The real reason for liability is that when he refuses to swear we treat it as established that the debtor paid his father. Accordingly, repayment is made from the property inherited from that father.
Even with an oath. See Shach, seif katan 56, who cites the Mechaber and Tur in siman 105 that if a woman says the debt was paid before she sold the claim, she is not believed. The Shach questions this, and the difficulty is real, because the Mechaber states the rule without distinguishing whether she says payment occurred before or after the sale, while in Even HaEzer regarding a ketubah he does distinguish. To understand the distinction, consider the wording of the Shulchan Aruch there: "If she does not wish to swear, the purchasers lose. If she says that she was paid afterward... But if she says that she was paid before she sold it, she is not believed." At first sight, what difference does it make whether she is believed? Even if she merely refuses to swear, the purchasers lose. One must say that when purchasers lose solely because the seller refuses to swear, a seizure by the purchaser is nevertheless effective, as explained in siman 82. By contrast, where the seller has a migo and is fully believed that the debt was paid, even seizure does not help the purchaser. On that basis one can distinguish between an ordinary debt instrument and a ketubah. With an ordinary debt instrument, even if the seller says payment occurred before the sale, the debtor must still pay the full amount to the purchaser, because the seller is not believed to exempt himself from his own liability to the purchaser. Indeed, even if the seller says nothing and merely refuses to swear, he must reimburse the purchaser fully, as the Shach writes. Therefore he may still be believed against the purchaser to say the debt was paid through the migo that he could forgive it. A ketubah is different. Tumim, seif katan 21, explains that the reason a seller who refuses to swear must reimburse the purchaser is not that refusal to swear itself constitutes actionable damage. At most it resembles withholding testimony. Rather, refusal to swear is treated as evidence that the debt was in fact paid, for if it were not paid the seller would readily swear. Ordinarily we do not suspect that payment occurred before the sale, because there is a presumption that a person does not knowingly sell a claim that was already extinguished at the time of the sale. With a ketubah, however, even if the woman says that it was paid before the sale, she is believed at least as against herself to avoid paying more than the amount representing the market value of the speculative ketubah right. She has a migo that she could have said, "I forgave the ketubah to my husband after the sale and before the divorce." A woman who swears concerning her ketubah must swear that she did not forgive it, as explained in Even HaEzer siman 96, seif 2. Tumim explains that although an ordinary creditor need not swear that he did not forgive the debt, a wife must swear that she did not forgive her husband because a wife is particularly likely to waive claims against her husband. If she says, "I am willing to swear that I was not paid, but I refuse to swear that I never forgave," she would have to reimburse only the value of the speculative ketubah interest. Even if we say that refusal to swear regarding forgiveness establishes that she in fact forgave it, one who causes loss by forgiveness need pay only the value of the claim at the time of the damaging act. Therefore, when she says that the ketubah was paid before the sale, she is believed through a migo as against herself to avoid liability beyond the speculative value. But she is not believed against the purchaser, because she lacks an equivalent migo against him. The migo that she could forgive the ketubah does not help against the purchaser, since by forgiving it she would become liable to reimburse the purchaser the entire damage, whereas according to her assertion that payment occurred before the sale she need return only the amount she actually received from the purchaser. Consequently, she is not fully believed against the purchaser. Her statement that payment occurred before the sale has no stronger effect than a refusal to swear, and seizure by the purchaser remains effective in either case. However, Tosafot in Bava Kamma 89, s.v. Kol Legabei Baalah, ask that if she forgives the ketubah she must reimburse the purchaser under the law of directly caused damage. They write that according to Rabbeinu Tam she need pay only the amount she received. This implies that according to the view requiring reimbursement of the full loss, even a wife who remains married and forgives her ketubah to her husband must pay the entire amount. If she owed only the speculative ketubah value, Tosafot's question would have been answered according to everyone. One must therefore say that Tosafot's question follows one of their earlier answers, in which the husband first divorces her, she then forgives the ketubah, and he subsequently remarries her. Since the forgiveness occurs after divorce, she must certainly reimburse the full loss. This resolves the matter.
