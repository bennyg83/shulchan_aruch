Shach, seif katan 22, seeks to rule against Tosafot, Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber and in favor of Ramban and Nimukei Yosef, that even a weak form of delivery, like the delivery used for a ship, suffices for debt documents. His proof is not strong enough to reject all those authorities. Indeed, Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 51, part 3, indicates that Ramban himself ultimately deferred to Tosafot's accepted view. Ramban rules against Raavad that chalipin does not substitute for delivery, and says explicitly that small bundles require hagbahah and large ones meshichah. This follows the received understanding that actual physical acquisition is needed. If Ramban himself moderated his earlier formulation, how can we decisively rule against Tosafot and Rosh on his basis? Shach's own rulings also seem inconsistent. Above he rules that chalipin never substitutes for delivery. Here he favors the weakest possible delivery, even though Ramban's chief reason for rejecting chalipin was that debt-note delivery requires an added act beyond ordinary meshichah and hagbahah. If minimal delivery suffices, Raavad's chalipin approach becomes far more plausible. Likewise, below Shach rules like Rosh that debt documents are not acquired by agav alone. But according to his present understanding, what strength does this minimal "delivery" possess that agav lacks? Agav is a recognized acquisition; a symbolic minimal handover that does not acquire ordinary movables anywhere else should not obviously be stronger. There is, however, a question even on Tosafot and Rosh. They say actual delivery means a meaningful physical acquisition, not merely the existence of another party's intent to transfer. Why should this be stronger than agav, which is a Torah mode of acquisition and, according to R. Yochanan, may be more fundamentally valid than meshichah itself? Ramban says that delivery must be hand to hand in all cases, and Ritva in Kiddushin likewise indicates a special hand-to-hand act. Perhaps that is the answer: the requirement is not merely acquisition of the paper but a formal act signifying transfer of the documentary lien. Were I not hesitant to innovate, I would suggest the two Tosafot answers are both necessary and correspond to the dispute between R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish. According to R. Yochanan, meshichah is not the basic Torah acquisition of movables. Therefore ordinary meshichah or hagbahah alone cannot explain the special transfer of a debt lien. Chazal required an additional hand-to-hand delivery that functions as a formal documentary transfer. Agav, by contrast, is a Torah acquisition and may therefore need no literal hand-to-hand act. According to Reish Lakish, meshichah itself is of Torah force. In that framework, the phrase "delivery" may merely exclude acquisition from hefker, where there is no transferor at all. The physical act can otherwise be ordinary meshichah. Thus one Tosafot answer fits R. Yochanan and the other Reish Lakish. Since our halachah follows R. Yochanan, practical document transfer should require actual delivery from transferor to transferee, together with the required writing. This also explains why the Gemara later treats delivery as stronger than agav in the initial stage: under R. Yochanan, the hand-to-hand documentary act has special transfer significance that ordinary agav may lack. The issue remains complex, but there is no justification for Shach's categorical rejection of Tosafot, Rosh, Tur, and Mechaber.
Therefore, one who acquires an ownerless document, and so forth. See Shach's question on Maharam Lublin. If we rule that ordinary documents require writing and delivery, how can one ever acquire an ownerless debt note? There is no transferor to write a transfer. Shach further asks that if the original creditor truly abandoned the debt, the borrower should already acquire freedom from the obligation, just as the death of a convert without heirs can extinguish certain claims. Shach answers that the case concerns a bearer note requiring no writing and perhaps a debt owed by a gentile. This is not satisfactory. If the debt itself was extinguished in favor of the borrower when the creditor abandoned it, the same should be true with a gentile debtor. The finder merely receives worthless paper. There is no remaining debt to collect by virtue of the note. And the first answer is also difficult. The dispute between Rebbi and the Sages itself concerns ordinary documents: Rebbi says they are acquired by delivery alone and the Sages require writing and delivery. If the case could be reduced to bearer notes that even the Sages agree need no writing, the dispute would disappear. The proper answer emerges from Tosafot Bava Batra 76b. Tosafot explain why Scripture needed to exclude documents from the law of ona'ah even if ordinary sale of documents is not of Torah force: a person can find a debt note after the owner despaired of the paper and later sell the paper back to the creditor at a high price. At first glance, if the creditor despaired of the note, did he not also despair of the debt? The paper would then be worthless. The answer is that a creditor can despair of recovering the physical note without despairing of the underlying debt. I discussed above in siman 65 how despair can affect the document separately from the personal claim. So too here. The original creditor abandons or despairs of the physical document but does not forgive or abandon the underlying debt. A finder acquires the physical paper from hefker. If the finder later approaches the original creditor and the creditor writes a transfer of the debt to him, the writing alone is not enough. Ordinary debt-note transfer requires writing together with delivery from the transferor. But the creditor can no longer "deliver" the paper because it no longer belongs to him; the finder already acquired it from hefker. Thus there is no valid delivery from the creditor to accompany the writing. That is why a finder of an ownerless debt document does not thereby acquire the debt itself. This also explains the Gemara's emphasis that document transfer requires another person who transfers it. Physical possession obtained independently from hefker is not the same as delivery by the creditor. Maharam Lublin's first question is therefore resolved. As for Semag's formulation concerning a convert who dies without heirs, Shach reads it as an ordinary loan debt and asks that the borrower himself should become free of the debt upon the creditor's death. In my opinion Semag uses the word "debt" loosely. The case concerns acquisition deeds belonging to a convert who owned fields or other property. When the convert dies, the fields are ownerless, but the seller does not automatically reacquire them. Someone might find the convert's acquisition deed and think that, according to the opinion that documents are acquired by delivery alone, possession of the deed itself transfers the land. Semag therefore teaches that it does not. Debt-document delivery requires another party who transfers the deed. Mere seizure of an ownerless paper does not transfer the underlying property. This also explains why Semag formulates the point particularly according to the opinion that documents are acquired by delivery alone. According to the view requiring writing and delivery, the case is obvious because no one exists to provide the writing. Thus both Maharam Lublin's and Shach's difficulties disappear.
