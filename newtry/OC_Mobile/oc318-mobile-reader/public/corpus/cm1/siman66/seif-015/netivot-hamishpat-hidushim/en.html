The purchaser swears that the seller did not tell him. Yet if the seller told him, even after the sale, that the debt had already been paid, the seller would be believed, Shach. See Beurim. Only the purchaser must swear. The heir need not swear, and even if the heir admits that the document was paid he is not believed. Likewise, the heir cannot waive the debt. The purchaser's oath is required even if the borrower does not expressly say, "Swear to me," because he comes to collect from orphans:
He too must swear. Meaning, the seller swears that he himself was not paid. The purchaser does not need to swear that the seller never told him of payment, because the seller is present and swears, and we do not suspect him of taking a false oath, Sema, unlike the Shach. In the final clause, where the borrower is alive, only the person whom the borrower says he paid must swear:
He must swear. We do not say that because the borrower died during the lender's lifetime the rule of "a person cannot bequeath an oath obligation to his children" applies. Once the document had been sold to another while the borrower was still alive, the seller's later death is irrelevant. The purchaser is now the creditor and he is alive. See there. The lender died. Before the document was sold, the lender was already in a status of one who could not swear and therefore could not collect. Once he later died, the rule that one cannot transfer such an oath obligation to heirs applies, and likewise such a claim cannot be sold.
