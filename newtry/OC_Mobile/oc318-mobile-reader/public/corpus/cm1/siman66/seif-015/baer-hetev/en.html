Said. This implies that if the seller said the debt had already been paid, he would be believed even after the sale to say that it was paid before the sale. Shach.
To swear. The reason is that beit din advances on behalf of heirs every claim their father could have made. Had he been alive, he could have claimed that he paid either the seller or the purchaser. Therefore, when the borrower has died, both of them must swear because of the uncertainty. If the borrower is alive, by contrast, only the one against whom he makes a definite claim that he paid him, whether the seller or the purchaser, must swear, for he certainly did not pay both. Sema.
The purchaser. Nevertheless, if the seller dies, the purchaser may collect upon taking an oath, since he acquired the note while the borrower was alive, and at the time of the sale the seller was not definitely obligated to swear because the borrower might never claim that he had paid him. This is therefore not included in the rule that a person cannot bequeath money whose collection is subject to an oath, as explained in siman 108. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach writes that this wording is imprecise, because even if the borrower had loudly asserted before the sale that he had already paid, the same rule applies. The principle that a person cannot bequeath money whose collection requires an oath was said only concerning an oath required to collect from heirs, not an oath against the borrower himself. We should not extend that rule beyond its stated case, as explained in siman 108, seif 14. Here the reason is as the Rosh and Ran write: since the seller sold the note, the primary obligation is no longer upon him, and he is required to swear only because he is believed to say that it was paid by virtue of a migo that he could forgive the debt. See there.
Paid. Meaning that the borrower claims he paid the seller and says, 'Let the seller swear to me.' The seller must then swear. But if the borrower does not demand, 'Let him swear to me,' then even if he claims to have paid, no oath is required, as stated in siman 82, seif 2. If the borrower has died, however, an oath is required against his heirs even if they do not demand one, since collection from heirs is only upon an oath, as stated in siman 108, seif 3. See there. Shach.
Upon him. Meaning that the purchaser may collect from property the heir inherited from his father, but not from the heir's own property, even if no inherited property remains. Although the heir caused loss by refusing to swear, he does not pay that loss from his own pocket, as the Tur writes in siman 107 that according to all views, if heirs sell their father's movable property, then even if the sale proceeds remain intact in their hands the creditor cannot collect from those proceeds, even after the rabbinic enactment making heirs' movable property subject to creditors. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach disagrees and rules that the heirs must pay the entire value of the note from their own property, as cited in his name above in seif katan 29. See there.
He does not. The Shach writes that this refers to a note without a clause of trust. If it contains such a clause, the purchaser collects, as stated in siman 108, seif 13. He further writes that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state in Even HaEzer, siman 105, seif 2, regarding a woman who sold her ketubah and was later divorced, that if she says she was paid after selling it she is believed by virtue of the migo that she could forgive it, but if she says she was paid before selling it she is not believed. This does not appear correct; rather, in every case she is believed. See there at length, where he brings several proofs for his view.
