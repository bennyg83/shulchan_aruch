(Shach, seif katan 110) For this is implied by the Gemara at the end of the first chapter, etc. Note: See Shitah Mekubetzet to Ketubot 19, in the passage citing Rabbeinu Yosef HaLevi. According to his explanation, the Rashba's ruling is not contradicted by the sugya of a found receipt. There, no opposing presumption is presently before us to defeat the migo. We merely suspect that perhaps she sold her ketubah and the document is in the purchasers' hands. Also, even though a lender who admits that a document was fictitious is not believed where his statement harms others despite his ability to waive the debt, we do not believe him by a migo because his admission makes him a wrongdoer, like the rule that a person cannot declare himself wicked. This supports the Rashba: where the seller says that he received payment, he makes himself wicked. If he was paid before selling the document, why did he sell a debt that no longer existed? If he was paid after the sale, why did he accept payment of a debt he had already sold? By contrast, where a receipt is found we may return it, because in truth she may never have sold the ketubah. If she had sold it and now sought collusion, it would have been better for her simply to waive the debt rather than claim that she had already been paid and had sold something she did not own. Nevertheless, the Rashba's novellae as cited in Shitah Mekubetzet to Ketubot indicate that the found-receipt case is specifically one where the purchasers are not present, and he concludes that the sugya there does not appear so. His responsum therefore seems inconsistent with his novellae.
