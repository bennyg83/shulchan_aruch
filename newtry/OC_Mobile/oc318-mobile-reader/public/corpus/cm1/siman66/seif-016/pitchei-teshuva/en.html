A document in his own name. See Baer Heitev, who cites the Ritva. Responsa Naot Deshe, siman 131, quotes the Ritva on the Gemara’s statement that if the purchaser is wise he should appease the debtor with money and have the debtor write a document in his name. The Ritva explains that the debtor should obligate himself by a new document to pay the purchaser whatever is written in the first document. Nevertheless, if the original lender subsequently waives the debt, the waiver is effective to the extent that the purchaser cannot collect from purchasers of the debtor’s property based on the lien dating from the first document. Naot Deshe explains the Ritva’s intent. If the first note in the seller’s name were simply torn up and a new note written in the purchaser’s name, the new note would necessarily bear the current date, because it would be forbidden to use the first note’s earlier date. The purchaser would then lose the lien against property sold before the new date, and there would be little advantage in the device. On the other hand, if a second independent note were written in the purchaser’s name while the first note remained in his possession, the debtor could be exposed to two claims. The Ritva therefore means that the new instrument is not an independent debt instrument. Rather, the debtor writes that from now on he personally obligates himself to Shimon, the purchaser, to pay him the amount stated in Reuven’s original note, so that whatever he had been required to pay Reuven he now undertakes to pay Shimon. From that point forward Reuven can no longer waive the debt. Reuven’s waiver can still affect the earlier lien, however, so that if he waives it the purchaser cannot collect from property sold before the date of the second instrument. Naot Deshe derives from this a ruling in a case where Reuven married a woman and at the time of marriage signed a document obligating himself that if his wife died before him he would return to her heirs from her first husband the dowry she had brought into the marriage. The document was delivered to her sons. Years later she died and her sons demanded the dowry under that document. Reuven produced a waiver signed by his wife in which she released him from the obligation recorded in the marriage document. Naot Deshe ruled that Reuven must nevertheless pay the sons as he undertook in the first document, because the wife’s waiver has no force. Reuven’s obligation was only toward her sons, and she herself had no right under that document to release or waive. Some questioned this, arguing that had the document simply said he was obligated to pay the heirs a fixed sum, the wife would clearly have no interest, but because it says that he would ‘return’ to the heirs the dowry ‘that she brought in,’ those expressions imply that she retained some right in the dowry, such that her sons would inherit that amount upon her death and she could therefore waive it. Naot Deshe rejects this objection and brings an explicit proof from Ketubot 86a as explained by the Ritva. There too, the debtor’s new instrument expressly states that he is obligated to Shimon for what he originally borrowed from Reuven, yet once he has independently obligated himself to Shimon, Reuven can no longer waive that personal obligation.
