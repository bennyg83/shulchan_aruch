And then he is circumcised in this way, and he is simply rewarded if the seller does not want to receive a break from him, and the familiar arche wants to receive a check-iner, and he will not only pay the seller to take care of him, but if he does not watch all the boxes and sells away from him
The purchaser may then recover from him. The Beit Yosef correctly explains that recovery is from the seller, not from the debtor. Even if the debtor violated beit din’s instruction and paid the seller, the purchaser cannot collect a second time from him. The Bach distinguishes a case where beit din issued a written judgment directing payment to the purchaser, but this distinction is unsound. An oral ruling is legally equivalent for this purpose, and in either case the seller’s receipt of payment extinguishes the debt just as an express waiver would. Rabbi Natan’s rule does not apply, because before payment the seller owes the purchaser nothing; his liability arises only when he accepts payment and thereby destroys the sold document. Once payment is made, the lien is extinguished. The purchaser’s claim is therefore against the seller, not the debtor, even if the debtor disobeyed beit din.
Some say that the purchaser must, etc. The Tur cites Baal HaTerumot that where the seller delivered the document without writing the transfer formula, and the debtor paid the seller, beit din compels the purchaser to return the document to the debtor, who pays him the value of the paper. The latter requirement is difficult, for the debtor originally paid for the writing material and the purchaser did not acquire even the paper according to the Tur. The rule should also apply where writing and delivery were properly made, according to the opinion that payment to the seller exempts the debtor, because once the debt is paid the paper belongs to the debtor. The Rosh states explicitly that after payment or waiver the document must be returned to the debtor, who paid the scribe. This also creates difficulty with the Tur’s ruling in Even HaEzer that a woman betrothed through a properly transferred debt document may be betrothed by the paper’s value, since the paper must ultimately be returned to the debtor.
That the purchaser must return, etc. Baal HaTerumot records a dissenting view that even where there was only delivery, only writing, or a proper written and delivered transfer, beit din does not compel the purchaser to return the document after the debtor paid the seller, unless the debtor reimburses what the purchaser paid. Although that view reasons that the purchaser acquired the paper for wrapping purposes, and the primary law is that no partial acquisition of the paper occurs, the result may still be defensible on another ground. The document itself may serve as a pledge in the creditor’s hand until payment. The purchaser may therefore retain it against the seller, and consequently against the debtor, much as a second creditor holding a repledged object may retain it. The matter is therefore one of legal doubt. In any event, so long as the original document exists in the purchaser’s hand, the debtor may refuse to pay the seller merely against a receipt, because he need not expose himself to later litigation over recovery of the document.
He must return it, etc. Rama here cites only the view requiring return of the document, while in seif 23 he first states without qualification the opinion that return is unnecessary. One cannot distinguish payment from waiver, because the authorities treat them alike. The suggestion that Rashba agrees to return after actual payment is not convincing. Rashba compares the document to a pledge that the original creditor repledged; the purchaser may retain the paper even after the debt has been extinguished as against the original creditor. Thus the same dispute applies to both payment and waiver.
Some say that if the debtor paid the creditor, etc. The first opinion is primary. The debtor’s money was his own, and paying it to the seller does not create a new obligation to the purchaser. Once the seller receives payment, the document is waived and extinguished. The purchaser has only a grievance against the debtor and a monetary claim against the seller. Baal HaTerumot’s argument that the debtor was negligent because he should have paid the purchaser is unpersuasive. The Gemara concerning a ketubah receipt proves no contrary rule, because its conclusion is precisely that Shmuel’s doctrine of forgiveness applies. Nor does publicity of the sale alter this, since the court may not know of purchasers even where a transaction ordinarily generates publicity.
