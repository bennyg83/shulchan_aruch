The wording appears to mean that the purchaser may again collect from the borrower, since beit din ordered the borrower to give payment to the purchaser and he violated beit din's instruction. What is written afterward, 'but if the borrower first paid,' would then refer to a case where he paid before the judgment, and therefore he is exempt. In the Perishah, however, I wrote that even in the first case the purchaser does not collect again from the borrower, but only from the lender who sold him the note and received its price. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach likewise rules this way and writes that the Bach and Rashdam, responsum 102, agree, contrary to Responsa Mahara Sasson, siman 166. See there.
To return. The Shach rules that this is an unresolved legal doubt. Even if the borrower wishes to pay the purchaser the value of the paper, the purchaser may say, 'I am retaining it as collateral against the borrower until he pays me.' The Beit Yosef cites the Baal HaTerumot that according to the latter view, if the borrower says to the lender, 'I will not pay you until you return my promissory note to me,' since it is known that the note remains in the purchaser's possession and still exists, he is not compelled to pay merely in exchange for a receipt. It appears to me that even according to the first view the law is the same, because the borrower may say, 'I do not wish to release the money from my possession and then become involved in litigation, since perhaps I will not find a beit din that will compel the purchaser.'

The Sema explains that this applies even if beit din had not instructed the borrower to give payment to the purchaser, and the borrower cannot say that he did not know the note had been sold, since every kinyan creates public notice. The Shach writes that the first view is primary, namely that the borrower is discharged from liability toward the purchaser. See there at length.
