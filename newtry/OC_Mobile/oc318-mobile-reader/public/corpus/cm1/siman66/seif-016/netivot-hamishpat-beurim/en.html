If he repaid. See Shach, seif katan 62, who questions this opinion. In my humble view, the rationale of this opinion may be explained as follows. It is established in siman 120, seif 2, that if a borrower says to the lender, “Here is your money,” and the lender refuses to accept it, and the borrower throws it before him, the borrower is discharged, because payment made against the creditor's will is deemed payment. Accordingly, it appears that in our case, if the matter occurred in this manner, namely, the borrower went and repaid the lender against the lender's will, while the lender refused to accept the money on the ground that the debt belonged to the purchaser by virtue of the assignment, and the borrower then threw the money before the lender and it was lost there through unavoidable circumstances, the borrower is certainly discharged as against both the lender and the purchaser. For by means of the involuntary payment the borrower's personal obligation to the lender is extinguished, and consequently the lien upon the borrower's property in favor of the purchaser is likewise extinguished. Accordingly, since the borrower discharges himself by tendering the money as payment, this is comparable to one who waives a claim in his own favor, discussed below in seif 23 and Shach, seif katan 82, who is liable to pay under the law of direct causative damage. The lender, however, is not liable under that doctrine, because he has caused no damage at all. It was the borrower who caused the loss. It therefore appears that all authorities agree in such a case, since the borrower discharged himself from his debt and thereby damaged another, and this is precisely analogous to one who waives a claim for his own benefit. For this reason, the alternative opinion likewise holds that when the borrower has paid, the purchaser is not discharged, because the lender did not wish to waive the debt until after the borrower had paid. The instant the borrower placed the money before the lender as payment, however, he was immediately discharged from the debt, even if the lender had not accepted it. The borrower is thus viewed as having discharged himself, and is liable as one who causes damage. The lender who subsequently accepts the money is not the damaging party, since the promissory note was already impaired at the moment the money was tendered. As for the proof brought by Baal HaTerumot from Bava Metzia, which Shach questions, it appears that it can be reconciled. Baal HaTerumot's proof is only that a sale becomes publicly known, so the borrower cannot claim that he was unaware of it. This is indeed well proved from the initial assumption of the Gemara. Necessarily, one must say that a sale becomes known, for otherwise, according to the initial understanding there, before the rule of Shmuel was known, the Gemara could simply have asked how one may write a receipt, since perhaps the creditor had sold the note and the borrower, unaware of the sale, might pay the original creditor and thereby cause loss to the purchasers. Rather, it must be that the borrower cannot make such a claim, because a sale becomes publicly known. The questions of Shach are thereby resolved.
