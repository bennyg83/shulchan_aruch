If he later forgave it, it is forgiven. Shach, seif katan 73, cites Responsa Maharanach. Reuven sold to Shimon a debt note he held against Levi. Shimon himself owed Levi money. Maharanach rules that Reuven can no longer forgive the note, because once Shimon holds Levi's debt note while owing Levi money, the two obligations are treated as already collected against one another. Shach rejects this. We rule in such cases "this one collects and that one collects." The obligations do not automatically merge. Therefore the purchased note is not considered already collected, and Reuven can still forgive. Shach is correct. There is a direct proof from the found-receipt sugya. The Gemara worries that the woman may have sold her ketubah for its market value and later use the receipt to harm the purchaser. It answers through Shmuel's rule that the seller can forgive. But perhaps she sold the ketubah to someone who himself owed money to the husband. If Maharanach were correct, the purchaser's possession of the husband's obligation would automatically constitute collection and the wife could no longer forgive. In a fallen-document context Chazal ordinarily consider even remote possibilities when relevant, so this possibility should have mattered. One might answer that a ketubah is not yet due during marriage. But Tosafot themselves note that by the time the receipt is found she may already be divorced, in which case the ketubah has matured. Maharanach's set-off theory would then apply. The fact that the Gemara still relies on Shmuel shows that even in such circumstances the note is not treated as automatically collected. The two debts remain legally separate until actual collection or set-off occurs. Therefore the original creditor retains the power of forgiveness.
