Sema explains that the seller's acceptance of responsibility must mean responsibility for the full value of the debt document, not merely return of the price he received. Otherwise, why would any special undertaking be needed? If the transfer fails, he obviously must return the money. Shach questions this. Even with respect to the price received, the responsibility can matter because without it the seller could say, "I still stand ready to give you a proper written transfer." Shach therefore concludes that absent an express undertaking, the seller is not liable for the entire face value. I am surprised by the entire discussion. If the seller undertakes that, should the buyer fail to collect from the borrowers, the seller will pay the full amount, this appears to be outright ribbit. Yoreh Deah siman 173, seif 4, treats such guaranteed debt-purchase arrangements as interest. Even where the seller guarantees only the purchase money, Shach himself there discusses the ribbit problem. Perhaps the responsibility mentioned here is only conditional: if the seller gives a proper writing and delivery, he has no further responsibility even if the debt later becomes uncollectible; but if he refuses or fails to give the required transfer, he must make the buyer whole. In that formulation there is no ribbit, because the seller can avoid all liability simply by completing the valid transfer. If so, however, Shach is not correct to say that because the seller accepted responsibility he can no longer offer a proper written transfer. On the contrary, that is precisely what should discharge him. Even this is difficult in a sale. If the attempted transfer never took effect, the buyer's money sits with the seller as a loan. If the buyer receives more than he paid merely because the transaction failed, he receives compensation for the use of his money. That is interest. Compare the rule that one who sells land he does not own does not pay appreciation because it appears like ribbit. Even according to authorities who sometimes treat the transaction as a sale until dispossession, here neither party is bound to the sale without proper writing and delivery. According to Rama there may not even be a Mi Shepara obligation. The buyer's money therefore has the character of a loan from the outset. So too, when a judicial appraisal is later reversed and the borrower redeems the property, Tur below in siman 103 says the borrower need not pay the creditor for automatic appreciation, because that would resemble ribbit. All the more so here, where the underlying acquisition never took effect. Hagahot Rosh in Perek Eizehu Neshekh likewise expresses concern where a Jew advances money to another Jew in exchange for a gentile debt and the first Jew says he is fully released from both parties and the future payments belong to the second Jew. If the transfer mechanism is incomplete, the arrangement can become interest. Rashba's responsum, the source cited for our rule, creates no difficulty because there the transaction was a gift, and the giver accepted responsibility. No purchase money was advanced and therefore no interest arises. Rama can likewise be understood as speaking of a gift even though he uses the language of a recipient or buyer, because he is not presently discussing the laws of ribbit. Alternatively, in a sale Rama may mean only that the seller must return the actual money he received. The practical novelty would be that because he accepted responsibility, those funds are treated as a debt owed by him rather than as a deposit. Without responsibility, the funds could have had the law of a deposit, as discussed above. This still requires study.
