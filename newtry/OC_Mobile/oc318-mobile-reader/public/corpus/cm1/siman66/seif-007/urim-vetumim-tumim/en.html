There is an opinion that a document written, and so forth. Shach, in seif katan 10, argues at length against Terumat HaDeshen. His objections are not solid. Terumat HaDeshen relies on Responsa Rosh, klal 68, which rules that if a note says the borrower obligates himself to the named lender "and to whoever produces it," the bearer does not need a formal authorization. Shach agrees no authorization is needed, but says a purchaser still requires written transfer and delivery. I do not understand how those positions can coexist. If the borrower is not directly obligated to the bearer, then the bearer is merely the original lender's agent. An ordinary agent cannot compel the debtor to pay without a formal authorization, as all the poskim rule. Harsha'ah functions like a transfer; where one cannot transfer the right, one cannot authorize collection under the strict Talmudic law. Why should this bearer be stronger than any other agent who also comes through the lender? The only explanation is that because the note expressly says "to whoever produces it," the borrower directly obligated himself to the bearer. The bearer therefore sues in his own right. Once that is true, he should not need written transfer and delivery either. If written transfer were still required, he would remain only the lender's representative until that transfer occurred, and authorization should likewise be required. This is even more evident from Rosh's responsum itself. A note was written by Reuven in favor of his wife Leah, payable "to Leah and to whoever produces it." Both died. Their son Levi inherited them. Yehudah then appeared with the note and sought collection because of the bearer language. Rosh writes that the phrase "to whoever produces it" takes the place of authorization or written transfer and delivery. The condition was written precisely so the note could be sold without a separate transfer writing. The lender need only hand the note to the purchaser, who can then collect. Rosh then adds that because an heir can forgive a sold debt, Levi can forgive this debt to himself. This is explicit. The bearer needs neither harsha'ah nor written transfer. Shach cannot answer that no writing is needed only against the borrower but the original lender can still retract. That would reduce the arrangement to mere authorization, whereas Rosh expressly says that if the holder wishes to sell the note, the buyer does not need written transfer. What rational buyer would purchase a note if the seller could revoke the transfer at any moment? The principal function of writing and delivery is precisely to bind the seller. Nor can the problem be solved by allowing the heir simply to revoke the authorization. If Yehudah held only a power of attorney, Levi could say, "I cancel my mother's authorization and my own inherited authority," and Yehudah would have no standing. Rosh would not need the separate doctrine of forgiveness. His resort to forgiveness proves that the holder's proprietary right already exists. The same appears from Rosh in Perek HaKotev in his dispute with Raavad concerning a clause "I obligate myself to you and to whoever comes through you." Rosh says the purpose of the clause is to enable the debt to be sold to others. Obviously he means without a separate transfer writing; with an ordinary valid writing and delivery, any note can be sold even without that clause. The matter is therefore as clear as daylight: according to Rosh, a note written "to you and whoever comes through you," and all the more "to whoever produces it," is acquired by delivery alone. Shach asks: if so, how can the original creditor still forgive? In his view the ability to forgive and the need for written transfer must rise and fall together. There is no difficulty. Rosh explains that even when the borrower writes, "to you and whoever comes through you," the borrower's personal body is not simultaneously obligated to two people. He did not intend to place his own personal obligation under two independent masters. The direct personal lien remains primarily toward the original lender. What the borrower extends to future bearers is the property lien and the right of collection from his assets. That is enough to eliminate the need for written transfer. The entire conventional sale of a note concerns the lien against the borrower's property, since the borrower's body itself cannot literally be sold. Here the property lien is already written from the outset in favor of future bearers. Nevertheless, if the original lender later forgives the borrower's personal obligation, the property lien falls with it because a person's assets are guarantors for his personal obligation. Once the principal personal lien is released, the guarantor lien is extinguished. This is also why, according to the view that ordinary sale of notes is rabbinic, the inability to transfer the borrower's personal obligation prevents Torah sale of the property lien. Here the borrower expressly created a future property lien for bearers, so rabbinically delivery is enough, yet the retained personal lien can still support forgiveness. One can also invoke Ramban's approach that because Torah law does not recognize retroactive clarification, an obligation to "you and whoever comes through you" may itself operate only rabbinically. Ran mentions this in Gittin. Rosh's responsum may sound more like a Torah obligation because he brings a proof from Shaul's promise that the king would enrich whoever defeated Goliath, but the practical result remains the same. Ritva suggests another formulation: "to you and whoever comes through you" means so long as the later holder remains legally "through you." If you forgive the debt, he no longer comes through you and the clause itself ceases to support him. This too answers Shach. But we do not need that additional theory. Rosh's own two-lien analysis suffices. Terumat HaDeshen and Rama therefore stand. Shitah Mekubetzet in Perek HaSefinah likewise cites Riaz that although ordinary documents require writing and delivery, where the note says it is payable to the lender and any bearer, the bearer can collect without a transfer writing. This is direct support for Terumat HaDeshen.
It is true, Shach later argues that even according to Raavad, who holds that such a bearer note cannot be forgiven, written transfer is still required because the bearer must prove that he "comes through" the lender. This too is unclear. Once the law says that delivery of this special note is the effective mode of sale, physical possession itself proves that the bearer comes through the lender, just as according to Rebbi, who holds ordinary documents are acquired by delivery alone, delivery itself demonstrates the transfer. If no formal authorization is needed, the borrower cannot say, "Who says you came through the lender?" Rashba, responsum 921, expressly says a bearer under such a clause need not produce authorization. The evidentiary function of writing and delivery is therefore unnecessary. Sema and Bach likewise understand that according to Raavad no separate transfer writing is required. In my opinion the same follows according to everyone. Ran also indicates that a clause "I obligate myself to you and whoever comes through you" can create a Torah property lien and therefore prevent forgiveness, while Ramban views it as rabbinic because of the problem of retroactive clarification. In either version, if the transfer itself is of Torah force there is certainly no basis to require the rabbinic form of written transfer that ordinary notes need. Shach also asks: if every ordinary loan is legally treated as though the borrower said, "I obligate myself to you and whoever comes through you," as one discussion in Gittin suggests, then according to Raavad no note should ever be forgivable. But one could ask an even simpler question on Shach's own view: if every loan contains that implied clause, why would any ordinary note ever require written transfer at all? The answer is that an oral understanding is not enough to create a purchaser-facing written lien. Collection from purchasers depends on what the instrument itself publicizes. When the note does not actually contain the bearer clause, a purchaser of the note needs written transfer to stand in the original creditor's documentary position. When the note expressly says "to you and whoever comes through you," the future bearer's right is written in the instrument itself, and he can collect from purchasers on that basis. This is why the challenge in Gittin focuses on forgiveness rather than transfer formalities. Accordingly, Terumat HaDeshen's ruling is correct and free of the difficulties Shach raises.
