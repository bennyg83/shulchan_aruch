Seif 40. But they lose their wages. See Netivot HaMishpat and what I wrote in my third-edition responsum on Choshen Mishpat, siman 211, where I raised a difficulty with this ruling. See there also regarding the Shach’s conclusion that a kinyan cannot create an obligation to swear, and what I wrote concerning the words of Machaneh Ephraim. The Mechaber’s statement here that some authorities impose liability for negligence is based on the view that one who is negligent is treated as one who directly causes damage. In my addenda to Even HaEzer I proved from Avodah Zarah 71b that negligence can indeed be treated as damaging. There, seif 40: some authorities impose liability for negligence. This is the Rambam’s view. In Hilchot Maaseh HaKorbanot 14:5 he writes that one who vows a korban and designates it remains responsible if it is lost or stolen until it is offered, whereas one who merely dedicates a voluntary offering is not responsible if it dies or is stolen. It is noteworthy that with the voluntary offering the Rambam writes “dies or is stolen,” rather than stating the stronger case that even if he was negligent he is exempt. This suggests that the Rambam regards him at least as a shomer chinam, liable for negligence, consistent with the Rambam’s broader position that negligent custodianship is a form of damaging. Although one who damages consecrated property is exempt from the laws of meilah and the added fifth, he can remain liable for the principal loss. The later case of one who says, “I undertake to offer it in the temple of Chonyo,” and nevertheless fulfills his vow, is different because by expressly stipulating that condition he is treated as having stipulated that he will not bear ordinary responsibility even if he causes the loss through the specified act. Without such an express condition, one who says “this animal is an offering” can still be liable if he damages it directly or is negligent. There is also room to examine whether one who says, “I undertake to bring an olah,” remains liable if the designated animal dies through a complete accident. The Rambam’s initial phrase mentions loss or theft and does not mention death, which could imply exemption for a true accident. Yet in the second clause he says that a voluntary offering is exempt if it dies or is stolen, implying by contrast that a vowed offering remains liable even if it dies. One can reconcile the wording by saying that in the first clause the Rambam wished to teach that the vower must replace the animal immediately even when it was merely lost or stolen and might later be found. He cannot postpone until after the festival on the hope that it will reappear, because his personal responsibility remains and he may violate bal te’acher. In the second clause the Rambam adds the case of death to teach that even where the animal certainly will never return, the voluntary donor has no further liability. His choice of theft there hints that the death contemplated is not necessarily a total accident, and perhaps a genuinely unavoidable death would exempt even the one who said “harei alai.” The Rambam also deliberately departs from the Mishna’s wording, which uses death in both clauses. This requires further study.
There, in the same seif: if they made a kinyan that he would be obligated like custodians. The Shach writes that a kinyan to impose an oath is ineffective because it is merely a kinyan on words. Machaneh Ephraim cites Mahara’sh and the Shach and asks from siman 22, where a kinyan to swear is treated as binding. The matters must be distinguished. At first one might answer, as Urim VeTumim and Netivot do, that an undertaking to swear is ultimately monetary because if he refuses the oath he must pay, so the kinyan is on money rather than mere words. Yet the deeper point is that in our case the monetary liability is itself an asmachta. A kinyan works because, in return for the trust placed in him, the custodian genuinely undertakes responsibility. That reasoning is sufficient where there is only one uncertainty, whether the item will be stolen. But if the undertaking is framed as “I will swear if it is stolen,” and one seeks to derive a monetary obligation from failure to swear, there are two contingencies: perhaps it will never be stolen, and even if it is, perhaps he will swear. The benefit of being trusted is not enough to overcome such a double asmachta. Thus the kinyan cannot be sustained as a monetary undertaking, while as an undertaking merely to perform an oath it is a kinyan on words. Siman 22 is different because the claim already exists when the kinyan is made. There is no future contingency and no asmachta of this sort. Therefore a kinyan to swear there can be effective because refusal to swear immediately entails payment. This also explains why the Shach’s discussion in siman 291 does not contradict his words here. There the kinyan was to assume monetary liability as a paid custodian, which can take effect. The remaining question is only whether, by voluntarily accepting that monetary status, he also accepted the ordinary oath that accompanies it. Perhaps he intended liability only upon admission and not liability based upon an evidentiary presumption. That is a separate issue from whether a direct kinyan on a future oath can take effect. Examine this carefully.
