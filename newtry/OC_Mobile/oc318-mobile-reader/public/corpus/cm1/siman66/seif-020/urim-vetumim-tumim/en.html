Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 96 writes that according to Rambam, who holds that a deathbed person can give debt documents without saying "acquire them and all their liens," a person claiming, "These notes were given to me as a deathbed gift," need not bring proof even against the borrower. Shach, seif katan 39, objects and cites Ran, Ramban, and R. Moshe ben Natan in Baal HaTerumot. But those authorities follow Rif's approach, not Rambam's. They hold that even against the seller the possessor is not believed to say, "I had a written transfer and it was lost." The note therefore remains presumed to belong to the original creditor. Naturally, every later claimant must bring proof. Rambam is different. Against the seller he is believed to say that he had proper writing and delivery or that the note was transferred through agav. He is therefore the possessor of the note. His problem against the borrower is only that the borrower can say, "Your possession alone does not prove the debt lien was transferred to you." If the holder says, "It was given to me as a deathbed gift," and under Rambam such a gift requires no special transfer language, then the case reverts to the system where documents are acquired by delivery alone. Possession itself proves the acquisition. There is no missing lien-formality to prove. Thus Beit Yosef is correct. This is almost compelled by Rambam's treatment of the dispute between Abaye and Rava in Bava Batra. Rava says one who holds another's documents need not bring proof; Abaye says he does. The Gemara challenges Rava from the baraita that one brother who produces family notes must prove that they belong to him. If Rava's rule applies only against the seller and not against the borrower, the challenge is weak. The baraita can simply concern collection from the borrower, especially where the other brothers protest and the borrower can say, "You are not my litigant." Yet the Gemara treats it as a direct contradiction. The answer is that if the possessor can claim the notes were given to him as a deathbed gift, then under Rambam he does not need further transfer proof even against the borrower. That is why the baraita truly challenges Rava. The same point appears in the Mishnah of two people with the same name who produce notes against third parties. The Gemara explains one view by saying documents are acquired by delivery and no further proof is needed. If, even under delivery-only acquisition, the borrower could always demand separate proof, the Mishnah would still be difficult. It clearly assumes that where delivery itself is the operative acquisition, possession is enough against the borrower as well. So too with a deathbed gift under Rambam. This also resolves Rambam's apparently inconsistent formulations. In Hilchot Mechirah 6 he says that against the seller no proof is needed, but against the borrower the transferee must prove the transfer. Raavad agrees in principle. In Hilchot Nachalot 9, however, Rambam writes that if an outsider possesses a note and says it was given to him or purchased from its owner, he can collect without bringing proof, whereas one brother holding family notes must prove his special ownership. Raavad attacks this as following Ri Migash, who broadly holds no proof is needed. But Rambam's words can be read precisely. "It was given to me" refers to a deathbed gift, the very context Rambam had just discussed. In that mode, no writing-and-delivery proof is required. "I purchased it" can be believed through a migo that it was a deathbed gift. Thus the collector can recover even against the borrower. Raavad himself, elsewhere in Baal HaTerumot, appears to agree that a genuine deathbed gift has this special effect, even though his gloss to Rambam sounds stricter. This also explains why Mechaber above in siman 64 requires witnesses of deposit and seeing. The relevant migo is not the uncommon claim, "I had a written transfer and lost it," but the commoner claim, "It was given to me as a deathbed gift." That migo must be eliminated. Rambam's own stricter result in siman 64 can be explained because where the dying person owed money to others, a deathbed gift of the notes to an outsider is itself uncommon. A dying debtor would not ordinarily give away assets and leave creditors unpaid. In such a context the migo becomes too weak. Thus Beit Yosef's ruling is sound: under Rambam, a plausible claim of deathbed gift can remove the need for separate transfer proof even against the borrower.
