Shach writes that if a married woman inherits the power to forgive a debt note her father had held against her mother or brother, and that note had been sold before the relevant deaths, then even if she forgives the debt to herself she need not pay damages until she is widowed or divorced. Whatever a married woman acquires is generally subject to her husband's rights, so she lacks presently available property from which to pay. This is not agreed by everyone. According to the view that in a case of loss caused by her wrongdoing the husband is treated as an heir rather than as a purchaser regarding her nichsei melog, she can be compelled to sell those property rights to satisfy the injured party. Bava Kamma says that absent the enactment of Usha a woman could sell her nichsei melog to pay a person she injured. Tosafot at the end of Perek Yesh Nochalin say one could even ask that, despite the enactment of Usha, in a case of actual loss Chazal treated the husband as heir. They give another answer that such cases are uncommon. Our case may not be so uncommon. Why should a woman not forgive to herself when doing so benefits her directly? Therefore, according to at least one Tosafot approach, her nichsei melog may be sold to cover the purchaser's loss. This is especially relevant under the authorities who treat dinei de-garmi as ordinary monetary law rather than a penalty. It also clarifies an otherwise puzzling passage in Baal HaTerumot. He cites early authorities explaining why R. Nachman advised his relative to forgive her mother's ketubah to her father without requiring damages: dinei de-garmi is only a penalty, and Chazal did not impose that penalty on a child for a parent's act. At first glance this makes no sense. The daughter herself performs the forgiveness. She is the damager. Why should the rule "they did not penalize the son after the father" matter? The answer is that some authorities explain she was already married. Shach's approach would therefore say she has no independently collectible property. Tosafot, however, did not use that answer because they hold that her inheritance is nichsei melog and, in a loss case, the husband's status may be that of heir, allowing the property to be reached. Baal HaTerumot's quoted authorities can answer that even if the husband is treated like an heir, the only basis for liability is the rabbinic garmi penalty. Chazal did not extend that penalty to the husband's inherited-type interest arising through the wife. The husband therefore does not have to surrender those assets for her garmi act. This makes the passage coherent. There is also a question why she cannot simply sell the future value of her nichsei melog, as the Gemara suggests for a woman who injures another. Rama in Even HaEzer siman 90, seif 9, writes that under the enactment of Usha, a woman's sale of nichsei melog can become effective after widowhood or divorce. Beit Shmuel asks how she can sell now something that is not presently within her unrestricted power, given the rule that one cannot sell something not yet in one's possession. The Gemara in Bava Kamma does say she can sell the future value. But that discussion can be according to R. Meir, who holds a person can transfer future property. The Mishnah there is R. Meir in several respects, as Tosafot note. According to our halachah, which does not broadly allow sale of future-acquired rights, the woman may not be able to realize that future value now. Thus Shach's practical conclusion may be correct under some approaches, but it is not universal. The legal status of the husband's interest, the nature of garmi liability, and the transferability of the woman's future rights all remain disputed.
