Then the original lender remains obligated to swear before collection, and if he himself says the note was paid, the purchaser's right is defeated. But if the note contains a credibility clause eliminating the lender's oath, the seller cannot simply say "paid" and destroy the purchaser's right. This is the same principle explained above in siman 47. Shach is surprised because the seller has a migo that he could forgive the debt. He rejects Raavad's responsum, cited by Baal HaTerumot, which says we do not use the migo of forgiveness to support a claim of prior payment. Shach's rejection is too strong, especially because Tosafot support Raavad. Tosafot Ketubot 19b ask why a creditor is not believed to say a note was paid through a migo that he could forgive it. They answer that he does not want to forgive, because he still expects the borrower to pay him. If he openly forgives, the borrower will have no reason to pay. Therefore forgiveness is not an equivalent attractive alternative. Tosafot then ask from the receipt case where the woman is believed through a migo of forgiveness. They answer that there an actual receipt is present and weakens the note; with that supporting evidence, even a weaker migo suffices. This is essentially Raavad's approach. A sold note carries a chazakah that it was not already paid. A person does not ordinarily sell a worthless paid document and make himself a thief. Saying "it was paid before I sold it" therefore contradicts a strong presumption. The migo of forgiveness is too weak to overcome that chazakah unless some independent circumstance, like a receipt or an unavoidable oath requirement, has already weakened the document. Therefore Baal HaTerumot reasonably limits the rule to a note without a credibility clause. In that case the purchaser cannot collect until the seller swears. The need for the seller's oath itself weakens the purchaser's position, somewhat like a receipt. The seller's assertion of payment can then be accepted through the weaker migo. With an express credibility clause, no such weakness exists, and the migo of forgiveness alone should not overturn the sale. It is true that many authorities disagree and say the migo of forgiveness is enough. Ramban, Rosh, and Ran in Perek Kol HaNishba'in contain language supporting that approach. But those passages generally concern notes without a special credibility clause, where the seller's oath remains relevant. Thus they need not reject Baal HaTerumot's distinction. The issue therefore remains a real dispute among major authorities. If the purchaser has already seized property, he may perhaps say kim li like Baal HaTerumot and Raavad, especially where the note contains strong credibility language. Maggid Mishneh gives two explanations for why the seller's admission of payment is effective: first, because he has a migo of forgiveness; second, because saying "it was paid" is itself equivalent to forgiveness. Mishneh LaMelech asks: if merely refusing to swear already makes the seller compensate the purchaser, all the more so an express admission of payment. Why does Maggid Mishneh need either theory? The answer is that these points are interdependent. If the seller had no legal power to destroy the purchaser's right by admission, then there would be no reason to require him to swear. What would the oath accomplish? Even if he openly admitted payment, the admission would be legally irrelevant. Only because the seller's admission can be effective, whether through migo of forgiveness or because it is itself treated as forgiveness, does his oath matter. Refusal to swear then creates the purchaser's remedy. Thus Maggid Mishneh explains the principal rule, and the oath consequence follows from it. This is also why, in a type of note that cannot be forgiven at all, the seller may not even be required to swear. If his admission cannot harm the purchaser, there is no purpose in imposing an oath concerning prior payment. This interdependence explains Rama's ruling in Even HaEzer siman 91 concerning a debt note brought into marriage that the wife cannot forgive and cannot say it was paid. Beit Shmuel there considers whether refusal to swear might still affect her. According to what I have written, the two issues rise and fall together. The matter remains subject to the distinctions explained above, but Baal HaTerumot and Raavad cannot be dismissed.
