But he cannot, etc. Sema explains the novelty as follows: although movable property can be acquired together with land, including movables that one will later acquire, this does not work for a debt instrument. The Shach objects that even ordinary movables not yet in existence cannot be transferred as present property; one may only create a lien on them. He therefore explains that because the underlying loan already exists, one might have thought the debt could be transferred even though the formal document had not yet been written. The law teaches otherwise. This additional explanation is unnecessary. Rivash's case involved an existing memorandum document already written and signed by witnesses for the scribe's records, from which a full deed was later to be prepared, as explained above in siman 61. Rivash rules that because the memorandum does not yet have the legal status of a complete deed and a new deed must still be written, incidental acquisition does not transfer it as a debt instrument.
