He must pay. Maggid Mishneh, Sema, and Shach agree that the seller must pay the purchaser the full value of the note even if he says explicitly that the debt was paid before the sale. I am uncertain about this. If we accept Maggid Mishneh's second theory, that the seller's statement "it was paid" is itself equivalent to forgiveness, then full reimbursement makes sense. A person who forgives a sold note after the sale may be liable for the entire value he destroyed. But if the seller is believed only because he has a migo that he could have forgiven, then why should he pay the full value? Indeed, the opposite question arises. Why is he believed at all to say the note was already paid before the sale? The alternative claim of forgiveness would expose him to full damages, while his present claim would expose him only to return of the purchase price. That is not an equal migo. Many authorities in the first chapter of Bava Metzia make precisely this objection. Thus one cannot both rely on the migo of forgiveness and at the same time impose the full damages of actual forgiveness without further justification. Shach strongly favors the theory that saying "it was paid" is itself a form of forgiveness. But this view is nearly singular. Tosafot, Rosh, Ramban, and Ran generally formulate the seller's credibility through migo of forgiveness, not through equivalence between the two acts. Shach tries to use this to solve Tosafot's question in the found-receipt case. Tosafot ask why the woman has a migo of forgiveness when forgiveness would force her to reimburse the purchaser for the full value. Shach answers that returning the receipt is itself equivalent to forgiveness, so she would owe the same amount either way. That does not resolve the Gemara. If the receipt is returned to the husband in one jurisdiction while the purchasers are elsewhere and do not know the history of the finding, the husband can later present the receipt and defeat them. How would those purchasers know that the receipt had once been lost and judicially returned, such that the woman should owe full damages? There is no general publicity of ordinary document loss comparable to gittin. Nor can we say beit din writes on the receipt that it had been lost. If so, the entire Talmudic concern and much of the surrounding sugya would collapse. "Return it" plainly means return the document substantially as found. Therefore the theory that mere restoration is automatically treated like formal forgiveness is not reflected in the sources. I already established above in siman 65 from Tur and Mechaber in Even HaEzer siman 105 that where a woman sold her ketubah and later says, "I had already been paid before the sale," she is not believed through a migo that she could forgive, because she does not want to expose herself to the full value. This directly contradicts Shach's broad equation of the two acts. Shach himself later challenges that Even HaEzer ruling, but I have defended it above. Accordingly, if the seller truly proves or is accepted as saying that payment occurred before the sale, he should not automatically owe the full face value. At most he returns the consideration he received because the sale was defective. In a gift, if the note was already paid before the gift, he should ordinarily owe nothing, absent fraud or a separate undertaking. Therefore the present ruling should be understood as speaking where the seller says payment occurred after the sale. Then his conduct destroyed the purchaser's debt and full damages can follow. If he says it was paid before the sale, the law is at least doubtful. Even if his claim is accepted, he should not ordinarily pay more than the purchase price, and in a gift he may be exempt altogether. This is the sounder practical distinction.
