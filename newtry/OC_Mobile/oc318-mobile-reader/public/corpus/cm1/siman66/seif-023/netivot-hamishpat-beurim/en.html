Nevertheless, the purchaser is not obligated to return the borrower's document. See Shach, seif katan 76, who cites Rashba as bringing a proof from Kiddushin 48. The Gemara does not challenge the Sages against their own position regarding the statement that one appraises the value of the paper, which indicates that if the paper itself is worth a perutah, the betrothal is effective according to everyone. Shach rejects this proof, arguing that the Gemara may simply have chosen not to raise an internal contradiction within the Sages because the dispute could instead be understood according to the alternative explanation, as concerning one who wrote but did not deliver, which depends upon the dispute of Rabbi and the Sages, or according to Rav Pappa. Tumim strongly questions Shach. If the baraita concerns a case where the document was delivered but not written over, so that according to the Sages it was not acquired, what reliance can she place upon the paper? The borrower must necessarily pay the original creditor, since she did not acquire the lien. If so, she too would be compelled to return the paper to the borrower. If, even where she acquired the document through writing and delivery and the creditor later waives the debt, the purchaser must return the paper, then certainly where she never acquired the debt at all and the borrower pays the creditor, she must return it. Ritva, who holds that it need not be returned, follows his own view that even upon waiver the paper need not be returned. But according to the view that after waiver it must be returned, then all the more so where there was delivery without writing, it must be returned after payment to the creditor. In my humble view, however, Shach can be reconciled. The essential rule is certainly that the promissory note belongs fully to the lender before payment, as Ketzot writes in the name of Rashba, and Ketzot himself develops this at length and agrees. This appears exceedingly clear to me from Tosafot and all the decisors, who rule that the lender may sell the physical paper for use as scrap, as explained above. The reason some authorities nevertheless hold that after the lender waives the debt the purchaser must return the paper is that they understand the purchaser's primary intent in acquiring the note to have been only to collect the loan, meaning that he should stand in the place of the lender for purposes of collection. The lender himself owned the paper only so long as the lien embodied in the document remained in force. Once the debt is waived, the paper reverts to the borrower. But if the lender explicitly sold only the paper itself for its physical use, there is no doubt that the purchaser need not return it when the borrower later pays, as is implied in Tosafot in several places. Accordingly, Kiddushin 48 is readily understood. When the Gemara says that one appraises the paper, it must be understood in the same manner as the statement on page 46 that one who betroths with a loan and a perutah intends the betrothal to rest upon the perutah. This remains so even where the loan is owed by others. For if her intent rested on the loan, why should the betrothal be effective when he said that he betroths her with the promissory note and with every right embodied in it? Since she cannot be betrothed with the debt owed by others, she would not be betrothed at all, as Rashi explains on page 86. Thus her intent rests only upon the paper, and the transaction is expressly one in which she acquires the paper for its physical use. She therefore need not return it even if the borrower later pays. For the same reason, one does not appraise the document according to its evidentiary value in the hands of the borrower after payment, as explained in siman 54, seif katan 4, because evidentiary value is not intrinsic monetary value. When she is betrothed through a combination of something having intrinsic monetary value and something lacking such intrinsic value, her intent rests upon the item having intrinsic value. Her intent therefore rests only upon the paper's physical value for use as paper.
And even the heir may waive it. See Sema, seif katan 18, who writes that if a minor waives the debt, it is a mistaken waiver. Shach objects that a minor's waiver is effective, as explained below in siman 235, and likewise in Tosafot, Ketubot 66 and Bava Batra 147. The words of Ketzot HaChoshen are correct on this point. It is explicit in siman 235, seif 1, in the gloss, that where the minor acts improperly, his gift is not a gift. There can be no conduct more improper than this, since it is forbidden to cause another person financial harm. This is not comparable to the cases discussed by Tosafot in Ketubot and Bava Batra. There, the minor certainly acted properly, for Rabban Gamliel himself advised her to act in that manner. Necessarily, it was permitted for the reason Tosafot explain there. The distinction is therefore sound.
For even in his own favor he may waive it. See Shach, seif katan 82, regarding a minor who waives the debt for his own benefit, where Shach writes that he need not pay. However, according to Shach in seif katan 109, even according to the opinion that does not impose liability for causative damage, he must nevertheless return the money he received, because repayment of money received is not imposed as damages but as an independent monetary obligation. If so, even a minor who waives the debt must return the money that he received for it out of his father's estate. Tumim likewise writes that even according to the opinion that there is no liability here for causing financial damage because there was no intent to harm, the money received must nevertheless be repaid as a matter of law. Accordingly, the well known question upon this opinion, which permits an heir to waive the debt for himself without paying for the damage he causes, from Bava Kamma 69, is not difficult. There the Gemara brings support for the enactment of Usha from a baraita in which witnesses testify falsely, “We testify that he divorced her and paid her ketubah,” and they are required to pay only the actuarial value of her ketubah. The Gemara argues that if the enactment of Usha did not exist, she could sell the ketubah entirely. According to the present opinion, however, one might ask that even without the enactment of Usha she could not sell it entirely, because if she dies the husband is her heir, and he could waive the obligation to himself and thereby destroy the purchaser's right without having to pay damages. According to what has been stated, there is no difficulty, because he would at least have to return the money she received. Still, this answer is strained, because no one would purchase such a right if, upon waiver, the purchaser would recover only his purchase money. Who would enter into such a transaction? It therefore appears that the original question is no question at all. It is explained in Even HaEzer siman 90 that a husband does not inherit loans owed to his wife in the same manner as a firstborn does. Only a loan made after the marriage belongs to him by virtue of the enactment of Usha. Concerning a loan owed by the husband himself, the authorities dispute the matter, as explained there in Beit Shmuel and Chelkat Mechokek. Accordingly, if the enactment of Usha did not exist, the ketubah would at least be no better than a debt owed by the husband himself, concerning which the rule is that the parties divide because of doubt. Thus, at minimum, half the ketubah would be fully saleable even if she later died. Why then does the Mishnah say that the false witnesses pay only its actuarial value? Rather, the enactment of Usha must exist, and the Gemara properly derives support from that source. When the questioner there says, “She should sell her ketubah entirely,” the wording is because the status of a debt owed by the husband himself is doubtful and the parties divide, as Tosafot write in several places. Therefore, the enactment of Usha must exist, for if it did not, one could resolve the doubt and conclude that a husband inherits a debt owed by himself. Otherwise the question would remain, why can she not sell the ketubah entirely? This is a common manner of argument in the Gemara. The Gemara there asks why she does not sell the ketubah entirely rather than simply saying that a husband does not inherit his wife's loans and that the debt therefore belongs to another heir after her death. It appears that this is because false witnesses pay only the amount for which the document could be sold to others, not its subjective value to the holder. A proof is the Gemara's question in Bava Kamma that if the actuarial value belongs to the husband, the witnesses can say to her, “What loss did we cause you? Had you sold it, your husband would have taken the proceeds from you.” This is difficult. Why can the woman not answer, “Appraise for me what it was worth to me personally, since I would not have sold it at all”? Rather, false witnesses pay only the market value of the document, namely, what it could be sold for in exchange for immediate cash. There is also another way to resolve the general question, and at the same time to resolve Tosafot's question in the passage beginning “Rav Idi bar Avin said, we too,” where they ask why the Gemara abandons the Mishnah in Makkot. At first sight, another question arises. Why do the false witnesses not pay the entire amount of the ketubah? Tosafot in Makkot ask, in the passage beginning “We testify,” that the witnesses should also be liable for the support and clothing they attempted to cause the woman to lose. Tosafot answer that since the woman herself said, “He divorced me,” she thereby waived those rights. If so, here the husband says, “I divorced you and paid your ketubah.” Without the false witnesses, the husband would be believed when he says that he divorced her, but he would not be believed when he says that he paid the ketubah, since the ketubah document remains in her possession. He would therefore have been obligated to pay the ketubah immediately. The false witnesses, by testifying that he paid it, would appear to have caused her the loss of the entire ketubah. Necessarily, the answer is that she herself says, “You did not divorce me,” and thus he is not presently obligated to pay the ketubah. Where the admissions of claimant and defendant contradict each other, we follow the admission that favors the party in possession, as is evident from the case where one claims wheat and the other admits barley, and in several other places. Here too, we follow the woman's admission that she was not divorced, and consequently the husband is not presently obligated to pay the ketubah. Accordingly, the general question is again resolved. How could she sell the ketubah, if the husband is her heir and could waive the debt to himself? It is explained in siman 280 that where Shimon says, “I am not my brother's heir,” we follow the admission of the person who declares that he is not an heir. So too here, we follow the husband's admission that he is not the heir. If we say that the husband takes the wife's property as a purchaser during her lifetime, then he is presently in the position of a defendant against her claim, and we follow the woman's admission. But if we say that the husband is only her heir after death, then we follow the husband's admission that he is not the heir. For this reason, the Gemara could not have raised the same question from the Mishnah there. In that Mishnah, the husband claims that he did not divorce her, and the woman is not believed against him to say that he did. Since the husband has not removed himself from the presumptive status of heir, the Gemara could not ask why she does not sell her ketubah entirely, because the original difficulty would then return: the husband is the heir and could waive the debt to himself.
