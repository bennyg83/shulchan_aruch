One who sells a debt document, etc. In Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 329, he ruled concerning a person who owed his fellow one hundred under a written document, and the creditor sold the document to another for less, that the debtor can say to the creditor, "I will give you the same amount that this other person gave you," and the creditor cannot sell it. Even if he already sold it, the debtor can prevent the transfer, because sale of debt documents is only rabbinic. See there. He brings proof from below in siman 316, seif 1. This does not appear correct, especially according to what I wrote above in seif 8, seif katan 1, that sale of debt documents is by Torah law. Nor is there any proof from siman 316, because there the house belongs to the owner, and one can also say that he rented it specifically to this tenant and not to another. The distinction is easy to understand. See what I write below in siman 175, seif 53.
If he later forgave it to the debtor, it is forgiven. Even according to the view that forgiveness is ineffective where the creditor physically holds the document, as I write below in siman 241, seif 2, that authority agrees here, because the document is not in the seller's possession to be returned. So writes Baal HaItur, folio 93b. Responsa Mahara ibn Chaim, siman 92, folio 137b, writes regarding a person who gave a debt document to Leah while Leah herself owed money to the debtor, that in such a case the original creditor's later forgiveness does not take effect. Since in an ordinary sale of a debt document the recipient collects until the transferor forgives it, whereas here Leah herself owed the debtor, the gift of the debt document to her is considered already collected. He sought support from the sugya in the second chapter of Kesubos concerning two persons who each produce a debt document against the other, where the law is that each collects from the other, and from Responsa Rivash, siman 396, regarding one who claimed, "I acquired wheat against my debt," where the Rivash disputed another sage whether he had acquired it from the outset. According to the dissenting view, he acquired immediately, and they disagreed there only because the parties were not expert in valuation. Mahara ibn Chaim adds that although in the present case the transferors of the documents still had to take an oath, that does not render the debt uncollected. His words are astonishing. On the contrary, the Gemara in the second chapter of Kesubos concerning two persons who each produce a debt document against the other repeatedly proves that each nevertheless collects from the other. The same is explicit in many other places in the Gemara and the poskim, and below throughout siman 85, especially in seifim 4 and 6. The Rivash's wheat case likewise proves the opposite when examined carefully. Therefore, even though Leah owes money to the debtor, the original creditor can still forgive the debt document. This is clear, contrary to Mahara ibn Chaim.
It is forgiven. It appears that there is no distinction whether the debt document contains a credibility clause or not. In every case the seller can forgive it. Although the Raavan, siman 47, cited briefly by Mordechai in Perek HaKosev, may imply that where the document contains a credibility clause the seller cannot forgive it, we do not rule this way. The poskim, Tur, and Mechaber state the rule without distinction. This is also proved by the Rif, Ran, and other poskim in Perek HaKosev, who show that the power of forgiveness is not dependent upon the debtor's ability to claim payment. On the contrary, the Gemara at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia implies that the seller is believed to say that the debt is paid because he could forgive it. I cited these authorities above in seif 15, seif katan 52. Many poskim likewise write in various places that the seller is believed to say "paid" through a migo that he could forgive. See what I wrote above in seif 11, seif katan 34. The Sema cites Maharik, Shoresh 89, that the seller may forgive even before the debt's due date. There was no need to cite Maharik for this, because it is evident from the Gemara and all the poskim from the laws of ketubah and many other cases. See siman 86, seif katan 11.
However, the purchaser does not, etc. The Sema, seif katan 56, cites Rabbeinu Yerucham, Nesiv 14, that the Rashba's position is primary, that the purchaser need not return the physical document, and notes Darkei Moshe, seif katan 53. In my humble opinion the Rashba's position requires study. His primary proof is from Perek HaIsh Mekadesh, as the Ran writes at the end of his discussion in Perek HaKosev, that the Rashba proves from Kiddushin 48a. There the Gemara raises a contradiction between Rabbi Meir's statements but does not raise one between the Sages' statements concerning appraisal of the paper. This seems to show that if the paper itself is worth a perutah, the woman is betrothed according to all views. See there. In my humble opinion this is no proof. The Gemara did not need to challenge the Sages against themselves, because perhaps the law follows the alternative explanation given earlier, that the dispute concerns a case where he wrote but did not deliver, either in the dispute between Rebbi and the Sages or under Rav Pappa's rule, and according to all views a woman relies upon the assumption that the creditor will not later forgive the debt. I cite below authorities who explain that perhaps we rule like that alternative. Accordingly, although she did not acquire the debt claim because he failed to write the necessary transfer formula, she could still acquire the physical paper. Since a woman can be betrothed with a perutah, anything of value that he gives her can effect kiddushin. It resembles a gift of a document where he delivers it without the necessary written transfer; even though the debt itself is not acquired, the recipient at least acquires the paper, as I wrote above in seif 1, seif katan 6, in the name of the Ritva. The Bach in Even HaEzer, siman 28, seif 11, likewise writes that even with delivery without written transfer she acquires the paper. Therefore, the Gemara could not raise a contradiction in that manner. The later answer there, that the case does not concern another person's debt document at all, also resolves the issue. This appears clear to me. What the Ran writes there, that Rashi's words in Perek HaKosev also appear to support the Rashba, is likewise not compelling. Rashi may be speaking where the debtor does not object and leaves the paper wherever it is, and Rashi merely explains the phrase that the purchaser can collect the value of the paper. Perhaps for this reason the Ran himself writes in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh that the paper is not appraised, because the purchaser must return it, as I cited above in seif 16, seif katan 58. This is contrary to what appears from the Beit Yosef here at the end of new material 40, that the Ran agrees with the Rashba. The Beit Yosef did not cite the Ran's discussion in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh. What he writes, that Rashi appears to support the Rashba, I have already shown is not compelled. The Beit Yosef further writes that in a responsum the Rashba brought proof and said, "I ruled this before my teacher the Ramban, and although he initially held the opposite, he struggled greatly and could not answer." In my humble opinion this adds no proof, because it appears to be the same proof the Ran cites in the Rashba's name, which I have already rejected. I myself saw in the Rashba's novellae to the first chapter of Gittin that he writes: "It is possible that he acquires the paper, and it appears to me that proof can be brought from Perek HaIsh Mekadesh concerning appraisal of the paper. However, the words of the Rif appear to the contrary from the incident of the bundle of documents, and so wrote the Ramban. They have support from the question in Perek HaMevi Tanyana concerning a tablet presumed to belong to her." Thus the Rashba himself ultimately records the position of the Ramban and Rif and says that they have support. Rabbeinu Yerucham's own ruling is also difficult, for he decides against the Rosh. The Rosh in Perek HaKosev appears to agree with the sages of Provence whom I cited above in seif 16, seif katan 58. The Ritva also appears to agree, writing in Perek HaKosev that when a debt document has been forgiven, the purchaser must give the document to the debtor. It is only like collateral in the creditor's hand until payment, and once the debt is paid or forgiven, the debtor acquires the body of the document and the purchaser must return it against his will, as held by some early authorities. The Bach in Even HaEzer, siman 28, seif 11, rejects the Ran's view in Perek HaIsh Mekadesh and argues that the debtor has no claim against the woman. When the creditor betroths her with the debt document and transfers it by delivery, she acquires the paper, no less than another person's collateral, through which kiddushin can be effected. He says he already discussed this under the case, "be betrothed to me with a debt document," seif 8. According to what I have written, the Ran is correct and many poskim agree with him. The Bach's comparison is not persuasive. Collateral is actually the creditor's until payment, whereas the physical paper of a debt document serves only as security for the debt itself. Nor is it comparable to her own debt document. There, because the creditor may retain the document until she pays him, when he now gives it to her before she has paid, he gives her something presently worth a perutah. With another person's debt document, however, after the debt is paid or forgiven and the obligation is extinguished, the physical paper belongs automatically to the debtor. This also follows from the Mechaber in Even HaEzer there, seif 13, who omits the Tur's statement that the paper is appraised and writes merely: "If he had a loan evidenced by a document and transferred the document to her by writing and delivery, she is only doubtfully betrothed." This clearly implies that he follows the Ran and does not appraise the paper here. The doubt is according to the stringent view cited by the Maggid Mishneh in chapter 5 of Hilchos Ishus, halachah 17, and brought by the Beit Yosef there. Their reasoning appears to be that perhaps we rule like the alternative explanation in the Gemara that a woman relies upon the assumption that the creditor will not forgive the debt. The Levush there states this explicitly. He writes that if a creditor transfers a debt document properly to a woman by writing and delivery, she is not certainly betrothed because perhaps she does not rely upon it, thinking that he may later forgive the debt. Here one cannot appraise the paper as in the earlier case of her own debt document, because we rule that the paper belongs to the debtor, as the Mishnah states that the debtor pays the scribe's fee. When the debt is paid or forgiven, she must return the paper. Therefore, even if it is presently worth a perutah, he has given her nothing of his own. By contrast, with her own debt document, when he returns her own paper worth a perutah, he truly gives her something, because he is not obligated to return it until she pays him. End of the Levush. All this is contrary to the Bach. Nevertheless, since the Rashba and many great authorities hold that the purchaser cannot be compelled to return the paper, it appears that this is a genuine doubt in monetary law, and the burden of proof is upon the one who seeks to take it. We therefore do not compel the purchaser to return the document. The same doubt applies to kiddushin. Although there is independently a doubt whether she is betrothed through the monetary value of the debt document because perhaps she relied upon the assumption that he would not forgive it, there is an additional practical difference where the purchaser acquired another person's debt document, the debtor paid the seller or the seller forgave the debt, and the purchaser then betrothed a woman with that document after the debt had been paid or forgiven. According to the Rashba and those who agree, she is betrothed if the paper is worth a perutah. Even if it is not worth a perutah here, she may still be doubtfully betrothed because it may be worth a perutah in Media, as the Tur, Mechaber, and later authorities write in Even HaEzer, siman 28, seif 8, regarding her own debt document. The same should apply to another person's debt document, contrary to what appears from the Mechaber in Even HaEzer, seif 13, and the Levush, that in this case she is certainly not betrothed. Perhaps the Mechaber too remained uncertain about this. See what I wrote above in seif 8, seif katan 9. I have discussed this at length because one must not be lenient in a doubt of kiddushin, especially in a matter disputed by the great authorities without a clear resolution. The conclusion from all this and from what I wrote above in seif 16 is as follows. Where one sold a debt document, whether he delivered it with the required writing so that the purchaser acquired it, or delivered it without the writing, and the debt was afterward forgiven or paid, it is doubtful whether the purchaser must return the physical document to the debtor. We do not compel him because the burden of proof is upon the claimant. Therefore, if this purchaser betroths a woman with the document, she is doubtfully betrothed because perhaps the paper is worth a perutah in Media. She is not certainly betrothed even if the paper is worth a perutah locally, because perhaps the purchaser must return it to the debtor. Likewise, if a creditor betroths a woman with a debt document he holds against another person, even if the paper is worth a perutah she is not certainly betrothed, because perhaps she did not rely upon the transaction, fearing that he would later forgive the debt, in which case the physical paper would have to be returned to the debtor. Rather, she is doubtfully betrothed.
That he should make a document in his own name, etc. The Ritva in Perek HaKosev explains that this is effective so that the debtor becomes obligated to pay him directly. However, if the original creditor later forgives the debt, the purchaser cannot collect from property encumbered from the original date. This is obvious.
And even if he stipulated with him, etc. The Bach writes here confused arguments and unusual distinctions between whether the seller admits that the debt is still owed to him or not, and therefore leaves the matter unresolved. In my humble opinion the law is clear. In every case such a stipulation is ineffective, and the Beit Yosef also holds this way. Although Baal HaItur derived the rule from the Gemara's failure to suggest this device, the Gemara itself still requires an explanation of why it would not work. Therefore, the Beit Yosef writes that a person cannot stipulate that he will be unable to do something that the law gives him the ability to do. The same applies if he stipulates that he will not forgive the debt. Since he retains the legal power to forgive, what consequence does his private stipulation have for the debtor? Baal HaTerumos likewise holds this way. When Baal HaTerumos writes regarding Baal HaItur, "his reason was not revealed to me," he is referring to Baal HaItur's statement that the text of the Yerushalmi does not prove the rule. The word "not" is missing from some copies of Baal HaTerumos; an examination of Baal HaItur, folio 92d, shows the correct reading. On that statement Baal HaTerumos says that the reasoning was not clear to him. It also appears that the phrase, "his reason was not revealed to me," may itself be copied from Baal HaItur, corresponding to the phrase, "this requires study," in our text of Baal HaItur concerning the Yerushalmi. Perhaps Baal HaTerumos had the words, "his reason was not revealed to me," in place of "this requires study," or paraphrased the same intent. In any event, as a matter of practical law it is clear that no such stipulation is effective in any case, and the seller can forgive the debt, as proved by the Gemara and poskim. No authority disagrees, contrary to the Bach.
He stipulated with him, etc. Even if the seller initially wrote that he disqualifies any witnesses who will later testify to a forgiveness, he can nevertheless forgive the debt. The Beit Yosef cites this at the end of the siman, new material 50, in the name of Responsa Rashba, and it is obvious. The Rambam writes in chapter 6 of Hilchos Mechirah, halachah 13, that it appears to him that even if the creditor transferred the debt document to the purchaser together with land, he can still forgive it. This is obvious and is compelled by many places in the Gemara and poskim.
And even the heir, etc. The Sema cites Maharik, Shoresh 89, that if a minor heir forgave the debt, it is a mistaken waiver. Darkei Moshe, seif katan 24, cites the same. The Sema should not have quoted that here. Maharik there speaks of a minor who waived because he thought the debtor had already paid his father, or a similar case. That is indeed a mistaken waiver, and the same would apply even to an adult in such circumstances. The point is only that a minor presumptively knows nothing of his father's business, as above at the end of siman 20. Here, however, we speak of a minor who deliberately forgives the debt document even though it has not been paid. It is obvious that his forgiveness is valid, as is explicit in Tosafos in Perek HaKosev, Kesubos 86a, Tosafos in Perek Mi SheMes, Bava Basra 157b, the Ran in Perek HaKosev, and the Rosh at the end of the first chapter of Bava Metzia. A minor who has reached the age of the pe'utos, as explained below at the beginning of siman 235, can forgive a debt document even where he inherited it. Those authorities also state that he need not pay compensation after reaching adulthood.
And there is one who disagrees and says that even for himself, etc. The Sema, seif katan 60, writes that in Derishah he explained that these dissenting authorities hold that the heir must compensate the purchaser even though he did not intend to cause damage. This is not compelled. It appears more likely that the Mechaber means that the heir is entirely exempt because he did not intend to damage. The Bach likewise concludes this. I also saw in Baal HaTerumos, Shaar 51, part 6, that after citing the Ramban in Sefer HaZechus, who holds that the heir cannot forgive, he later writes: some say that even if one assumes that a person who forgives must pay the full amount written in the document, a daughter is exempt. Liability for forgiveness applies where one intends to harm the purchaser and gains nothing himself, merely destroying the purchaser's acquisition. But this daughter forgives for her own benefit, so that the debt returns and she can inherit from her father. The same applies to any heir who forgives, who pays nothing, as written above. The Raavad wrote this view in his responsa and it has substantial basis. Nevertheless, as a matter of practical law this is not primary, as I explain in the next seif katan.
That even for himself he may forgive. This appears to be the view of most poskim and is primary. The Mechaber himself rules this without qualification in Even HaEzer, siman 105, seif 6. Nevertheless, the heir must compensate under the law of garmi. He cannot say, "I did not intend to cause damage," because wherever one is liable under the law of garmi, liability applies even without specific intent to damage, as is proved in many places in the Gemara and poskim. One practical consequence is that the compensation obligation does not arise from the original debt document. It comes into existence only when the heir forgives the debt, not when the debt document was written. Therefore, he cannot collect compensation from encumbered property by virtue of the original document. Even according to the authorities I cite below in seif 35, seif katan 100, that tort claims generate public notice, the liability still begins only at the time of the forgiveness. He also may later claim, "I already paid you the amount I became liable for under the law of garmi." Another consequence is that if the heir was a minor and forgave the debt for his own benefit, he need not pay compensation even after reaching adulthood. Likewise, a married woman whose father died leaving a debt document can forgive the debt and thereby inherit her father's estate, and she need not pay compensation under the law of garmi until she is widowed or divorced, because whatever a married woman acquires belongs to her husband. All this appears primary in the Gemara and poskim. Shiltei Giborim in Perek HaKosev, at the end of folio 515, writes that one may advise an heir to forgive the debt if the heir will derive some benefit from doing so, provided that the adviser is not one of the judges, because a judge must not teach litigants arguments and legal stratagems. This follows from the Gemara there in the incident involving Rav Nachman's relative.
