Waived. The Sema writes that even according to the opinion that sale of promissory notes is Torah law, this applies only to the lien upon the borrower's property. The personal obligation of the borrower to the lender, which arose at the time of the loan, cannot be sold. Therefore, when the seller waives that personal obligation, the lien on the borrower's property is also extinguished, because the property is liable only as a guarantor. When the principal debtor is released, the guarantor is likewise released, as the Tur and Mechaber write at the end of siman 49. This is unlike the death of the borrower, where the lender may collect from his property, because there the borrower was not released through any act of the lender. Maharik writes that the lender may waive even before the due date. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that this is clearly proven by the Gemara and all the authorities in Ketubot and elsewhere. He further writes that there is no distinction between a document containing a clause of trust and one without it. This is also evident from the Rif, Ran, and other authorities, because the ability to claim waiver does not depend on payment, contrary to what appears from the Raavan. Thus the Shach.
They disagree. The Sema writes in the name of Rabbeinu Yerucham that the Rashba's view is primary, namely that the purchaser need not return the physical document after the debt is waived. The Shach writes that this is an unresolved legal doubt. Therefore, the burden of proof is on the one who seeks to extract, and we do not compel the purchaser to return it. With respect to kiddushin as well, the status is doubtful. Although there is already a separate doubt whether a woman is betrothed through the monetary value represented by a promissory note, because perhaps she does not rely on it since the seller can waive it, there is a practical difference if the purchaser betroths a woman with that note after it has been paid or waived. According to the Rashba and those who agree, she is betrothed if the paper itself is worth a perutah, or even if it is not worth a perutah locally but may be worth one in Media, as the Tur and Mechaber state in Even HaEzer siman 28, seif 8, regarding one who betroths a woman with her own note. The same applies here, contrary to what appears from the Mechaber there in seif 13, who seems to say that in such a case she is certainly not betrothed. Perhaps he too remained in doubt. I have discussed this at length because Heaven forbid that one be lenient in a doubt concerning kiddushin, especially in a matter disputed by the great earlier authorities and left unresolved. Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 329, rules that if one person owes another one hundred by document and the creditor sells the document to another for less, the borrower can say, 'I will buy it for the same amount the other purchaser paid,' and can even block the sale after it has occurred, because sale of documents is rabbinic. He cites proof from siman 316, seif 1. This does not appear correct, especially according to what I wrote above that sale of documents is Torah law. Nor is there proof from siman 316, because there the house belongs to the landlord, and one can also say that he rented it to this tenant and not to another. See what I wrote in siman 175, seif 53. Responsa Mahari ben Lev, siman 92, writes that if a promissory note was given to Leah and she herself owes money to the borrower, waiver is ineffective, because since she is indebted to him the gift is regarded as already collected. His words are difficult, for the Gemara and authorities clearly show that even when the recipient owes the borrower, the donor can still waive the document. Thus the Shach; see there.

The heir. Maharik writes that if a minor heir waives, it is a mistaken waiver. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that the Sema should not have quoted this without qualification. Maharik's case was where the minor thought the borrower had already paid his father. In such a case even an adult's waiver would be mistaken; the point is only that a minor ordinarily does not know his father's affairs. Here, however, where the heir knowingly waives the document even though it has not been paid, it is obvious that a minor's waiver can be valid once he has reached the age at which children may conduct ordinary transactions, as stated at the beginning of siman 235. Tosafot and other authorities expressly state that such a minor can waive a promissory note even if he inherited it, and they further state that he need not pay when he becomes an adult. Thus the Shach.

This opinion holds that the seller must compensate the purchaser even though he did not intend to cause damage, and he therefore has the same law as any other person who waives a sold debt. Thus the Sema. The Shach writes that this appears to be the view of most authorities and is the primary ruling: the seller may waive the debt even to his own benefit. The Mechaber himself rules this plainly in Even HaEzer siman 105, seif 6. Nevertheless, he must pay under the law of garmi even if he did not intend to cause damage, as is proven in the Gemara and authorities in many places. A practical difference is that the purchaser cannot collect this compensation from property encumbered before the waiver. Even according to the opinion that tort liability becomes publicly known, the obligation to compensate arises only from the moment of the waiver, and he cannot collect by virtue of the original promissory note. Another practical difference is that the seller can claim that he already paid this garmi obligation. Further differences apply to a minor or a married woman. See there. The Shiltei Gibborim writes that one may advise an heir to waive the debt if the heir will derive some benefit from the waiver, provided that the person giving the advice is not one of the judges. Thus the Shach.
