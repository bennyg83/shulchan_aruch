If he subsequently waived it to the borrower, it is waived. In the Perishah and Derishah I explained the reason. According to the view that the sale of promissory notes is not a complete acquisition by Torah law but only rabbinic, the reason is obvious: since by Torah law it was not sold, the seller can therefore waive the debt. But even according to the view that the sale of promissory notes is effective by Torah law, this applies only to the lien on the borrower's property. The personal obligation of the borrower, to which he was bound to the seller at the time of the loan, cannot be sold and remains with the seller. When the seller, by waiving the debt, releases the borrower's personal obligation that remained in his hands, the purchaser's lien on the borrower's property is automatically extinguished as well. The borrower's property is obligated to the lender only by the law of a guarantor, for a person's property is his guarantor. When the borrower himself is discharged by the lender, the guarantor is discharged as well, as the Tur and Mechaber write at the end of siman 49. This is unlike the case where the borrower dies, in which the lender collects from his estate, because there the borrower was not discharged by the lender. See the Derishah. The Maharik writes in shoresh 189 that the seller may waive the debt even before its due date, and even if he initially wrote that he disqualifies all witnesses who testify to a waiver, he may nevertheless waive it. The Beit Yosef cites the Rashba to this effect. See also the beginning of this siman and seif 26 below, where Rama writes that in some cases the seller cannot waive the debt.

And even if he stipulated with him, etc. The reason is that one cannot stipulate that another person should be unable to do something that is within his legal power to do. So writes the Beit Yosef. In the Perishah and Derishah I explained that this means as follows: once the debt has been waived, the borrower has already been discharged, and the seller cannot make a stipulation that diminishes the borrower's right by nullifying the waiver. This is not comparable to one who stipulates with a borrower that the Sabbatical year shall not cancel the debt, because there the borrower accepts upon himself a condition that waives his own right. Still, this is difficult, because for this reason Rama should have written that in such a case even according to the opinion that the person who waives the debt need pay only the money he received, as stated at the end of seif 32, it should be agreed that the condition is effective, since by accepting the condition he undertook to pay the full value of the document that would have been collected had he not waived it. It therefore appears that only regarding the Sabbatical year is it obvious that the year would otherwise cancel the debt, so his condition is tantamount to saying expressly, 'I will not allow the Sabbatical cancellation to operate, but will give you the money as a gift.' Here, however, when he says, 'I will not waive,' his words do not imply that if he does waive, he will pay the value of the document.
