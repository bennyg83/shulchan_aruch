And if he later waived it to the borrower, the waiver is effective. Three principal explanations were given for this rule. Rambam says that sale of debt instruments is rabbinic, and because the purchaser's right is only rabbinic the original creditor retains the power to waive. Rabbeinu Tam holds sale of debt instruments is biblical, but distinguishes two liens: the personal obligation of the debtor and the lien upon his property. The personal obligation is intangible and cannot be sold. Only the property lien is transferred. Since the debtor's property is merely guaranty for his personal obligation, when the original creditor waives the personal debt the property lien falls away. Raavad gives a third explanation. He does not distinguish personal and property liens. Rather, after the creditor waives, the debtor can say to the purchaser, "I never subjected myself to you." Therefore if the original document states, "I subject myself to you and to anyone who comes through you," the debtor cannot use that defense and the creditor can no longer waive after sale. Rashi's language in Ketubot, that the debtor tells the purchaser, "You are not my litigant," closely follows Raavad. Baal HaTerumot likewise cites Raavad that unless the assignment contains effective language empowering the purchaser to litigate in his own right, the debtor can refuse him. These approaches have important consequences. Under Rabbeinu Tam, the creditor's ability to waive rests upon the personal obligation remaining with him. Under Raavad, it rests upon the debtor's objection that he never undertook toward the purchaser. Under Rambam, it rests upon the rabbinic character of the sale. This also clarifies whether the purchaser can collect before the creditor waives. According to Raavad, the purchaser can collect because the seller's transfer is effective until the debtor invokes his defense after waiver. According to Rabbeinu Tam, the purchaser collects through the property lien while the personal obligation remains outstanding. According to Rambam, rabbinic law simply gives the purchaser collection rights. A further issue arises under Abaye's view that a creditor acquires encumbered property retroactively from the time of the loan once he ultimately collects it. That does not make sale of the lien immediately biblical. Retroactive acquisition occurs only if the creditor actually collects in the end. If he waives before collection, he never reaches the collection that would make the earlier lien retroactively his. Even without waiver, the purchaser cannot say that the debt was already his from the loan date, because until the creditor himself collects, the purchaser's sale is incomplete. The lien itself is outside the creditor's present possession and therefore not directly saleable. If the creditor ultimately collects, an earlier consecration or disposition may take effect retroactively. But until then the purchaser has no independent Torah right against the debtor. The same analysis applies even to Rabbi Meir, who allows transfer of future property. Such a transfer takes effect only when the subject matter later comes into existence. Until the creditor actually collects, the purchaser's future transfer has not yet matured. Therefore the debtor can still say, "You are not my litigant."
Nevertheless the purchaser. Rashba holds that if the creditor later waives the debt, the purchaser need not return the physical paper, and he proves this from Kiddushin concerning betrothal with another person's debt instrument. The Sages say the paper itself is appraised, implying that even if the debt is later waived the recipient retains the paper. Rashba compares this to a creditor who held collateral and then transferred the collateral to another person: even if the first creditor later waives the debt, the second possessor need not return the collateral. Ran disagrees and holds that once the debt is waived the purchaser must return the paper. The difference may be whether the paper has the status of collateral. If the creditor did not receive it as collateral but merely as evidence, perhaps it is not collateral at all. This difficulty extends even to the borrower's own debt instrument. Rambam rules that even there the paper may not be appraised in the same way, because it did not come into the creditor's hand as collateral. The Shach attempts to distinguish a debt instrument owed by the woman herself from one owed by others, but I see no principled basis for that distinction. If the paper has collateral status, it should work in either case; if it lacks collateral status because it was not received as collateral, neither case should work. It therefore appears that Rashba's ruling does not truly depend upon the ordinary law of collateral. The more accurate principle is that once the physical document reached the purchaser as part of the sale, the original creditor's later waiver cannot undo what has already come into the purchaser's possession. This resembles the law that even a mortgage on land, although the creditor does not "acquire collateral" in the ordinary movable sense, cannot necessarily be waived after the land has been transferred to the purchaser. What has already entered the purchaser's legal control is beyond the original creditor's later waiver. On this basis the purchaser may retain the paper to the value of the paper itself. But if the debtor pays him the paper's actual value, the purchaser must return it. He cannot say, "To me the paper is worth the entire debt." Even authorities who allow a creditor holding collateral to say that the collateral is worth the whole debt speak where the entire debt itself remains intact against that collateral. Here the debt was waived. Only the purchaser's already acquired interest in the physical paper survives. Therefore payment of its actual value requires return. This is also proven by the Kiddushin passage, which says the paper is appraised. If the holder could always demand the entire debt value merely to surrender the paper, it would always be worth at least a perutah. The very need to appraise it shows that only its actual paper value remains. This resolves the apparent contradiction in the Tur. In Even HaEzer he rules that the paper of a debt instrument can be appraised, while here he says the purchaser must return the paper after waiver. The meaning is that he must return it upon receiving the paper's value. Thus the purchaser does not lose the value of the paper, even though he cannot retain it indefinitely. The deeper foundation can be explained through the ownership of the paper itself. The borrower pays the scribe's fee, but a legal instrument must be the creditor's paper at least until repayment because a document of conveyance must belong to the recipient. The borrower therefore transfers the paper to the creditor, effectively on condition that it be returned after repayment. During the debt period the creditor owns the paper itself. This is not ordinary collateral. It is a temporary ownership right in the paper. When the creditor sells the instrument, he transfers whatever rights he has in that paper. The purchaser therefore legitimately owns the paper until the condition of repayment or equivalent satisfaction requires return. This explains why he may use the paper for ordinary purposes in sugyot that allow one who acquired a document to use it as paper. It also explains the Gemara concerning one who takes possession of the documents of a deceased convert. If the paper were merely collateral of the convert's debtors, ordinary rules of collateral upon a convert's death would govern. The sugya instead treats the paper itself as property capable of acquisition. Rashba himself essentially states this. He explains that the body of the paper belongs to the creditor until the borrower repays or the debt is waived, because the instrument must function as the creditor's property during the debt. Once the purchaser receives it, the original creditor cannot destroy the purchaser's already acquired physical right by later waiver. The purchaser's surviving right is therefore not because the paper is classic collateral, but because it was his property through transfer of the creditor's temporary ownership. This also resolves the Shemittah question raised by Taz. A debt instrument is not a movable pledge taken as collateral. Therefore the fact that the creditor owns the paper temporarily does not prevent the debt from being canceled by Shemittah. Accordingly, where a purchaser acquired the instrument through proper writing and delivery, later waiver of the debt does not remove the paper from him until its value is paid. Where the purchaser received only the paper without the written transfer necessary to acquire the lien, the same physical ownership can still pass to the extent the seller actually owned the paper. That explains authorities who allow the purchaser to retain the paper even where the formal lien assignment was defective. But the paper cannot thereby support collection of the debt itself. This framework also supports Maharanach's ruling concerning a person who gives a debt instrument to Leah while Leah herself owes money to the debtor. Where Leah's existing obligation places the equivalent value effectively in her hands, the instrument can be considered already received in satisfaction to that extent, and later waiver is ineffective. The Shach objects, but once the relevant right has actually reached the recipient, the original creditor's power of waiver has ended. The case of mutual debt instruments likewise shows that even where formal setoff does not occur automatically, actual possession can be enough to defeat later waiver. One remaining difficulty concerns the borrower's own debt instrument used for kiddushin. If the paper is not ordinary collateral and was not taken for security, why can the paper itself sometimes constitute value? The answer is that its value derives from the creditor's temporary ownership, not from collateral law. The woman receives the paper itself as property. The debt may still be subject to waiver, but the paper's value is real. The exact question whether a paper worth less than a perutah locally but worth a perutah elsewhere can create kiddushin depends on the separate dispute concerning value in another place. According to the Tur, concern exists because a person from that place may be present and regard it as worth a perutah. According to Rambam's more objective rule, only actual local value matters. The final result is this. The physical paper of a debt instrument belongs to the creditor during the debt under a conditional ownership that ends upon repayment. It is not classic collateral. Sale transfers that temporary ownership to the purchaser. A later waiver can extinguish the personal and property debt rights according to the various theories, but it cannot retroactively erase ownership of the paper already transferred. The borrower can nevertheless compel return by paying the paper's actual value, because after the debt is gone retaining the paper beyond that value would serve no legitimate purpose. This reconciles Rashba, Tur, the Kiddushin sugya, and the broader rules of waiver.

Even the heir. The Sema cites Maharik that if a minor heir waives the debt, the waiver is regarded as mistaken. The Shach objects that a minor who has reached the age at which his commercial acts are rabbinically effective can validly waive a debt, as Tosafot and Rosh state. In my view the Sema is correct where the minor's waiver harms the purchaser. Waiving a sold debt is wrongful conduct, because an adult who does so is a tortfeasor and must compensate the purchaser. The Sages gave effect to a minor's commercial acts only as an enactment for his benefit. They did not validate his conduct where he acts improperly to damage another. Rama in siman 235 similarly says that because a minor's power is only rabbinic, his sale is ineffective where it is used improperly, such as where daughters already acquired rights in a small estate. Here the wrongdoing is stronger, because an adult who waives would be an actual damager. In such a setting we revert to Torah law, under which a minor lacks power to transfer or waive. The episode in Ketubot where a young heir was advised to waive does not prove otherwise. There the waiver benefited the heir herself and did not wrongfully deprive purchasers of value they had paid, and the Sages allowed the advice because she gained substantially. The authorities state that such advice is permitted where the heir gains a real benefit. Thus a minor's waiver can be effective where it is proper and beneficial to him, but where it is merely damaging to another, the rabbinic enactment does not validate it.
He can waive it to himself. The Tur records two opinions whether a person can waive a debt to himself. Yet in Even HaEzer he rules simply that if a woman sold her ketubah, the husband died, and she then died, her son can waive the ketubah even where he is the sole heir and ultimately would have to pay it from his father's estate. Bach already asks why the dissenting view is omitted there. It appears that the Tur and Shulchan Aruch accept Baal HaTerumot's principle that a person cannot literally waive a debt to himself. Therefore, where a creditor sold a debt that he had against his son and the son later inherited the creditor's personal right, the son cannot simply waive to himself. But in the ketubah case the personal obligation remained with the mother. At her death the son inherits that personal right and waives it against his father. He is not waiving to himself. He waives the father's personal obligation that he inherited from his mother. Even though the financial consequence ultimately benefits his own inheritance, the legal act is a waiver directed toward the father. This fits Rabbeinu Tam's theory, under which waiver works through the personal obligation remaining with the seller. Tosafot's language likewise says that the daughter should waive "toward her father." Thus the apparent contradiction is resolved. Literal self waiver does not work, but waiver directed against another person's obligation can work even where the waiving heir ultimately benefits.
