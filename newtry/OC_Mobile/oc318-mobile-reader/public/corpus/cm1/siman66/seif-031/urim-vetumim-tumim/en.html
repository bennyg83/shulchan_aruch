And afterward the lender died, he cannot collect even with an oath. Shulchan Aruch distinguishes between two cases. If the purchaser bought the note while the borrower was alive, no oath obligation yet rested on the original lender. If the borrower later dies, the debt is already owed substantively to the purchaser. The fact that the original lender later dies does not destroy the purchaser's right. But if the purchaser bought the note only after the borrower had already died, the original lender was already subject to the heir-facing oath. That oath obligation follows the debt. If the lender then dies before swearing, the note fails under the rule that a person cannot transmit to his heirs or successors a monetary claim dependent on an oath he can no longer take. Ramban states this, and Rosh and Ran cite him in Perek Kol HaNishba'in. Yet Even HaEzer siman 105 appears to contradict this. There Mechaber follows Rambam: if a woman sold her ketubah while her husband was alive, the husband later died, and afterward she herself died before taking the necessary oath, the purchaser loses. That is exactly a sale before the debtor's death, which should be valid under our present distinction. Rosh himself, after citing Ramban's rule here, writes that Rambam also appears to agree, citing Rambam's ruling that if the woman dies before taking the ketubah oath, the purchaser receives nothing. He treats the two systems as consistent, though at first glance they are opposites. In my chiddushim to Even HaEzer I brought a proof for Rambam from the Mishnah in Ketubot. The Mishnah says the husband's heirs may impose an oath on the widow, her heirs, and "those who come through her," which Rashi explains as purchasers of the ketubah. This must include purchasers who bought during the husband's lifetime. Otherwise, if the sale occurred only after his death, the special Mishnah language is unnecessary. The Gemara later asks how the husband's heirs can impose the oath on the woman's heirs if we hold that a person cannot transmit an oath-bound claim to heirs. It answers that the Mishnah is to be read in separate situations: "her" refers to a widow, "her heirs" to a divorcee. But why not say "her and those who come through her" apply even to a widow who sold before the husband's death, while only "her heirs" require the divorcee case? The placement of "those who come through her" after "her heirs" implies that purchasers too fall under the restriction. This supports Rambam. The resolution, in my opinion, lies in the nature of the oath. With an ordinary sold loan note, if the sale occurred during the borrower's life, there is very little real concern that the original lender had already been paid. First, the borrower remained alive and could protest the sale if the debt was already paid. Second, sale of a debt has publicity. If the borrower heard that his paid debt was being sold and did not object, that silence supports validity. Third, we do not ordinarily presume a person wicked enough to sell a worthless paid note. After the sale, why would the borrower pay the original lender instead of the purchaser? Such payment is unlikely. Therefore any later requirement that the seller swear is largely a stringency. It is not a strong substantive doubt. Chazal did not extend the rule "one cannot transmit an oath-bound claim" to destroy a purchaser's right based merely on that remote concern. A ketubah is different. Rambam in Hilchot Ishut 16 requires the woman to swear not only that she was not paid but also that she did not forgive the ketubah. Forgiveness is a real and substantial concern with a wife who sold her ketubah. The Gemara Bava Kamma 89 says plainly that with respect to her husband, a wife is highly likely to forgive. Rosh cites authorities who say purchasers discount a ketubah precisely because "toward her husband she will certainly forgive." Thus the oath is not a mere formality. It addresses a powerful substantive concern. Even if the ketubah was sold during the husband's life, the possibility of later forgiveness remains strong. When the woman dies without swearing, the purchaser's claim falls within the true category of an oath-bound right that cannot pass. This reconciles Rambam with Ramban's rule for ordinary notes. It also explains why a sudden death of the debtor can sometimes remove the oath in ordinary loans: we assume there was no opportunity to provide security or payment immediately before death. In a ketubah, however, the concern of forgiveness is independent of the husband's dying preparations and therefore remains. Accordingly, the present Shulchan Aruch distinction is sound for ordinary debt notes, while Even HaEzer's stricter rule for a sold ketubah is justified by the unique and substantial concern for forgiveness.
