And some say that if he paid, and so forth. Shach strongly challenges this opinion. Their principal proof is from the found-receipt sugya. The Gemara asks why a receipt may be returned: perhaps it was written in Nisan to be given later, but before delivery the woman sold her ketubah. Returning the receipt would then harm the purchaser. They infer that after sale, the seller no longer has the right simply to accept payment and give a receipt. Shach objects that this proves nothing. If Shmuel's rule allowing forgiveness of a sold debt did not exist, then indeed the seller would have no power to accept payment or issue a receipt after sale. But since we do follow Shmuel, why should actual payment be weaker than forgiveness without payment? When the seller receives payment, his own personal lien against the borrower ends. Once that principal lien is extinguished, the purchaser's derivative property lien should likewise fall, just as with forgiveness. This objection is strong. Still, I found a basis for the dissenting opinion in Responsa Rosh, klal 73, siman 1. A recalcitrant debtor's money had been attached by beit din in the hands of a third person under R. Natan's rule. The third person violated the court's order and paid the recalcitrant debtor directly. Rosh required him to pay again to the creditor, giving as one reason that he had damaged another person's lien and was liable under dinei de-garmi. This implies that Rosh held the original creditor could perhaps have forgiven the debt. Otherwise the third person's direct payment would simply be legally ineffective from the outset, because under R. Natan the debt was already owed to the second creditor and could not be discharged by payment to the first. Rosh nevertheless imposes liability on the payer as one who damaged another's lien. The distinction may be that forgiveness is an act done for the creditor's own benefit or at least within his own legal sphere, and is not therefore classified as actionable damage in the same way. But a debtor who knowingly pays the wrong person receives no corresponding benefit from that particular act and thereby destroys the purchaser's lien. He can be treated as a direct cause of the loss. This may be the basis of the authorities cited here, and the rule is therefore not as strange as it first appears. Another possibility is that they follow Raavan's view, which I cite below, that if the seller has no assets, even after forgiveness the borrower must still pay the purchaser. Their case can then be one where the seller is insolvent. One might have thought: forgiveness is one thing, because the borrower has not already lost money and can still be required to pay the purchaser. But if the borrower actually paid the seller, perhaps we should not make him pay twice. The authorities therefore teach that the borrower had no right to pay the seller. His legal discharge derives only through the seller's power of forgiveness. If, under Raavan's view, that power does not protect the borrower where the seller cannot compensate the purchaser, then actual payment likewise does not protect him. He must pay the purchaser a second time. This can reconcile the opinion. There is still a question on the primary view, accepted by most poskim, that the borrower may pay the original lender. In Kiddushin, one explanation of the dispute whether a woman is betrothed through a debt note held against another person is that R. Meir follows Shmuel: the seller can forgive, but the woman nevertheless relies on the note because the creditor will not abandon her benefit and forgive another person's debt. Why is she confident? The borrower might simply pay the original lender directly. Even if she can later recover from her husband, Tosafot Bava Batra 147b say a woman can be embarrassed to sue her husband in beit din. One answer is that, as a practical matter, why would the borrower do that? The proper initial course is to pay the purchaser, and the borrower gains nothing by deliberately paying the wrong person. The woman can reasonably assume he will not do so. A better answer is that the embarrassment principle concerns suing one's husband for damages under dinei de-garmi, where he is treated as a tortfeasor. A wife may hesitate to bring such a personal damages claim. But if the husband actually received money that belongs to her, her claim is a straightforward debt for money he holds on her behalf. The same embarrassment concern need not apply. This also explains Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer siman 28, seif katan 35, citing Shiltei HaGibborim that a man can betroth a woman by giving her a debt note representing money he himself owes her. At first this seems contrary to Tosafot's statement that a woman is embarrassed to sue her husband. According to the distinction above, there is no contradiction. The embarrassment applies to a damages claim, not to an ordinary debt for her money. And especially if the view under discussion holds dinei de-garmi is a rabbinic penalty that does not pass to heirs, the woman may still rely on eventual recovery from the husband's heirs. If she dies first, he inherits her rights in any event; if he dies first, she can claim from the heirs without the same embarrassment. Thus the primary opinion remains defensible.
