By writing. The Gemara's expression is, 'These are mere words, and words cannot be acquired by words.' Meaning, a kinyan sudar and exchange take effect only upon an item whose substance has monetary value and is in the transferor's possession. A promissory note, by contrast, serves only as evidence that the borrower's property is encumbered to the lender, but the property is not yet the lender's, because we rule that a creditor acquires property only from the time of collection onward. Therefore, even if he physically delivers the note to the transferee and orally says, 'Acquire it and all its liens,' this is merely words by words and is insufficient until he writes, 'I, so-and-so, transfer to you, so-and-so, this note of so-and-so, it and all its liens.' See the Ran in the chapter HaKotev and the Nimukei Yosef in the chapter Mi Shemet regarding whether the sale of promissory notes is d'oraisa. A practical difference arises where one sells his notes to a non-Jew. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach rules that the sale of promissory notes is d'oraisa, cites many poskim who hold so, and argues at length that this is also the Rif's view. See there. He further writes that Rabbeinu Yerucham states that promissory notes are acquired by money like land, and says that this is clear from the Gemara in Bava Kamma. This is unpersuasive, since Tosafot write that the word 'documents' is not part of the correct text, and this appears to be the view of all the poskim. The Ritva writes that the principle 'a declaration to sacred property is like delivery to a private person' applies likewise to consecration for the poor in our time. The phrase 'like delivery' does not mean literal physical delivery, but rather that it is equivalent to whatever act of acquisition is required for that type of property. Thus, for promissory notes it requires writing and delivery, and for land, chazakah. Accordingly, one who says, 'This promissory note is for the poor,' must say, 'and all its liens,' for although the acquisition to sacred purposes does not require the ordinary declaration of transfer, the acquisition itself still requires the appropriate form.
However. The Sema writes that this gloss was not printed in its proper place. It refers to what is written later, 'and even the paper... he did not acquire,' and should therefore appear at the end of this seif. The gloss printed there properly belongs here.
To fulfill. Meaning that he must pay according to the value of the note as an instrument for collection, since he accepted responsibility. Otherwise, what novelty would there be, since he must in any event return the purchase money? See below, seifim 32 and 34. Mahari Weil, responsum 162, writes that since the sale is invalid, with respect to the money the seller is only a paid custodian. Even though he gave the purchaser a note for the money, he is believed to say that he repaid it by virtue of a migo that he could have claimed that the money was lost through an unavoidable accident. Thus writes the Sema. The Shach objects and writes that the Gemara and all the poskim explicitly indicate that the money has the status of a loan. Mahari Weil also was not speaking about the money, but wrote concerning the documents themselves that they are not at Reuven's risk to make him liable for unavoidable accidents, since the sale was no sale, and at most he is liable as a paid custodian. See there.
Who paid. The Sema writes that nevertheless there is an issue of being untrustworthy in commerce, as the Tur writes in siman 204, contrary to the Ir Shushan. See the Shach, who objects to this ruling, brings proofs for his view, and concludes: It therefore appears clear to me that just as in siman 204, where there were words and payment, one who retracts must accept the curse of Mi Shepara, the same applies here, contrary to Rama. See there.
Who sells. In a gift, however, the recipient acquires at least the paper when it was delivered even without a written transfer, except that the document may still have to be shown in beit din as evidence for the right of the other party, just as other forms of evidence are produced under the law of testimony. So writes the Ritva, and his words are correct. Thus writes the Shach. See there for his reasoning.
Who produces it. See the end of siman 50, where Rama writes that a document payable 'to so-and-so or to whoever produces it' must be paid to the person who produces it, provided that he comes by virtue of the named person. Here Rama makes no such distinction. The reason is that here the holder has witnesses or a written instrument proving that it was sold to him, and therefore it is effective even where he comes by his own right, whereas there he has no witnesses that it was sold to him. See also siman 61, seif 4. Thus writes the Sema. The Taz objects that since such a document is acquired by exchange, its law is plainly like other movable property: whoever has it in his possession is believed to say that he acquired it. Here too the case is where he comes by virtue of the first lender, and Rama relied upon what he wrote in siman 50, where the principal rule is stated. The Sema further writes that with respect to forgiveness, the original lender can still forgive the debt to the borrower, because the wording, 'I obligate myself to you and to whoever produces it,' implies that the original lender remains the primary creditor and the debtor was certainly obligated to him. The Shach, however, discusses these laws at length and objects to Rama with proofs and arguments. He concludes: The result is that where a document states that so-and-so obligated himself to a named person and to whoever produces the document, the person producing it may collect without authorization and without a written transfer and delivery from the lender, and the borrower cannot say, 'You are not my litigant,' provided the holder comes by virtue of that named person. But if he does not come by his authority, he cannot collect. Likewise, if the note was sold so that the sale itself should be legally effective, it can be acquired only by writing and delivery like any other promissory note, and the lender can forgive it after the sale just as with other notes. If the document expressly states that it is payable whether the holder comes by the named person's authority or not, or if no lender is named at all and it says only 'to whoever produces this document,' it is acquired by delivery alone, and the lender cannot forgive it after sale. Nevertheless, it is not acquired through exchange, because exchange is not equivalent to delivery.
