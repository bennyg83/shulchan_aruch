Documents, and so forth. Shach argues at length that the sale of debt documents is of Torah force. He follows Tosafot's explanation that there are two liens, the personal lien of the borrower and the property lien, and that the seller transfers one while retaining a residual personal power sufficient to forgive. Shach's main argument against the contrary authorities is that Rosh, Ran, and Maggid Mishneh all understood Rif as holding that sale of documents is only rabbinic, but in Shach's view they misunderstood Rif. That is not persuasive. If their sole proof had been Rif's question why an heir can forgive a sold debt even though a deathbed gift cannot later be forgiven, Shach could answer that even if the sale is of Torah force, the residual personal lien may still permit forgiveness. The deathbed gift can be different for another reason. But Rif's answer itself proves the contrary. Rif says a deathbed gift cannot be forgiven because Chazal treated it like a Torah gift, as though the debt had actually reached the recipient. If ordinary sale of a debt document were already a Torah sale, then every purchaser would already hold a Torah gift equivalent to full receipt. Rif's stated distinction would be meaningless. He should instead have said that a deathbed recipient has the special status of an heir by Torah law and therefore another heir cannot forgive. Rif's wording therefore clearly indicates that ordinary document sale is rabbinic, whereas Chazal strengthened a deathbed transfer as though it were of Torah force. There is another difficulty with Rabbenu Tam's theory. If the purchaser acquires only the property lien on a Torah level, what happens according to the opinion that property liens themselves are not of Torah force? Shmuel holds that liens are not of Torah force. According to that view, what exactly is being transferred by Torah law? One would have to say, as Rashba suggests in one answer, that where the borrower expressly accepted property warranty the lien becomes of Torah force. But then the Torah status of document sale would differ depending on whether warranty was expressly written. That is a very strained distinction, especially since Shach himself holds above in siman 39 that liens are not generally of Torah force. There is also support from Kiddushin. Rabbenu Yerucham, followed by Darkei Moshe and Bach in Even HaEzer siman 28, holds that if a woman is betrothed through ma'amad sheloshtan, the kiddushin is only rabbinic because that acquisition is rabbinic. The Gemara groups together kiddushin with a loan, including both a written loan and an oral loan transferred through ma'amad sheloshtan, under one expression that she is "betrothed." It is difficult to say that the same word means full Torah kiddushin for a written note but merely rabbinic kiddushin for ma'amad sheloshtan. The more natural reading is that transfer of an ordinary written debt is also rabbinic. At first glance, however, there is a strong proof for Torah acquisition from Bekhorot. R. Yehudah HaNasi derives from the redemption verse that slaves and land are excluded by one interpretive method, while debt documents are excluded by another. This appears to concern a father who owns a debt document against a third person and gives it to the kohen for pidyon haben. If the kohen does not acquire the document on a Torah level at all, why is a verse needed to exclude it? There has been no Torah transfer. It is forced to say that the passage concerns R. Yehudah HaNasi according to his view that documents can be acquired by delivery alone, and that his particular mode of acquisition is of Torah force. But that is difficult, because the authorities dispute whether his delivery rule is itself merely a special rabbinic acquisition, and one would not want to build the verse on that uncertainty. The correct answer is that a special class of bearer note is different. If the note says that the borrower obligates himself "to the lender and to whoever produces this note," then the borrower has expressly created a direct obligation to every future bearer. When the note reaches a new person, the borrower's own original undertaking attaches directly to him. That is not merely a rabbinic transfer of the original creditor's claim. It is a Torah-level obligation created by the borrower from the outset. This explains why virtually all authorities hold that the original creditor cannot later forgive such a note. The debt is not merely his transferred chose in action; the borrower directly obligated himself to the bearer. Thus the redemption verse is needed for precisely such a document. Even though the kohen acquires it on a Torah level, Scripture excludes debt documents from pidyon haben. This also opens the possibility that such a bearer document is acquired like ordinary movables, perhaps even by money, because it is not governed by the special written-transfer rules of ordinary debt documents. The Gemara in Bava Kamma 14 contains a reading that slaves and documents are acquired with money, and Rashi says the mechanism for documents is not explained. Perhaps such a bearer instrument is the case. At any rate, this special case does not prove that ordinary debt documents are sold by Torah law. One can similarly explain Tosafot's failure to raise the Bekhorot proof against the rabbinic view. The verse can concern a bearer note or another form where Torah acquisition exists independently. Accordingly, apart from a note expressly written to the lender and every future bearer, ordinary debt-document transfer is rabbinic. Rif, Rambam, Ramban, Baal HaTerumot, Rosh, Ran, and many other authorities all point this way. Their collective position should not be rejected.
