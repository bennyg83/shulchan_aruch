Documents. See Shach, seif katan 1, who proves that the sale of debt instruments is effective by Torah law. Tumim wishes to prove this from Bekhorot, where the Gemara derives by a general term and a specific term that only documents are excluded. If sale of documents were only rabbinic, Tumim argues, the Kohen could not acquire them by Torah law in any event. In my opinion there is no proof, because the exclusion is needed where the father gives the Kohen a debt instrument issued by the father himself and previously acquired from the Kohen. Even there the son is not redeemed because Scripture excludes documents through the general and specific formulation. Tumim objects that with one's own debt instrument everyone agrees that the son is redeemed. I am surprised by this. Ketubot 102 says explicitly that by Torah law the son is redeemed only when the money is ultimately paid, implying that before payment the son is not redeemed because Scripture excluded documents. If redemption could be effected through a debt instrument, the son would have been redeemed at the moment the obligation was given, provided it then had value, even if it later became impaired. Taz in siman 64, seif katan 3, indeed writes that for this reason the son is redeemed where the father obligates himself to the Kohen in a document for the redemption. His intent must likewise be that redemption is complete when payment is actually made, not before. Tosafot in Ketubot, s.v. I Hachi, write explicitly that before the document reaches the Kohen the son is not redeemed, because at that stage there is only a lien obligation. See Even HaEzer siman 29, Chelkat Mechokek seif katan 8, and Beit Shmuel seif katan 5. According to Raavad, one's own debt instrument is no worse than another person's debt instrument. According to Rashba, however, cited there by Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel, a woman is not betrothed with the man's own debt instrument because he remains attached to the obligation. By the same reasoning, a firstborn is not redeemed through it, because this is not a completed act of giving while the giver remains bound up with the instrument. Tumim's question would then return, since according to Rashba one's own instrument would fail even without the Scriptural exclusion, and the exclusion would have to address other documents. One must answer that Rashba either holds that sale of documents is effective by Torah law, or accepts one of Tosafot's other answers to the question from the laws of overreaching in Bava Batra 76. There Tosafot answer that Scripture needed to exclude documents from the laws of overreaching in a case where someone finds documents after the owner has despaired of recovering them, thereby acquiring the physical documents, and the original owners then sell them, in which case the sale has Torah force. Tumim in siman 65, seif katan 13, argues that despair cannot operate with a document, based on the Mishnah in Bava Metzia 13 that a lost debt instrument without a property lien may be returned even though it has no identifying mark. Ketzot HaChoshen raises the same question here. According to what I wrote in siman 65 there is no difficulty. Despair regarding a document without an identifying mark applies only where travelers are common and there is doubt among many possible creditors, so that it certainly will not be returned to the original creditor. In that setting the owner despairs, the finder acquires the physical instrument, and he may sell it to the debtor, as Tosafot write in Ketubot, s.v. HaMokher Shtar Chov. But where the only doubt concerning the document is whether it belongs to the creditor or debtor, the creditor does not despair, because he assumes the debtor will admit the truth, just as one who loses an object among a majority of Jews does not necessarily despair. This is the principal rule, as I explained there. Ketzot HaChoshen further writes that despair transfers the debt itself because despair is stronger than ownerless status: ownerless status does not operate on an item outside one's possession, whereas despair does. This is incorrect. Even if despair can affect something outside the owner's possession, the finder cannot acquire the underlying chose in action that is outside anyone's physical possession. If despair transferred the debt itself, it would be the debtor who acquired release from the obligation, not the finder. Rather, Tosafot's intent resembles what they write in Ketubot 85, s.v. HaMokher Shtar Chov. Although the debt itself is not sold by Torah law through sale of the paper, the transaction still has monetary significance because the paper is valuable to the purchaser, who can sell it for a high price to the debtor. Once the physical paper may be sold to the debtor, it can also be sold to another person for that purpose. When the Gemara says that the paper may be used merely as scrap, that expression is not exact. Its point is that the purchaser may hold the physical paper and sell it to anyone who has reason to acquire that paper. This is also clear from Ran in the chapter HaKotev, in the episode of the bundle of documents. Ran asks why the possessor is not believed to claim that she seized them as collateral through a migo that she could have claimed they were purchased, a claim that would be believed regarding the bodies of the instruments, which are tangible property like other movables and have value as paper. Ran answers that if she claimed that the physical papers were transferred to her merely as scrap she would not be believed, because people do not normally sell their debt instruments merely as scrap. But if she claims that they were sold both for their physical paper and for evidentiary use, and they were delivered without a written assignment, then because the sale fails as to the debt lien it also fails as to the paper itself. Ran's words show that when one acquires only the physical paper, he may hold it and sell it either to the creditor or to the debtor, just as a person claiming a collateral right need not return or even show the collateral to its owner until his claim is satisfied. Accordingly, one who claims that he acquired the paper itself may hold it until the creditor or debtor pays whatever price they are willing to pay. Since a sale and transfer of the physical instrument is effective by Torah law, the same right can be acquired through abandonment or despair. The finder acquires the instrument itself for the purpose of holding it and selling the physical paper to the creditor or debtor. The reason despair works for this purpose is that a debt instrument is considered something that causes monetary gain. In other settings where something causing monetary gain is treated as money, such as an ox condemned to stoning in the hands of a guardian according to Rabbi Shimon, if it is lost and the guardian despairs of recovering it, the finder would acquire it and the guardian would need to purchase it from him, since its value as a cause of monetary gain is treated as value. Certainly the same is true regarding acquisition of the physical paper of a debt instrument. Ketzot's further objection that the paper belongs to the debtor is likewise incorrect, as is clear from Ran and as I explained in siman 65. Tumim also writes that where a document is payable to whoever produces it, its sale is effective by Torah law. His conclusion is correct, and I have established it independently.
Through writing and delivery. See Shach, seif katan 3, who cites Ritva that a verbal dedication of debt instruments to the poor has the same effect as writing and delivery. This is difficult, because Yoreh Deah siman 258, seif 7, rules that one who dedicates a debt instrument requires writing and delivery. Tumim raises the same objection. As for what Ketzot HaChoshen writes concerning Tosafot to Bava Batra 76, there is certainly no proof from the passage in Pesachim. Tosafot's proof is instead that nowhere do we find that one who dedicates a debt instrument must use writing and delivery, whereas that form is specifically stated regarding a sale.
Liability. Tumim asks that if the seller accepts liability, the transaction becomes actual interest, as explained in Yoreh Deah siman 173, seif 4. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The case does not involve the seller guaranteeing payment if the debtor becomes poor, which would indeed constitute forbidden interest. Rather, the seller accepts liability if the debtor refuses to pay on the ground that the purchaser is not his legal claimant because the purchaser lacks a written assignment and delivery. The original creditor obligates himself to pay the purchaser the value of the instrument as it stands at the time of the demand. If at that time the instrument is not worth even a perutah because of the debtor's poverty, the seller pays nothing. Likewise, the seller may accept liability if he himself retracts, obligating his own property to uphold the transaction. The Mechaber uses precisely this language above in siman 60, seif 6, regarding one who sells something not yet in existence: he writes that the seller "accepted liability upon himself to maintain it in the purchaser's possession." Such an arrangement is permissible. It resembles the stipulation, "Whether it rises or falls in value is at your risk, but if it becomes defective the risk is mine," which is permitted in Bava Metzia 64 and Yoreh Deah 173. It further appears that even if he says, "If the debtor is willing to pay you I bear no liability, but if he refuses solely because he says you are not my legal claimant, I will pay you the full amount of the debt instrument," this too is permitted, because it is comparable to the seller retaining liability for a defect in the transaction. As for Tumim's proof from Hagahot HaRosh cited by the Shach in Yoreh Deah 176, seif katan 8, I explained in my work Chavat Daat that where the sale itself remains valid there is no prohibition of interest. See there.
He is not obligated to accept the curse of one who reneges. See Shach, seif katan 8, who leaves the matter unresolved. Tumim explains that we rule that the curse imposed on one who reneges applies only to a transaction in which money effects acquisition by Torah law, unlike Rabbi Yosef Migash, who holds that whenever there is both a verbal agreement and payment there is such a curse. On that basis the Rama's ruling is well understood. The Shach also challenges Baal HaTerumot from the rule that when one sells debt instruments merely for their fragrance or physical use there is overreaching. According to what I wrote above in seif katan 1 in the name of Tosafot, however, the sale of the paper itself is a real transaction because the purchaser may resell the paper to either creditor or debtor, and Tosafot in Ketubot, s.v. HaMokher, indicate that such a sale is not governed by overreaching in the manner the Shach assumes.
He may retract even though he received the money. See Sema, who writes that since the sale is not legally effective, the seller is only a paid guardian of the money and is believed to say that he returned it through a migo that it was lost through unavoidable circumstances. Shach, seif katan 8, writes that this is an error, as is proven from Bava Metzia in the incident involving the orchard in the chapter Eizehu Neshekh and from Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 47. Tumim writes that Tosafot to Bava Metzia, s.v. Mai Irya, imply that according to the opinion that money does not acquire movables by Torah law, the recipient is merely a paid guardian of the money. Baal HaTerumot does not disagree, because there the purchaser had already entered into the substance of the transaction, and for that reason the money in the seller's hands had the status of a loan. Here, by contrast, where the purchaser has not entered into the acquisition at all because there has been no written assignment and he merely holds the physical paper, everyone would agree that the seller is only a paid guardian. I am surprised, because Baal HaTerumot states the same rule where one sells property that he expects to inherit from his father, even though the purchaser has not entered into any effective acquisition at all. Ketzot HaChoshen similarly distinguishes between a case where an effective mode of acquisition exists but remains incomplete, such as movables lacking meshichah or documents lacking writing and delivery, in which case the seller may not use the money until the relevant acquisition is completed and the money is therefore not a loan, and a case where no acquisition can take effect at all, such as one who sells property that is not his, where the transaction is a mistaken sale and the money is a loan as explained in siman 232. This distinction is also difficult, because Baal HaTerumot applies the loan rule even to an asmachta, although an asmachta can be made effective by an appropriate acquisition made immediately in an important beit din. The correct distinction appears to be as follows. With movables, the buyer certainly gives the money on the understanding that he will later perform meshichah and bring the goods into his possession. He therefore certainly does not intend the seller to acquire the money for use before that meshichah occurs. The seller may not use the money and is merely a paid guardian, according to one answer of Tosafot in Bava Metzia 43, s.v. Mai Irya. By contrast, when one sells something not yet in existence, the parties ordinarily do not intend to perform some additional acquisition later. The same is true of an asmachta. Although there exists a mode by which it could be validated, namely an immediate acquisition in an important beit din, if they did not stipulate that the seller would perform such an acquisition and the buyer nevertheless took the object or right and paid the money, one must assume either that they mistakenly thought the present act was effective or that they knew it was not legally effective but relied on the seller's promise not to retract. In either case the buyer must have transferred the money for the seller's use, because otherwise there would have been no reason to deliver it before any further acquisition was contemplated. The money therefore has the status of a loan. Accordingly, the same distinction applies to a debt instrument. If the seller explicitly sold it on condition that he would provide a written assignment, then until the written assignment is delivered the seller may not use the money and is merely a paid guardian. But if he sold the debt instrument without qualification, delivered it, and the buyer paid without any stipulation that a written assignment would follow, the parties either mistakenly thought delivery alone was effective or the buyer relied on the seller's promise not to retract. In such a case the money is a loan, as explained above. If the debt instrument itself has not yet been delivered, however, the case resembles movables before meshichah. The seller remains merely a paid guardian of the money, because there is no obligation of the curse for reneging, as explained in siman 198.
Therefore, that which he sold. See Shach, seif katan 10, who writes that the physical paper must be shown in beit din so that the purchaser may preserve his right. This follows the Shach's view that the purchaser acquires the paper merely as scrap, like one who buys debt instruments for their physical material. According to what I wrote above in the name of Tosafot, however, sale and delivery of the paper also give the purchaser the right to transfer the instrument itself to the debtor. Certainly, then, he need not take it out merely to show it to beit din. This rule must therefore be treated as a matter of unresolved law.
And even the paper merely for its material value. See Shach, seif katan 9, who asks that the Tur's rulings contradict one another. It is possible to reconcile them by saying that in seif 15 the Tur speaks where the seller refuses to repay the purchase money because he claims that he explicitly sold only the physical debt instrument, not for collection but merely so that the purchaser could retain it and sell it to either creditor or debtor. Such a sale is indeed possible, as explained above in seif katan 1. Since the seller is in possession of the money, he is believed because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Accordingly, the purchaser may use the physical paper, since even according to the seller's account the paper belongs to the purchaser. Only after the debtor has paid, when it becomes forbidden to retain a paid debt instrument, may the purchaser no longer hold the document merely for leverage. He must return it, but only after receiving the value of the physical paper.
And to whoever produces it. See Sema, seif katan 8, who asks from siman 50. It appears that the Sema understood the limitation there, "when he does not come by virtue of that named person," to mean that the present holder claims the instrument is his because the named person sold it to him, and that in such a case he cannot collect. He therefore asks from our case. The Sema answers that there the holder lacks witnesses that it was sold to him. According to the Sema, the rule in siman 50 that one may collect only when he comes by virtue of the named person means that if he says the debt is still owned by the named person and he is merely acting on his behalf, he may collect without a power of attorney and without proof that he is an agent. But if he says it was sold to him, he requires proof of the sale. This is difficult. Since delivery is sufficient for the sale of such a document, the only issue is that the holder is not believed merely to say it was sold to him because we suspect loss or deposit. The same concern should arise when he says he is merely the agent. It seems that the Sema's intent is different. Later, in seif katan 33, Sema writes that if the debtor says, "I already paid, the document fell from me, and this person found it," the debtor swears and is exempt. This resembles siman 41, where when a debt instrument emerges from another person's hand we believe the debtor under oath that he paid. Yet at the end of siman 41, Sema writes that if the document emerges from a depositary, the debtor is not believed to say that it fell from him. One must say that a depositary is different because he has a migo that he could have returned the document to the creditor, as Sema explains there in seif katan 30. Here, however, where the holder claims that he purchased the document, he lacks such a migo because he wants to maintain the claim of purchase in order to acquire the debt for himself. Since that purchase claim is not believed and is treated as though he has said nothing, the case resembles a document found in another person's hand where that person does not know its circumstances, which must remain deposited and cannot be used for collection. This, then, is the Sema's meaning here. When the holder comes by virtue of the named person, meaning he claims to be that person's agent, he is believed because the case resembles siman 41, where an agent is comparable to a depositary and the debtor's claim that the note fell from him is ineffective. But where he does not come by virtue of that named person and instead claims ownership by purchase, we suspect the debtor's claim of loss, as in Sema's seif katan 33. Taz, however, correctly questions this. Since a document payable to whoever produces it can be transferred by delivery and even by exchange, it should be like other movables, where a possessor is believed to say he acquired it without producing a deed of proof. Moreover, according to the explanation I gave of Sema's words in seif katan 33, Sema's reasoning here still does not fit.
Or by delivery without a written assignment. See Shach, seif katan 10, who disagrees and holds that writing and delivery are still required vis a vis the original creditor, so that he cannot retract. Tumim properly refutes him and brings a conclusive proof from a responsum of Rosh, who had to advise an heir regarding such an instrument that he should forgive the debt. According to the Shach, the heir could simply retract the sale without any forgiveness. The principal ruling therefore follows the Rama. The Shach also asks from seif 4, where the Rama rules that acquisition by exchange does not work for ordinary debt instruments. There is no difficulty. Wherever the Torah recognizes other ordinary modes of acquisition, such as meshichah and lifting, there is no reason that exchange should be inferior, as Tosafot write. Ordinary debt instruments, however, which name a specific creditor and do not state that they are payable to whoever produces them, require actual delivery from hand to hand. Other modes of acquisition do not suffice, with the exception of acquisition together with land, which is a particularly strong acquisition that the Gemara recognizes even for money. Exchange is a weaker form and therefore does not work for ordinary debt instruments. By contrast, with a document explicitly payable to whoever produces it, ordinary forms of acquisition do work. Terumat HaDeshen, cited by the Shach, writes explicitly that because such a document is payable to whoever produces it and is also written for the holder, it resembles other movables. Certainly meshichah or lifting suffices, and no hand to hand delivery is required. Exchange should therefore also be effective, as Tumim writes.
