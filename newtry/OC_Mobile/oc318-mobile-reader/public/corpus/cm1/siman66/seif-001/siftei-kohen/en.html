Written obligations are not acquired, etc. The Sema refers to the dispute whether sale of debt documents is biblical or rabbinic. In my view the primary law is that it is biblical, as Ritva rules in a responsum, stating that all his teachers agreed against the Rambam. Ir Shushan and the Mechaber likewise imply this. The plain Gemara supports a biblical sale, and one should not invent a rabbinic limitation absent from the Gemara. The creditor’s ability to forgive after sale does not prove otherwise. Rabbeinu Tam explains that the creditor possesses two liens: the debtor’s personal obligation to pay, which is primary and cannot be sold, and the lien on the debtor’s property, which is sold. When the personal obligation is later forgiven, the property lien, which is only secondary like a guarantor, falls away. Nor does the Rif prove that sales are rabbinic. His discussion addresses why a deathbed gift cannot later be forgiven, not why an ordinary sold debt can be forgiven. He understands the Gemara to mean that the Sages treated a deathbed donee like a biblical heir, so the donor’s heir cannot forgive another heir’s share. This reading is explicit in Tosafot, Ran, and Nimukei Yosef and is required according to the majority who hold that debt-document sales are biblical. The Rif’s language likewise shows that he needed to explain only the special strength of a deathbed gift. Although many authorities attribute the rabbinic view to the Rif, his true position appears to be that such sales are biblical, as Rabbeinu Yerucham also records. This is the correct ruling.
Except through writing and delivery. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that documents are acquired with money like land, citing Bava Kamma, but this is incorrect. Tosafot and the other authorities omit documents from that text. Regarding sale of interest contained in a document, see below, seif 32. Ritva explains that the rule ‘one’s declaration to sacred property is like delivery to an ordinary person’ applies also to contemporary charity. ‘Delivery’ means the legally appropriate mode of acquisition for each class: writing and delivery for documents, and possession for land. Therefore one who says, ‘this document and all its liens shall belong to the poor,’ transfers it completely, provided he expressly includes all its liens. A private transfer requires an act of acquisition, whereas charity can be transferred by declaration.

Some say that he is not required to accept the curse of “He Who Punished,” etc. This ruling appears incorrect. Baal HaTerumot says that where the buyer is exempt because he can retain the paper for ordinary use, the seller need not return the purchase money and no curse applies. That logic is unsound. The buyer’s claim that he purchased the paper for use merely prevents the seller from recovering it; if the buyer demands his money because the intended transfer of the debt failed, the seller must return it. The transaction was plainly made to collect the debt, not merely to obtain paper. The rules excluding documents from overreaching concern a valid debt document sold above value, not a transaction void from the outset. Moreover, if the sale were truly of paper for physical use, ordinary overreaching rules would apply. The Rama’s ruling is also inconsistent with the authorities who hold that no part of the paper is acquired when the transfer of the debt fails. Since the Tur and Mechaber rule that acquisition cannot be divided and that even the paper is not acquired, the transaction is mistaken, the paper is returned, and the money must be returned. As in siman 204, one who retracts after words and payment is subject to the curse of ‘He Who Punished.’
Therefore, one who sells, etc. In a gift, however, delivery without writing at least transfers ownership of the paper, although the recipient can use it only as evidence in beit din. Ritva’s distinction is correct. In a sale, the buyer plainly did not intend to buy paper alone, so failure to transfer the debt voids the entire transaction. In a gift, whatever was delivered was intended as a gift. A further proof is that a woman can be betrothed with such a document when the paper itself is worth a perutah; since betrothal can rest upon the perutah alone, all that was delivered is effective. The same principle applies here.

He did not acquire it. The Sema cites Mahari Weil as saying that because the sale is invalid, the seller is only a paid custodian of the purchase money and may claim repayment through a migo of unavoidable loss. This is an error. The Gemara and authorities show that money paid under an invalid sale becomes a loan. Mahari Weil was speaking of the documents themselves, not of the money: since the sale was invalid, the buyer is not liable for unavoidable loss of the writings and is at most a paid custodian.
Even the paper, etc. The Bach criticizes the Beit Yosef and the Mechaber’s omission of Baal HaTerumot’s ruling that the buyer may use the paper. The criticism is misplaced. Baal HaTerumot indeed implies that the paper is acquired, but Rashba and Rabbeinu Yerucham disagree and hold that the buyer is not compelled to return the document. The Beit Yosef considered their view primary, yet elsewhere the Mechaber appears uncertain rather than fully adopting it. He therefore omitted Baal HaTerumot’s ruling here.
Some say, etc. The Rama rules that a document payable to the named creditor and to whoever produces it may be acquired either by barter or by delivery without writing. Neither ruling appears correct. The language allowing any bearer to collect merely dispenses with formal authorization when the bearer comes through the original creditor; it does not transfer ownership of the debt. Until the creditor writes, ‘acquire this document and all its liens,’ the buyer acts only through the creditor, and both parties may retract. This follows from Rashba and Rabbeinu Yerucham and from the Rosh himself, who holds that the original creditor or his heir may still forgive such a debt after sale. If the document had truly been written in the buyer’s own name, forgiveness would be impossible. The Rosh’s statement that no writing and delivery are needed means only that the bearer can collect from the debtor without the debtor objecting that he lacks standing, not that the sale is irrevocably complete. Accordingly, a document payable to the creditor and any bearer is collected by a bearer who comes through the creditor without authorization, but a true sale still requires writing and delivery and remains subject to forgiveness. A different document, payable to any bearer whether or not acting through the original creditor, or naming no creditor at all, is acquired by delivery alone and cannot later be forgiven by the original creditor. Even such a document, however, is not acquired by barter. Terumat HaDeshen inferred otherwise from a responsum attributed to the Geonim, but Rav Hai Gaon himself expressly rejects acquisition of debt documents by barter and requires writing and delivery. Barter is not superior to transfer together with land, and the reason debt documents are not acquired by barter, namely that their substance is not money, applies equally to bearer documents. The clause permitting any bearer to collect concerns enforcement, not the mode of acquisition. Therefore the Rama’s ruling concerning barter is incorrect.
