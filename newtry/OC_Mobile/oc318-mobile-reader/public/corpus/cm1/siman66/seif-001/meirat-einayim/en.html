Promissory notes are not acquired, etc. The Gemara says, 'They are mere words, and words are not acquired through words.' Meaning, a kinyan sudar or chalipin takes effect only on something whose body has monetary value and is presently possessed. A promissory note is merely evidence that a certain amount is owed and that the borrower's fields are encumbered to the lender, but those fields are not yet the lender's, because we rule like Rava that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward. Therefore, even if the seller physically hands the document to the purchaser and orally says, 'Acquire it and all its liens,' it is still words acting upon words until he writes a transfer stating, 'You, So-and-so, acquire from me this document against So-and-so together with it and all its liens.' That writing is considered an act and is itself the acquisition; no additional kinyan is needed. This resembles the rule that land is acquired through a deed, where one writes on paper, 'Such-and-such field is sold or given to you,' as explained in siman 191. For transfer of promissory notes, however, the debt instrument itself must also be delivered together with the transfer writing containing, 'Acquire it and all its liens.' The same is true if he writes those words directly on the promissory note itself. In Perishah I proved that merely writing the phrase, 'Acquire it and all its liens,' is insufficient; he must write a complete formulation such as, 'I, So-and-so son of So-and-so, transfer to you, So-and-so son of So-and-so, this document against So-and-so, together with it and all its liens.' See Ran in chapter HaKotev and Nimukei Yosef in chapter Mi Shemet regarding whether sale of promissory notes is Torah law, with a practical difference where one sells his notes to a non-Jew. Until he returns his money. Likewise, if the purchaser seizes property, it is not removed from him, as will be explained below in seif 17. See what I write there in seif katan 34.
They collect from the borrower, etc. This is under Rabbi Natan's rule, whose law will be explained in siman 86. Mahari Weil, siman 162, writes that since the sale is ineffective, the seller is only a paid custodian over the purchase money, even though he gave the purchaser a document concerning the money, and he is believed to say, 'I paid,' through a migo that he could say the money was lost through an unavoidable accident. So cites Darkei Moshe, seif 3.
The seller must fulfill the responsibility he accepted. Meaning, he must pay according to the collectible value of the document because he accepted liability for it. Otherwise, what novelty would there be? The purchase money would in any event have to be returned. See below seifim 32 and 34.
To receive mi shepara. The law of mi shepara is explained below in siman 204. It appears that the ruling means specifically that he need not accept mi shepara because he did not acquire an object having intrinsic monetary substance. Nevertheless, there remains the prohibition of acting faithlessly, also explained there. The Tur writes in siman 204: a person should stand by his word even if no money was paid, no marking made on the merchandise, and no collateral given. Whoever retracts, purchaser or seller, is considered lacking in trustworthiness and the spirit of the Sages is not pleased with him. Eir Shushan therefore erred in writing here that he may retract because there was only a verbal acquisition and words carry no concern of faithlessness. Rama's phrase that he 'may retract' means only that he need not accept mi shepara.
Even the paper, etc. The reason is that the buyer intended to acquire the debt and its lien. Since that acquisition failed, no part of the transaction takes effect, for an acquisition is not divided.
And to anyone who produces it, it is acquired, etc. At the end of siman 50 Rama writes that a document stating, 'So-and-so owes So-and-so or anyone who produces it,' must be paid to whoever produces it, specifically where that person derives his right from the named lender. Here Rama does not make that distinction, and there as well he wrote the rule without qualification before adding it. Here he cites it in the name of 'some say.' The difference is that here the holder has witnesses or another document proving that it was sold to him, and therefore he can acquire even in his own right. There, by contrast, he has no witnesses that it was sold to him. See below siman 61, seif 4. It is possible that although such a holder is treated as the party in interest and does not need writing and delivery, the first lender can nevertheless waive the debt to the borrower, because the document says, 'I encumber myself to you and anyone who produces it,' indicating that the first lender remains the principal person to whom the debtor is at least initially obligated.
