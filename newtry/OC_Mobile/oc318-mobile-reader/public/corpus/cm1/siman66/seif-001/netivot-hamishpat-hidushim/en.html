Writing and delivery are required. The decisors dispute whether sale of a debt document is from Torah law or rabbinic law. The Shach argues at length that it is from Torah law. See Beurim. With respect to selling the physical document itself, not for collection but so that the purchaser may later sell it to the borrower or lender, the sale is certainly from Torah law and the law of ona'ah does not apply. Likewise, a document written payable to whoever produces it is sold under Torah law. The Sema writes that whether sale of ordinary debt documents is Torah or rabbinic law has a practical consequence when a debt document is sold to a non Jew. This will be explained below, God willing:

The Rama means that he may nevertheless retain the paper, Sema. This applies where the debt has not yet been paid and he retains it against the creditor. They collect from the borrower. The seller still owes money to the purchaser. Accordingly, the purchaser may collect from the underlying borrower through the rule of Rabbi Natan. See above, siman 86:
He must honor the responsibility. This means payment of the full value of the document, because if the seller were liable only to return the purchase price, there would be no need for a special responsibility clause, Sema. The Shach writes that responsibility is meaningful because without it, if the debt later became uncollectible, for example the borrower became poor, yet the purchaser still wanted the seller to provide the written transfer rather than cancel the transaction for some reason, the seller could provide the writing and thereby free himself. If he accepted responsibility, however, even providing the writing would not exempt him from paying for the failed debt. See Beurim. An undertaking to pay if the borrower becomes poor is full interest and is unenforceable because of the prohibition of ribbis. The responsibility discussed here instead means that the seller encumbered his own assets to pay if he himself retracts, or if the underlying borrower refuses to recognize the purchaser and says, "You are not my litigant," so that the seller must compensate the purchaser. If the borrower later becomes poor, the seller is exempt. Such an arrangement is permitted. Even if the purchaser did not yet receive the written transfer and the purchase of the document was therefore incomplete, so that the purchase money is in the seller's hands like a loan and normally the seller could not later give more than he received, if the seller genuinely intends to fulfill the sale and now completes it, there is no prohibition:
To be subject to mi shepara. The sanction of mi shepara applies specifically where money was paid for movable property but no meshichah occurred, because by Torah law money itself effects acquisition and there is at least a Torah level kinyan. Here, where there was no valid kinyan at all, mi shepara does not apply, unlike the Shach. Nevertheless, retracting may violate the obligation to keep one's word, Sema and Beurim:

He did not acquire. The purchase money remains in the seller's hands as property of a paid custodian, and the seller is believed with an oath to say that it was lost through unavoidable accident. Therefore, even if he received the payment by a document, he is believed to say that he repaid through a migo of unavoidable loss, Sema, unlike the Shach, who treats him as fully responsible. See Beurim. If at the time of sale the seller expressly said that he would provide a written transfer, then until that writing is given he may not use the purchaser's money and has only the liability of a paid custodian. If he merely sold and delivered the debt document without expressly promising a written transfer, and the purchaser delivered the money, the seller may use the money and it has the status of a loan in his hands. But if the purchaser gave the money and the seller did not deliver the document itself, the seller may not use the money and remains only a paid custodian over it:
And even the paper for wrapping. Since the purchaser intended to acquire the document because of its lien, and the lien does not transfer without writing, he does not acquire the paper either. There is no partial acquisition. If the document was given as a gift without writing, however, the recipient acquires the paper itself, though he must produce it before beit din for the benefit of the original creditor. Shach and Beurim. According to Tosafos, he need not produce it in beit din until the original creditor purchases the paper from him for money. The matter is one of unresolved law:
And to whoever produces it. The Shach disagrees and holds that where the lender's name is written, such as "to you and to whoever produces it," writing and delivery are still required and the first lender retains the power to waive the debt. But where no lender's name is written at all and it simply says "to whoever produces it," writing and delivery are unnecessary and the first lender cannot waive it. Such an instrument is not acquired through chalipin, however. See Beurim. The primary ruling is that even where the lender's name is written, "to you and whoever produces it," delivery without additional writing is enough, though the first lender can still waive the debt. Such a document is not acquired through chalipin. All the more so, a document payable only to whoever produces it is acquired through chalipin.
