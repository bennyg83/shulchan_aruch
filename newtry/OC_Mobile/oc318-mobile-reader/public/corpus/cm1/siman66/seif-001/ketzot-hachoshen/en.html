Debt instruments. It is well known that the early authorities dispute whether sale of debt instruments is effective by Torah law or only rabbinically. Tosafot and Semag explain the Torah exclusion of documents from overreaching by a case where someone finds a debt instrument after the creditor has despaired of recovering it and then sells it back to the original creditor. This requires explanation. What did the finder acquire? If he acquired the debt itself, why does the debtor not acquire it first? If despair does not apply to a debt at all, as Chacham Tzvi argues, how could the finder acquire the debt? If he acquired only the paper, why does Semag emphasize that the sale back to the original creditor is a Torah sale because the document is written in that creditor's name? Moreover, according to many authorities the paper belongs to the borrower, not the creditor. It therefore appears that despair can transfer the debt itself to the finder. The debtor cannot acquire through the creditor's despair because the debt came to him originally under an obligation of repayment and therefore entered his control improperly for purposes of acquisition. The finder, by contrast, receives the item after despair in a permitted manner. Despair is stronger than ordinary sale in this respect. The reason sale of a debt instrument may be only rabbinic is that the lien is not fully in the creditor's present possession. Despair can operate even upon property outside one's possession, as it does with stolen and lost property, because despair is not the same as ownerless designation. Therefore the finder can acquire the debt without writing and delivery. This also explains why Tosafot exclude ownerless debt instruments and property of a convert from ordinary acquisition through meshichah. Ordinary ownerless transfer does not reach such a lien without the special mode of transfer, but despair has an independent Torah mechanism. A difficulty remains from the Mishnah of a found debt instrument: if the lender supplies a valid identifying mark he may collect even from encumbered property, while if he supplies no mark, why should even a document without property responsibility be returned rather than acquired by the finder? This requires further study. There is also a proof from redemption of a firstborn son. Scripture excludes debt instruments from the property used for redemption. If sale of documents were purely rabbinic, how could the kohen ever acquire another person's debt instrument on a Torah level so that Scripture would need to exclude it? The answer may be that a debt instrument can become collateral by Torah law. As I wrote in siman 64, lien is stronger than sale and can attach to a debt instrument without the formal writing needed for transfer. Since redemption can be performed with collateral in some contexts, Scripture must exclude debt instruments even where the kohen could otherwise hold them as collateral.
By writing and delivery. The Shach cites Ritva, who applies the principle that a declaration to consecrated property is like delivery to an ordinary person and says that in the case of debt instruments the declaration is treated like writing and delivery. This is surprising because Rama rules in Yoreh Deah 258 that one who consecrates a debt instrument must use ordinary writing and delivery, citing Rashba. The Shach himself appears to accept that rule. Ritva therefore represents a differing approach. The underlying dispute is whether the rule that speech to consecrated property equals delivery applies to charity and similar consecrations in our era. Yet even the requirement of writing and delivery deserves analysis. Tosafot in Bava Batra says that because the Sages created sale of debt instruments as a special enactment, they did not necessarily create an equivalent mechanism for consecration, which was less commercially necessary. But that sugya concerns one who seeks to consecrate the debtor's underlying property or lien directly. It does not clearly prove that one cannot consecrate the debt instrument itself by the same writing and delivery used in sale. Indeed, the passage says that if a creditor sells or consecrates the debtor's property directly, ordinary rules of possession apply. It may therefore be that sale and consecration of the instrument itself are parallel and both can work through writing and delivery. For charity, the case is stronger. The poor are no worse than ordinary recipients regarding enactments created by the Sages. We find that transfer in the presence of all three parties can operate for charity obligations. Thus ordinary writing and delivery should certainly suffice for debt instruments dedicated to the poor. Tumim argues from Rashba that if speech to charity were equivalent to delivery, then a verbal dedication of a debt should function like the three party transfer, because such a transfer is one available mode of acquisition. This is not correct. The three party transfer does not work solely by the donor's own act. It depends upon the debtor's participation, who is treated as acquiring for the recipient. Speech to consecrated property can substitute only for an acquisition that the donor himself can perform. It cannot substitute for the debtor's independent participation in a three party transfer. Therefore a verbal declaration alone does not equal the special three party mechanism.
The "Who exacted payment" curse. The Shach disagrees with Rama and holds that if money was given but the debt instrument was not transferred with the required writing and delivery, the seller must nevertheless accept the "Who exacted payment" curse if he retracts. Rama's ruling appears more correct. In Bava Metzia the Gemara explains that the curse applies to money because by Torah law money acquires movables, even though rabbinic law requires meshichah. The conclusion is that mere words plus money receive this sanction because the Torah itself recognized the money as an acquisition. Where money has no Torah power to acquire the subject matter at all, the basis for the curse is absent. A debt instrument is not acquired through money alone even by Torah law. It requires the special written transfer and delivery. Therefore, if the buyer gave money and the seller has not completed writing and delivery, there is no "Who exacted payment" sanction. Nimukei Yosef expressly says that a loan, which does not acquire the object, does not create the curse, because the Torah rule that money acquires does not apply. The same reasoning applies to debt instruments. If the seller delivered the instrument but did not write the assignment, there is still no Torah acquisition and no curse. If, however, the buyer gave money and the seller executed the written assignment but has not yet delivered the paper, there is greater room to say that Torah law regards money together with the written assignment as sufficient in place of delivery, depending upon whether money can substitute for that physical transfer. The authorities dispute whether other modes such as exchange can substitute for delivery. Therefore this form remains doubtful. In any event, where writing itself is missing, there is no curse. This also has consequences for interest. If a discounted transfer of the debt would be considered a sale only after proper writing and delivery, then before those acts are complete the arrangement cannot be protected merely by the possibility of a "Who exacted payment" sanction. This is why the authorities require both writing and delivery in order to avoid the law of interest.
To retract. The Sema writes that because the sale is not yet complete the seller is only a paid custodian of the money and can say, "I repaid," through a migo that the money was lost through unavoidable accident. This follows Tosafot in Bava Metzia. According to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds money acquires by Torah law, the Sages did not entirely deprive the seller of the right to use the money. He is therefore like a borrower. According to Reish Lakish, where money has no Torah acquisition force, the seller may not use it and is only a paid custodian. The same applies here. As explained above, money does not acquire a debt instrument by Torah law without the required writing and delivery. Therefore the seller may not use the buyer's money and is only a paid custodian, not a borrower. The Shach says this is an error and cites cases of a mistaken sale where the money becomes a loan. Those cases differ. Where the buyer performed the normal act of acquisition and the sale later fails because the item did not belong to the seller or because of another substantive defect, the buyer had already given the money to the seller as his money. The seller then holds it as a loan once the sale is undone. Here, however, the subject matter is fully saleable and there is no mistaken sale. The buyer has simply not yet performed the mode of acquisition required for that subject. Until the debt instrument is acquired, the buyer does not intend to transfer ownership of the money to the seller. The two acquisitions stand dependent on one another. Therefore the seller remains only a custodian of the money until the proper acquisition of the instrument is completed.
