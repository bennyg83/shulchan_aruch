Only through writing and delivery. See Responsa Noda BiYehuda, second edition, siman 45, who writes that with respect to the sale of debt instruments there is certainly no distinction between a note against a Jew and a note against a non-Jew. See the gloss in seif 32, and this is also evident from the gloss in seif 4, which makes the rule depend on the fact that the document contains no obligation but only grants authority. It follows that a document embodying an obligation is acquired by writing and delivery, and this is clear. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 230, concerning Reuven, who held a debt instrument against Shimon that contained a defect such that Reuven could not collect unless he first seized property, and who sold the instrument to Levi, who already held property belonging to Shimon. The question was whether Levi’s possession of Shimon’s property should count as seizure. Chavot Yair ruled that it certainly does not. As a general matter, otherwise no one could live securely, for whenever one held a handwritten claim or another claim that could not be judicially collected, he could sell it to someone who happened to possess property of the debtor. More specifically, Levi has no monetary claim at all until it is first established that Shimon is liable and that the note is valid and collectible even without seizure. See there. See also Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 157, concerning a widow who held a note against Reuven but could not collect because the language of the note was doubtful and the holder of a document has the weaker hand, although if she seized property it would not be removed from her, as stated in siman 42, seif 8. She later married, and her husband held property of Reuven. The question was whether that property could be retained on account of his wife’s note. Shevut Yaakov cites the ruling of Chavot Yair that a purchaser of a defective debt instrument cannot rely on property of the debtor already in his possession, but writes concerning it: ‘I see the man, but I do not see the proofs.’ In his view, neither the general nor the particular reasoning is sufficient to remove property from one who presently possesses it. Whatever right Reuven had in the note if he seized property was sold entirely to Levi, and there is no difference between Reuven himself and one acting through his right. Indeed, the logic points the other way, and the concern that otherwise no one could live securely is inapplicable, for such circumstances do not arise every day. All the more so in the case before him, involving a husband, those arguments do not apply, for he is stronger than an ordinary purchaser of the note. His seizure therefore certainly helps. See there carefully, and see below, siman 75, seif 23. On the Mechaber’s words, ‘through writing and delivery,’ Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes that one may sell half the debt instrument and write that he transfers all the liens he has with respect to such and such an amount, citing Maharikash.
