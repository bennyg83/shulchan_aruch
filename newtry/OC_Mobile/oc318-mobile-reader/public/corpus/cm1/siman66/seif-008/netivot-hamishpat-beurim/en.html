There is a scribal error, and the text should read: since we rule that the signature is the essential element and therefore the seller cannot forgive the debt, it follows that we rule accordingly. At first sight this is difficult. Kiddushin teaches that one who betroths a woman with a loan secured by collateral can effect kiddushin by virtue of Rabbi Yitzchak's rule. This implies that if he betroths her with land pledged as collateral, which according to the Shach in siman 278, seif katan 8, is not acquired under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule, she would not be betrothed. Yet if the underlying debt were transferred to her and the creditor could no longer forgive it, why should she not be betrothed? Even where one betroths a woman with a debt instrument, according to the opinion that rejects Shmuel and holds that the seller cannot forgive the debt, the kiddushin is valid, as is clear in the first chapter of Kiddushin. Tumim also asks that Even HaEzer siman 28 cites a dispute whether a pledge taken at the time of the loan can effect kiddushin, while here the ruling is stated without qualification. There too a distinction is made between collateral from a Jew and collateral from a non Jew, while no such distinction appears here. It seems that the proper analysis begins with Even HaEzer siman 28, seif 8, where the Tur rules that if a man says, "Be betrothed to me with this debt instrument," and the paper itself is worth a perutah, she is betrothed. Beit Shmuel explains that the paper is no worse than collateral. This is indeed the Tur's language, but it is difficult because the Tur himself rules that collateral taken at the time of the loan does not effect kiddushin. Tumim answers that the paper of a debt instrument is stronger than ordinary collateral because its entire function is to remain with the creditor until repayment, and the debtor therefore conveys this proprietary right to the creditor, who can transfer it to others. Tumim leaves this in difficulty. Yet there is no real basis to distinguish this way, because every pledge is held precisely so that it need not be returned until the debt is paid. In my opinion, the Tur's statement that the paper is no worse than collateral shows that the two share the same legal structure. Regarding the paper of a debt instrument, the creditor has a genuine proprietary right. He can sell the physical paper for its material value, as Tosafot, Ran, and many other authorities state in numerous places. As I explained above, the expression "for scrap" does not mean that he may tear or destroy the document, because the physical document ultimately belongs to the debtor in the sense that once he pays, the creditor must return it intact. Rather, the creditor may sell his present proprietary right to retain the paper until payment. He can require either creditor or debtor to pay whatever amount they are willing to pay in order to obtain the document. That proprietary right is itself transferable by Torah law. But the creditor cannot by Torah law sell the debt itself through mere delivery of the paper so that the purchaser can collect from the debtor. Nor, when the creditor himself wishes to collect from the paper or collateral, does he automatically own the pledged asset in satisfaction of the debt. He must first demand payment from the debtor, and the pledged property functions only as security for the debtor's personal obligation. The same is true of collateral. The creditor has a genuine proprietary right to hold the pledge until payment, as Tumim writes regarding the paper of a debt instrument. He may transfer that possessory right to another. Indeed, as I explained in siman 72, collateral taken at the time of the loan, although it is not acquired under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule for all purposes, nevertheless gives the creditor a real proprietary interest with respect to the principle that collection is retroactive. If the creditor consecrates or sells his interest, the consecration or sale has effect to that extent. This also explains Ran, cited by the Shach in siman 72, seif katan 71, that if the creditor remains silent and does not wish to collect from the collateral, the debtor has only a hesset oath against him. At first sight, one could say that the creditor is holding the debtor's garment and should be required to release it if he cannot prove his claim. But since the pledge is effectively transferred to the creditor for the limited purpose of retention until payment, the creditor's claim resembles a claim of purchase with respect to that limited interest, and only a hesset oath is required. Accordingly, when the creditor sells a debt secured by collateral, even if he later forgives the debt, the purchaser can still hold the collateral until he is paid, because that limited proprietary interest was sold to him outright. This applies even according to the Rama, who holds that where one buys an ordinary debt instrument and the seller later forgives the debt, the purchaser must return the paper. There the purchaser bought the instrument for the purpose of collecting the debt. Since the collection right is destroyed by forgiveness and the transaction is not divisible, the proprietary right in the paper also fails. But if the physical paper had expressly been sold only for its possessory value, the purchaser would not need to return it merely because the debt was forgiven. With collateral, by contrast, one can separately sell the limited right to retain the pledge until payment. Such a sale is complete. Yet the purchaser does not thereby acquire the debtor's personal obligation and cannot independently sue the debtor or collect from the debtor's other property. The debtor may say to him, "You are not my legal claimant." Only collateral taken after the loan through beit din, which under Rabbi Yitzchak's rule is acquired for collection, carries the debt itself to the transferee by transfer of the pledge. This resolves the dispute in Even HaEzer siman 28. The opinion that kiddushin is invalid with collateral taken at the time of the loan speaks where the man attempts to betroth her with the underlying debt secured by the collateral. She does not acquire the personal debt, because the creditor cannot transfer the debtor's personal obligation merely through the pledge. By contrast, if the pledge was taken after the loan through beit din, the pledge itself functions as collection and can carry the debt. The opinion that kiddushin is valid can be understood as speaking where he betroths her with the benefit and proprietary interest he has in the collateral itself, namely the right to hold it until payment. That interest has value and can effect kiddushin according to everyone. This approach also resolves Tosafot's question from shemittah. Even collateral taken at the time of the loan prevents cancellation, although Rabbi Yitzchak spoke only of collateral taken later, because the creditor nevertheless has a real possessory interest in the pledge, and the debtor's property is in the creditor's hand to that extent. It also explains Pesachim 31 regarding chametz pledged to a non Jew. Since the non Jew has a genuine proprietary interest in the chametz until payment, it is not considered fully in the Jew's possession for the prohibition of owning chametz. By contrast, in Bava Metzia regarding responsibility for loss, the debt is not reduced when collateral taken at the time of the loan is destroyed through unavoidable circumstances, because the creditor has not acquired it for actual collection, only for possession. This is analogous to a debt instrument. The creditor owns a present right to hold the paper, but if the paper is destroyed through unavoidable circumstances the value of the debt is not automatically reduced. Only collateral taken after the loan through beit din, which is acquired for collection, causes the debt to be lost when the collateral is lost. Accordingly, the ruling here that a purchaser acquires pledged land and the seller cannot forgive the debt must be understood as a sale of the right to hold the collateral until payment. As to that right, the purchaser has complete ownership, and forgiveness does not destroy it. But if the creditor sells the underlying debt, intending the purchaser to be able to sue the debtor and collect from his property or from the pledge, that personal lien cannot be transferred merely through the proprietary interest in collateral taken at the time of the loan. The original creditor can therefore still forgive the debtor's personal obligation. If he sells the collateral and expressly includes the underlying debt, and later forgives the debtor's personal obligation, the result depends on the dispute in seif 23 whether a purchaser of a debt instrument must return the paper after forgiveness. According to the opinion that the document must be returned because the transaction cannot be divided, the pledge must likewise be returned. According to the opinion that the purchaser keeps the paper because the sale remains effective as to the physical document, he likewise keeps the pledge. A pledge and the paper of a debt instrument share the same basic structure, as the Tur writes in Even HaEzer siman 28. This appears clear.
