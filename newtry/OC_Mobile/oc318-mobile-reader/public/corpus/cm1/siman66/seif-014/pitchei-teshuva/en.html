And some disagree. See Baer Heitev regarding the ruling of Responsa Mabit and the objection to it. See Tumim below, seif 26, end of seif katan 58, who writes that even according to the Mabit, if the debtor’s circumstances materially improved, for example the note was purchased while the debtor was poor and afterward he became wealthy, the debtor certainly cannot then say, ‘I will reimburse what you paid for the note.’ The purchaser risked his own money when the debtor was poor and might have been unable to collect; now that his fortune improved and the debtor became wealthy, the debtor cannot appropriate the purchaser’s gain and render his investment futile. The Mabit’s ruling applies only where the debtor’s circumstances remained unchanged from the time of sale until the present. In that situation the Mabit gives the debtor priority, while the Shach disagrees even there, and the correct ruling follows the Shach. Responsa Naot Deshe, siman 20, likewise brings several proofs for this. He further discusses the Shach in siman 175, seif 53, seif katan 55, where the Shach also cites the Mabit, rejects his proof, and writes that as a matter of logic the seller can say, ‘I wish to leave it with this person and not with you.’ One might initially distinguish that case as involving a sale below market value, which resembles a gift, so the seller can express such a preference, as with the law of a neighboring landowner where a gift is different, whereas in a sale at full value he could not. Naot Deshe shows, however, that this distinction is incorrect and that the Mabit’s ruling does not stand even in a sale at full value. He then addresses a case where Reuven held a note against Shimon and sold it to Levi for less than its value because the due date had not yet arrived and Shimon was not then wealthy. Reuven delivered the note to Levi but did not write the formula, ‘Acquire it and all its liens.’ Later, when the due date arrived and the debtor had become prosperous, Shimon claimed that he wished to purchase his own note for the amount Levi had paid and that he had priority, while Reuven did not seek to rescind the sale. Naot Deshe concludes that whether or not the note’s value or circumstances changed, once the debtor objects only after written assignment and delivery, his objection is ineffective, even if the seller agrees with him and even if the seller says that at the time of sale he had no particular preference for the purchaser over the debtor. Since at the moment of written assignment and delivery the seller could have maintained the sale by saying, ‘I prefer this purchaser,’ the sale was already complete and he cannot later retract it. But if the debtor seeks to prevent the sale before written assignment and delivery, then whether or not circumstances changed, if the seller says, ‘I prefer this purchaser,’ the sale remains valid. If the seller is silent and does not care in whose hands the note remains, the debtor can prevent the sale. The Shach’s statement in siman 175 that the seller may say, ‘I prefer this person,’ applies only where the seller actually makes that claim; otherwise the Mabit’s reasoning there remains. Even if afterward the seller gives the purchaser written assignment and delivery only because the purchaser pressures him by saying that he is already obligated to complete the transfer and otherwise will incur mi shepara, such later assignment does not help if it was not given because the seller truly preferred that purchaser. At the time of assignment it was already known that the seller was indifferent. Since an objection raised before assignment prevents a later assignment from helping, the seller also need not accept mi shepara on account of that ineffective assignment. Nor does his failure to say, ‘I prefer this purchaser,’ itself create any obligation toward the purchaser. Thus, in the case under discussion, where the debtor objected before written assignment and delivery, Reuven did not retract the sale but also did not say that he preferred Levi, the debtor can cancel the sale and there is not even mi shepara. Nevertheless, Naot Deshe adds that in this particular case, since the due date has already arrived and the note is now worth far more than the price for which it was sold, the seller’s present failure to retract is surely only because he fears mi shepara. Once the debtor objects and thereby has power to cancel the sale, the seller may retain the note for himself without mi shepara and may say that he will not sell at all. This is exactly analogous to the Shach’s ruling in siman 175, seif katan 5. However, if after the debtor’s objection the seller then gives the purchaser written assignment and delivery, the seller can no longer retain the note for himself. Although the debtor can still cancel the sale even in such a case, the seller himself has lost that power. The Shach in siman 175 carefully wrote that because no acquisition had yet taken place, the seller could retract. That reasoning serves only to remove the oath or personal commitment, not to undo an acquisition. Once the seller’s rights have been removed by an act of acquisition, he is like a stranger to the property. There is no basis to distinguish whether the acquisition was given before the debtor’s objection or afterward. Indeed, when the acquisition was given after the objection, there is even more reason to say that the seller can no longer retain the property. In the present matter our entire discussion concerns written assignment and delivery given after the objection, because if it had occurred before the objection the debtor could not undo anything. Such later assignment certainly suffices at least to prevent the seller from retaining the note for himself. See there at length.
