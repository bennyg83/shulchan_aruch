See the gloss, which writes that although the debt instrument has already created a monetary obligation and the written document is only evidence, it is nevertheless acquired through writing and delivery because it was written to enable collection from encumbered property. Ketzot HaChoshen asks that if property liens exist by Torah law even for an oral loan, then by Torah law the document is only evidence, which is difficult according to the view that sale of debt instruments is effective by Torah law. There is no difficulty. One can certainly find a case of sale of documents effective by Torah law, for example a written instrument that itself creates the obligation. When the authorities say more generally that sale of debt instruments has Torah force, they speak in our present legal reality after the Sages enacted that an oral loan does not collect from encumbered property. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 4, s.v. Ein Nishba'in, write that where the Sages removed the property lien of an oral loan, the result is as though the creditor waived that lien. Consequently, a written debt instrument today functions as an instrument of acquisition with respect to encumbered property, and its sale may therefore be effective by Torah law. The Mechaber also holds that even where publicity is not necessary, a document that itself conveys the additional right is required. For this reason, in siman 45 he invalidates a document whose witnesses left two blank lines and the space was later filled in by erasure, as Tumim explains in siman 49, seif katan 8. Even according to Tosafot and Rosh, who may not require a formal conveyancing document where publicity alone suffices, as appears from Tosafot in Gittin, once an additional lien exists because of the instrument it makes sense to say that the additional lien can attach to the underlying obligation. Where the instrument adds no lien at all, there is no such independent proprietary element to transfer. Ketzot also asks from the secular court document discussed in seif 7. How can writing and delivery work if secular courts lack power to create a Jewish legal acquisition? There is no difficulty. According to the Rama's gloss in siman 68, which allows collection from encumbered property through such a document, writing and delivery surely work like other debt instruments. Even according to the Mechaber there, who allows collection only from unencumbered property, I do not understand why this should be more difficult than a debtor's handwritten acknowledgment, which also collects only from unencumbered property and yet is transferred by writing and delivery. One must say that the Sages gave this mode effect to prevent the closing of the door before borrowers, as Sema writes in seif katan 38. Ketzot further cites Tosafot in Bekhorot 49, who write regarding redemption of a firstborn that property acquired after the original obligation is not considered after acquired property in the usual sense, because at every moment a new obligation rests upon the father. Ketzot infers that a new obligation can attach on top of an earlier one. This is not even the beginning of a proof. There, since the Torah commands him anew every day, it is as though the Torah writes a new obligation upon him each day. Each day creates a fresh obligation against the newly acquired field because of that day's command. If, however, property liens are already imposed by Torah law, even against after acquired property, what additional strength could a written debt instrument contribute? It would add nothing. The principal explanation is therefore as stated above. Once the Sages removed the lien of an oral debt and the written instrument itself creates the enforceable lien, the instrument functions as a conveyancing document and may be transferred by writing and delivery even by Torah law.
