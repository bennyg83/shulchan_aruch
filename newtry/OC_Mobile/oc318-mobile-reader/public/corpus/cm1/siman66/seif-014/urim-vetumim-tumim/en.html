But a debt secured by collateral, and so forth. Tur and Mechaber later rule that such a debt cannot be forgiven once transferred. I am surprised by Tur's consistency. Baal HaTerumot derives this law primarily from the rule in Kiddushin that if one betroths a woman with a debt secured by collateral, the kiddushin are valid because a creditor acquires the collateral. Yet in Even HaEzer siman 28 Tur ultimately cites his father Rosh, following Tosafot, that this is true specifically of collateral taken after the loan. If the collateral was given at the time of the loan, kiddushin through the debt are not effective. How, then, can Tur here state broadly that a debt secured by collateral is transferred in a way that prevents forgiveness? Perhaps Tur did not derive the present law from kiddushin, despite Baal HaTerumot's proof. One could instead learn it from the Gemara's discussion of buying a debt note and retaining the physical paper. The paper itself belongs ultimately to the borrower; the creditor has only a possessory lien allowing him to withhold it until payment. That resembles a debt secured by collateral. If the collateral right can transfer together with the debt, perhaps the buyer acquires without the ordinary writing requirement. But the difficulty remains regarding forgiveness. The Gemara says a woman is not betrothed with an ordinary debt note either because the purchaser did not fully acquire it or because the original creditor can forgive and she therefore lacks confidence. If a debt secured by collateral is acquired and cannot be forgiven, it should produce full kiddushin according to everyone, just like R. Meir's view of an ordinary note. Why then does Tur follow Rosh that collateral given at the time of the loan does not create kiddushin? This requires study. It is strained to say Tur silently relies here on his Even HaEzer qualification. Rama's position is less difficult. In Even HaEzer he cites Rosh's distinction only as a stringency in matters of marriage, where doubt affects the severe prohibition of a married woman. He may hold that the primary monetary halachah follows the broader view. Rama there also cites Mordechai that if the collateral belongs to a gentile, kiddushin are ineffective because a Jew does not acquire a gentile's collateral in the same way. That too suggests a monetary consequence: where we rule in the laws of Pesach that a Jew does not acquire a gentile's collateral, the creditor's interest should likewise be weaker and perhaps transferable forgiveness remains possible. It is somewhat surprising Rama did not mention that qualification here. The practical rule therefore requires care. The strongest version, that transfer of a debt secured by collateral prevents forgiveness, is clearest where the creditor has the full halachic acquisition in the collateral, such as collateral taken after the loan. Other configurations remain subject to the underlying dispute.
