He does not need witnesses for the acquisition itself. Maggid Mishneh says it is difficult to explain why the holder is believed against the seller but not against the borrower. According to what I explained above, the distinction is understandable. The purchaser can say against the seller: "I acquired this note, and even if the transfer of the debt itself was defective, the physical paper is worth to me the entire value of the debt." Since the paper is in his possession, he is treated as possessor against the seller. Rambam nevertheless holds that a claim such as "I had a written transfer and lost it" or "the note was transferred to me through agav" is an uncommon claim. A prudent purchaser normally preserves proof of such a transfer. This is especially true because against the borrower the purchaser cannot rely merely on possession. The borrower can say, "You are not my litigant. Prove that the debt was transferred to you." Knowing that, a purchaser would be particularly careful to preserve the transfer evidence. Agav also ordinarily has publicity through the land acquisition and can be proved. Therefore, if the purchaser actually makes such a claim against the seller, the physical possession can support him. But he does not receive a migo based on the theoretical possibility that he could have claimed a lost written transfer or agav. That is an uncommon migo, and we do not use a migo based on an unusual claim. This resolves the case of the elderly woman in Sanhedrin who held bundles of documents. According to one version, once the documents had been established before beit din she was not believed through a migo that she had purchased them with writing and delivery and lost the transfer paper. Such a claim is uncommon. When the view is that documents are acquired by delivery alone, however, the claim becomes ordinary. The holder's physical possession is the normal acquisition itself, even against the borrower, and no special proof is expected. That is why the Gemara can distinguish between the two systems. The same explains the elderly woman's case in Ketubot. A migo that she bought the papers through an unusual formal transfer would be too remote to use once the transaction is otherwise established. Thus Rambam's distinction is coherent: possession can defeat the seller where the purchaser affirmatively claims an effective transfer, but it does not create an additional migo based on unlikely lost formalities.
