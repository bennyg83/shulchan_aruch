Seif 1. And if he sold him good-quality goods and they were found to be inferior, etc. Note: See what I wrote on this in my Torah commentary for the year 5608. This applies specifically to other modes of acquisition where the seller did not receive money, or where the buyer gave exchange property rather than cash. But if the seller actually received money, the buyer cannot rescind. This is a novel ruling. I explained the reason there. I also wrote there that if Levi sold something to a person whom he believed to be Shimon, and it later became known that the buyer was Reuven, Levi cannot rescind and the transaction is not a mistaken sale. In seif 1, one who sells to his fellow, etc. Note: At first glance, one can readily derive a new law from Pesachim 48a, where the Gemara distinguishes between inferior and superior grain, and Rav Pappa says: "Learn from this that inferior wheat is more deficient relative to superior wheat than inferior barley is relative to superior barley," etc. Rashi explains there that this has practical relevance to buying and selling. Apparently, his intent is that if one sold superior wheat for flour and later gives inferior wheat, he must give one third more. With barley, he must give one quarter more. This might appear to prove that when the poskim say that if superior goods were sold and inferior goods were delivered the transaction is void, that is only where the seller does not add additional quantity. But if he wishes to add sufficient quantity, the buyer would be compelled to accept it. In truth, there is no proof. One may say that the Gemara concerns a case where the seller did not say "these particular wheat kernels," but sold superior wheat generally. In such a case he may give inferior wheat in a greater quantity according to the stated proportion. But if he sold these particular wheat kernels, showed them to the buyer, and said that these were superior, and they were later found inferior, since these very goods were the subject of the sale the buyer can say that he does not want other goods, even if the seller offers a somewhat greater quantity. Even in a general sale there is room for doubt. It may be that if one sold superior wheat, under no circumstances may he provide inferior wheat even if he adds much more. The Gemara may instead concern one who sold ordinary wheat for a gold coin according to the market price at that time, for example where one measure of superior wheat was worth a gold coin. If he wants to give inferior wheat, he must provide one third more, while for barley one quarter more. Alternatively, the case may be where he stipulated that he would provide enough wheat to produce a specified amount of flour. In that case he must provide a certain quantity of superior wheat, but one third more if the wheat is inferior, and one quarter more if it is inferior barley. It is surprising that the early and later poskim did not cite this law at all. Examine this carefully.
