The buyer may retract, but the seller may not. If the buyer was overcharged by one sixth and demands return of the overcharge, this can be learned from Ramban's novellae to Bava Batra. His words imply that according to the view that in a transaction subject to cancellation the party who caused the overcharge cannot retract merely because the injured party can say, "if you had not overcharged me, you could not have retracted," this remains true only so long as the injured party does not demand the ona'ah remedy. In a case where superior goods were sold and were found to be inferior, the situation is treated as cancellation of the sale, and for that same reason the injured buyer may retract while the seller may not. Consequently, if there is also an exact one sixth overcharge and the buyer demands the ona'ah, then even the seller may retract, just as in other cases of cancellation. It is surprising that the Mechaber follows this view in siman 227:4, as discussed by the Sema there, yet omits here the resulting rule that if there is exact one sixth ona'ah and the buyer demands it, the seller too may retract. According to the Rosh, cited by the Rama there, the party who caused the overcharge may retract even in ordinary cancellation, while "superior found inferior" is not treated as cancellation from the seller's perspective. On that view the buyer can apparently demand return of exact one sixth ona'ah without giving the seller a right to cancel. In Sema, seif katan 3, there is a textual error. It should read, "However, the Mechaber did not write this opinion," and the word "Rama" should be deleted.
Even though they are not superior. See Sema, seif katan 4, through his statement that one might have thought the buyer could retract. His wording is imprecise. The more natural possible conclusion would be that the seller could retract, because there are also grades of inferior goods below these. The ruling teaches that he cannot.
The sale remains effective and he returns the ona'ah. This is from Terumat HaDeshen, siman 322, which derives the rule from Beitzah 7 concerning eggs of a particular type. At first glance the Bach appears correct that the Ran in HaIsh Mekadesh, regarding brothers who divided an estate, contradicts this. Terumat HaDeshen apparently overlooked the Ran. It therefore seems to be a doubt in the law. According to the Rama's approach, the same applies to any discrepancy that beit din considers insufficiently material to show that the particular feature was essential to the buyer.
Impure silver. See Shach, seif katan 1, who rules that the sale is void. Even though the silver can be refined and corrected, doing so requires an expenditure. This resembles Pesachim 6 concerning one who rents a house on the assumption that it was searched for chametz and finds that it was not. See what I wrote there in my work Mekor Chaim. Here too, the silver is not fit for its intended work without additional expense.
