And if he wrote liability upon his property, some say that he acquired it. See Sema, seif katan 9, who cites the Tur that an undertaking of liability is effective only where the seller has been established as a robber specifically regarding this field. If he is generally known as a violent robber, the undertaking does not help. This view has support from the Gemara. The Gemara challenges Rav Huna, who says that a coerced sale is valid, from the law of one who purchases from a sicarius, and answers that it was stated concerning that case that Rav said: this applies only where the owner merely tells the purchaser, “Go take possession,” but if he writes a deed the purchaser does acquire. Thus Rav Huna’s rule concerns a sale with a deed. The Gemara then asks what Rav Huna can answer according to Shmuel, who says that even with a deed the purchaser does not acquire unless the seller writes an undertaking of liability. Why is that a difficulty? Perhaps Rav Huna’s case itself concerns a deed containing liability, just as according to Rav the Gemara establishes it as a deeded sale. We must conclude that in the case of one who is so violent that he kills for money, an undertaking of liability is ineffective even according to Shmuel. The Gemara’s challenge is therefore proper. This appears to be the intention of Tosafot on page 47b, in the passage beginning “but,” where they write that Rav holds a deed alone suffices because he holds that liability is an scribal error, while Shmuel follows his own view that liability is not an scribal error. Tosafot then write that there remains a slight difficulty: below, the Gemara challenges Rav Huna’s rule of a coerced sale from Shmuel’s statement that even with a deed the purchaser does not acquire, and if everything depends on liability, why is the question stronger according to Shmuel than according to Rav? At first glance, if Tosafot’s question is simply that perhaps Rav Huna speaks of a deed containing liability, this is unrelated to what they had just written and should have been asked directly on the Gemara. And if they regard it as strained to establish Rav Huna’s statement specifically where liability was written, since the expression “a deed” naturally means an ordinary deed on land, there is still no connection to Tosafot’s earlier discussion. According to what we have written, however, their words are clear. Tosafot understand that in a fully coerced sale an undertaking of liability is itself ineffective. Therefore, if Rav also agrees that the essential acquisition depends on liability and differs from Shmuel only because Rav holds that liability is automatically included as a scribal error, then in a coerced sale even explicit liability does not help, all the more so liability merely implied in an ordinary deed. The Gemara’s challenge should therefore apply equally to Rav. This is straightforward.
Now, the Mechaber omits the rule that liability is effective, even in the case where the seller is established only as a robber regarding this particular field, as Sema explains. In truth, he follows Rambam, and Rambam omits everywhere the rule that an explicit undertaking of liability is effective, even in the case of one who purchased from a sicarius. Maggid Mishneh already questions this. It appears that Rambam understood Shmuel’s reason as follows. Shmuel holds that the seller would not trouble himself to undertake liability unless he truly consented to the sale. This accords with Shmuel’s own position that an undertaking of liability must be expressly authorized, because he does not accept that liability in a sale is an automatic scribal error. Therefore, when liability appears in the deed, the witnesses necessarily consulted the seller and he agreed to it. Such an additional favorable term would be given only with full consent. According to our ruling, however, that liability is automatically included as a scribal error and witnesses may write it without consulting the owner, as explained in the first chapter of Bava Metzia according to the opinion that liability is a scribal error, there is no proof of consent at all. Not only does an ordinary deed containing liability fail to prove consent because the witnesses may have written it without consulting him, but even if we know that he agreed to the inclusion of liability, there is still no special indication of consent. Even had he remained silent and the witnesses omitted it, the purchaser would still receive liability because it is treated as a scribal error. To insist expressly that the deed be written without liability would certainly be something he might refrain from doing out of fear of the robber. Thus, according to our halachah, the writing of liability does not help. This explains why the Gemara challenges Rav Huna from Shmuel, as above, and does not answer that Rav Huna speaks where liability was written. According to our law that liability is an automatic scribal error, the writing itself proves nothing. Rambam’s omission is therefore well explained. But the Mechaber, who below in siman 236, seif 4, rules that an undertaking of liability is effective, unlike Rambam, is difficult. I do not know why he omitted that rule here, since it is strained to distinguish between a sicarius and a robber who is violent only regarding one particular field, when no early authority makes such a distinction. Perhaps the Mechaber relied upon what he wrote below in siman 236, since that is the principal location of the laws of a sicarius.
