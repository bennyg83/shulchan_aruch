But with nichsei melog he has proof. Although the Gemara implies, according to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds regarding one who sells his slave while stipulating that the slave will continue serving him for thirty days, that neither the seller nor the buyer has the law of “a day or two,” that a wife has no power to sell nichsei melog to her husband, and Rambam at the end of chapter 5 of Hilchot Avadim rules like Rabbi Eliezer, nevertheless this does not establish the contrary. First, one can say that we do not accept the Gemara’s answer that Ameimar follows Rabbi Eliezer, because that explanation is not compelling. Rather, the halachah follows the first answer, according to which there is a valid sale with respect to nichsei melog. Moreover, it appears that the Rashbam, who holds that just as husband and wife together cannot sell nichsei melog to others, so too the wife cannot sell them to her husband, follows his own approach. He understands the law of sale to be directly derived from the law concerning “a day or two,” as Ramban explains in his Chiddushim according to the Rashbam’s view. Therefore, just as neither spouse has that law, the wife cannot sell the property to her husband. But according to Ramban, who writes that the law of sale cannot truly be compared to the law of “a day or two,” and that Ameimar’s statement is based only on an independent rationale that a sale is ineffective when neither party possesses a fully distinct interest, it is enough to say that husband and wife cannot jointly sell the property to outsiders. If the wife sells it to her husband, however, he already owns an interest in the property through his right to the produce, and she merely extinguishes her own remaining right and sells him the entire ownership, including the corpus. Such a sale is plainly valid. Thus Ameimar’s statement that he follows Rabbi Eliezer means only with respect to a sale by both spouses to outsiders. Even Ameimar can agree that if a wife sells her nichsei melog to her husband, the sale is valid. We should not extend the comparison further, especially since the very analogy between sale and Rabbi Eliezer’s law is strained, as Ramban himself writes.
