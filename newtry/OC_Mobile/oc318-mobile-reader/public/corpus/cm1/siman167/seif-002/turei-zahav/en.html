Moreover, even if the ruling rests upon possession, it remains difficult: at the very least, the portion of the branches that the upper owner can reach should belong to him. In what sense is the lower owner in possession of that portion? It therefore appears that the authorities disagree fundamentally. The Tur rules that when the law is uncertain, the parties divide, following his general position concerning unresolved questions of law, as stated in siman 388, seif 4. The Rambam rules differently here because three Tannaim dispute the case. Rabbi Meir holds that everything growing between the two properties belongs to the upper owner; Rabbi Yehuda holds that it belongs to the lower owner; and Rabbi Shimon holds that whatever the upper owner can reach by extending his hand belongs to him. Ordinarily, the law follows Rabbi Yehuda against both Rabbi Meir and Rabbi Shimon. Here, however, the Gemara expressly rules like Rabbi Shimon. Accordingly, whenever there is uncertainty about the scope of Rabbi Shimon’s words, we revert to the ordinary rule that the law follows Rabbi Yehuda. Although the law was specifically fixed like Rabbi Shimon, the lower owner may say: perhaps this doubtful case was never included in Rabbi Shimon’s ruling. If the upper owner wishes to depart from the general rule favoring Rabbi Yehuda, he must prove that the case falls within Rabbi Shimon’s words. Since the matter remains evenly balanced, we rule like Rabbi Yehuda and award it to the lower owner. This appears correct to me.
