And the remainder belongs to the lower owner. See Sema, seif katan 1, who explains that the upper owner abandons the part that descended into the lower owner's domain. Ya'arot Devash questions how the lower owner acquires if airspace through which something passes without coming to rest does not effect acquisition, and concludes that the upper owner can retract until the lower owner performs an act of acquisition. In my view this is incorrect. Tosafot in Gittin 22 explain that the body of a tree also derives nourishment from the air, although that nourishment is not equivalent to the root's nourishment. Likewise Tosafot in Bava Metzia 107, concerning a tree standing on a boundary, show that the owner of the branches has a proprietary relation to them. Ordinarily we attribute the branches to the roots, but once the root owner withdraws and abandons his claim, there is no longer any reason to attribute the branches to the roots. The body and branches are then treated as belonging to the one whose property nourishes them. This also answers why the poskim state the rule broadly even regarding the root. Although the Gemara says that with the root all agree it belongs to the upper owner, that statement can be limited to R. Meir and R. Yehudah. According to R. Shimon, whose rationale is abandonment because the upper owner cannot enter and remove it, the same abandonment can apply even to the root.
