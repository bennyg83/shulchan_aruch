And likewise if the wind was, etc. On the rationale stated here, see Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 136, concerning Reuven and Shimon who traveled together on a wagon loaded with Reuven's cotton. Shimon was smoking tobacco, and sparks from his pipe fell onto the wagon. Reuven protested and told him to get off the wagon when smoking so that his merchandise would not burn, but Shimon ignored him. Later an unusual wind arose, sparks entered the cotton, and all of it burned. Shevut Yaakov ruled that Shimon must pay. Although smoking tobacco is plainly less direct than the Talmudic case of singeing another's grain, which may be exempt, Shimon is liable for a different reason. Since the unusual wind had already begun, he should have anticipated the danger and stopped smoking on the wagon, especially after Reuven expressly warned him. By continuing, even without actively blowing on the sparks, he is treated like one who brought the fire and is liable for the damage under the law of fire, payable from the best of his property.
