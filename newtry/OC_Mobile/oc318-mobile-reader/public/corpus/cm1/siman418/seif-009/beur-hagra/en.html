That is uncommon, etc.; and if, etc. An ordinary wind is treated like direct damage, as with one's stone, knife, or burden, and likewise one who bends another's standing grain toward a fire. Tosafot cites the Yerushalmi, whose phrase 'sometimes, etc.' refers to an uncommon wind. See 445.
Likewise, if, etc. Tosafot continues accordingly. This is why Rava says that the case is where he fanned it during an ordinary wind. The initial assumption was that an uncommon wind was already blowing. The Gemara did not answer that an ordinary wind arose later, because in such a case he would be liable.
(Supplement) If an ordinary wind, etc.; likewise, if, etc. This refers to the first two clauses and does not yet discuss a case where both the person and wind fanned the flame, which is addressed afterward. In that later case there is no distinction whether the wind was already blowing or began later. Where the person's fanning alone was sufficient, he is liable even if an uncommon wind arose afterward. Where neither alone was sufficient, the exemption applies in all cases.
He fanned, etc. Rav Ashi gives this explanation. Rambam follows his broader position that indirect causation is exempt, as stated in siman 386, and similarly in Ketubot concerning coercion. Tosafot and Rama disagree there, which explains the dissenting view here. The objection from siman 155, seif 34, is addressed by Shach.
(Supplement) He fanned, etc. This differs from siman 155 because Bava Batra distinguishes a case where the actor desires the harmful force to travel. Here it resembles a spark, since he wants the fire to spread. Rav Ashi nevertheless distinguishes it from a spark, as Tosafot explains, but we do not follow Rav Ashi on that point. Rambam's general view is that garmi liability applies only where the actor intended the harmful result, unlike the case there. See 155 and 386.
