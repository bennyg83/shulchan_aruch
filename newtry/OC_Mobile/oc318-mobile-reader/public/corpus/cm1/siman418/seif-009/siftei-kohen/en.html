He fanned it and the wind also fanned it, he is liable. This is the Rambam's wording. The Raavad objects that the Rambam should have distinguished, as the Gemara does, between an ordinary wind and an unusual wind. The Maggid Mishneh and the other commentators explain that the Rambam speaks where the person's own fanning was a substantial cause of the fire, so that the wind merely joined an effective human act. Where his act alone could not have produced the fire and only an unusual wind completed it, the law depends upon foreseeability and upon whether his act is legally regarded as the direct creation of the damaging force. The baraita and the Gemara distinguish among one who actually blows, one who merely moves air with a garment or tool, and one whose act only prepares fuel for a later wind. The decisive question is whether the fire's spread is attributed to his force, to an ordinary wind that he should have anticipated, or to an extraordinary event. Tosafot, the Rosh, and the other authorities explain the passage in differing ways. According to the principal view, if his act and an ordinary wind together caused the fire, he is liable because he joined his force to a foreseeable natural force. If an unusual wind alone transformed an otherwise harmless act into a damaging one, he is exempt unless he should specifically have anticipated it. The Rambam's broad language should therefore not be read as abolishing the Gemara's distinction. It refers to a case in which his fanning materially contributed to the combustion, or in which the wind was ordinary and foreseeable. The Raavad's qualification remains relevant where the wind was genuinely extraordinary and the human act lacked independent causal force.
