And it injured him. If he died because of the burning, the owner of the fire is liable to death as though he shot an arrow at him. So writes the Tur.
For his pain and his humiliation. Although it will be explained in siman 421 that one is not liable for humiliation unless he intended to humiliate, here, since the fire traveled through an ordinary wind, it is as though he knew and intended that result. We must say that Rambam and the Mechaber distinguish this from one who falls from a roof through an ordinary wind and causes injury, where Rambam in chapter 1 of Hilchot Chovel and the Mechaber below in siman 421, seif 11, rule that he does not pay for humiliation because he did not intend it. Falling from a roof and causing injury is considered more nearly accidental, even through an ordinary wind, than kindling a fire that travels and injures a person. Rashi and Ran, however, write explicitly regarding fire as well that he is exempt from humiliation, and bring proof from the case of falling from a roof. Rosh and Tur state without qualification that in this case he is liable for four categories. In Perishah I explained that in my view they too agree with Rambam that humiliation is included, and when they say four categories they mean the four in addition to bodily damage, since bodily damage is imposed for negligence according to everyone even where liability for fire does not arise from the principle of "his arrows" but only because it is his property. As though he damaged with his own hands. The correct text continues, "for even though his fire, etc." We derive this from the verse, "If fire goes out and finds thorns," implying that it went out on its own, yet the verse concludes, "the one who kindled the fire shall surely pay," treating it as though he himself kindled the damaging fire directly.
It is as though he damaged with his arrows. Meaning, for purposes of liability for the five categories of bodily injury and also liability to death if the person died from the fire, as explained above. Yet fire is no less than ox and pit, which are one's property and impose liability at least for bodily damage. The practical difference is that even where he is not liable for the fire under the doctrine of his arrows, such as where the fire could not initially have traveled that far, he may nevertheless remain liable because it is his property, as the Mechaber concludes.
And he is exempt from the four categories. For the verse says, "If a man injures his fellow," excluding damage caused by one's property, as explained above.
