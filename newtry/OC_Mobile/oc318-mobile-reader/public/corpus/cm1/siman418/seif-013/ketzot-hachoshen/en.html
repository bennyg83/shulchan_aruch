He swears. Sema says that even if the tortfeasor makes a definite denial, the injured party can swear and collect under the enactment for a robbed person. The Shach and Yam Shel Shlomo disagree and limit the enactment for an ordinary tortfeasor to cases where the tortfeasor himself is uncertain, because he is not disqualified like a robber. There is difficulty from the law of hidden property burned in a fire according to Rabbi Yehudah. The Gemara says the robbed-person enactment was extended to fire. Rabbi Yehudah elsewhere requires at least partial admission before that enactment applies. If the defendant were uncertain, ordinary inability to take the required oath could itself produce payment, making the enactment difficult to explain. One can establish the case where the admitted part is *heilach*, which resembles partial admission sufficiently for Rabbi Yehudah according to Ran but does not create the ordinary Torah oath. Tosafot and Raavad, however, appear to require a true partial admission that itself generates a Torah oath, which revives the difficulty. The Jerusalem Talmud, as cited by the Shach, indicates that the core dispute concerning a person who lights another's property is precisely whether the robbed-person enactment was made for fire. The exact scope of the defendant's plea therefore remains tied to the competing readings. See the Shach in siman 90 for a possible resolution.
