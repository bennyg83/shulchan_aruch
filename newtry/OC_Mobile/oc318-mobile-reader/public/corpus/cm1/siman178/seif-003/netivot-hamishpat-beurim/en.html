And he protests against him, if it is something that cannot be built separately. This means a case where the partner's house is in danger of collapse and one partner cannot rebuild his own portion without rebuilding the other's portion as well. If the other refuses to build, beit din compels him to contribute his share, descends to his assets, and collects the amount needed for the common building. He cannot say, "Build the whole house yourself and live in it until I reimburse you." Why is this different from the case of a house and upper story, where the lower owner is not compelled immediately, and the upper owner instead builds and lives there until reimbursed? There the lower owner can say to the upper owner, "What do you gain by compelling me? Whatever expense you need for the upper structure, build below and live there. Even if I rebuild the lower structure, your own building expense will not be reduced." Beit din therefore need not seize his property. Here, by contrast, the willing partner must bear extra expense because he cannot rebuild his own portion without also rebuilding the other's. He can say, "Since both of us require this building, let us pay the cost together. I am not willing to advance the entire expense alone." This principle is not limited to formal partners. Whenever a necessary repair serves two people and one cannot correct his own situation without simultaneously correcting the other's, each can compel the other to contribute his share. This is proved from the rule that townspeople compel one another to build a wall and other communal necessities, and from the rules of residents of an alley and courtyard in the laws of neighbor damages. Siman 163, seif 6, likewise requires all neighboring towns exposed to the same danger to contribute. This does not resemble one who surrounds his neighbor's field in siman 158, which is compared to one who benefits while the other suffers no loss. There the protection is not truly indispensable, since a minimal low-cost guard may suffice. Where the need is unavoidable for both, each may compel the other. The Mishnah expressly says that owners of fields in a valley compel one another to make a ditch and embankment. Further proof appears in siman 161, seif 6, concerning five courtyards draining into one sewer, where all must participate in repairing the lower section because otherwise the water backs up against the upper courtyards. The upper owner cannot simply say to the lower one, "Let your courtyard be damaged." Likewise in siman 170 concerning five gardens. Were there only the lower garden, no one could compel its owner to make the repair, but because the upper garden also requires it, the upper owner can compel the lower to contribute. The law in siman 264, seif 4, concerning two people arrested together and one who redeems himself without obligating the other, deals with arrests arising from separate matters where each could be saved independently. Where both were seized for the same cause and neither can be rescued without the other, each can compel the other to contribute, analogous to a caravan attacked by raiders. If life is threatened, contribution is assessed by persons; if property is threatened, by property, according to the usual rules. It therefore appears to me that the same applies where a jointly owned house has collapsed entirely and only the land remains, provided the land is not intended to be converted to a different use and remains designated for rebuilding. Either partner may compel the other to rebuild. The site is otherwise standing in a state of loss, and rebuilding is like rescuing the common property from damage. This is not comparable to one partner who merely wishes to improve partnership assets, where one cannot ordinarily force the other. Here the property is not being upgraded but preserved from existing loss.
