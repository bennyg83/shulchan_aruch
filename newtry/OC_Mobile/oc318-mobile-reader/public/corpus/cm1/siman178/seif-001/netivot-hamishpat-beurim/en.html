Partners who requested. See Shach, seif katan 1. His cross reference should be to siman 77, not siman 73. He alludes to Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, and his intent is as Taz writes: Maharshal rules like Maharam, cited by Rama in siman 163, seif 6, concerning a partner whose share of taxes the ruler waived, that even when the waiver came through the partner's request it belongs to him personally. It is therefore surprising that Rama does not cite Maharam's opposing opinion here as he does in siman 163, since the two laws are linked in Mordechai and Terumat HaDeshen, siman 341. More fundamentally, Mechaber's view here rests on the rule that a partner cannot divide the partnership without his fellow's knowledge. Terumat HaDeshen explains that this differs from the caravan case in siman 181, where a partner may separate in a time of danger and say, "I am saving for myself," because there he risks his life against armed men, whereas here the gain came merely by petition. If so, Rama's ruling above in siman 176, seif 28, becomes difficult. There partners held a debt against a violent non Jew, one partner appeased him, and Rama rules that if he first said, "I am saving for myself," the recovery belongs to him personally. That rule is learned from the caravan case. Yet according to R. Simchah, once the gain came through appeasement, he should not be able to say, "I save for myself." The rulings therefore seem inconsistent, and siman 77 raises a similar question where two borrowers owe one lender and the lender waives one borrower's share. Sema there says the same even where the waiver followed that borrower's petition, while here the gain is shared. In my view the matters can be reconciled by identifying the real reason a partner cannot say "I save for myself" when the gain arose through petition. The reason is not simply that division is impossible. Rather, the gift was generated through effort and petition, and each partner is obligated to exert himself for the common enterprise. When one makes the request while the partnership obligation remains, he is effectively acting as agent for all. His mouth is legally their mouth, and the resulting gift is treated as though it came through the petition of all partners. He cannot separate merely from the duty to exert himself for the partnership. Accordingly, where two people merely borrowed from the same lender but are not partners in any enterprise and each borrowed for himself, neither is obligated to exert himself for the other. If one secures a waiver through his own petition, the benefit is his alone. This resolves siman 77. Likewise, when a partner separates himself entirely from a particular partnership asset, he may say, "I save for myself," even though other partnership relations continue. Thus in siman 176, concerning one particular debt, he can separate from that debt and save for himself. The same applies in siman 181 to one particular item seized by raiders. Terumat HaDeshen's distinction regarding taxes must therefore mean that the tax obligation remains inherently attached to a common enterprise from which one cannot separately withdraw. For example, where partners owe customs on merchandise they are jointly bringing to a place, they cannot divide merely from the customs obligation while remaining jointly engaged in the merchandise. The request for remission of customs is an act required for the common merchandise, so the requesting partner acts as agent for all. Similarly, public taxes arise because members live under the ruler's authority and benefit collectively from public protection. The larger the community, the greater the public burden, and each individual is obligated to participate rather than separate from the community. Therefore, when one petitions for remission, his petition is made while he remains bound to act for the community, and the benefit is shared. R. Meir disputes this reasoning, but Rama here follows R. Simchah because Rosh, the later authority, ruled that way concerning customs. Nevertheless, in siman 163 Rama records both opinions concerning general taxes, because even Rosh may concede that ordinary taxes differ from customs. With customs tied directly to a joint commercial shipment, a partner clearly acts for all. With general taxes due the ruler, remission often comes only because one person has special favor with the ruler. In that setting there is reason to say that the favor belongs personally to him. There is an additional distinction. In a normal debt one can sometimes argue, "Had you not requested remission for yourself, I could have appeased the creditor and secured a general reduction," because creditors often compromise further only before granting a partial waiver. Thus one partner's benefit may directly worsen the other's position. With fixed taxes, however, it is not customary to ask the ruler repeatedly for further remission after assessment. Rather, a ruler may excuse a favored servant personally. That supports Maharam's view in siman 163. For this reason Rama records the dispute there while ruling here in accordance with R. Simchah in the commercial partnership case.
