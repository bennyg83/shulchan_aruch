Except that whenever the authorized agent is forceful, etc. The Shach in seif katan 32 challenges the Rama's understanding of Tosafot and explains that when Tosafot speak of an "overbearing" person they mean that the defendant is overbearing. The claimant is therefore afraid to litigate with him personally and appoints an authorized agent instead. Apart from the fact that the Shach's resulting law is incorrect, for in such a case the agent acts properly by not fearing any man, and if the claimant is fainthearted and would otherwise lose his rights while the agent girds himself with strength to stand against the overbearing defendant and not be intimidated, he should be blessed for supporting one who would stumble. Our Sages labored greatly to break the teeth and power of violent men, even compelling such a person to bring the evidence held by his opponent. How then could we object when an authorized agent stands in the place of one who has been abandoned and intimidated? Moreover, Piskei Tosafot to Shevuot expressly state: "An overbearing and argumentative person should not intrude to argue on behalf of his fellow." This plainly means, as the Rama understood, that the authorized agent himself is overbearing and excessively argumentative. As for the Shach's main question, what harm could arise merely from the agent's forcefulness and arguments if he is forbidden to argue falsely, there is no difficulty. Through his many arguments the defendant may agree to a compromise because he grows weary of the contentious proceedings and the burden imposed on beit din. Witnesses too may fear him, as in the incident of Mari bar Isak. See Terumat HaDeshen siman 306 and Maharshal, Bava Kamma chapter 4, law 18, concerning whether one may employ numerous procedural claims and pressures to force another to compromise. See there that where the contentions have substance, it is permitted. The Rama's ruling here is therefore primary.
