He must be careful how he writes it. Taz writes that according to Sema's explanation, if the effectiveness of the kinyan depends on whose sudar is used, the decisors should have discussed the sudar rather than the wording of the document. One might answer that the two depend on each other: if specifically the witnesses' sudar is used, one writes, "and we effected a kinyan." But this cannot be right, because in Talmudic times the purchaser himself commonly supplied the sudar and nevertheless documents were written with the language, "and we effected a kinyan," as stated in Perek Yesh Nochalin. It therefore appears to Taz that the witnesses may give their own sudar to a gentile so that he acquires through it. They must only avoid writing, "and we effected a kinyan from him," because that wording implies that the witnesses acted as his agents in accepting the kinyan, and there is no halachic agency for a gentile. Rather, they should write that the gentile acquired, meaning that he acquired through the witnesses' sudar. Chacham Tzvi commented upon these words, "The rabbi's words have overcome me." However, at first glance one might think that when the witnesses give their own sudar, they act only as agents. Yet in siman 195, seif katan 9, I proved that this cannot be the reason. The Shulchan Aruch there says one does not effect a kinyan on Shabbat, but if they did so, although they committed a prohibition, the kinyan is valid. Already in their time it was customary for the witnesses to give their own sudar, as the Tur cites from the Rosh. If their act depended upon agency, there would be no agency for a transgression, even for a rabbinic prohibition. I therefore concluded there that agency is unnecessary. Once the witnesses give their sudar, the recipient acquires under the principle learned from a Canaanite slave. Kiddushin teaches: if one says, "Here is a maneh, and let your field be sold to so and so," that third person acquires through the law of the Canaanite slave. In the law of a Canaanite slave, the acquisition can operate even without the slave's will, because the master's receipt effects the change. Although an ordinary acquisition cannot be forced upon someone against his will, where the recipient wants the acquisition no agency is required. He acquires automatically through the sudar supplied by the witnesses and through the transferor's receipt. The same therefore works with a gentile, because agency is not needed. The essential point is only that the document not use language implying agency. It should say that the gentile acquired, for he acquires through the witnesses' sudar. This is clear. Old Tosafot in the first chapter of Kiddushin implies the same. They write that one should not write, "and we effected a kinyan from him," because that wording implies that the witnesses are agents to receive the kinyan, and there is no agency for a gentile. The concern is therefore only with writing a formulation of agency.
And the gentile acquires through barter in Eretz Yisrael. See the Shach. Tumim writes that this passage appears to show that acquisition incidentally with land does not work for a gentile. Otherwise, he asks, why did Rabbeinu Tam not instruct that an authorization written for a gentile should use acquisition incidentally with land? Likewise, in the laws of chametz and firstborn animals, why did the decisors not instruct that the property be transferred incidentally with land? He concludes that nowhere do we find acquisition incidentally with land functioning for a gentile. These words were written too hastily. Rabbeinu Yerucham already states expressly, in Netiv 20, part 2, that to satisfy all opinions one should receive money from the gentile for the animal's ear, or transfer to the gentile the place where the animal stands and transfer the ear of the fetus to him incidentally with that land. Thus acquisition incidentally with land is explicitly stated regarding a gentile. Indeed, this method has become widespread throughout Israel in the sale of chametz. It follows the ruling of the Gaon Rabbi Shmuel of Koidanov, who questioned acquisition through a courtyard because a courtyard functions through agency, and there is no agency for a gentile. The practice therefore relied upon acquisition incidentally with land. This is clear.
