A non-Jew. The Sema cites the Tur that Rabbeinu Tam derives from Scripture that a non-Jew may acquire through barter. Therefore, when the non-Jew gives his cloth to the Jewish principal, the principal takes hold of it and transfers in exchange the debt or deposit that he has in another's possession, the non-Jew acquires it as an authorized representative. This works only when the non-Jew personally gives the barter object to the Jew. Witnesses cannot make the kinyan on his behalf in the usual manner, where witnesses give their own cloth to the transferor and thereby acquire for the recipient under the rule that one may acquire for another in his absence. The Tur states in siman 195 that most kinyanim in his time were made that way, and the witnesses would write that they acquired from the transferor for the recipient with a valid utensil. When the authorization is made directly to a non-Jew, however, the witnesses do not act as agents to acquire for him. They therefore write that the non-Jew himself made the barter acquisition. The Shach writes that Maharshal disagrees and concludes that for a loan or other property that cannot itself be transferred by barter, authorization to a non-Jew plainly does not work because there the representative acts only as an agent, and a non-Jew cannot serve as a halachic agent. Land or a deposit, however, can be transferred to the non-Jew by a valid kinyan sudar if barter works for a non-Jew. In that case the authorization should omit the clause binding the principal to everything litigated, because that is agency language. Responsa Maharashdam 59 writes similarly. The Shach suggests that Rabbeinu Tam may also mean only this. The Mechaber's wording, however, seems to imply that authorization to a non-Jew works in every context. The Shach remains doubtful even about the underlying premise that barter works for a non-Jew. He discusses the proofs at length and notes that Responsa Maharam Mintz 5 states simply that no barter acquisition exists for a non-Jew. Maharashdam 59 likewise says that Rabbeinu Tam's view appears to be an isolated opinion, though without giving the reason.
Agency. Responsa Rashach II, 33, writes that where both the principal and representative are non-Jews, or forced converts, authorization is ineffective under either Jewish law or their civil law even according to Rabbeinu Tam. Responsa Divrei Rivot 301 writes that according to Rabbeinu Tam a non-Jew may authorize a Jew. Maharashdam 59 rules that even Rabbeinu Tam agrees that a non-Jew cannot appoint a Jew as authorized representative. Shach.
