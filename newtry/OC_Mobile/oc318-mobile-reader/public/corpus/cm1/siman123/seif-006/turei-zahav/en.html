(Seif 15, gloss). “In his absence.” Mordechai writes all this in the name of Rivak, and afterward suggests another explanation distinguishing a violent or highly contentious litigant, as Rama writes immediately below. Rama appears to understand that there is no dispute. Mordechai's concluding citation of Rabbi Yitzhak adds another distinction: where authorization is permitted and even fulfills a mitzvah, it may be effective even when made in the person's absence. Rama therefore carefully writes that one may not impose a detriment upon a person in his absence, implying that where the appointment is certainly a mitzvah it is effective. The prooftext, “that which is not good,” shows that only where the verse's criticism applies is the appointment considered detrimental. Sema understands Mordechai as recording a dispute between Rivak and Rabbi Yitzhak, with Rama deciding like Rivak that even a mitzvah appointment is detrimental because most people do not wish to serve as representatives. In my view, where it is a mitzvah there is no detriment, since a person is pleased to perform a mitzvah.
