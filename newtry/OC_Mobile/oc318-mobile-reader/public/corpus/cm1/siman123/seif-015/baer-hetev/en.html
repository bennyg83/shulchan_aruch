A forceful person. Rama appears to have understood Beit Yosef's citation of Tosafot as referring to a forceful representative. This is not the intent. Tosafot mean that sometimes a creditor does not wish to litigate with a debtor because the debtor is forceful and skilled in contentious argument, and the creditor does not want to quarrel with him. In such a case, a representative who inserts himself into the dispute is not acting properly, because he is involving himself in a quarrel that is not his own. The creditor can pursue his claim personally. If, however, the creditor is unable to undertake the trouble of collecting his money, the representative performs a mitzvah by assisting him. This also appears from Kesef Mishneh, which understands Tosafot as referring to a forceful debtor. According to this, there is no dispute at all. Everyone agrees that when the representative acts because the principal is genuinely unable to do what must be done, he performs a mitzvah. Only when they are in the same city and the principal could do exactly what the representative does, but refrains merely because of the debtor's forcefulness, is the representative considered one who involves himself in another's quarrel. In his presence. Meaning, not in the presence of the person for whom he is acting as attorney. The witnesses cannot acquire on his behalf without his knowledge in a matter that may be detrimental to him, for this is called one who has done what is not good. Therefore, even if he later consents, it is of no effect, since the acquisition did not take effect initially. This is the view of Rivak in Mordechai. Although he cites the view of Ri that where the principal cannot trouble himself the agent performs a mitzvah and this is not detrimental, Rama nevertheless rules in accordance with Rivak, whose reasoning is persuasive: even if he performs a mitzvah, most people do not consent to involve themselves in a quarrel that is not theirs, and at times the verse, one who has done what is not good, is applied to such a person. Therefore, even if he later consents, we do not say that the matter is revealed retroactively; his consent is effective only from then onward, and the acquisition did not initially take effect. This is the Sema. Shach objects that Rama too speaks only of the case explained earlier, such as where the opposing party is violent or the like; where the agent performs a mitzvah, however, one may certainly appoint an attorney for another even without his presence.
