And some say. Rama's language is difficult. His statement that the rule applies while the agent is forceful appears to understand Tosafot as describing a forceful agent. That cannot be correct. If the agent argues truthfully, his forcefulness is irrelevant. If he lies or wins improperly, the prohibition is obvious and applies even to a litigant acting for himself. Tosafot mean that the borrower is forceful and argumentative, so the creditor avoids litigating with him. An agent who unnecessarily inserts himself into such a quarrel “intervenes in a dispute not his own.” By contrast, where he works to recover money that the creditor cannot personally pursue, he performs a mitzvah by rescuing the oppressed. Accordingly, Raavad, Tur, Mechaber, and Tosafot do not disagree. Whenever the principal cannot practically act, because of distance, burden, or similar circumstances, the agent performs a mitzvah. Where both are in the same city and the principal could act equally well, but avoids doing so only because the debtor is forceful, the agent improperly enters another's quarrel. Kesef Mishneh's statement that an agent acts meritoriously where the creditor cannot recover because of the debtor's force or distance refers to a case where the debtor would wrongfully resist the principal but not the agent, perhaps because the agent is influential or understands the debtor's tactics.
Some say that a person cannot appoint an agent. Mordechai first cites Rabbi Yehudah ben Kalonymus broadly, but then limits the rule according to Ri: where the agent merely intrudes into a quarrel because the debtor is forceful, the appointment is detrimental; where he labors to recover money the principal cannot recover, it is a mitzvah. Sema struggles to explain why Rama omitted Mordechai's conclusion and suggests that Rama rules like Rabbi Yehudah ben Kalonymus, since people generally do not wish to be drawn into others' disputes. In my view this is strained. Even Ri agrees that where accepting the agency is detrimental, it cannot be imposed without the agent's knowledge, because one may not impose a detriment upon another in his absence. Later consent does not validate the original authorization, since the acquisition never took effect. This is also the plain ruling of Shiltei HaGibborim, Ramban, and Maggid Mishneh. Rabbi Yehudah ben Kalonymus differs only in treating every such appointment as presumptively detrimental, whereas Ri allows appointment without prior knowledge where the agent's service is beneficial and meritorious. Rama's “some say” therefore concerns the detrimental case described immediately beforehand. In such a case later consent is ineffective according to all views. Ir Shushan and Sema did not state this accurately.
