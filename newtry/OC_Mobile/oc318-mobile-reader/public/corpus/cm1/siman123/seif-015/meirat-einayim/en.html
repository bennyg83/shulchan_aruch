Not in his presence. Meaning the principal should not effect a kinyan authorizing another person outside that person's presence, saying before witnesses, "I transfer and authorize so-and-so over my claim against so-and-so." Witnesses cannot acquire for a person without his knowledge something that may be a burden to him. The Gemara's expression regarding a representative, "he did what is not good," reflects that serving as representative can be an unwanted burden. Therefore, even if he later agrees, the original kinyan may not have taken effect. Mordechai first writes this in the name of Rabbi Yehudah bar Kalonymus. He then cites Rabbeinu Yitzchak, who distinguishes: the criticism applies when someone appoints a representative merely because that representative is forceful and skilled in claims, whereas one who undertakes the burden in order to recover another's money when the owner cannot trouble himself is performing a mitzvah by rescuing the oppressed from the oppressor. According to that approach, later consent may perhaps validate the authorization. Levush rules only according to this latter conclusion and says later acceptance reveals consent retroactively. Rama, however, appears to follow Rabbi Yehudah bar Kalonymus. Even if representation can be a mitzvah, most people do not want to insert themselves into another person's dispute, and sometimes the verse "he did what is not good" truly applies. Therefore later consent does not retroactively create a kinyan that never initially took effect; it works only from the time of consent onward.
