The same applies to one who had a claim, etc., but he must write, etc. Darkei Moshe cites the Mordechai at the beginning of Mi SheMet and explains it in the manner reflected by Rama's gloss here; the Ir Shushan follows him. In my glosses to Darkei Moshe I wrote that this does not appear to be the Mordechai's meaning. Rather, the Mordechai deals with a person who attempted to transfer a claim by an ordinary kinyan without writing an authorization. Such a transfer does not work, because something not presently tangible cannot be fully transferred to another as his property by a normal kinyan. But appointing another to stand in one's place through a formal authorization is effective, just as we write authorizations for every loan, even an oral loan and even one the defendant denies. The Mordechai therefore distinguishes between an ordinary acquisition of a claim and our authorizations concerning loans that are not presently tangible by saying, 'There he merely made him an agent.' That is, an authorization works because it is not a complete transfer of ownership, but rather makes the representative the principal's agent so that the custodian or debtor cannot refuse to deliver to him. This differs from a purported full transfer of the claim itself. See further in my glosses to Darkei Moshe. The Ran in Shevuot HaEdut writes that authorization cannot be written concerning money that never came into the claimant's possession at all, such as claims for humiliation, bodily depreciation, or damages, contrary to Rashi.
