If he compromised with him, his act is ineffective. Shaar Mishpat writes that he heard that the sages of the preceding generation disputed a case in which a creditor sent his fellow with a debt document to compromise with the debtor. The agent compromised and tore up the document. The creditor later sued the debtor, claiming that the agent had deviated from his instructions, because he had instructed him not to settle for less than a specified amount and the agent settled for less. Some wished to compare this to siman 182, seif 4, concerning a purchaser's agent who erred: so long as the principal cannot prove that he appointed him simply to buy without qualification, the seller may say that the principal authorized the agent whether to improve or impair the transaction, and the burden of proof rests on the claimant. Likewise here, they argued, the debtor is exempt because he can say to the creditor, "I do not believe you that the agent deviated from his instructions." Others rejected the comparison. There, with respect to the seller, the claim is equivalent to "I do not know whether I ever borrowed from you," which is exempt. Here, however, between creditor and debtor it is equivalent to the debtor saying, "I do not know whether I repaid you," which is liable. Shaar Mishpat says that in his view there is no question that if the compromise arose because of claims and counterclaims between them, the debtor is certainly exempt. Since siman 205, seif 3, explains that a compromise has the law of a sale, the case is comparable to siman 185, seif 5. Even if the compromise did not arise from claims and counterclaims, but because of the debtor's poverty the creditor instructed the agent to compromise and waive the remainder, which is not a sale but a simple waiver, the debtor is still exempt if the agent told him that the creditor had authorized him to take a specified amount and waive the balance. The agent is believed as a third party entrusted with the matter, as is proven by Responsa Rosh cited by the Shach in siman 96, seif katan 5. These are his words. See there. Noda B'Yehudah, second edition, siman 35, was likewise asked about such a case and also ruled that the debtor is exempt. He writes concerning a debt document delivered by its owner to another to compromise for a specified amount, where the agent deviated and settled for less. Some judges wished to say that because the customary Polish promissory note requires neither writing nor delivery for transfer, the agent's act stands. Noda B'Yehudah responds: does the fact that no writing and delivery are required give one permission to steal? Suppose one stole a debt document and compromised with the debtor. If the creditor proves that it was stolen, the thief's compromise is plainly ineffective. It is true that whatever money the thief received would be credited to the debtor, because the market protection enactment applies, but the debtor would still have to pay the creditor the excess amount by which the debt exceeded the compromise. In the case before him, however, the debtor can say to the creditor, "I do not believe you that the agent deviated from your instructions." Even if the creditor had told the agent before witnesses how much he was authorized to accept, the debtor can say, "You later gave him permission to settle for less." Even if the agent himself admits that he deviated, he is not believed to impose liability on the debtor. See also Netivot HaMishpat, siman 182, seif 2, and siman 185, seif 5.
