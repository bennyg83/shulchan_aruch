They may send an authorization through a non-Jew, etc. This applies to a matter in which there is an effective kinyan, because the law of agency does not apply to a non-Jew, as Maharshal and the Shach write, and this is simple. Maharshal writes that one should not include in the authorization the phrase, "Whatever you undertake, etc.," because by doing so he makes him an agent. Yet according to what I wrote above in the name of the Rashba and Mordechai, that the arrangement is treated like a gift on condition that it be returned, the same device should work even with a non-Jew. The principal transfers ownership to him and writes, "Whatever you undertake," which merely sets an accompanying condition that the property be returned. This is simple and correct. The Shach raises a challenge to Rabbenu Tam's underlying ruling that chalipin is effective with a non-Jew. He argues that this is not implied by the Gemara. Moreover, why did the poskim labor to find other methods for transferring chametz on Pesach and the ear of a firstborn animal, rather than simply instructing that it be transferred to the non-Jew by chalipin? Rabbenu Tam's proof is from the verse, "from the money of his purchase," regarding a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew. The Gemara derives that such a servant is not acquired by chalipin. This implies that in other acquisitions a non-Jew can acquire by chalipin. The Shach responds that this is not a proof for two reasons. First, the verse "from the money of his purchase" does not refer only to a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew, but also to one sold to a Jew. Otherwise, from where would all the poskim know that a Hebrew servant is not acquired by chalipin even when sold to a Jew? Second, precisely because the Torah excluded chalipin for a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew, we can derive from that exclusion that all acquisitions by a non-Jew are not effected through chalipin. Rabbi Yosef similarly explains the baraita's derivation from "from the money of his purchase," that a Hebrew servant is not acquired with produce or utensils because their value is not fixed, and from this we derive the same rule for all acquisitions such as kiddushin, redemption of a firstborn son, and similar matters involving something of unfixed value. We do not say that a Hebrew servant is exceptional. So too, the exclusion of chalipin from a Hebrew servant could teach the general rule that chalipin does not effect a non-Jew's acquisitions. This is the essence of the Shach's argument against Rabbenu Tam. These arguments are not sufficient to undermine such great authorities. On the contrary, according to the Shach's latter argument, the verse "from the money of his purchase" is the source from which we would learn that chalipin does not work in a non-Jew's acquisitions. Without that verse, we would assume that chalipin does work, as the Shach himself states that the exclusion is what teaches otherwise. If so, Rabbenu Tam's ruling is actually compelled. We rule like Rav Nachman that produce does not effect chalipin. According to Rav Nachman, the Gemara expressly explains that "from the money of his purchase" is not interpreted to exclude chalipin, but rather to exclude produce and utensils that are worth less than a perutah even though they are significant, as the Gemara explains. The matter therefore returns to its original presumption, namely that chalipin is effective in acquisitions by a non-Jew, because the verse "from the money of his purchase" is used for a different derivation. The Shach's further argument, that "from the money of his purchase" must apply also to a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew, because otherwise from where would the poskim know that chalipin is ineffective even when a Hebrew servant is sold to a Jew, is likewise refuted. According to his own approach, the difficulty remains: in the Gemara's conclusion "from the money of his purchase" does not address chalipin at all, but according to Rav Nachman, who holds that produce does not effect chalipin, it is used for another derivation. We must therefore derive the rule concerning a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew from some other source. One can explain this according to Rashi's version, that "a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah." This creates a difficulty according to Levi, who holds that chalipin is performed with the utensil of the transferor. The Gemara's question would then be that a woman should be acquired by chalipin by giving the man a cloth. What difference does it make that the cloth is worth less than a perutah? On the contrary, if the purchaser takes something from her of small value and she does not consider it beneath her dignity to accept the arrangement, she certainly consents. The statement that "she does not consent to be acquired" cannot logically apply where she is the one giving the object to him. Thus according to Levi the question remains: why should she not be acquired by chalipin? We must say that Rashi holds that according to Levi, produce can serve for chalipin, and "from the money of his purchase" is then interpreted to exclude chalipin. That exclusion teaches that chalipin is absent from all acquisitions of a non-Jew. There is therefore no difficulty from learning from the field of Ephron, as Tosafot ask. But according to the Gemara's conclusion accepted by us, where produce does not effect chalipin and "from the money of his purchase" does not exclude chalipin as explained above, the Gemara properly asks that perhaps a woman should likewise be acquired by chalipin. According to our ruling that chalipin is performed with the utensil of the acquirer, the Gemara properly answers that a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah. This is precise. Admittedly, Tosafot in Kiddushin 3a, beginning "And a woman," do not appear to say exactly what I have written, that according to the initial understanding that "from the money of his purchase" excludes chalipin, we would say that all acquisitions of a non-Jew lack chalipin, as the Shach suggests. Nevertheless, Rabbenu Tam's ruling remains correct. Apart from the fact that Tosafot's wording does not expressly establish the Shach's reading, if that were Tosafot's intention their question would be difficult. Tosafot ask why a verse is needed to exclude chalipin for a Hebrew servant when one can learn from a woman that chalipin is ineffective, and if it does not work for a Jew it certainly should not work for a non-Jew, as the Maharsha explains. According to the Shach's reading, the verse is needed to exclude chalipin from all acquisitions of a non-Jew, not merely the acquisition of a Hebrew servant. This is not a decisive difficulty, because Tosafot later say that it would be strained for the Torah to write an exclusion in the section of a Hebrew servant solely to teach rules concerning other acquisitions when it is unnecessary for the servant himself. The wording of Tosafot, however, does not support the Shach's reading at all. Rather, it appears that Tosafot understand the verse to apply specifically to a Hebrew servant. One cannot say that it establishes a rule for all acquisitions of a non-Jew, because the verse does not apply only to a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew. It also addresses one sold to a Jew. Yet chalipin plainly works for a Jew in other types of acquisition, so how could the rule for a Hebrew servant serve as a source for all acquisitions? If one asks that if the verse also applies to a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew, how can Rabbenu Tam prove from the need to exclude chalipin that chalipin otherwise works for a non-Jew? Perhaps the exclusion is needed only for the Jewish purchaser, as the Shach argues. The answer is that if the exclusion had no relevance at all to a non-Jew and were needed only for a Jew, the Torah would not have placed it in the section concerning a servant sold to a non-Jew. Even if the verse can also be applied to a sale to a Jew, its natural location would have been in the section of a servant sold to a Jew. Its placement here demonstrates that the exclusion is also needed regarding a non-Jew, meaning that outside the special law of a Hebrew servant chalipin can be effective for him. We also cannot derive from this special exclusion that chalipin never works for a non-Jew, because the verse demonstrably addresses a Jewish purchaser too, and chalipin certainly works for Jews in other matters. Indeed, it seems that this itself is Tosafot's proof. The Mishnah states that a Hebrew servant is acquired by money and a document, and does not list chalipin. This implies, as is evident throughout the sugya, that a Hebrew servant is not acquired by chalipin even when sold to a Jew. The Shach asks from where this is known. If it came from some entirely different source, then according to the opinion that "from the money of his purchase" excludes chalipin, that exclusion would be unnecessary regarding a Jewish purchaser, because chalipin is already ineffective there, and all the more so should it be ineffective for a non-Jew. One cannot answer that the verse comes merely to teach that all acquisitions of a non-Jew do not work through chalipin. It is strained to say that the Torah placed an exclusion in the law of a Hebrew servant where it is wholly unnecessary, only for the sake of other acquisitions. More fundamentally, one cannot derive other acquisitions from a Hebrew servant, for we see that even for a Jew chalipin works in other matters and nevertheless does not acquire a Hebrew servant. We must therefore say that there is no independent exclusion from chalipin for a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew. Rather, his law is learned by a binyan av or other indication from the servant sold to a non-Jew, as the language of Tosafot itself indicates. This works only if chalipin is generally effective in other acquisitions of a non-Jew and is excluded specifically for a Hebrew servant. We can then extend that servant-specific rule to a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew. But if a non-Jew can never acquire anything by chalipin, how could we infer from that fact that a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew is not acquired by chalipin? In the non-Jew's case there would be no distinction at all between acquiring a Hebrew servant and acquiring any other property, while for a Jew chalipin works generally. Tosafot are therefore compelled to hold that chalipin does work for a non-Jew in other acquisitions and is excluded only for a Hebrew servant, allowing us to derive the law of a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew from it. That Tosafot understand "from the money of his purchase" as applying specifically to a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew, and not to one sold to a Jew, is also compelled by Tosafot in Bava Batra 54b, beginning "A non-Jew, from the time the money reaches him." They write: "Furthermore, it is taught there, 'from the money of his purchase,' he is acquired with money but not with produce and utensils, meaning chalipin. This implies that similarly he is not acquired by a document, for even all forms of money-equivalent would have been excluded from 'money' had Scripture not written 'he shall give.' But elsewhere this is not so." If the verse also governed a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew, how could a document be excluded? A Hebrew servant sold to a Jew is expressly acquired by a document. We must therefore say that this derivation concerns only one sold to a non-Jew. The fact that chalipin does not work for one sold to a Jew is learned from the one sold to a non-Jew. A document is different, because it cannot be learned across that comparison. The law of a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew is linked to a Hebrew maidservant, who in turn is linked to a woman, and a document is effective there. This also resolves another apparent difficulty. If Tosafot infer from the exclusion of chalipin for a Hebrew servant acquired by a non-Jew that chalipin generally exists for a non-Jew, why not similarly infer from the exclusion of a document that documents generally work for a non-Jew? Yet in that same passage Tosafot conclude that documents are not an acquisition method for a non-Jew even regarding land, because the verse concerning a deed of acquisition is written with respect to Jews. One might answer that Tosafot's proof is not from the verse itself but from the wording of the baraita. The baraita says, "from the money of his purchase, not with produce and utensils," which specifically denotes chalipin. Since the verse itself excludes every non-cash method, including a document, why did the baraita select produce and utensils? It could be because chalipin is generally available to non-Jews and is therefore a greater novelty to exclude for a Hebrew servant, whereas a document never works for them. But this explanation is strained and not especially indicated by Tosafot's wording. According to what I wrote, the matter is simple. Tosafot's entire proof is that if chalipin did not work for a non-Jew in other acquisitions, we could not derive from his inability to acquire a Hebrew servant by chalipin that a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew is likewise not acquired by chalipin. With a document, however, we indeed say that it does not work for a non-Jew in other acquisitions, because a Hebrew servant sold to a Jew is independently acquired by a document. Everything fits well. Tosafot ask why "from the money of his purchase" is needed to exclude chalipin when even for a Jew chalipin does not acquire a Hebrew servant by the comparison to a woman. They do not answer that it excludes documents, which do work for a Jewish purchaser of a Hebrew servant but not for a non-Jew. It is possible that their question is directed at the wording of the baraita: why did it formulate the exclusion as produce and utensils, meaning chalipin, a method already ineffective even for a Jew? It should instead have identified the novelty of a document, which is effective for a Jewish purchaser of a Hebrew servant but not for a non-Jew. The Gemara similarly asks according to the opinion that produce does not effect chalipin why the verse is needed, rather than answering that it excludes a document. Evidently the difficulty is with the baraita's formulation. In any event, there is no real difficulty, because a document is nowhere established as a mode of acquisition for a non-Jew and therefore needs no special verse to exclude it. Documents are mentioned by Scripture specifically among Jews, as Tosafot write. There is thus no need to force the explanation that Tosafot in Bava Batra wrote according to the Rashbam's initial view, that a non-Jew withdraws because all his acquisitions are by money, while under that initial view a document might have worked for him in a gift or another case where money has no role, so that a Hebrew servant required a special exclusion from "from the money of his purchase." According to Tosafot's ultimate conclusion that a document is never an acquisition mode for a non-Jew, they would then hold that the verse excludes only chalipin, which is why the Kiddushin passage does not mention exclusion of a document. According to what I have written, no such forced distinction is necessary. The Shach's answer to Tosafot's other question is, however, good. Tosafot ask how we could say that chalipin acquires a woman through the gezeirah shavah of "taking" from the field of Ephron if chalipin itself did not acquire Ephron's field. The Shach answers that the derivation is not literally from Ephron's field, but rather the Torah compares the acquisition of a woman to acquisition of a field in general. The Rashba in his chiddushim to Kiddushin explicitly writes this answer. Nevertheless, the Rashba concludes that Rabbenu Tam answered in Tosafot that chalipin does apply to a non-Jew; see there. Thus the Rashba concedes Rabbenu Tam's ruling. Indeed, Rabbenu Tam's explanation is more straightforward, because according to the Shach and Rashba one must still explain how the Gemara knew to ask that a woman should be acquired by chalipin. Perhaps the gezeirah shavah specifically compares her to Ephron's field, where chalipin did not work, rather than to fields in general. Yet I have a further difficulty. If Tosafot hold that we learn literally from the field of Ephron and that chalipin was applicable there, why did Tosafot labor, according to Rashi's interpretation, to ask that we should also derive that a document acquires a woman, making the verse "she shall leave and become" unnecessary? A document does not apply to Ephron's field, as Tosafot in Bava Batra expressly write and as cited above. How then can a document be derived? According to the Rashba's and Shach's understanding, Tosafot's question is clear, because we compare the acquisition of a woman to acquisition of a field generally, and a document does acquire fields. One must say that in truth the question from documents is not so strong. How could we derive that a document of kiddushin works from the acquisition of a field? In a field the seller gives the document to the purchaser, whereas in kiddushin the man, who is the acquirer, gives the document to the woman, who is acquired. See Kiddushin 9a, where the Gemara concludes that these are independent halachot, and see Tosafot there. We must therefore say that Tosafot's main question was from chazakah, and they mentioned a document only incidentally with the flow of language. Still, the matter requires study. I later saw in Atzmot Yosef that he leaves these two questions unresolved. Regarding the second question he strengthens it further from Tosafot's explicit later statement that we cannot derive a document for kiddushin by a kal vachomer from a Hebrew maidservant, because there the father writes the document whereas here the husband writes it. The second question can actually be answered. If we were seeking to derive by kal vachomer that a document should acquire a woman because it acquires a maidservant, Tosafot properly object that in the maidservant case the father writes the document. How can one infer that a document written by the husband should work? Here, by contrast, Scripture writes "when a man takes," and with respect to a field Scripture also uses the language of taking. Through the gezeirah shavah we learn that all acquisition methods included in the "taking" of a field are included in the "taking" of a woman. If the word "taking" in the field context includes a document, then "when a man takes" likewise includes a document. Once the meaning of "taking" has been established to include a document, we return to the immediate context of the verse, which says "when a man takes," meaning that he is the one who gives it, as the Gemara later explains. We are not deriving the mechanics of one case from the other, only the meaning of the word "taking." There it means money and a document, and here too "when he takes" means money and a document. We then apply the local context of each verse. This is precise. The first question, however, still appears difficult. The Maharsha already asks Tosafot's question why a separate verse is needed for a document if it can be learned from Ephron's field, since the Gemara ultimately says that a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah. It therefore appears that Tosafot's questions operate as alternatives: either the first question or the second. We asked above that according to Tosafot in Bava Batra, even a document is excluded by "from the money of his purchase." If so, just as Rabbenu Tam infers from the special exclusion of chalipin that chalipin must generally be effective in acquisitions of a non-Jew, we could infer from exclusion of a document that a document too generally works for a non-Jew. In truth there is no difficulty. The verse must exclude from a Hebrew servant sold to a non-Jew some acquisition method that works in other acquisitions of a non-Jew, otherwise the exclusion would be unnecessary. But it is enough that one such method exists. Thus either the verse excludes a document because documents generally work for a non-Jew, or it excludes chalipin because chalipin generally works for him. Rabbenu Tam finds it more reasonable that the verse excludes chalipin rather than a document, because he regards it as certain that a document never works for a non-Jew. The verse concerning a deed of acquisition is written with respect to Jews. The sugya in which a non-Jew's property becomes like ownerless property after payment likewise supports this, as Tosafot write in Kiddushin 14b, beginning "Since." Regarding Tosafot's question on Rashi, that if the only reason chalipin does not work for kiddushin is that a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah, why should that depend on her will, similar to the Gemara's later question regarding Beit Shammai, one can say that Rashi agrees with the force of this question. Rashi, however, holds that chalipin does not operate in acquisitions of a non-Jew, as appears from Tosafot's understanding of Rashi. The Gemara's question, "Perhaps so too," is therefore answered in the manner of the Rashba and Shach, namely that perhaps the gezeirah shavah compares a woman's acquisition to fields generally rather than to Ephron's specific field. Yet how do we know this? Particularly to rule leniently that a woman is not betrothed by chalipin at all and requires no get, perhaps the gezeirah shavah is from Ephron specifically and would not include chalipin. The Gemara answers that because both interpretive possibilities exist, and there is an independent reason that chalipin should not work, namely that a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah, it is more reasonable to interpret the gezeirah shavah from Ephron himself, where chalipin was absent, rather than from fields generally so as to create an acquisition by chalipin against that independent rationale. This is a satisfactory resolution. Tosafot's question does not arise, because the statement that a woman does not consent is not itself overriding an otherwise established acquisition. It merely determines which of two possible readings of the gezeirah shavah is preferable. According to this, Rashi holds that chalipin is ineffective for a non-Jew. We must then say that a document does work for a non-Jew, because otherwise what purpose would "from the money of his purchase" serve? It could not be needed to exclude chalipin, since chalipin is already absent from every acquisition by a non-Jew. Tosafot therefore properly ask, on this interpretation, why a separate verse is needed to establish acquisition of a woman by document. It should be learned from Ephron, because according to this approach a document necessarily can be used in a non-Jew's acquisition. This also answers the Maharsha's objection. We are presently assuming that the statement "a woman does not consent to be acquired for less than a perutah" serves only to choose the correct direction of the gezeirah shavah. If the gezeirah shavah itself clearly established an acquisition, her lack of subjective consent would not negate the Torah's acquisition mechanism. This is precise. In my chiddushim I discuss at length that if we learned literally from Ephron, even less than a perutah might suffice, because a descendant of Noach is liable for theft of less than a perutah, so for him such an amount constitutes money and could acquire a field. This is not the place for that discussion. However from all these discussions it appears that Rabbenu Tam did not build his ruling that chalipin works for a non-Jew upon this secondary resolution of Tosafot's question. His principal proof is the verse "from the money of his purchase," and, as we have clarified, that is a complete proof without a sufficient refutation. Rashi and Rashbam, however, interpret the statement in Kiddushin 14b that "all of a non-Jew's acquisitions are with money" to mean all of his acquisitions generally, implying that he has no acquisition by chalipin, since all his acquisitions are by money. I already used this to resolve Rashi against Tosafot's question. Rabbenu Tam follows his own view in Tosafot there, that "all his acquisitions are with money" does not refer to every type of property, but only to a Hebrew servant, concerning whom all his acquisition is by money. It therefore remains entirely consistent that in other matters even chalipin works. See the Rashba's chiddushim to Kiddushin, who asks against Rashi's explanation that every acquisition of a non-Jew is by money from the fact that chazakah also exists; see there. This demonstrates that Rashi understands "all his acquisitions are with money" as excluding all other acquisition methods. The Shach's objection from the methods used to transfer chametz on Pesach and the ear of a firstborn animal is now answered. In monetary matters, and especially in the technical context of authorization, we rely on Rabbenu Tam because his reasoning is compelling and many commentators agree with his reading that the statement does not mean every acquisition by a non-Jew is limited to money. But regarding prohibitions, the poskim in those areas labor to satisfy all opinions, as is evident from their discussions. They therefore also took into account Rashi and Rashbam and did not wish to rely solely on Rabbenu Tam. It also appears from here that acquisition by way of land, agav karka, does not work for a non-Jew. Otherwise why did Rabbenu Tam not advise, for an authorization through a non-Jew, that the claim be transferred agav land? That is a standard mode of transfer used in authorizations and is more effective for several matters for which a sudar is ineffective. Likewise, regarding chametz and firstborn animals, why did the authorities not advise transfer agav land? Evidently it is ineffective. Indeed, we do not find anywhere that agav karka works for a non-Jew. Tosafot write that a document does not work for them because the verse concerning a deed of acquisition is written regarding Jews. Similarly, the verse "and he gave them gifts," from which agav is derived, is written concerning Yehoshafat. Chalipin is different. Without specific proof we would indeed have assumed that it works for a non-Jew, and the verse "this was formerly the custom in Israel" does not refute Rabbenu Tam, despite Maharshal's formulation as though Rabbenu Tam contradicted Scripture. The verse means that this was the recognized custom among Israel. Non-Jews did not customarily use it because they did not know these civil laws. Therefore Scripture says, "this was formerly the custom in Israel," rather than simply, "this applies in Israel." According to this I initially thought there was an additional proof for Rabbenu Tam. The Shitah Mekubetzet cites a question in the chapter HaZahav: according to the opinion that movable property is not acquired by meshichah, how can a gift of movable property ever be acquired? Tosafot answer, as cited there and in Avodah Zarah, that a gift itself is not enough to create acquisition and the issue is undisputed. The Ramban answers that other acquisition methods exist, namely kinyan sudar, agav karka, and chatzer. The Ramban's words seem correct, for why should there be a distinction between gift and sale? Otherwise, why were all the poskim so concerned about transferring the ear of a firstborn animal without relying on meshichah? According to Rashi, who holds that meshichah does not acquire for a non-Jew, one could simply give him the ear as a gift. Meshichah would then work according to all views. The Jew does not need payment for it. According to this, however, a difficulty arises for Reish Lakish, who holds that a non-Jew acquires specifically with money. How can a non-Jew ever acquire a gift? Agav karka is unavailable to him, as explained above. Chatzer is likewise unavailable according to Reish Lakish, because Reish Lakish says in Bava Metzia 10b that a minor girl has no acquisition through chatzer, since chatzer is derived from agency and a minor lacks agency. The same should apply to a non-Jew, who likewise has no law of agency. By what method can he acquire a gift? We must therefore say, in accordance with Rabbenu Tam, that a non-Jew can acquire by chalipin. The conclusion is nearly compelled. It is therefore simple that one should not depart from Rabbenu Tam's ruling, and who would dare rule against him. Maharshal's extra formulation that Rabbenu Tam is a lone opinion was unnecessary, for Tosafot, the Rashba as cited above, the Mordechai, other authorities, the Tur, and the Shulchan Aruch all support his position.
