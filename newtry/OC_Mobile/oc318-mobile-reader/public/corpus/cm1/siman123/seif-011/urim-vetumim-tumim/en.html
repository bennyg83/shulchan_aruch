If he gave her a gift, she acquired it and the husband does not consume the produce. See Sema, who explains that if the husband consumed the produce, her benefit from the gift would not be recognizable at all. She would have no present produce, and perhaps she would die during her husband's lifetime, in which case he would inherit the corpus as well. According to this reasoning, if he authorized her to litigate over the principal but not the produce she could not litigate. Apart from the strained nature of this reasoning, as the Shach notes in seif katan 29, I do not understand it. Even in the case before us, according to the Sema the woman's present benefit is not recognizable. The produce generated by the gifted property does not belong to her for present consumption. Land is purchased with it, and the husband consumes the produce of that land. Thus throughout the husband's lifetime she derives no actual benefit from this produce, since the husband receives its benefit. Her only benefit is that if the husband dies first she retains the original gift and all the lands purchased with its produce. Yet according to the Sema, that future possibility is not called a sufficient benefit because it is uncertain, for if she dies first the husband inherits everything. If so, according to his own reasoning there is no difference whether the husband consumes the produce or not. The Shach seeks to explain that because the corpus of the land itself is not hers and she appears only by virtue of authorization, she cannot litigate over the produce. I do not understand this either, because we rule that one who has standing to litigate regarding part has standing regarding the whole. It must be that the Shach follows his own view, agreeing with Maharshal that the partial interest must itself have been transferred by a fully effective kinyan. He can therefore say that the Tur speaks even where the husband merely authorized her without an intrinsically effective kinyan. In that case the rule that standing regarding part extends to the whole does not apply, because even the partial share was not transferred by a fully effective kinyan. But according to what I wrote above, that the poskim do not appear to follow Maharshal, the Shach's explanation here cannot stand. In truth, there is no need for all this concern. The Tur and Shulchan Aruch simply state the actual law of a gift: she acquires it and the husband does not consume its produce. Since his control over the property has therefore been so extensively removed, why should he not be able to authorize her to litigate regarding it?
