To litigate with an absolute guarantor. This is specifically an absolute guarantor, because the lender may collect first from whichever party he chooses. The same would apply to an ordinary guarantor where the borrower has no means of payment. But where the borrower does have assets, the lender must first collect from the borrower, so the borrower cannot become an authorized representative to collect first from the guarantor. Therefore the text specifies an absolute guarantor, for whom the arrangement is valid in all circumstances. It also appears that where the borrower presently has nothing with which to pay, we accept the borrower's testimony against either an absolute guarantor or an ordinary guarantor in order to extract payment from the guarantor for the lender. We do not say that the borrower is disqualified as an interested witness merely because perhaps today or tomorrow he will acquire assets and the lender would then collect from him, giving him an interest in having the lender collect from the guarantor now. He gains nothing presently, because even if the guarantor pays now, the guarantor will return against the borrower if the borrower later acquires assets. It is strained to argue that the borrower prefers the guarantor because the second creditor is easier for him than the first. Moreover, this does not make him an interested witness. In Bava Batra, chapter Chezkat HaBatim, the Gemara asks in a comparable case why the witness should be considered interested if he presently has no land. Tosafot ask that there is nevertheless a future benefit, since if he later becomes wealthy he would thereby be exempt, and answer that perhaps such a remote future possibility does not disqualify him because he gains nothing now. Terumat HaDeshen siman 309 cites the Or Zarua and Hagahot Ashri as stating this answer definitively and rules accordingly. A similar principle is stated above in siman 37:10. Terumat HaDeshen derives that a benefit not presently available, but dependent only upon uncertain future circumstances, does not disqualify a witness. Accordingly, the Rambam's statement in Hilchot Edut that these matters depend upon the judge's understanding, and that if a witness has any possible benefit from his testimony, even remote and extraordinary, he may not testify, must refer to a remote benefit that is nevertheless presently available in some concrete way.
