It appears. The Sema asks why the Mechaber writes "it appears to me," since the Tur says the same thing: one who has witnesses supporting his claim should not appoint one of those witnesses as his representative. The Shach answers that some versions of the Tur read, "he should not appoint one of the witnesses' relatives," and Maharshal has that text and rules accordingly, while the witness himself is disqualified. As a matter of law, however, the Mechaber's ruling is primary, and Responsa Mahari Weil 119 appears to agree. That responsum further implies that if no compensation was initially fixed for the representative and the opposing party later seeks to disqualify him, he may then fix compensation and testify, because in monetary law an interested party can remove his interest, as stated in siman 37. Shach.
A kablan guarantor. The Sema writes that the same applies to an ordinary guarantor when the borrower has no assets with which to pay. If the borrower does have assets and the creditor must first collect from him, however, how can the borrower serve as representative to collect first from the guarantor? Therefore the text specifies a kablan guarantor, against whom direct collection is permitted in every case. It appears that when the borrower presently has no means of payment, his testimony against the guarantor is accepted and money may be extracted from the guarantor. We do not consider him interested merely because he thinks he may become wealthy tomorrow and then the creditor would collect from him, so he prefers collection from the guarantor now. Ultimately he gains nothing, because if he later has funds the guarantor will seek reimbursement from him. It is strained to say that he is interested because one creditor is easier for him than another. Tosafot in Bava Batra likewise write that possible future wealth does not disqualify a witness, and the Tur and Shulchan Aruch state a similar rule in siman 37, seif 10. See the Shach.
