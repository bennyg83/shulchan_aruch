He stipulated with him whether for improvement or impairment, etc. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 124, who writes that this wording is effective only when written in the first clause of the authorization, before the clause "go litigate and acquire," namely in the clause saying, "from now on your hand shall be as my hand," where he adds, "and your act shall be as my act, whether for improvement or impairment." In that location the language is effective to empower the agent even to waive the entire debt. But if this language is not written in the first clause and appears only later, after the phrase concerning whatever judgment is imposed upon the agent, or if it merely says "whether for benefit or detriment," it does not empower him to waive the entire debt. This remains so even if the document later adds, "and I will not be able to say that I sent you only to improve," etc. He discusses the reason at length and brings proof from the wording of the Mordechai. See there. He further writes that where the principal authorized the agent "whether for judgment or compromise," one can say that his intent was only a compromise issued by beit din, unless he expressly said that the agent may compromise on his own. So long as he did not say this explicitly, the principal may perhaps annul the compromise, because he can say that he authorized only a compromise by the judges, which would be close to the legal outcome according to his rights, but did not authorize the agent to compromise based solely on his own judgment. See there. Shaar Mishpat also cites this.
