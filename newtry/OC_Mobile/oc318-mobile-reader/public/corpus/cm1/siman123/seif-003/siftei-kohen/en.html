Sema explains that even without an express revocation, appointment of the second agent presumptively revokes the first, unless it is known that the principal intended whichever agent arrived first to collect. In my view, Tur means that even if the first authorization was revoked, so long as the defendant did not know of the revocation he may pay whichever agent he chooses. This is why Tur conditions the rule upon the defendant's knowledge. Baal HaTerumot states explicitly that authorization has only a limited rule of priority. If Levi was first authorized and afterward Yehudah, and Levi collected before the debtor learned that Levi's agency had been revoked, Levi acquired the money and Yehudah cannot claim again from the debtor. If the debtor knew of the revocation and both agents demand payment, he must pay Yehudah, not Levi. It also follows that if the debtor knowingly paid the first agent after revocation and the money was lost, he must pay again to the second agent or to the principal.
The defendant need not answer. If he litigated with the first agent and the plaintiff lost, the principal may reopen and overturn the judgment, as Beit Yosef writes. If the defendant was found liable, however, he cannot overturn the judgment by arguing that the agent's authority had been revoked and that the agent was therefore not his opposing litigant. One may acquire a benefit for another in his absence, and the defendant's liability is plainly beneficial to the principal. The agent was therefore properly treated as the opposing litigant. Mahari's ruling that one who intervenes beneficially on behalf of another may compel the defendant to litigate supports this all the more, since we know the principal will approve a claim that has already produced a favorable judgment.
