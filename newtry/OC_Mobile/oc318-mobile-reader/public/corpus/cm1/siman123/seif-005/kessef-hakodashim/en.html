If an authorized representative engages in verbal abuse, the opposing party may say that he is willing to litigate but not with a representative who insults and demeans him. The principal can appoint someone who will present the claim without abuse. The objecting party bears the burden of showing that the representative's conduct truly amounts to improper verbal oppression. The permission to appoint a representative in order to rescue an oppressed person appears to refer primarily to property that came into the wrongdoer's hands through a prohibited act. An ordinary debt does not automatically place the debtor in that category, for there are prohibitions against improperly pressing a debtor, and Scripture itself condemns oppressive collection. A third party also cannot know every hidden claim or possible old debt between the parties, and the debtor's avoidance may sometimes rest on a legitimate explanation. This resembles the rule against seizing for one creditor where others may be harmed. But where a litigant refuses to obey beit din, even an ordinary loan can become a case of rescuing the oppressed because the wrong lies in rebellion against the obligation to submit to lawful judgment.
