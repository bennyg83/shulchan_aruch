If he made a compromise with him, he accomplished nothing. Shaar Mishpat records a dispute among earlier sages concerning one who sent another with a debt document and authorized him to compromise with the debtor. The agent compromised and tore the document. The creditor later claimed that the agent deviated from his instructions, because he had authorized a compromise only down to a stated amount and the agent accepted less. Some wished to compare this to siman 182, seif 4, where an agent of a buyer errs. As long as the principal cannot prove that he appointed the agent without qualification, the seller may say that the agent stipulated that his acts would bind the principal whether beneficial or detrimental, and the burden of proof is on the principal. Here too, they argued, the debtor should be exempt because he can say to the creditor: I do not believe that your agent deviated from your instructions. Others rejected the comparison. There, as against the seller, the matter is analogous to one who says, "I do not know whether I ever became indebted to you," and he is exempt. Here, between creditor and debtor, the debtor is effectively saying, "I do not know whether I paid you," in which case he is liable. Shaar Mishpat concludes that if the compromise concerned an actual dispute between creditor and debtor, the debtor is certainly exempt. Siman 205, seif 3, establishes that a compromise has the legal character of a sale, and the case therefore resembles the agency rules in siman 185. Even if the compromise was not because of a substantive dispute, but merely because the debtor was poor and the creditor instructed the agent to accept a lesser sum and forgive the remainder, the debtor is still exempt if the agent represented that the creditor had authorized that amount. The agent is believed in his capacity as a stakeholder, as appears from Responsa Rosh cited by the Shach in siman 96, seif katan 5. Responsa Noda BiYehudah, second edition, siman 35, was asked a similar question and likewise exempted the debtor. He writes that although in Poland a transferable note may be assigned without formal writing and delivery, that does not give an agent authority to steal from the creditor. If a thief stole a debt document and compromised with the debtor, proof of the theft would invalidate the thief's act. Money actually paid to the thief might nevertheless be credited under the market-protection enactment, but the debtor would still owe the balance. In the case of an appointed agent, however, the debtor may say to the creditor: I do not believe that the agent exceeded his instructions. Even if the creditor originally told the agent before witnesses the minimum sum he could accept, the debtor may claim that the creditor later authorized a lower compromise. Even the agent's admission that he deviated from instructions does not impose liability on the debtor. See also Netivot HaMishpat, siman 182, seif 2, and siman 185, seif 5.
He stipulated with him, whether for benefit or for detriment, etc. Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 124, writes that this language is effective only when inserted in the first clause of the authorization, before the clause "go, litigate, and acquire," namely where the document first states, "from now on his hand shall be as my hand," and then adds that "his act shall be as my act, whether for benefit or detriment." In that setting the phrase empowers the agent even to forgive the entire debt. If, however, the phrase appears only later, after "whatever results to you from the law," or the document merely says "whether for benefit or for liability," it does not authorize complete forgiveness, even if it also adds that the principal will not be able to say that the agent was sent only to improve and not to impair. He explains the reason at length and brings proof from the Mordechai. He further writes that where the agent is authorized "whether for judgment or for compromise," the principal may have intended only a compromise proposed by the beit din, unless he expressly empowered the agent to compromise on his own initiative. A judicial compromise remains close to law and is shaped by the parties' legal rights, whereas a private compromise made solely at the agent's discretion may exceed the authorization. See there. Shaar Mishpat also cites this and remains uncertain regarding the point.
