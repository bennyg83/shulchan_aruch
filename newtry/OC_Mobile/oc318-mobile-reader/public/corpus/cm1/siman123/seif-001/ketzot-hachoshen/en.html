By strict Talmudic law, Nehardea said that one does not write an authorization over movables that were robbed, because a person cannot transfer or consecrate property that is not presently in his possession. The later version permits authorization where the defendant does not deny the claim. The authorization is not a simple ordinary acquisition. It combines agency with language giving the representative sufficient personal standing to litigate. Ramban explains that an ordinary agent cannot compel the defendant to litigate because the defendant can say, "You are not my litigant." An authorization therefore adds language such as, "Litigate, acquire, and extract for yourself." Even if the underlying debt or stolen property cannot presently be transferred in the ordinary way, so long as the principal has not retracted, the representative has enough personal interest to compel litigation. If he later actually obtains the property under the principal's continuing direction, his possession can complete the transfer. This analysis strongly supports Terumat HaDeshen's rule that later seizure can validate an attempted transfer even if the transferor did not know at the exact moment of seizure, provided he had not retracted. The authorization acts like a standing direction that becomes effective upon actual receipt. The first version of Nehardea, however, remains difficult. If even a deposit in another's hands is not presently acquired by the authorization document itself, why should authorization work for a deposit but not for stolen property? One answer is that an ordinary deposit remains capable of genuine transfer once received, so later receipt not only gives the representative standing but also discharges the custodian. With stolen property or a debt not presently transferable, the representative may gain standing through later possession, yet the original debtor or holder may remain exposed to a second claim from the principal if the initial transfer never truly passed the debt. This explains Rashi's statement that if the property is later lost from the representative through unavoidable accident, the principal may return and sue the original holder. The authorization did not fully transfer the principal obligation; it chiefly created standing for litigation. Under the later version, the authorization also contains ordinary agency, so delivery to the representative can discharge the holder. Rashba develops a different approach and suggests that under the first version even a written loan could not be authorized unless the owner could have consecrated it, because authorization was viewed as an imperfect acquisition. I find several parts of that approach difficult. If a debt instrument can be sold by Torah law through writing and delivery, it is not obvious why consecration should be weaker. Likewise, one generally cannot transfer property outside his possession, whether by sale, gift, abandonment, or consecration. The distinction between an intact deposit and stolen property therefore remains best explained through the different consequences of later receipt and discharge rather than by assuming a general power to transfer property outside one's possession.
One does not write an authorization over property in another's hands when the other party denies holding it. Yam Shel Shlomo explains that for stolen movables the authorization exists only by special enactment because the property is outside the owner's possession. Where the claim is denied, writing the authorization would appear false. Land is different because land is always deemed in its owner's legal possession, so authorization concerning land is valid by strict law and does not appear false. Rashi gives a related but somewhat different explanation. For denied movables there is doubt whether the claimed object even exists in the defendant's hands, so writing that the claimant "has" property with him appears false. Land, by contrast, is visibly in existence and is not described as being physically under the defendant's hand in the same sense. The original owner's legal relation to the land remains recognizable. Yam Shel Shlomo further criticizes Rambam for extending the no authorization rule to a denied deposit, arguing that a deposit remains in the depositor's possession like land. That criticism is incorrect. Once a deposit is denied, it has the same legal character as stolen property for this purpose. Rambam expressly rules elsewhere that a denied deposit is considered outside the owner's possession. Therefore Rambam's ruling is well founded.
