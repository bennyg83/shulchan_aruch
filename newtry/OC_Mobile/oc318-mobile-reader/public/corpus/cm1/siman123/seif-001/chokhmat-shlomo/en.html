Seif 14: They may send a power of attorney through a non-Jew. Note: See the authorities cited in seif katan 30 regarding authorization through a non-Jew. In my work on Hilchot Pesach, siman 448, I wrote in a responsum that even according to the Shach, the limitation is only that a non-Jew does not acquire from a Jew by chalipin, but a Jew certainly can acquire from a non-Jew by chalipin, by an a fortiori argument. See there. Regarding the basic view of Rabbeinu Tam that a non-Jew can acquire through chalipin, Pnei Yehoshua strongly questions him from the verse, “Formerly in Israel,” which describes acquisition through removal of a shoe. In my humble opinion, the opposite is true: Rabbeinu Tam’s position is supported by the Gemara in the fourth chapter of Bava Metzia. The Gemara says that since a Jew acquires from a non-Jew through one mode, a non-Jew likewise acquires from a Jew through one mode. This is difficult because there is also chalipin. Tosafot sensed the difficulty and explain that “one mode” means one mode apart from chalipin, but that reading is strained. If another mode exists, why describe the law as “one”? According to Rabbeinu Tam the wording is precise. The Gemara means that with respect to the modes in which Jew and non-Jew differ from one another, there is one distinctive mode on each side. In addition, there is a mode in which they do not differ, namely chalipin. Thus the passage itself supports the effectiveness of chalipin with a non-Jew. There is another proof from the second chapter of Pesachim. Concerning acquisition of a pledge, the Gemara distinguishes a Jew from a Jew because the verse says, “and to you it shall be righteousness,” whereas the same rule does not apply when a Jew deals with a non-Jew. This implies that only where a specific reason in the verse distinguishes Jewish parties do we create a difference. Where there is no reason to distinguish, monetary acquisition rules stated with respect to Jews can apply to non-Jews as well. Otherwise the Gemara would not need the special reason of “to you it shall be righteousness”; it could simply say that the Torah stated the law only for Jews. It follows that in ordinary monetary acquisition, even if the verse is expressed in terms of Israel, we do not distinguish a non-Jew unless there is a substantive reason. With chalipin there is no such reason, so it should apply to both. Nor can one answer that Pnei Yehoshua’s challenge is upon the Gemara itself: since the verse says “in Israel,” perhaps it expressly excludes a non-Jew. If the verse were presenting a legal command, that inference could be strong. But the verse is describing an earlier custom: “Formerly in Israel, concerning redemption and exchange, to confirm every matter a man removed his shoe.” It tells us how transactions were customarily completed among Israel. It does not state that the legal efficacy of the act exists only for Jews. It is therefore entirely possible that the same act is effective with a non-Jew as a matter of law even though the verse describes Israelite practice. Indeed, the word “formerly” itself seems otherwise unnecessary. The verse could have said simply, “This was in Israel.” It may teach that even in earlier times, before Israel received the Torah, they used chalipin. At that time they had the status of the descendants of Noach for these purposes, which indicates that chalipin was effective even then. This strongly supports Rabbeinu Tam. While considering this matter. There also appears to be a proof for the authorities who hold that a merchants’ commercial custom can create a binding mode of acquisition, and perhaps even on a Torah level. Why does the verse need to tell us that this was what people used to do? It could simply narrate that Boaz removed his shoe, from which we would know that the act was an acquisition. The emphasis that “formerly in Israel, to confirm every matter, a man removed his shoe” indicates that chalipin became legally effective because this was the established commercial practice. Thus commercial custom can generate a mode of acquisition, even with Torah force, and once the source of the acquisition is general custom there is still less reason to distinguish Jew from non-Jew. This also explains the word “formerly,” indicating that the practice existed before the Torah was given. With Hashem’s help, this provides a defense of the holy Rabbeinu Tam, whose name I am not worthy even to mention. May it be His will that Rabbeinu Tam advocate for us before our Father in Heaven, that He grant us a sign for good. Amen.
