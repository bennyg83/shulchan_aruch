Seif 5. (I sent you to improve matters for me.) If an authorized representative was empowered to collect a promissory note and returned the note upon receiving less than its full amount, the debtor may perhaps say that the representative was presumably authorized to compromise in that manner, leaving the agent responsible to the creditor for any unauthorized reduction. Even where the power of attorney contains language preventing the principal from saying, "I sent you to improve matters, not to impair them," the holder of the document is subject to the rule that ambiguous documentary language is construed against him. Such wording may have been intended only to strengthen the agent's standing in the lawsuit against the debtor. Even without such wording, the principal ordinarily bears the burden of proving that the agent deviated from his authority. Yet a presumption that an agent performs his mission is not conclusive proof for every purpose, particularly where a Torah prohibition or destruction of another's instrument is involved. An agent certainly may not destroy the principal's note merely on the basis of a doubtful inference about his authority. If the creditor himself returns the note after accepting less than the face amount without expressly stating that he waives the balance, one can argue that he retained the right to sue orally for the remainder. The same applies to related litigation expenses. Accordingly, even if the principal told the agent to surrender the note after receiving a reduced payment, the agent may not infer authority to waive the unpaid balance unless waiver was expressly authorized. If the debtor initially claims that he paid the full amount and it is later established that he paid less, it is doubtful whether he may then change his theory and claim that the agent waived the balance. Payment and waiver are related but distinct explanations, and the rules governing retraction from an earlier claim must be considered. An authorized representative who accepts by kinyan sudar that he will not sue or collect can generally retract, especially if he was never authorized to transfer, waive, or relinquish the principal's right. Even if he possessed such authority, a kinyan sudar merely to delay collection does not necessarily operate as a transfer or waiver. A representative of townspeople who negotiates with a governmental authority and reaches a compromise according to his reasonable assessment is not ordinarily liable merely because, before the dispute fully developed, he yielded somewhat to the stronger party. This is certainly so if several respected members of the town advised him to compromise in that manner.
