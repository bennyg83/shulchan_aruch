Under Talmudic law, they do not write an authorization, etc. The reason is that every authorization requires the claimant to transfer to the authorized representative the money or right being litigated, as explained in siman 122, where the document says, "Go litigate and recover for yourself." Therefore the subject must be something upon which a kinyan can take effect, such as existing land, a document representing a lien on land, or a deposit that was not given for expenditure and is not denied, so that wherever it is, it is treated as though still in the claimant's possession and can be transferred. A loan, by contrast, was given to be spent and is no longer physically present. Likewise, a deposit that the defendant has already denied cannot readily be transferred because the transfer appears false when the defendant says nothing is owed.
Through agav with land. Money cannot be acquired through chalipin, as explained below in siman 203.
He must write, "Acquire it itself and its lien," etc. Without this language the document itself cannot be transferred, as explained at the beginning of siman 66. We do not commonly write this formula in authorizations over our documents because our debt instruments customarily obligate the debtor to "you and those who come through you," and in such a case writing and delivery are unnecessary. It was also explained there that a document transferred through agav does not require writing and delivery, and our present practice is to use agav. Strictly, according to that route we would still need orally to say to the representative, "Acquire it and its lien," as the Tur cites from the Rosh, but that is not our custom. In any event, we are lenient to write an authorization even over an oral loan that the debtor denies, although ordinary acquisition and lien language do not readily apply and ma'amad sheloshtan is unavailable once the defendant denies the debt, except perhaps according to the Rosh and Tosafos, who hold that even an oral loan creates a Torah lien on land.
But the Geonim enacted, etc., whether by document, etc. The Tur likewise says this. It means that even if the debt instrument itself is not physically available for writing and delivery, the Geonic enactment permits authorization over it. I proved this interpretation against Beit Yosef.
But concerning an oral loan, etc. Since it is unusual for a person to lend without a document, the Geonim did not enact authorization for such an uncommon case, just as Chazal did not extend certain enactments to uncommon situations.
It is no worse than an agent appointed before witnesses. If the borrower or bailee pays him and the money is later lost or taken by force from the representative, the borrower is exempt, because the lender or depositor's writing at least directed the debtor to send the money through him. But he does not have the full legal force of an authorized representative to compel payment, because the defendant can say, "Perhaps your agency was revoked or your principal died." A complete authorization avoids that concern.
Some say an authorization may be written for everything, etc. Their reason is not merely to prevent someone from taking another's money and traveling abroad, for no special enactment was made for the uncommon case of an oral loan. Rather, Rabbeinu Tam and the Rosh hold that even under Talmudic practice, although the principal does not fully transfer ownership of the claim in such a case, he can still authorize the other person to litigate as his agent.
