He cannot delay. Tur cites an opinion that the local beit din should send its writing to the principal's beit din, impose a ban before him, and then allow the defendant to swear. Maharshal rules accordingly. The first opinion appears to apply even to a hesset oath, unlike Mabit's limitation to a Torah oath. Maharshal further writes that if the agent owns one half or one third of the claim, he cannot say, “The principal may waive the oath or refuse the ban, but I do not waive it as to my share.” His power is no stronger than the principal's, from whom it derives. The principal may even forgive the entire debt, including the agent's share, just as one who sells a promissory note may later forgive it. If the agent seeks reimbursement because his share was forgiven, the principal is generally exempt, though Baal HaTerumot and Mechaber rule that a donor may be liable. Even if the agent spent money traveling in reliance upon his share, he receives at most his actual expenses, not lost wages, which are merely indirect damage. If the subject is land or a deposit and the agent acquired the corpus of the property, the principal cannot forgive the agent's half or third. Nevertheless, the principal's testimony and admissions remain effective. The agent cannot reject them by saying that he trusts the principal only concerning the principal's own share, because the agent's entire standing derives from the principal. If the principal admits the deposit was paid, it follows that the agent acquired nothing. The agent may, however, impose a ban upon the principal to affirm that his admission is genuine and not collusive. Where the agent did not acquire the corpus, even if the instrument assigns him a half or third, authorization transfers nothing beyond the agency created by rabbinic enactment. The assignment is therefore like a sale or gift of part of a debt, which the principal may forgive. Maharshal's rule that the principal is believed to say the debt was paid applies specifically where the agent's share was written only within the authorization and the same document identifies him as agent for the remainder. If the principal separately gifted part of the debt or deposit through a valid acquisition and authorized him only as to the balance, the principal is not believed to say that the gifted part was paid. According to my ruling above, however, wherever the principal retains power to forgive he is also believed to say that the debt was paid. The exception is a deposit whose actual money was validly transferred to the agent.
