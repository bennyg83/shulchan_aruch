If an authorized representative accepted under ban that the principal would appear with the defendant in a certain city, and the principal later claims that appearing there is dangerous, Mahari Weil writes that we presume the principal never authorized the representative to bind him to enter danger. A general authorization is interpreted according to normal human intent and does not include exposing the principal to a known hazard. Mahari Weil also suggests that an agent may sometimes impose an oath or ban that binds the principal, drawing support from communal leaders who swore on behalf of the people. Yet Tosafot in Nazir, Mordechai, Ramban, and Ran indicate that vows and oaths generally cannot be undertaken through agency. This follows the broader rule that an act which cannot be performed through an agent cannot ordinarily support a condition dependent on agency. Ran likewise explains that abandonment cannot be performed through an agent because abandonment has the legal character of a vow. Ramban, citing Raavad, discusses communal oaths imposed by a prayer leader and notes a basis for stringency where the leader acts with the community's knowledge. Still, he rejects a general rule that one person can become sworn merely through another person's utterance, except in special categories such as the oath of testimony or where the listener expressly accepts. Accordingly, the case of communal leaders is best understood through the special authority of communal representatives. The leaders of a city can impose communal bans and enactments, sometimes even without each individual's separate agency. It does not prove a general law that private vows and oaths can be created through an ordinary agent.
