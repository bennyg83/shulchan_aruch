He cannot delay. See Baer Heitev, which cites the view that beit din should send their written demand, and concludes that Maharshal so ruled. Responsa VeShav HaKohen, siman 91, rules like the Shulchan Aruch. Shaar Mishpat writes that as a matter of practical halachah, although this is a legal doubt and a defendant is generally exempt from an oath in a doubtful case, here the requirement that the claimant first impose a general cherem is only a Geonic enactment, while the oath, even a rabbinic oath, rests on Talmudic law. A doubtful enactment cannot displace the certain law of the oath merely because the oath may facilitate collection. He qualifies this, however, to cases where the authorization itself is effective under Talmudic law. Where the authorization is valid only by Geonic enactment, as in the cases described in seif 1, or where movable property had already been denied and authorization is nevertheless customarily written, the oath too arises only through the enactment. In such a case the claimant may indeed have to impose the general cherem before the defendant is sworn. Perhaps even the Rambam would agree, because his reason for not delaying the oath is that a minor enactment should not suspend the substantive law. Where the authorization itself exists only by enactment, that rationale no longer applies. See there.
