With slaves he did not acquire the movables. Tosafot and Rosh explain that incidental acquisition with land is rabbinic, with the verse concerning fortified cities serving only as support. For biblical matters, a slave can be treated like land, but for this rabbinic acquisition the Sages did not extend the land status of slaves. This raises a difficulty from the law of second tithe. Bava Metzia says that one who lacks cash at the threshing floor can transfer produce to another and then redeem it on money at home. The Gemara asks why he does not transfer the produce incidentally with land. If incidental acquisition is only rabbinic, how can it affect the biblical requirement of adding a fifth in second tithe? The answer can follow Beit Yosef's rule concerning a three party arrangement used for kiddushin. Once the Sages validly transfer ownership, the Torah itself recognizes the resulting ownership because beit din has power to confiscate property. Thus a rabbinic acquisition can produce Torah level monetary ownership and thereby affect Torah consequences. Rashi's explanation in Gittin that a get can be acquired incidentally with land might seem to require incidental acquisition to be biblical, since divorce is a Torah matter. But even a rabbinic acquisition can affect kiddushin and divorce where the Sages have power over marital status. The general principle that one betroths subject to rabbinic conditions can allow the Sages to validate the resulting divorce framework. I discussed this at greater length in my Even HaEzer notes. Nimukei Yosef, however, has a different approach to the classification of slaves and can maintain that incidental acquisition itself is biblical.
Provided that it is tied. Beit Yosef cites Hagahot Maimoniyot as saying that sleeping is required only for a get, because divorce is detrimental to the woman and therefore requires a courtyard guarded for her. For an ordinary sale or gift, where another person transfers the property and the transaction benefits the recipient, even an awake slave who cannot walk can serve as a courtyard. The version of Hagahot Maimoniyot presently before us says the opposite, requiring both tied and sleeping. Perhaps Beit Yosef had a different text. In any event, the conceptual distinction accords with what I wrote in siman 200: where another person intentionally transfers the property, the usual requirement of guarding is greatly relaxed.
