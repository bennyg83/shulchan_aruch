Any amount of land. At the beginning of Perek HaZorek, a deathly ill man had written a get for his wife shortly before Shabbat. Rava advised that he transfer to her the place where the get lay and that she perform hazakah there. Rashi explains that he was giving her the courtyard with the get in it and relies on the rule that movables can be acquired incidentally with land. Ramban and Rashba object that if a get can be acquired incidentally with land, there was no reason to transfer specifically the place where the get lay, because incidental acquisition does not require the movables to be physically on the land. They therefore explain Rava through courtyard acquisition. Rashi can be defended from the rule that a get must create complete severance. Bava Metzia says that where a get is in the woman's hand but the husband still holds a cord attached to it and can pull it back, she is not divorced because the get remains tied to him. If the husband transferred some other parcel of land to her while the get remained physically in his own courtyard, then even if she acquired the paper incidentally with the other land, the get would still physically remain under his control and lack complete severance. Therefore Rava required him to transfer the very place where the get lay. Once both the get and its location pass to her, it is no longer attached to him. Even according to Rashi, however, a get cannot be acquired through sudar kinyan. The Gemara in Perek Mi SheMet treats a get as something not subject to ordinary kinyan in contrast to a deathly ill gift. The reason is that divorce requires "he shall give," while barter is the form of sale and exchange rather than giving. This parallels the explanation in Bava Metzia concerning Rabban Gamliel's tithes, where barter was not used because the Torah required giving. Thus a get can be connected to land by incidental acquisition while remaining excluded from sudar kinyan.
