And some say even if they are piled upon it. See Shach, seif katan 3, through his statement that a person's courtyard acquires for him only where the property is already in his courtyard, unlike here where the courtyard and the gift come to him simultaneously. At first glance this is astonishing. Bava Metzia 11 states that Rabban Gamliel conveyed property to Rabbi Yehoshua by saying, "and its place is rented to him," even though the courtyard and the gift came to him simultaneously. Likewise, Gittin repeatedly states that a get and the woman's courtyard can come to her simultaneously. Ketzot HaChoshen already raised this difficulty, and I later saw that Machaneh Ephraim, Hilchot Kinyan Chatzer, siman 17, likewise rejects the Shach's formulation on the basis of these proofs. To avoid attributing to our master the Shach an error concerning an explicit Mishnah, I believe his intent is as follows. If the movable property is in the courtyard in the seller's presence, or if the seller conveys the courtyard and movables together and the buyer acquires the land in the seller's presence, such as by payment or by chazakah before him, then the buyer certainly acquires the movable property as well. His courtyard is like his hand, and this is equivalent to drawing or lifting the movables in the seller's presence, where the seller need not expressly say "go, draw it and acquire it." The Shach is speaking of another case. The parties agreed upon prices for the land and the movable property, and the seller told the buyer, "go, take possession and acquire." If the movable property is piled upon the land and the seller also said that it should be acquired agav the land, then even if the buyer later performs chazakah outside the seller's presence he acquires the movable property, because the chazakah on the land is legally an act of acquisition for the movables as well. The seller's prior instruction to "go, take possession and acquire" therefore authorizes acquisition of both. According to the Mechaber, even without expressly saying agav, chazakah of the land acquires piled movables because the movables are treated as though attached to the land, as Sema, seif katan 3, explains. According to the Rama, however, if the seller did not say agav, even piled movables are not acquired through chazakah of the land. If the seller merely said "go, take possession and acquire" and the buyer then performs chazakah outside the seller's presence, the chazakah acts only upon the land. It is no act of acquisition for the movables. True, once the land becomes his courtyard, that courtyard is like his hand and can ordinarily acquire movables, but acquisition by meshichah or lifting outside the seller's presence requires the seller to have said "go, draw and acquire." Here he said only "take possession," language directed to the land. Since chazakah, absent agav, is not an acquisition act for the movables, that instruction does not authorize the courtyard to acquire them. This is the Shach's meaning when he says that a courtyard acquires only where the property was already in one's courtyard. He means that at the moment the seller conveys the movables, they must already be within a courtyard that functions as the buyer's hand in the seller's presence. Here the courtyard and the gift arise together outside the seller's presence, and the seller never gave the instruction necessary for an independent courtyard acquisition.
This appears to me the principal explanation. It follows that with ownerless property, where there is no other person's intent conveying title and no declaration of agav, movables are not acquired agav land merely by taking chazakah of the land. Ketzot HaChoshen writes in siman 275 that according to the Rambam, who does not require an express statement of agav when the movables are piled upon the land, a person who takes chazakah of ownerless land acquires the movables upon it as well. He derives this from Rashi in Bava Kamma 117 concerning one who robbed a field with a cow lying upon it, where Rashi explains the acquisition through agav. In my humble opinion this is not convincing. The verse from which agav is learned concerns a gift, where another person's intent conveys title. The Ran in Kiddushin 26 compares agav to the rule of one who sells ten fields in different places, where a single chazakah can acquire all because they are one transaction. That rule too depends on another party conveying title. Even according to our ruling that payment of the value of all fields is not required, it remains a special rule of a transaction. Since Scripture teaches agav in a setting where another person conveys title, what source extends it to ownerless property? Rashi's statement in the robbery case can be explained differently. He may mean acquisition through the robber's courtyard, for with respect to acquiring stolen property the robbed courtyard can still function as the robber's courtyard for the relevant liability. Alternatively, Rabbi Eliezer may hold that ordinary robbery causes the owner's despair, and Tosafot in Bava Kamma 69, s.v. Kol Sheliktu, write that despair functions like a gift to the robber. If so, another person's intent is effectively present. Even regarding the robber's acquisition solely for purposes of liability for unavoidable accidents, one can say that the owner himself prefers that the robber acquire responsibility, and this too is sufficient to regard it as acquisition with another person's conveying intent. None of these cases establishes acquisition of ownerless movables agav ownerless land.
