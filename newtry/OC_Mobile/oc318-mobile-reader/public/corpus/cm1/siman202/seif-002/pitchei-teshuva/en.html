He must say. See Baer Heitev and Noda BiYehudah, second edition, Orach Chaim, siman 63, who finds the Shach's ruling difficult. Rabban Gamliel transferred produce to Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Akiva in Maaser Sheni 5 by saying, “and its place is rented to him,” and according to some explanations in Bava Metzia 11b they acquired through the law of courtyard even though the acquisition of the place and the movable property occurred simultaneously. Rambam, Laws of Sale 3:7, likewise rules that one who rents the place where the sold movable property is situated acquires the property, as does Shulchan Aruch 198:5. Noda BiYehudah suggests a distinction: if the sole purpose of acquiring the place is to acquire the movable property, and he does not intend to hold the place as an independent acquisition, the movable property can be acquired. If, however, he intends to acquire both the land and the movable property as independent, complete acquisitions simultaneously, the Shach rules that the movable property is not acquired. A similar distinction appears in Shach 313:1. Dagul MeRevavah also suggests that the Sema's and Shach's question, why the movable property should not be acquired by the law of courtyard, can be answered if the place was conveyed only for a right of passage. Such a limited right does not function as a courtyard, as explained in siman 198, but the movable property may nevertheless be acquired agav the land. See Pnei Yehoshua to Kiddushin 27, Responsa Shemen Rokeach I, siman 74, Ketzot HaChoshen, Netivot HaMishpat, and the lengthy discussion in Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 66. Baer Heitev also refers to Shev Yaakov, siman 15, where a rabbi challenged Rambam's rule that where the movable property is physically located on the land one need not expressly say “agav,” from Kiddushin 27.
