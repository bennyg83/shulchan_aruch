Where one finds ownerless property and says, "I pull this animal in order to acquire it and the utensils upon it," Rosh rules that he acquires because, in a case of doubt without a prior owner, the first possessor is treated as holding. In a sale, by contrast, unresolved acquisition is left in the prior owner's possession. Rosh applies the same principle to the doubtful modes of leading a camel or pulling a donkey, and to an object thrown through a courtyard where the airspace is not destined to rest. In a sale or gift, the prior owner retains possession. With ownerless property, the first person is treated as possessor and a later seizure does not defeat him. Shach cites Rosh's discussion of one who made hazakah on one field of a deceased convert intending also to acquire another field or a boundary strip. Those questions remain unresolved, and Rosh says a second person who later makes hazakah can acquire because the first was never established in the other field. Shach distinguishes: there the first person never physically held the disputed field at all, whereas in the animal case he physically holds the animal and thereby can be treated as possessing the utensils. Maharam Mintz already raised and answered the same tension. My brother, in Kuntres HaSefeikot, nevertheless asked from the broader rule that possession without a valid kinyan is not considered legal possession. The answer requires distinguishing acquisition from evidentiary possession.
Acquisition and possession are not one concept. Sometimes possession is stronger than acquisition, and sometimes acquisition is stronger. For example, an unguarded courtyard does not acquire property unless its owner stands beside it, according to Rambam and the Shulchan Aruch. Yet for purposes of present possession in a monetary dispute, property located in a person's private domain can make him the possessor even if the courtyard would not acquire a new ownerless object. Bava Metzia repeatedly says to look in whose domain the disputed animal stands, and distinguishes only where it stands in a meadow or public domain. Even an unguarded private courtyard therefore supports possession. The reason is simple: acquisition requires a Torah or rabbinic mode such as hand or courtyard analogous to hand. Possession is a factual rule, "the one who suffers pain goes to the physician." If the object stands in Reuven's domain, the person seeking to remove it bears the burden of proof. Conversely, acquisition can sometimes be stronger than possession. Where a house belongs to Reuven and Shimon is merely staying there, Reuven remains the principal possessor of objects in the house, as Rivash and Rama rule. Yet if ownerless property enters that jointly used space under conditions enabling courtyard acquisition, both occupants can sometimes acquire equally. This resolves the found animal case. When the finder says, "I pull this animal to acquire it and the utensils on it," he acquires the animal itself. The animal then becomes his domain. Although a moving courtyard cannot acquire the utensils because it is not sufficiently analogous to a hand, the animal is still factually his domain and he is therefore the possessor of the utensils. His possession is enough to prevail in an unresolved doubt where there is no prior owner. That is why Rosh carefully says, "I pull the animal to acquire it and the utensils upon it." If he said only, "I pull the animal to acquire the utensils," without acquiring the animal itself, then the animal would not be his domain and there would be no possession. The remaining question whether the meshichah alone acquires the utensils would be unresolved, and a later finder could take them. Bach says there is no such distinction and that any doubtful meshichah makes the first person possessor. In my view Rosh's wording is exact. Possession arises because the animal itself became his domain. The same explains other doubtful cases. Even if a method of leading an animal is not its proper mode of acquisition, the person who physically controls it can still be its possessor. Likewise, an object thrown into the airspace of one's courtyard can be treated as within one's domain for possession even if that airspace does not complete acquisition because the object is not destined to rest. A difficulty remains in a sale. If the purchaser has physical possession even through a mode insufficient for acquisition, why does the law still leave the item with the original owner? The answer follows the rule that seizure with the owner's permission helps only when the seizure itself is fit to acquire. A buyer who receives an object from the seller through an uncertain and legally insufficient mode is not treated as one who has seized with the owner's authorization for ownership. The seller authorized a transfer only if the kinyan was legally effective. Therefore the purchaser's physical possession cannot defeat the seller's prior ownership presumption merely because of the unresolved acquisition.
Where one has seized with the owner's permission, the possession must indeed be of a type fit for acquisition. Terumat HaDeshen explains that seizure with permission works because the owner intentionally placed the property in the claimant's hands so that it should become his. If the owner handed it merely as a loan or deposit, that is not "seizure with permission" in the relevant sense. Accordingly, if the manner of holding was not a valid mode of acquisition, the owner cannot be said to have placed the property in the claimant's hands for the purpose of ownership. The claimant may physically hold the object, but against the owner's established prior title he is no stronger than a borrower or custodian. This is why an unresolved acquisition in a sale does not let the purchaser prevail through possession. Since the kinyan may be invalid, the seller's delivery did not necessarily create ownership and the purchaser's holding is not a recognized possession with the owner's permission for title. In ownerless property, by contrast, there is no prior owner whose title must be overcome. Factual possession itself can therefore decide the unresolved doubt, even if the manner of holding is not a valid mode of acquisition. This distinction resolves the apparent contradiction.
