They may testify. Baer Heitev notes that Rosh rules that even a distinguished person may testify, while Yam Shel Shlomo says the text contains an error. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 23, discusses the issue at length and concludes that the Gemara's unresolved question concerns a person distinguished only in the eyes of government officials, who trust him like two witnesses, but who is not similarly established among Jews. If, however, he is also publicly known among us as righteous and is trusted like two witnesses, then although a Torah court could not extract money solely on his word, his testimony could at least undermine a document, no worse than the daughter of Rav Chisda in Ketubot 85a. Therefore, when beit din hears such a righteous person contradict an ordinary man and knows with certainty that the truth is with the righteous person, his testimony merely prevents the ordinary man from profiting by evading a true debt. Combined with concern for desecration of Hashem's name among the nations, a distinguished person should certainly go and testify, and in my view no one would disagree. If their law requires the witness to swear, he should swear truthfully and state the truth. If the secular court then imposes more on the Jew than was actually due, the witness must reimburse the excess from his own funds, because although a person may save himself by using another's property when necessary, he must repay the resulting loss. If, Heaven forbid, the testimony creates a danger of imprisonment, the entire community is obligated to redeem the persecuted captive, and certainly they must intervene early to prevent matters from reaching that point. See Responsa Rama, siman 88.
