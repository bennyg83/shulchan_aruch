Anyone who knows, etc. See Urim for Sema's discussion of one who accepted a confidence not to reveal information. If the restriction was merely an ordinary verbal promise of secrecy, then certainly Mahari Weil did not mean that the witness can permanently suppress testimony. The person who imposed the confidence should release him. If that person refuses or is unavailable, beit din can order the witness to testify. A private promise cannot empower him to suppress testimony needed by beit din. But if he actually swore a valid oath not to reveal the information to anyone, the matter is more complex. If he is only one witness and swore not to testify, the oath takes effect. A single witness who fails to testify does not violate the Torah prohibition of "if he does not tell," as Bava Kamma 56 explains. He may have a Heavenly obligation because his testimony can help, but this is not the direct mitzvah whose violation would make the oath one taken to nullify a mitzvah. Indeed this is even weaker than the case of half a shiur, which is prohibited by Torah law and yet Ran and many authorities still do not always classify it as a classic oath to nullify a mitzvah. The single witness therefore needs regret and formal release from the oath. If there are two witnesses and a person swore specifically not to testify, then the oath is an oath to nullify a mitzvah and does not take effect. But if he swore more broadly, "I will not reveal this matter to any person," thereby including ordinary people as well as beit din, then the oath takes effect at least regarding disclosure to other people. The question is whether, through the principle of kolel, the oath also extends to testimony before beit din. This depends on the two approaches of Tosafot in Shevuot 24 concerning an oath that broadly includes permitted matters together with an explicit failure to perform a mitzvah in a passive form. See there, and see what I wrote above in siman 73.
And his fellow benefits from his testimony. Sema writes that if witnesses heard a claimant say, "You owe me a maneh," and the defendant answered, "Yes," they are still not necessarily obligated to testify. The reason, according to Sema, is that the defendant can later say, "I was joking with you." And if the only benefit of the testimony would be to impose an oath, then, he says, even without witnesses the defendant would have to swear if he denied the claim. I do not understand this. If the claimant sues on the basis of these witnesses, then even if the defendant is believed to say, "I never admitted a debt," and is not made into an established denier, he can still be required to swear that he owes nothing, as Bach writes explicitly in siman 32. Without the witnesses, however, if the claimant himself does not have a definite claim, there may be no oath at all. The witnesses therefore provide substantial benefit: their testimony can create an oath obligation just as one witness can. This is clear. See also what I write at length below in siman 81. Another point requires explanation. Why does the law specify that the witness must be "fit to testify"? If this merely excludes relatives and other disqualified witnesses, that seems redundant, because their testimony ordinarily provides no legal benefit anyway. One might answer that their testimony can sometimes help indirectly, but that is forced. Rather, the phrase teaches a distinct rule. Suppose the parties explicitly accepted a relative or otherwise disqualified person as a witness. His testimony can now have practical legal force just like that of a valid witness. Nevertheless, because Torah law does not classify him as a true witness, he is not personally obligated by the Torah duty to testify. If he wishes, he may decline, and he is not included in the punishment for suppressing testimony. This is explicit in Yerushalmi Sanhedrin, chapter 1, halachah 10, and similarly in Shevuot: if one says to a single witness, "You are accepted by us as two," one might think he is thereby obligated to testify. Scripture says "and he saw or knew," referring to one who is intrinsically fit for Torah testimony. Ran, citing Ramban, explains the Mishnah similarly. The Mishnah says that where the knowledge is through another witness or through a relative or disqualified person, the witness is exempt. Ramban asks: why mention relative or disqualified person? That was already taught earlier. He answers that the novelty is even where the parties accepted the relative or disqualified person. Despite the practical force of his testimony, he is not included in the Torah obligation to testify. Ramban proves this from the Yerushalmi above. This entire discussion was overlooked by the author of Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 146, who entered into lengthy argument over a matter already explicit in the early sources. See also Tosafot Yom Tov on the Mishnah.
Whether he is alone. Tur gives the reason that just as two witnesses can obligate payment, one witness can obligate an oath, and perhaps the defendant will choose to pay rather than swear. According to this, one might say that if the claimant demands one hundred, the defendant admits fifty, and a single witness knows that the full hundred is owed, perhaps the witness is not obligated to testify. The defendant already owes the oath of one who admits part of the claim concerning the remaining fifty, so the witness adds nothing. Kenesset HaGedolah cites R. Avraham ben Chaim that where an oath would be required even without the testimony, the witness is not obligated to testify. But according to our halachah, where we impose a shevuat hesset even without a witness, this would lead to a difficulty. If the defendant is prepared to swear falsely, why should the presence of one witness change anything? It is strained to say that an oath taken while holding a sacred object is psychologically more severe and therefore more likely to produce payment. The original baraita proves only that in the era before shevuat hesset, suppressing a single witness could eliminate any oath obligation entirely. One could therefore establish the case where the claimant's entire definite claim depends on the witness. Without the witness, the claimant has no bari claim and no oath can be imposed. Then the witness clearly has a duty to testify. Yet this restriction is also difficult, because the poskim should have stated such a significant limitation explicitly. It therefore appears that even where some oath obligation exists independently, the witness must still testify. A person is embarrassed to swear publicly against a witness who directly contradicts him, because he appears as a liar before the community. This is especially true according to the authorities below, siman 34, who hold that if the defendant swears against the witness and afterward the litigant joins with that witness, the two can combine to disqualify him from testimony and from future oaths. Because of that risk, the defendant may refrain from swearing and instead pay. The witness's testimony therefore creates real practical benefit even where another oath would otherwise exist. Accordingly, the ruling of R. Avraham ben Chaim is not accepted. The single witness remains obligated to testify.
And he is liable before Heaven. Shach cites Maharshal that if the injured party seizes property from the person who is liable only by the laws of Heaven, beit din removes it from him. Maharshal distinguishes the rule from cases of kim lei bid'rabbah minei, where Rashi writes that if the injured party seized, we do not remove it. There, the monetary obligation exists in principle, but beit din does not impose it because one does not receive two punishments. In ordinary cases of liability only before Heaven, however, seizure does not help. Maharshal seeks proof from the beginning of Perek HaKones. The Gemara says that one who makes another person's property hidden from a fire is liable before Heaven. Tosafot ask that Heaven knows his true intention and answer that even if he genuinely intended to protect the property from fire, he is still liable before Heaven because he should have anticipated the resulting loss. Maharshal asks: why not say the Gemara's novelty is that if the injured party seizes, we do not remove it? The Tosafot discussion would then be unnecessary. He concludes that seizure must not help. I do not understand this proof. According to Tosafot's initial thought, if the person truly acted in order to save the property from the fire, he is not liable even before Heaven. Suppose the injured party seizes property from him and the defendant says with certainty, "I acted only to save your property and owe you nothing even before Heaven." The claimant merely doubts the intent. How could uncertain seizure defeat the present possessor's definite claim where the supposed Heavenly liability itself is uncertain? The rule that bari does not prevail over shema against an established possessor certainly does not give an uncertain claimant authority to seize. Indeed, the claimant may not know at all whether the defendant's intention was harmful. It would be extraordinary to allow someone who says, "I do not know whether you owe me," to seize from one who says, "I certainly owe you nothing." Thus Tosafot's question proves nothing concerning a case where liability before Heaven is definite. Their answer establishes that even with a protective intention the Heavenly liability remains. Once liability is definite, the separate seizure question remains open. Shevut Yaakov, part 1, responsum 146, adopts Maharshal's supposed proof but nevertheless distinguishes. He says that where the Heavenly obligation itself arises from Torah law, such as two witnesses who suppress testimony and thereby violate a direct Torah duty, if the injured party seizes, we do not remove it. I do not accept his distinction. Maharshal's proof, as shown, is not a proof at all. But in practical law I agree with the outcome that outside kim lei bid'rabbah minei, seizure generally does not help. Rivash, responsum 392, cites Ramban explicitly that seizure does not help for an obligation only before Heaven, and Rivash agrees. There is also support from Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma. Rashba proves that one who admits a fine is exempt even from a Heavenly obligation from the incident where Rabban Gamliel knocked out the tooth of his slave Tavi and R. Yehoshua told him that his admission to a fine exempted him. Rashba says: if a Heavenly obligation remained, Rabban Gamliel should have freed Tavi voluntarily. But he could have made an even stronger proof. A slave in that circumstance is effectively in possession of himself, as Raavad explains, and Rashba himself cites that principle elsewhere. If seizure helped for a Heavenly obligation, Tavi should already have been able to retain his freedom. The fact Rashba argues only from a voluntary duty shows that possession or seizure does not create enforceable ownership merely from a Heavenly obligation. Shevut Yaakov further claims that Rashi and Or Zarua, as quoted by Maharshal, say generally that where one is liable before Heaven and the injured party seized, we do not remove it. But that is not their language. Rashi and Hagahot HaRosh cite Or Zarua specifically in the context of kim lei bid'rabbah minei. Maharshal himself adds the explanation that the reason is the remaining Heavenly obligation. Shevut Yaakov built too much on words that are Maharshal's gloss rather than the words of Rashi or Or Zarua. There is another difficulty. Tosafot and Rashba in Bava Kamma and Sanhedrin discuss the case where sheep stolen during a burglary were later returned to Rava. Rava refused to accept them because Rav had ruled that a burglar in that circumstance acquires even unbroken property because he has no blood-price liability. Tosafot explain that the thieves returned them by mistake, thinking they were legally obligated to do so. If seizure ordinarily helped whenever there remained a Heavenly obligation under kim lei bid'rabbah minei, then once the animals came into Rava's hands, why should he return them? This is stronger than ordinary seizure. The very objects that had belonged to him came back into his possession voluntarily. One could force the Sanhedrin version to mean that before actually giving the animals, the thieves asked Rava whether they had to return them, and after he refused to state that they were obligated they did not hand them over. But Tosafot in Bava Kamma and Rashba's language sound as though the animals were actually returned and the return was simply mistaken. Perhaps the answer is that Rava was concerned with Rav's own position. According to Rav, even the intact animals themselves are legally acquired by the burglar because his life was forfeitable at the time of the burglary. That theory may imply that even later seizure by the former owner cannot help, since Rav does not regard the objects as remaining within the owner's property. Our final halachah does not follow Rav in that broad respect. Where the objects remain intact, they must be returned. Therefore the special seizure rule in kim lei bid'rabbah minei can still be true under our halachah even if Rava, out of deference to Rav's teaching, refused to retain them. The matter still requires study. Maharshal also limits the rule that seizure helps under kim lei bid'rabbah minei to a case where the offender was not actually executed or punished, such as inadvertent conduct or contemporary times. I do not understand that limitation. Rashi states the rule in the case of one who muzzles an animal while threshing, where the offender receives lashes and nevertheless must pay at least before Heaven. The Gemara speaks of an intentional act with warning. Hagahot HaRosh likewise applies the seizure rule in the very case where lashes are actually administered. Thus seizure helps under kim lei bid'rabbah minei even where the more severe punishment is genuinely imposed. For practical law, therefore, I conclude: In cases of kim lei bid'rabbah minei, seizure can be effective even where the offender acted intentionally and received the greater punishment. In other cases where the obligation is only before Heaven, seizure does not create a right and beit din removes the property from the seizer. Chavot Yair, responsum 44, likewise accepts this distinction. There is further proof for Maharshal's distinction. The Torah calls payment given to a forbidden sexual partner an etnan even where the relation was with one's mother, because although beit din cannot impose payment due to the capital offense, the monetary obligation remains before Heaven. Yet in Avodah Zarah the Gemara asks why a gift delivered only after relations counts as etnan if the woman had not acquired the money beforehand because acquisition of movables requires meshichah. Tosafot note that although Torah law may regard money as acquiring, rabbinic law can uproot the acquisition. But even where formal acquisition failed, there is still a strong moral obligation to honor one's agreement, with the curse of "He who exacted punishment from the generation of the Flood" hanging over one who retracts. If every serious Heavenly obligation gave the same seizure-status as kim lei bid'rabbah minei, the gift should still count as etnan. Necessarily the case of a capital offense is different. There the underlying debt is fully created by law and beit din merely withholds collection because the greater punishment supersedes the lesser. In an ordinary case where the law never creates a collectible debt, even a serious moral or Heavenly obligation does not give the same status. Maharshal's distinction is therefore compelled.
