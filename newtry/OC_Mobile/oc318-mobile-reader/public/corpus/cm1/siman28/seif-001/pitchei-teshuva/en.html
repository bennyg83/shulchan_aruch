And he is fit to testify about it. Baer Heitev explains that this excludes a relative or otherwise disqualified witness. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 146, cites Halachot Ketanot, siman 120, that even if the parties accept such a person and validate him as a witness, he is not obligated under the law of withholding testimony, because he was unfit to testify at the time he saw the event. Shevut Yaakov disagrees and holds that once accepted he is considered fit to testify and is also subject to punishment for failing to testify because he thereby causes monetary loss. Tumim, seif katan 2, objects that Shevut Yaakov overlooked the Yerushalmi in the first chapter of Sanhedrin and Ran in the name of Ramban in the chapter Shevuat HaEdut, also cited by Tosafot Yom Tov, which explicitly state that even a relative or disqualified witness whom the parties accepted is not included in the obligation to testify. As to the Yerushalmi, Shevut Yaakov himself later noted it in the addenda to volume 2 and wrote that its case of one witness accepted as two does not prove the point because it conflicts with the apparent meaning of the Bavli, which expounds that verse differently. Tumim's objection from Ran in the name of Ramban, however, remains strong. Birkei Yosef, letter 2, discusses the issue at length and adds that Rashba and Ritva likewise support Ramban. Although Tosafot, Agudah, Rambam, and Bartenura explain the mishna differently, he concludes that this is only an interpretive dispute; as practical halachah all agree with Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, and Ran. See Responsa Rabbi Akiva Eiger, siman 179, and Parashat Derachim, Derech Mitzvotecha on Vayikra. Netivot HaMishpat writes that even though a relative or disqualified witness accepted by the parties does not violate 'if he does not tell' by remaining silent, he nevertheless has a biblical obligation to testify under the mitzvah of returning another's lost property.
