And he is fit to testify. This excludes a relative or otherwise disqualified witness. Even if the parties accepted a relative or disqualified person as a witness, he does not violate the Torah prohibition of withholding testimony if he refuses. Nevertheless he must testify because of the obligation to restore another's loss. See Netivot, seif katan 1. The Sema writes that if a witness says he accepted the matter as confidential, the litigant should release him from the confidence. If he swore not to testify and he is a single witness, whose obligation is only rabbinic, the oath takes effect and requires regret and annulment. If there are two witnesses who are obligated by Torah law, the oath requires no annulment because it is an oath to nullify a mitzvah. If he swore not to disclose the matter to anyone at all, then testimony before beit din is included in a broader oath. Whether such an inclusive oath takes effect against a mitzvah performed passively depends on the known dispute. See siman 73.
There is benefit in his testimony. This excludes an admission made before them without a claim, where the person can say it was made only to avoid appearing wealthy, and even an admission in response to a claim where he can say he was joking, so the testimony has no legal benefit. If the claimant knows of the admission only through the witnesses, however, they must testify because their testimony can obligate the defendant to swear. If a witness knows himself to be wicked and legally disqualified, but his disqualification is unknown to beit din and his testimony would be accepted, he must testify. If he knows himself to be a relative, he is exempt from testifying. Urim VeTumim. But if he knows himself to have a financial interest in the matter, he should not testify, because this resembles the rule cited by the Shach in siman 75 from the beginning of Shevuot HaDayanim.
