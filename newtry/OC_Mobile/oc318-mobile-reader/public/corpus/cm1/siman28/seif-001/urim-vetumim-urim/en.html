For example, if a debtor merely said, "I owe such and such person," without saying, "You are my witnesses," he can later claim that he made the admission only so people would not think him wealthy. He is obligated to testify. Chavot Yair questions a witness who privately knows that he is disqualified, whether because of kinship, personal interest, or transgression. Must he nevertheless come forward? If he is disqualified by kinship, he certainly should not testify as a valid witness. He should either refrain or come before beit din and disclose, "I am a relative." See the next discussion concerning whether communal bans include relatives. If his disqualification is because of his own transgression and no one else knows of it, a person does not disqualify himself by his own confession. More importantly, the reason a wicked person is disqualified is suspicion that he may lie, not an unexplained scriptural decree like kinship. If he himself knows that he is telling the truth, there is no reason for him to withhold true testimony solely because of a private sin unknown to the court. The same applies to disqualification through personal interest. The concern is that interest may lead a person to lie. If he knows with certainty that his testimony is true, why should he suppress the truth? See below in siman 37 concerning whether an interested party can testify to another's detriment.
