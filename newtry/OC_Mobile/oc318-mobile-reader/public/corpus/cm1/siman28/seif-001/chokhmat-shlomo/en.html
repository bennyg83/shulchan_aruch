Seif 1. “Anyone who knows testimony for his fellow, whether another witness is with him or he is alone, and he withholds his testimony,” etc. Note: The Sema writes that this is learned from the verse, “If he does not testify, he shall bear his sin.” At first glance I have a serious difficulty with the Tur, Shulchan Aruch, and the authorities who equate one witness with two. In Bava Kamma, chapter HaKones, the Gemara teaches that one who suppresses testimony is exempt under human law but liable under the laws of Heaven. The Gemara asks: is this only a heavenly liability? Is it not a Torah obligation from the verse “If he does not testify, he shall bear his sin”? The Gemara answers that the case concerns a single witness. This seems to prove that the verse does not apply to a single witness. If it did, the original question would remain. Yet our authorities imply that one witness and two witnesses are governed alike. The same difficulty appears below in siman 29, where a single witness is said to be unable to retract his testimony. That rule is learned from the same verse: once he has testified, he may not return and testify differently. If the verse, as the Gemara indicates, speaks specifically of two witnesses, since wherever the Torah says “witness” it may mean two, then perhaps the rule of irrevocability should likewise apply only to two, not to one. A single witness who is not included in the prohibition of withholding testimony should perhaps be able to retract. This is especially plausible where he initially said, “I do not know testimony for you,” since he may simply have been unwilling to testify then and now is telling the truth. Yet all the authorities appear to say otherwise. This requires substantial study. Ketzot HaChoshen also cites the Yerushalmi and Ran in a manner that supports the conclusion that the verse does not apply to one witness. There. “There is no difference whether he is alone.” Urim VeTumim suggests that nowadays, since one who denies is in any event liable to a rabbinic hesset oath, a single witness may not be obligated to testify, because his testimony merely changes the type of oath. If the defendant will not swear falsely, he will also not swear a false hesset oath. Urim VeTumim later rejects this, but he does maintain that where the defendant is already obligated in a Torah oath because he admitted part of the claim, the single witness need not testify, since his testimony would add nothing. In my view this is not correct. Even in the first case there is a major difference: a hesset oath can be reversed onto the claimant, whereas a Torah oath cannot. The defendant may be unwilling to swear falsely himself but willing to say to the claimant, “Swear and collect.” The claimant may in turn be unwilling to swear even truthfully. If the witness testifies, the defendant becomes subject to a Torah oath which he cannot reverse. He must either swear or pay. If he pays and later brings a claim against the claimant, the witness may also function as a supporting witness and exempt the claimant from a hesset oath. Thus the testimony has clear monetary significance. Even where the defendant is already subject to a Torah oath because of partial admission, the single witness can matter. The defendant may later produce one witness supporting his own version. Without the first witness, the supporting witness might exempt him from the oath. If the claimant’s witness testifies, the two single witnesses oppose each other and neutralize one another, leaving the defendant obligated in the Torah oath because of his partial admission, as explained by the Shach in siman 87. Therefore the witness has a practical effect and is obligated to testify. The later authorities cite Bava Kamma 56, which appears to show that the verse “If he does not testify” does not apply to one witness. I saw a work quoting Sefer Yereim, siman 243, who expresses doubt whether the verse applies to one witness, and the author wondered how he could doubt an explicit Gemara. In my view there is no difficulty for two reasons. First, one witness can create an obligation to swear, and an oath can lead to money, since the defendant may prefer to pay. The Rosh writes that an oath can lead to monetary consequences, and the Shach in siman 386 cites Baal HaTerumot that indirect causation of money and dina degarmi are closely related. According to the view that indirect monetary causation is treated as monetary damage, the single witness may indeed have the Torah obligation to testify. The baraita in Bava Kamma can be understood according to the opinion that such causation is not treated as money. Second, when a single witness testifies and the defendant says, “I do not know,” the rule can be that one who cannot swear must pay. Thus the witness may directly produce payment. The Gemara’s discussion may follow opinions that do not accept that rule, or the baraita can be placed in cases such as slaves, documents, land, or heirs, where the rule does not apply. Therefore there remains room to say that according to our halachah even one witness is included in the obligation to testify.

Urim VeTumim and Ketzot HaChoshen discuss at length whether one who is exempt from payment because the greater punishment applies nevertheless remains obligated under the laws of Heaven, and whether seizure is effective in such a case. I believe there is proof from Rambam, Hilchot Meilah 1:3, who writes that one who misappropriates consecrated property intentionally receives lashes and pays. The gloss there asks how he can both receive lashes and pay, when the Rambam elsewhere applies the rule that one does not receive both. I suggest that the Rambam holds that seizure is effective where the greater punishment exempts the person from human payment but the heavenly obligation remains. Once the claimant has validly seized the money, if the defendant takes it back he must restore it by law, since the seizure created ownership. Consecrated property is considered continuously in the possession of Heaven. Scripture and the Sages treat hekdesh as especially strong in possession, and we say that consecration can uproot certain liens and that the hand of hekdesh has the advantage. In Bava Metzia, consecrating property is compared to physically seizing it. Hekdesh is therefore regarded as already holding the payment. The exemption of the greater punishment does not prevent the consecrated estate from retaining what is due. This does not contradict Pesachim 29 concerning one who eats consecrated chametz, where one opinion exempts him from meilah because karet exempts from payment. The rule that one remains liable under the laws of Heaven, and that seizure can be effective, applies where the underlying monetary obligation exists independently and the greater punishment merely blocks collection in human court. But a special addition such as the fifth paid in meilah exists only because the Torah created that specific sacred obligation. If the greater punishment prevents the special liability itself, there is no independent heavenly debt upon which seizure can operate. The levels of liability are different. Ketzot and Urim VeTumim ask from Sanhedrin 72 concerning Rava and stolen rams. If seizure is effective, why did Rava refuse to accept them? In my view seizure helps only where the claimant makes clear at the time of taking that he is seizing in satisfaction of the heavenly obligation. If he first receives the object without such intent and only later declares it seized, the seizure does not work. Once an object is received as a deposit or into custody, the holder’s domain can be considered the depositor’s domain for that purpose. Ordinary seizure in a civil doubt is different, because if the claimant’s version were known to be true, beit din itself could award the property. In the greater-punishment case, human law certainly exempts the defendant and only the heavenly obligation remains. Therefore seizure must be explicit from the beginning. Rava could not have announced in advance that he was taking the rams as seizure, because the owners would then not have given them to him; once he accepted them ordinarily, his possession was no longer an effective seizure. This appears clear and correct.
