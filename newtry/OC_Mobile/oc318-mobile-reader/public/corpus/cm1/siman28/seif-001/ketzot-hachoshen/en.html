And he is liable under the laws of Heaven. Rivash writes that whenever a person is obligated only to satisfy Heaven, if the claimant seizes property it is removed from him. This is cited by Knesset HaGedolah and discussed by Mishneh LaMelech in the laws of interest. Yet Rashi in Bava Metzia appears otherwise. The Gemara discusses one who muzzles a cow while threshing. Rava says that the Torah prohibits an illicit payment to a prostitute even where the intercourse was with his mother. Rashi explains that although beit din could not compel him to pay the promised lamb because the greater capital punishment exempts the monetary judgment, if he voluntarily gives it the lamb nevertheless has the status of the prohibited payment. Rashi concludes that the monetary obligation still rests upon him morally, only beit din lacks power to impose both punishments, and therefore he does not fulfill his duty until he pays. Alternatively, if the recipient seizes it, we do not remove it. This implies that an obligation to satisfy Heaven can support seizure. Maharshal distinguishes. In cases of "the greater punishment exempts the lesser," the monetary obligation fundamentally exists, but beit din cannot impose both punishments. Therefore if the greater punishment is not actually imposed, as in accidental conduct, lack of warning, or our era, seizure may be effective. But in ordinary indirect damage, where there is no legal monetary obligation at all and only a heavenly duty, seizure is ineffective. Maharshal further implies that if the greater punishment actually applied, even seizure would not help, because no secondary monetary duty remains. At first that seems unsupported, since one might say that even where the greater punishment applies, the monetary obligation remains in the background as a heavenly duty. But Sanhedrin provides strong support. When sheep were stolen from Rava by men entering through a tunnel and later returned, Rava refused to accept them because Rav had ruled that a burglar acquires what he takes through liability for his blood. Tosafot asks that they should at least be obligated to return the sheep to satisfy Heaven and answers that the thieves were not returning them for that reason; they thought they were legally obligated. If seizure were effective even after the greater capital rule applied, then once Rava received the sheep he should have kept them regardless of their intent. The fact that he returned them shows that when the greater punishment truly applies, the monetary claim no longer supports seizure. A burglar caught in the tunnel is like one who acted deliberately with full warning, even if he happened to survive, because the law permitted killing him. This differs from one who acted without warning or in a time when the court could not impose the severe punishment, where the greater penalty never actually attached in the operative sense. Chiddushei HaRan answers Tosafot differently: Rashi's rule about heavenly payment concerns honoring one's word, whereas the stolen sheep were fully acquired by the burglar and are no longer subject to return. That answer is difficult, because in Bava Metzia if one promised payment for intercourse and gave it only afterward, the Gemara asks how the payment can be prohibited if the woman had not acquired it at the time and meshichah was lacking. Mere moral duty to keep one's promise does not ordinarily create the necessary acquisition. The better approach is Rashi's combined rule, limited by Maharshal: where the greater penalty was not actually operative, the monetary obligation can remain for Heaven and seizure may help; where the greater punishment itself applied, the monetary claim is extinguished more fully. This also resolves a difficulty in Keritot. The Mishnah lists a person who in one act incurs four sin offerings and one guilt offering. Rabbi Meir adds that if it occurred on Shabbat and he carried the food in his mouth, another liability is added. One might ask that by adding a capital Shabbat violation he should eliminate the guilt offering for misuse of sacred property, since Pesachim states that according to Rabbi Nechunya ben HaKanah, one who eats consecrated chametz may be exempt from misuse because a karet liability can displace the monetary component. Tosafot explains that an asham meilot is tied to principal and surcharge. If those monetary obligations disappear, the sacrifice disappears too. According to Rashi, however, in a case of inadvertence the monetary duty can remain at least to satisfy Heaven, and sin offerings and guilt offerings are not seized by beit din in any event. Thus Rabbi Meir's added Shabbat liability need not remove the asham. The Pesachim opinion that there is no misuse can be established where the chametz transgression was deliberate, so the severe punishment truly applied and, according to Maharshal, no monetary duty remains even in Heaven. This also clarifies Rashi there, who insists that the case must involve deliberate chametz but inadvertent consecrated property. If the chametz too were inadvertent, the severe penalty would not be actively imposed and the heavenly monetary obligation would remain, so misuse would still exist. Although the Bava Metzia wording can sound as though even one who actually receives lashes still bears payment morally, it can be read as referring to inadvertent circumstances in which the payment remains.
