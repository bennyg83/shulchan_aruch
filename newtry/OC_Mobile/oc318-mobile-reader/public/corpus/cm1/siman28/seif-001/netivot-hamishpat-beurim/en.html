Anyone who knows testimony for his fellow. Bava Kamma teaches that a single witness does not violate the Torah prohibition by withholding testimony, but only a rabbinic duty. A practical difference is one who swore not to testify: where Torah law obligates testimony, the oath does not take effect because it is an oath to nullify a mitzvah; where the duty is only rabbinic, the oath can take effect. The same appears true of separate testimony where one witness does not know of the other. The first witness does not violate Torah law, but once the first has testified and the second knows that his testimony will combine, the second can be under a Torah duty. A lone witness who merely creates an oath is not obligated by Torah law to testify, but if his testimony can create monetary liability, for example because the litigant accepted a relative as equivalent to two witnesses, there remains a Torah obligation of returning lost property even if the formal verse of withholding testimony does not apply. Ran writes that relatives accepted as witnesses do not violate the specific verse because the Jerusalem Talmud limits it to persons fit to testify by Torah law. The case of a witness to death who is believed rabbinically and incurs a sacrificial liability is different, because rabbinic enactments were modeled on Torah structures; accepting a relative is instead the litigant's private self-obligation.
And he is liable under the laws of Heaven. See Shach citing Maharshal that seizure helps only in cases where the rule of greater punishment exempts from earthly payment, not in every case of heavenly liability. Urim VeTumim asks from Sanhedrin concerning stolen grain returned after a burglar's death, where Tosafot say the thieves thought they were obligated to restore it despite only heavenly liability. If seizure were effective under the greater-punishment rule, their mistaken return should still count as effective seizure. It can be answered that even if beit din does not remove property already seized, the claimant may still be forbidden to seize initially. The reason seizure helps is the principle that a person may sometimes execute his own law. Where beit din itself is forbidden to impose liability, the claimant likewise may be forbidden to execute that liability for himself. Ketzot HaChoshen also raises a question from misuse of sacred property where one act generates multiple sin offerings and an additional guilt offering despite the greater-punishment rule. The answer is that the passages involve different forms of sanctity. In a case of monetary sanctity, principal and additional fifth are restitution for theft from sacred property and therefore depend on monetary value. In bodily sanctity, the misuse liability functions as atonement rather than ordinary restitution, like payments for terumah, and can remain even where ordinary monetary recovery would be barred. Ketzot HaChoshen's attempt to make Maharshal exempt even in heavenly law under the stricter side is mistaken. Maharshal agrees that heavenly liability remains; his distinction concerns only whether seizure is effective.
