Testimony in a document. We find four principal approaches explaining why monetary law can be decided through written testimony. First, Rashi and Baal HaMaor hold that since a document is not written without the knowledge of the person who becomes obligated by it, it is not considered testimony "from their writing" in the prohibited sense. It is the obligor's own written instrument. Second, Ramban holds that the objection of written testimony does not apply to a formal document at all. Once testimony is cast into the legal form of a document, it is regarded as though the testimony had already been investigated in beit din. The disqualification of written testimony applies only to ordinary written statements outside the legal form of a deed. Third, Tosafot in the name of Rabbeinu Tam holds that the problem of written testimony applies only to someone who is unable to testify orally, such as a mute. If he could testify orally, the writing is acceptable, on the principle that a required mode of performance does not invalidate when the person is capable of it. Fourth, Rambam holds that written testimony is not effective by Torah law because it is testimony through writing, and its use in documents is a rabbinic enactment. Yet according to all these approaches, the sugya in Ketubot 24 concerning using documents to establish priestly lineage remains difficult. The Gemara asks whether a person can be elevated to priestly status from a document that says, "I, so and so the kohen, borrowed one maneh from so and so," signed by witnesses. Are the witnesses attesting only to the loan in the document, or to the whole statement including his priestly status? Rav Huna and Rav Chisda say he may be elevated. If the witnesses are testifying in writing that he is a kohen, why is that not disqualified written testimony? According to Baal HaMaor the borrower's authorization to write the debt cannot itself authorize written testimony establishing his priestly lineage. According to Ramban, formal deed status helps only for monetary matters contained in the deed. Priestly lineage is not itself part of the legal deed. According to Rambam, the rabbinic enactment validating documents was made for monetary matters, and perhaps for gittin because of the danger of abandoned wives, but not generally for lineage. The sugya seems readily explained only according to Rabbeinu Tam, who validates written testimony whenever the witnesses are capable of oral testimony. Perhaps one can answer even for the other approaches because all families have a presumptive status of legitimacy and one witness ordinarily suffices in prohibition law. The additional stringency concerning priestly lineage is only rabbinic. For a rabbinic matter, written testimony may have been accepted, just as Rama in siman 46 allows written testimony for validation of documents. Still, the matter needs further explanation. Tosafot asks another question: if the witnesses are not viewed as attesting to the whole statement, then witnesses may sign even without knowing whether the borrower is a kohen. How then can the Mishnah in Bava Batra say that two people of the same name can distinguish themselves in their documents by writing "kohen"? What benefit does that provide if the witnesses do not know it? Perhaps the question in Ketubot is not whether the witnesses know the fact. They certainly attest to the entire text factually. The question is instead whether their attestation to priestly status is legally part of the document. If it is included in the monetary content of the document, Ramban's rule that formal document testimony is treated as investigated can apply. If it is separate collateral information, then regarding priestly status it is ordinary written testimony and the written testimony objection applies. Thus the witnesses can know he is a kohen and still sign it for identifying purposes, yet the question remains whether the document itself has evidentiary force to elevate him in lineage.
