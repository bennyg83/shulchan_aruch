Monetary cases are decided on the basis of testimony contained in a document. The Sema writes that this dispensation applies specifically to a fully valid document. It was permitted as a measure for the orderly functioning of society, lest the door be closed before borrowers because lenders fear that the witnesses may die or travel overseas. Although the Mechaber and the Sema derive this from Maimonides, who writes explicitly in chapter 3 of Hilchot Edut that by Torah law testimony is accepted in neither monetary nor capital cases except from the witnesses’ mouths, as Scripture says, “By the mouth of two witnesses,” meaning from their mouths and not from their handwriting, while by rabbinic law monetary cases are decided on documentary testimony even if the witnesses are no longer alive, so that the door not be closed before borrowers, whereas fines, lashes, and exile are not adjudicated through a document, this view is not persuasive. Numerous passages in the Gemara demonstrate that documentary testimony is effective by Torah law, and that capital matters, fines, and bills of divorce may rest upon it. The Kesef Mishneh records the Tashbetz’s objection from the verse, “Fields shall be purchased for money, and write in a document and seal it, so that they may endure many days.” The Kesef Mishneh answers that this verse is from the Prophets rather than the Torah, but this is not a satisfactory answer. In Gittin 36a the Gemara asks concerning witnesses signing a bill of divorce for the sake of social order: Is that merely social order? It is required by Scripture, as it says, “Write in a document and seal it.” Rava answers that the enactment was needed only according to Rabbi Eliezer, who holds that delivery witnesses effect the divorce, and the Sages instituted signature witnesses lest the delivery witnesses die or travel overseas. This appears to be the Tashbetz’s intended proof, though he stated it briefly. The same principle is evident at the beginning of Gittin 3a, where the Gemara asks why two witnesses should not be required and answers that, by strict law, even validation of ordinary documents should not require two, in accordance with Reish Lakish’s rule that witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony had been examined in beit din; the Sages required validation, but were lenient here because of the concern for an abandoned wife. The same appears at the end of the second chapter of Ketubot, in Rashi and Tosafot there, and throughout the decisors. The Ramban, in his strictures on Maimonides’ enumeration of the commandments, at the end of the second principle, records that the sages of the generations raised conclusive objections and clear proofs against Maimonides on this matter. Rabbi Yitzchak Leon, author of Megillat Esther, labored to defend Maimonides from the Ramban’s objections, but in my opinion his arguments have no substance. He says that the Gemara’s expression “it is by Torah law” is not literal, but merely contrasts the Mishnah’s attribution of the enactment to Rabban Gamliel with the fact that it already existed in Jeremiah’s time. Yet the Gemara’s emphatic question plainly means that it is an actual Torah rule. The Ramban’s observation elsewhere, that the Gemara occasionally calls a rabbinic command “Torah,” concerned incidental phrasing, not a formal objection introduced with astonishment. Had the institution originated only with Jeremiah, the Gemara should have asked, “Did Rabban Gamliel institute it? Jeremiah already instituted it,” just as elsewhere it distinguishes between decrees of earlier authorities. Moreover, Maimonides’ formulation indicates that documentary testimony was instituted only in monetary law because of the concern that the door not be closed before borrowers. In bills of divorce that rationale does not apply, and according to his language documentary testimony should not be accepted unless the witnesses remain alive. Yet the sugya in Gittin proves otherwise. Even if one argues that the Sages also enacted documentary testimony for bills of divorce, the Gemara could have answered that Jeremiah instituted it only for loans, while Rabban Gamliel extended it to bills of divorce. The term “get” in the Mishnah may sometimes include documents generally, but it certainly includes a bill of divorce. Furthermore, the Gemara’s answer according to Rabbi Eliezer cannot refer merely to ordinary loan documents, for lending without a document is plainly permitted, and the only prohibition mentioned by the Gemara is lending without witnesses because of “Do not place a stumbling block.” Maimonides himself rules accordingly. Thus the discussion in Gittin is specifically about bills of divorce. If Jeremiah’s enactment did not include bills of divorce, the Gemara did not need to invoke Rabbi Eliezer at all. The unavoidable conclusion is that the verse in Jeremiah does not describe a limited rabbinic enactment for monetary cases, but reflects a Torah rule that documentary testimony is effective in every field even after the witnesses die. The Gemara therefore properly asks that witness signatures are already required, and answers that according to Rabbi Eliezer Rabban Gamliel instituted that one should not divorce initially with delivery witnesses alone, because they may die or travel overseas. Rabbi Yitzchak Leon further answers the Ramban’s proof from Ketubot and Gittin concerning Reish Lakish’s rule by saying that this rule merely requires us to believe the contents of the document, but by Torah law the witnesses must still appear and orally testify to everything written in it. This explanation has no foundation. The Gemara says that, by strict law, validation of documents should not require two because signed witnesses are treated as though their testimony was examined in beit din. It plainly follows that by Torah law no validation is required and collection may proceed on the document alone. Were the witnesses required to testify orally, no greater validation could exist than their presence, and the Gemara’s discussion would be meaningless. In bills of divorce the Sages were in fact lenient even when the witnesses are absent, relying upon the Torah presumption that the document needs no validation. He would have done better, according to his own position, to say that the passages speak after Jeremiah’s prophetic enactment and that the later Sages merely required validation, but even that is not the meaning of the sources. The primary conclusion is therefore that documentary testimony is effective by Torah law, contrary to Maimonides’ formulation.
Rabbi Yitzchak Leon further extends his defense of Maimonides against the Ramban’s statement that capital cases and cases of fines may be adjudicated through documentary testimony, but his discussion rests on premises that have no foundation. There is no need to answer him at length. Anyone who studies the Ramban and Rabbi Yitzchak Leon fairly will judge that the Ramban’s words are clear and correct. The Bach at the end of siman 46 also appears to agree with the Ramban. Since the Tur cites Maimonides without qualification, and the Mechaber, Ran, Ir Shushan, Sema, and other later authorities followed him, I was compelled to discuss the matter at length and establish that Maimonides’ view on this point is not accepted.
