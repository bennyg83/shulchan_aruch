We decide monetary cases through testimony in a deed. See Shach, who cites Ramban's question on Rambam from gittin, kiddushin, and acquisition documents, where the Gemara says that by Torah law signed documents are like testimony already examined in beit din. There is a textual correction in Shach: the sense is that by Torah law the deed needs no authentication at all. In my view Rambam can be reconciled by distinguishing constitutive documents from evidentiary documents. In a get, kiddushin document, or acquisition deed, the signed writing is itself part of the act creating the legal result. Tosafot in Gittin explain that where a husband tells two people to instruct others to write and sign, this is not ordinary hearsay because the first pair are functioning as the husband's agents to appoint the actual actors. At first glance that would imply a similar rule in money, which no authority accepts. The distinction is that in a get the writing and signature are part of preparing the legal instrument, and the final divorce is completed at delivery. The first pair's statement therefore concerns appointment and qualification of agents before the final matter of personal status is completed. Testimony concerning such preliminary qualification can be received outside beit din, just as identity testimony concerning a brother for chalitzah can function before the final personal-status act. The same general principle exists in ritual law: preliminary facts can be established before the ultimate prohibited-status question matures. Ramban extends 'say to others' even to delivery, but explains that the later actors are agents rather than witnesses to the husband's underlying statement; if the woman's status were to be proved in beit din solely through them, two sets of testimony would be needed. This demonstrates the distinction. A constitutive deed does not function merely as written narration that an earlier event happened. Its legal force is in the instrument itself. An evidentiary deed stating that someone already became obligated through a prior loan, kinyan, or sale is different. There the writing's whole purpose is to testify to a completed past event. According to Rambam, such written testimony is disqualified by Torah law as testimony from writing, and formal documentary reliance is rabbinic. This also explains why the date written in a get can be relied upon. The signatories testify only that the get was written on that date, not that divorce was completed then. Completion through delivery is established separately, often through presumptions or delivery witnesses. Only where the writing itself is later used to prove a completed past legal event does the problem of testimony from writing arise. This is the basis for Rambam's distinction between constitutive and evidentiary documents.
