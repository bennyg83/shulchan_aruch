We decide, etc. These words come from Rambam, Hilchot Edut chapter 3. Rambam writes that by Torah law testimony in both civil and capital cases is accepted only orally, as Scripture says, "by the mouth of two witnesses," meaning from their mouths and not from their writing. Nevertheless, by rabbinic enactment civil cases are decided on the basis of written testimony in documents even when the witnesses are no longer alive, in order not to close the door before borrowers. Ramban challenges Rambam strongly. If written testimony is only rabbinic, how can acquisition documents, gittin, and kiddushin function by Torah law? Scripture itself speaks of writing and signing deeds, and the Gemara treats signed gittin as effective on a Torah level. The answer requires distinguishing between a document as the legal instrument that creates the transaction and the document as testimony presented later to beit din. Rambam can agree that Torah law sometimes requires a written and signed instrument as part of the very act of acquisition or divorce. A get, for example, is not merely evidence that a divorce occurred. The writing and delivery are themselves the mechanism by which the divorce takes effect. Likewise, a kiddushin document or a deed transferring land can function as the constitutive instrument of the transaction. The signatures can be part of the formal completion of that instrument. This is entirely different from beit din later relying on those signatures as testimony establishing what happened. The Torah can say: if the properly signed instrument was actually delivered, the transaction takes effect. Yet when a later court is asked to determine whether that delivery actually occurred, the court may still require oral witnesses, because "from their mouths and not from their writing" governs judicial proof. Thus a woman can truly be divorced before Heaven because a valid get was delivered to her, even if later no court can establish that fact from the signatures alone under pure Torah procedure. Similarly, a woman can be truly betrothed through a valid kiddushin document, and land can be truly acquired through a valid deed, while the later judicial proof of delivery remains a separate evidentiary question. This resolves the verse about fields being bought through written and signed deeds. The signatures can be necessary for the deed's own legal validity without automatically turning the paper into courtroom testimony. It also resolves the Gemara concerning witnesses signing a get. According to the initial assumption, the signatures are part of the Torah-law validity of the get itself. According to R. Elazar, who holds that witnesses to delivery create the divorce, the signatures are not essential at the Torah level. Chazal nevertheless required them because delivery witnesses may later die and the woman needs proof of her divorce. Once Chazal enacted that courts may rely on signed documents as evidence, the signatures became practically essential for that purpose. Kesef Mishneh's proof from the end of Perek Arba'ah Achin can then be understood. The Gemara says Chazal did not establish a date in a kiddushin document because the witnesses might later look at the writing and testify from it, which would be testimony from writing. Why does this not contradict the ordinary rule that documents are accepted? Because a kiddushin document is effective as the instrument of kiddushin, but for a later court to establish the exact date for a matter carrying severe personal-status consequences, the court cannot rely on written recollection as ordinary Torah testimony. In civil cases Chazal enacted reliance on documents because of commercial necessity. In severe personal-status matters that can lead to capital consequences, the Torah-law evidentiary standard remains. Thus Kesef Mishneh's explanation is sound. One remaining question is the well-known statement that witnesses signed on a document are treated as though their testimony had already been examined in beit din, and that only rabbinically is formal verification later required. At first glance this sounds as though written testimony is already valid by Torah law. Several answers are possible. One can say there were two rabbinic enactments at different periods. The first, perhaps very ancient, allowed beit din to rely on signed documents for civil adjudication so as not to close the door before borrowers. Initially no formal verification was required because forged documents were uncommon. Later, when falsification increased, Chazal required verification of signatures. Accordingly, the Talmud can say that witnesses signed on a document are presumed valid and that Chazal later required verification without implying that the original evidentiary use of documents was itself Torah law. There is another possibility. Even at Torah law, there can be cases where the existence of the signed document is independently established by oral witnesses. For example, witnesses may testify orally that the borrower received the money under this particular document and that the document remained outstanding as the marker of the debt. The document then serves as the identifying instrument rather than as unverified written testimony. In such a case the debtor cannot simply say it is forged. This can explain some Talmudic statements without difficulty. However, I believe Rambam's true position is even more precise. Below, siman 46, Rambam rules that where the handwriting of the witnesses is not independently known, the witnesses who come to authenticate the document must remember the substance of the testimony. If their handwriting is independently recognized, the document is accepted even if they say they do not remember. Rambam explains that in the latter case they are not believed to destroy the document by saying they do not remember, because perhaps they intentionally suppress their memory. This implies that, were their statement of lack of memory believed, the document could indeed be weakened. Why should memory matter at all if every document is accepted only through a completely independent rabbinic enactment that functions even after the witnesses die? The answer is that Torah law itself gives a special status to a document where the witnesses are still alive and presently capable of oral testimony. The document is then "fit for oral expression." Although Rambam does not accept Rabbeinu Tam's broad rule that any witness can testify from a private writing, a formal legal document stands differently. The Torah recognizes the signed instrument when the witnesses are still capable of coming before beit din and confirming it orally. In that situation the document is treated as their own judicial expression. If they credibly say they no longer remember the matter, the Torah-level basis can fail because it is no longer truly fit to be rendered orally. Where the witnesses are alive and their signatures are independently established, however, we presume they retain the necessary memory unless they have a valid migo to say otherwise. But when the witnesses have died, the document is no longer fit for oral testimony at all. Pure Torah law would therefore no longer permit a court to rely on it as testimony. Chazal enacted that civil courts may nevertheless decide on the basis of such documents to avoid destroying commercial credit. This explains Rambam's exact wording: civil cases are decided from written testimony "even though the witnesses are no longer alive" by rabbinic enactment. His main novelty is not that every use of a document in court is rabbinic from the first moment. Rather, once the witnesses are dead and oral confirmation is permanently impossible, the Torah-law evidentiary route has ended and the rabbinic enactment sustains the document. This also explains why Rambam says that penalties and similar matters are not adjudicated from documents. Where the witnesses are dead, the special civil enactment does not extend to those areas. While the witnesses are alive and the document is still fit for oral confirmation, its Torah status is stronger. This harmonizes Rambam with the accepted principle that legal documents are fundamentally recognized by Torah law, while preserving his explicit statement that continued civil reliance after the witnesses' death is rabbinic. It also explains why a deaf person, who is incapable of oral testimony, remains disqualified from serving as a witness on a document according to everyone. The document must at least originate from witnesses who are fit for oral testimony. Accordingly, the difference between ordinary written testimony and a formal shtar is not that writing itself becomes testimony in every circumstance. The shtar has a special Torah status so long as it is rooted in witnesses who are fit to render their testimony orally, and Chazal extended its courtroom effectiveness after their death for civil needs. This is, in my view, the correct interpretation of Rambam and removes Ramban's principal objections.
