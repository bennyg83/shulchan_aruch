If the gentile sues him, etc. This implies that in the case above, even if the gentile formally sues the witness, he may not testify. The Bach writes so. See Tumim, where I explained the Gemara's wording.
And likewise in a place where they do not extract, etc. See Tumim, where I wrote that even if the gentile court corrupts its own procedure and extracts money on the basis of this testimony, since that result is contrary to its own law and accepted practice the Jewish witness bears no guilt merely for having testified truthfully. Regarding an eminent person, the Sema holds that he may testify, while Yam Shel Shlomo and the Shach hold that he should not testify initially. If he did testify, however, he is not placed under cherem. See Tumim. If he testifies to a matter where under Jewish law the Jewish defendant would be exempt even if the facts are true, such as where he became a bailee for a gentile and under Jewish law the laws of bailees do not apply to gentile property, but because of the testimony the gentile court obligates the bailee to pay, the witness must compensate him under dina d'garmi. Rama. See Tumim.
On the basis of one witness he may testify. The Sema, seif katan 23, writes that if the gentile was required to swear and the Jewish witness's testimony exempts the gentile from the oath, the witness may likewise testify, because even under Jewish law a supporting witness exempts from a hesset oath. He deliberately says hesset. If the gentile admitted part and was therefore subject to a Torah level oath, one might think the Jewish witness may not testify, because under Jewish law a gentile is presumed unreliable and one supporting witness would not exempt him from a monetary oath. Beit Yosef, however, does not agree and holds that even in such a case a supporting witness exempts. See below simanim 75 and 87, where I discussed at length whether a supporting witness exempts one who is otherwise suspected regarding oaths.
Two relatives, etc. Meaning, in a jurisdiction where relatives are accepted as witnesses to impose liability. Under Jewish law they are not believed either to benefit or to harm a litigant, and they therefore may not testify there, just as a single witness may not testify where secular law gives him greater effect than Jewish law.
