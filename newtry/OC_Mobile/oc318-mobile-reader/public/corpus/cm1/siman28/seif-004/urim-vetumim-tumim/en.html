All the more so two witnesses may testify. This is Rosh's language and sounds as though two witnesses are permitted to testify initially. Tosafot imply otherwise. They write that although withholding a non-Jew's loan can sometimes be permitted, nevertheless we do not place two witnesses under nidui. This implies that ideally they still should not testify; the rule is only that once they did, nidui is not imposed. Rif likewise omits an affirmative permission for two witnesses. He records only that the special punishment applies to one witness where the secular court extracts money on the basis of his testimony. From that distinction we can infer that two witnesses are not punished in the same manner, but we cannot infer that they are positively authorized to go testify in the first place. Had Rif intended such an affirmative permission, he should have stated it. Therefore the stricter position of Tosafot should be followed. Two witnesses are not placed under nidui merely for having testified truthfully in the circumstances described, but one should not infer from this that they are always permitted initially to testify before the secular court.
Likewise, in a place where, etc. This rule appears in the Gemara: in a secular tribunal whose law does not extract money on the basis of one witness, a Jew who testifies alone is not punished. At first glance this seems obvious. The entire nidui is intended to remove the harm caused by his testimony. If their law merely requires the defendant to swear against the witness, what monetary harm is there to remove? The correct explanation is more precise. Ordinarily, the law of that tribunal does not award money on the basis of one witness. In this particular case, however, the tribunal improperly departed from its own rules and extracted money because of the single witness's testimony. One might have thought that the witness must compensate the injured Jew because his testimony in fact caused the wrongful extraction. The Gemara teaches otherwise. Since the accepted law of that tribunal does not normally allow collection on the strength of one witness, the witness had no reason to anticipate that the judges would distort their own law. His decision to testify was therefore not the proximate wrongful cause of the later judicial corruption. Even if the secular judges acted unlawfully and extracted money, the witness bears no responsibility for a result he had no reason to foresee. This also explains why Rif records this case separately rather than relying on the general distinction between one witness and two. The novelty is not merely that one witness ordinarily does not cause collection. It is that even where this particular tribunal nevertheless corruptly extracted money, the witness remains exempt because he was entitled to rely on their normal legal procedure.
He may testify. Regarding an important person, Sema understands Rosh to mean that if such a person cannot evade the demand to testify, he may testify. Shach, following Yam Shel Shlomo, says there is a textual error in Rosh and the correct reading is that he should not testify initially. Nevertheless, if he already testified, everyone agrees he is exempt from nidui and payment, because the Gemara leaves the case unresolved and we do not extract money in doubt. Kenesset HaGedolah asks against Rama's responsum, siman 88, where Rama rules that an important person who testified can be liable to pay. If an ordinary single witness can be exempt after testifying, all the more so an important person who had greater difficulty avoiding the demand. But the comparison is mistaken. Where an ordinary witness testifies concerning a debt and then says, "I testified truthfully," he can sometimes avoid payment because, if his testimony was true, perhaps he caused no unlawful loss at all. Rama's responsum concerns cases such as bodily injury or laws of bailees, where even if every fact testified to was true, Torah law would not impose the same liability that the secular court imposed. For example, a Jew may not have the Torah-law status of a bailee toward a non-Jew in the manner assumed by secular law, yet the witness's testimony that he accepted responsibility can cause the secular court to impose payment. In that circumstance the witness has caused a monetary loss through dina d'garmei. If he argues that he could not evade the demand, that merely means he saved himself from difficulty at the expense of another person's money, which does not exempt him from compensation. Rama's ruling is therefore correct. Bach also infers from the phrase "an important person cannot evade" that the non-Jew must have specifically demanded his testimony. Otherwise the expression "cannot evade" would make no sense. This implies that an ordinary person remains prohibited from testifying even when the non-Jew demands it. That is correct, but our printed Gemara says: "A Jew who knew testimony for a non-Jew, and the non-Jew did not demand it from him, went and testified." This sounds as though if the non-Jew did demand it, he would not be placed under nidui. Rif omits the phrase "and he did not demand it," though Rosh preserves it. We must therefore explain that "did not demand it" refers to the earlier stage: the non-Jew had not originally designated him to become a witness to the event. If from the outset the non-Jew specifically asked him to attend the transaction and serve as a witness, there is greater basis to allow his later testimony, as Rosh and Mechaber indicate. This reconciles the texts.
