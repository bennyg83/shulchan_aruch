Seif 12. “Monetary cases are decided through testimony in a document,” etc. Note: The authorities have raised many questions on the Rambam here, and elsewhere I suggested several resolutions. At present another approach appears to me. Rabbeinu Tam holds that where witnesses are not mute and are capable of oral testimony, written testimony can be effective through the principle that whatever is fit for the required act may sometimes be treated as though the act were performed. The same type of reasoning can explain instruments whose writing itself completes the legal transaction, such as a get: since witnesses capable of serving as witnesses of delivery can complete the act, witnesses of signature may also have effect because they are fit for the alternative form of witnessing. But where a document is needed for one legal function and testimony before beit din is also independently required, for example collection from purchasers where one needs both the status of a document and judicial testimony about its contents, one would have to invoke two separate constructive fictions: first, to treat the signatures as though the witnesses had served as witnesses of delivery, and second, to treat the written document as though the witnesses had now testified orally in beit din. We do not employ two such “fitness” constructions together. I have elsewhere proved, from Eruvin and in my work on Yoreh Deah, laws of treifot, that two layers of this principle are not combined. Accordingly the Rambam’s precise language is understandable: “Monetary cases are decided through testimony in a document by rabbinic law.” Where the court must actually decide a monetary case on the strength of a document, and the instrument requires both document status and present evidentiary force, Torah law would not suffice through two constructive steps. The Sages validated it for the sake of orderly commerce. This principle resolves many difficulties with the Rambam. Earlier I cited Rabbeinu Tam’s view that “by their mouth and not from their writing” disqualifies written evidence only in capital cases, not monetary cases. I find a serious difficulty from Makkot 6b, concerning litigants who spoke a foreign language before Rava and the rule that the Sanhedrin should not hear evidence through an interpreter. Why does the Gemara challenge Rava in a monetary matter? Immediately earlier Rav Nachman says that separate testimony is valid in monetary law because the verse “one witness shall not cause him to die” was written specifically regarding capital cases. The verse “by the mouth of two witnesses” also appears in a capital context. Perhaps the restriction on interpreters likewise applies only to capital law. It must be, as Tosafot explain there, that monetary and capital procedure are generally linked by the principle of one law for both, except where a verse expressly limits a rule to capital cases. The verse “by the mouth of two witnesses,” however, governs monetary testimony as well. Therefore the Gemara properly challenges Rava from the rule against an interpreter. If so, a derivation from that same language excluding written testimony should also apply to monetary cases. Written evidence is, if anything, further removed from oral testimony than testimony through an interpreter, because the witnesses are not speaking before the court at all. This is a strong difficulty against Rabbeinu Tam and requires study.
