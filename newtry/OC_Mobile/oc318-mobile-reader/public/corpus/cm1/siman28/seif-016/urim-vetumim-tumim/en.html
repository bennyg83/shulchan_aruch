Or if the witnesses were ill, etc. See Urim, where I cited Sema's statement that Rama's addition, "and if the matter was urgent," is unnecessary because it is already included in Mechaber's rule. There is, however, an independent difficulty. Rama concludes, "And so it appears from what is ruled below in siman 140, seif 10." There the law is that we receive the testimony of tenants who lived in a house before the protester appears to challenge the possessor, so that their testimony will not later be lost after they pay the rent. At first glance this creates a contradiction in Tur. Here Tur follows Rosh's first view, that even where the matter is urgent, testimony is not received outside the litigant's presence unless he was summoned and failed to appear. Rosh's reason is that where the litigant was not negligent, the possible loss of evidence is attributed to his mazal and does not justify bypassing him. Yet below in siman 140 Tur rules that the tenants' testimony can be received in advance. One must say that there the protester was negligent. He allowed another person to remain in possession of what he claims is his property and did not come promptly to beit din. It is therefore fair that the testimony be taken without him. If so, what proof does Rama bring from that case? It may depend entirely on the protester's negligence. The correct explanation requires returning to the Gemara. The Gemara gives two teachings. First: if the litigant or the witnesses are ill, or the witnesses are about to go overseas, and the other party was summoned but did not come, testimony may be received outside his presence. Second, R. Yehudah says in the name of Shmuel: if proceedings were already opened against him, he was summoned, and he did not come, testimony may be received. Rif omits Shmuel's second statement. He evidently holds that it disagrees with the first and rules like the first. Why assume there is a dispute? Why not minimize disagreement and say that the first statement deals with urgent circumstances where proceedings had not yet begun, while Shmuel teaches that once proceedings began, a mere summons and nonappearance are sufficient even without illness or impending departure? Rif must understand that the whole significance of "proceedings were opened" is that otherwise the defendant can say, "I will go to a different beit din." If so, even the first statement must already concern a situation in which the litigant is bound to this particular court. Otherwise, what good does urgency do? The absent party could say, "Who appointed you as judges over me? Had you summoned me, I might have selected another court." As long as he is not obligated to litigate before them, concern for losing testimony does not automatically give this beit din jurisdiction over him. Accordingly, Rif understood the first teaching to include a case in which this court already has jurisdiction, and then the illness or urgency is the reason testimony can be taken without the party's presence. Shmuel's statement would add only that once proceedings were opened, summons alone is enough even without those urgent circumstances. That is a genuine disagreement, and Rif rules like the first view. Tosafot's discussion of the phrase "and they sent for him" also fits this reading. Perach Mateh Aharon, citing Mahari ben Lev, asks how Tosafot could have overlooked that Shmuel's statement explicitly includes both opened proceedings and summons. According to what I have explained, Tosafot understood that the first teaching also already assumes the court had jurisdiction. Shmuel's only novelty is the reduced urgency requirement. Thus their question is sound. Now the dispute among the authorities becomes clear. According to Mechaber, who follows Rif, even in an urgent case where we do not require prior summons, we must still be speaking of a court before which the litigant is already bound to litigate. Otherwise he can still say, "This is not my beit din." But according to the authorities who hold that Shmuel does not disagree with the first teaching, the first teaching must concern a case where proceedings had not been opened. In an urgent situation, Chazal authorized the court to receive the testimony even though the absent party could have said, "I do not accept you as my judges." This is precisely Rama's point. He first cites the view that once the party was summoned and proceedings were opened, the testimony can be received. That assumes Shmuel is not disputing the earlier teaching. Rama therefore adds that where the matter is urgent and there is risk of losing evidence, testimony may be received even without summons and without opened proceedings. His proof from the tenants below in siman 140 is not primarily about the protester's negligence. Even if one says the protester was negligent, he could still argue, "This is not my beit din. Who appointed you to receive testimony against me?" The fact that the court nevertheless receives the evidence shows that in a case of serious risk of losing testimony, the court can act despite that objection. This is what Rama means to prove. The gloss is therefore not redundant at all.
If the defendant was ill, etc. Rama presents this as though everyone agrees that where the defendant is ill, testimony is not received outside his presence. I do not think that is correct. The matter appears directly dependent on the dispute just discussed. If one holds that even where the claimant or witnesses are ill and the matter is urgent, testimony may not be received unless the absent party was summoned and was negligent, then the same logic applies where the defendant is ill. Even if the claimant will suffer a substantial loss by having to wait until minor heirs grow up, and even if the assets may deteriorate in the meantime, the defendant himself was not negligent. One can say that the claimant's loss is caused by his own mazal, and testimony is not received against the sick defendant. But according to the authorities who hold that even without any negligence, where there is a serious risk of losing testimony it may be received, why should the defendant's illness be different? The potential loss to the claimant can be greater than the case of witnesses about to go overseas, who may return within a year or two. Indeed, Rashba in his novellae to Bava Kamma asks why, after a defendant dies, testimony is not received against his minor heirs. Is that not worse than witnesses about to go overseas? He answers that minor heirs cannot properly investigate and defend their rights. Nimukei Yosef answers that in the case of traveling witnesses the litigant himself still exists, whereas minors are treated as though the party is absent. Those explanations relate specifically to the death of the defendant. Where the defendant is merely alive but ill, neither reason applies in the same force. If the claimant faces serious loss, why should testimony not be preserved? Rosh himself clearly connects the two rules. He cites the law that testimony is not received where the defendant is ill as support for his broader position that absent negligence we do not receive testimony outside the party's presence. That shows the rules rise and fall together. Bedek HaBayit likewise notes that according to Rambam, it appears that testimony can be received even where the defendant is ill. That fits perfectly, because Rambam does not require negligence by the absent party and does not say that the potential loss is simply the claimant's mazal. Therefore the law concerning an ill defendant is not unanimous. Mechaber, who follows the view that where the witnesses are ill no negligence or prior summons is required, appropriately omits the rule that an ill defendant blocks testimony. Rama's presentation of the latter rule as undisputed therefore requires study.
If proceedings were opened against him, etc. Sema, following Tur, explains that this means the defendant already began presenting his claims before this beit din, so he can no longer say, "I will not litigate before you; I will go to another court in this city." That is correct under Rama's later enactment above in siman 14, where local custom prevents a litigant from demanding to go to the Great Beit Din. But Tur himself rules in siman 14 that a litigant can say, "Let us go to the Great Beit Din," and he does not mention Rama's later limitation. This creates a difficulty. The Gemara itself says that even after proceedings were opened, the defendant can still say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." Ravina answers that the case concerns one where a written directive from the Great Beit Din had already been obtained. Thus, without such a directive, even opened proceedings do not necessarily bind the defendant to the local court. Why then does Tur here say simply that once proceedings were opened, testimony can be received, without mentioning the directive from the Great Beit Din? Kenesset HaGedolah already asks this in siman 14. He asks similarly about Rivash, responsum 354. But Rivash presents no difficulty. Rivash writes that where proceedings had not yet been opened, the defendant could say, "I will go to the Great Beit Din," even though the local court held authority from that Great Beit Din. Where proceedings were already opened, he could no longer say so. This language itself indicates the case involved a directive or authority from the Great Beit Din, as Rivash explicitly says. He is discussing the Gemara's qualified case. Tur and Rosh remain difficult. One could force a different reading of the Gemara. Perhaps the Gemara already knew from tradition that sometimes even after proceedings had begun a litigant can demand the Great Beit Din. It asks on that basis, and Ravina answers that this exceptional right exists only where the plaintiff had already begun the matter before the Great Beit Din and obtained a directive from there. Without that prior connection, opening proceedings locally would indeed bind the defendant. But this is strained. Rashi and the other authorities do not explain the passage this way. A better explanation emerges from the Mishnah concerning one who accepts three cattle herders as judges. R. Meir allows retraction, and the Sages do not. Reish Lakish says their dispute is before final judgment. R. Yochanan says it is after final judgment, and the Gemara asks whether according to R. Yochanan they also dispute before final judgment or only after it. If an ordinary litigant, even after beginning before a valid court, could always say, "I wish to go to a greater beit din," then certainly one who accepted three cattle herders should be able to retract before final judgment and say, "I now wish to go before competent Torah judges." A valid beit din is incomparably greater than three cattle herders. The Mishnah itself gives no indication that a formal selection document was written. Indeed Ramban and other poskim derive from this Mishnah that with qualified judges, retraction before final judgment can already be barred in ordinary circumstances. That proof would fail if the case depended upon a written selection. Therefore there must be Tannaitic and Amoraic positions according to which beginning the case itself can create binding acceptance even before final judgment. Now note Shmuel's separate position in the Mishnah of accepted disqualified judges. Shmuel holds that where the acceptance is in the form "I will give you," all agree the party can retract, because a bare verbal undertaking to pay does not create a binding obligation without a kinyan. Only a waiver type of acceptance can become effective through speech alone. Applying that to ordinary beit din, according to Shmuel, when the defendant begins presenting claims before the judges, there remains a problem in a case equivalent to "I will give you." Mere verbal acceptance is not enough to obligate him, because such an obligation normally requires a kinyan. Therefore Shmuel can still allow him to say after the beginning of proceedings, "I will go to the Great Beit Din." This is exactly why the Gemara challenges Shmuel's own statement about receiving testimony once proceedings were opened. According to Shmuel's broader legal theory, beginning the case does not necessarily create irrevocable acceptance. The Gemara therefore answers with the directive from the Great Beit Din. But our halachah does not follow Shmuel's limitation. We rule that even in a case of "I will give you," after final judgment the acceptance can become binding, and more generally judicial acceptance does not always require the same kinyan as an ordinary private undertaking. Once a litigant voluntarily begins presenting his claims before qualified judges, that acceptance has genuine force. Accordingly, for us there is no necessary distinction between waiver and affirmative obligation in this context. Thus Rosh and Tur omit the Great Beit Din directive from the practical rule. The Gemara needed it specifically within Shmuel's own view. For our halachah, once proceedings have begun before a valid beit din, the defendant can no longer freely withdraw merely because he now prefers another court, subject to the separate rules of siman 14. This explains Tur's wording and resolves the apparent contradiction.
