And if the defendant was ill. Urim VeTumim asks from Rosh, whose discussion seems to tie the rules together: if in the claimant's illness one can receive testimony without first summoning the defendant, why should the defendant's illness prevent reception? Rosh's words can be explained as follows. Where the claimant is ill, reception outside the defendant's presence is justified only when two elements combine: genuine danger of loss to the claimant and some fault of the defendant, such as his having been summoned and failed to appear. If the defendant himself becomes ill, then even if he had earlier been summoned and failed to appear, we still may not receive testimony after his illness in a manner that principally prejudices his heirs. The heirs themselves did not act wrongfully, and testimony is not received irregularly against them merely to avoid delay. Under this reading Rosh can indeed treat delayed payment as a kind of loss, yet still protect the heirs because they are innocent. Rama, however, appears to understand the Gemara differently: the phrase 'he became ill' refers to the claimant, and 'they sent for him' is not a necessary condition in that branch. Then the distinction is simply that the claimant's illness creates a risk of substantive loss, whereas the defendant's illness creates only delay in collection. That is why testimony may be received in the first but not the second. Urim VeTumim's argument from Rashba that small orphans cannot investigate defenses applies equally where the defendant himself is ill, because his incapacity likewise prevents proper investigation. The rationale of Nimukei Yosef fits the same framework.
