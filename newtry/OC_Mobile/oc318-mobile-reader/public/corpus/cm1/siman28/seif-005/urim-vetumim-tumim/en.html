But if there is an element of preventing a prohibition, etc. Because Shulchan Aruch states this rule without distinguishing among different kinds of prohibition, it is necessary to clarify several practical distinctions. Tosafot in Shevuot discuss the rule that where a matter of prohibition is involved, a Torah scholar must testify despite the indignity of appearing before a lesser beit din, because "there is no wisdom and no understanding against Hashem." They ask from Berakhot, where the dignity of human beings can override certain obligations, such as becoming impure for a met mitzvah. Tosafot answer that the present case involves only a minor indignity, namely appearing before an inferior court. For a small indignity, even passive nonperformance is not permitted where a prohibition is involved. In the cases of a met mitzvah and similar matters, however, the indignity is substantial, and where the violation is passive, human dignity can prevail. This distinction also resolves Tosafot's question concerning a Kohen Gadol. For him, appearing before a lower tribunal can constitute a major degradation. Therefore even in a matter of prohibition he may be exempt where the indignity is severe. Mishneh LaMelech wonders why Rambam omits the rule that a Kohen Gadol must testify in matters of prohibition. According to the distinction above there is no omission: because the degradation to a Kohen Gadol is substantial, the ordinary rule does not necessarily require him to appear. There is support for Tosafot's distinction from several practical rulings. In the laws of tzitzit, if one discovers in public that the garment he is wearing lacks valid tzitzit, he must remove it despite embarrassment. That is treated as a relatively minor indignity. Likewise, where a kohen learns that there is a corpse in the house, he must leave immediately even if this means departing without normal clothing. Chavot Yair was troubled by this, but the embarrassment is limited and understandable to observers, since they know he is leaving because of tumah. Thus, for a small humiliation, one does not invoke human dignity against a Torah prohibition even where the violation is passive. The Gemara's derivations concerning a met mitzvah and returning a lost object can also be reconciled on this basis. The exemption of an elder for whom handling a lost object is beneath his dignity generally concerns a relatively small humiliation. Yet with monetary obligations, Torah specifically allows him to refrain. By contrast, the derivation from a met mitzvah teaches the rule concerning a major degradation in a passive context. Therefore, in our case of testimony, if the indignity is truly substantial, there is strong basis to say that even in a matter of prohibition the scholar need not appear, provided the prohibition would be violated only through his passive nonappearance. Still, there is another conceptual route. According to Rambam, the rule concerning an elder and a lost object means that he is exempt from the strict obligation, though it is praiseworthy to waive his honor and act beyond the strict letter of the law. If so, where the matter involves honoring Hashem, the scholar should certainly waive his own honor. Even where ordinary monetary concerns would not require him to do so, he should not insist on personal dignity while the honor of Heaven is being violated. This is different from the dignity of another person. A person may waive his own honor, but he cannot waive the dignity of someone else. That is why the Torah can require us to attend to a met mitzvah. The deceased person's dignity is not ours to surrender. This distinction between one's own honor and another person's honor may therefore underlie Rambam's position. Rambam's wording in Hilchot Kilayim supports this. He writes that if a person sees kilayim upon his fellow, he jumps upon him and tears the garment away immediately, even if that fellow is his teacher. Kesef Mishneh understands this as the Gemara's general rule about removing kilayim in the marketplace. Why does Rambam formulate it specifically as one person tearing kilayim from another, rather than simply saying that the wearer himself must undress? Because the Gemara's question from human dignity concerns another person's dignity. The observer must strip even his teacher because the prohibition is presently being violated. Where the dignity at issue is one's own, there is an additional reason to require waiver: one must subordinate personal honor to the honor of Heaven. Accordingly, the distinction between major and minor embarrassment remains important, but it is especially relevant where the dignity belongs to another person. Where it is one's own dignity, there is strong reason to be stringent even in a case of substantial embarrassment. Rambam's omission of the Gemara's distinction between passive and active violation in this context can also be explained. The older textual versions of Berakhot derive the rule from several sources and discuss whether the underlying prohibition applies equally to all people. The question from the paschal offering depends on the view that women are obligated in Pesach Sheni. Rambam rules that women participate in Pesach Sheni only voluntarily. Accordingly, Pesach Sheni itself is not a command equally applicable to all persons. Once that is so, the Gemara's need to distinguish between passive and active violation according to that textual version disappears for the practical halachah Rambam adopts. This can explain why Rambam does not formulate the rule through the passive versus active distinction. The older text can therefore be preserved. From this emerges a practical hierarchy. For a minor embarrassment, even a prohibition that is not equally applicable to everyone can override personal dignity. Therefore a scholar ordinarily must testify in a matter of prohibition despite the indignity of appearing before a lesser court. For a major embarrassment, there is greater room to invoke human dignity, particularly where the prohibition is not equally applicable to everyone and the violation is passive. Where the prohibition is universally applicable, one should be stringent even in a passive case of substantial embarrassment, because according to Rambam the distinction between passive and active conduct is not the decisive factor. With tzitzit, which is not equally applicable to everyone, Magen Avraham's distinction between major and minor embarrassment therefore has a basis. Likewise with a kohen and tumah, because the prohibition applies only to kohanim, a minor embarrassment does not justify delay, but a major embarrassment raises a more serious question. However, where the issue is the person's own honor rather than another person's dignity, I incline to greater stringency. A person can and should waive his own honor for the honor of Heaven. Therefore even substantial embarrassment may not excuse him where he can personally prevent a Torah violation. By contrast, one cannot waive another person's dignity. If the prohibition is rabbinic, human dignity has broader force. The Gemara explicitly says to set aside a rabbinic restriction such as a beit hapras where human dignity is at stake, and Rambam rules this plainly. Thus, for rabbinic prohibitions, human dignity can override even in circumstances where a Torah prohibition would require greater stringency. I have written at length because these distinctions are not confined to testimony. They affect many broader areas of halachah. The practical rule is therefore not a single undifferentiated statement. One must consider the severity of the embarrassment, whether the dignity is one's own or another person's, whether the prohibition is Torah or rabbinic, whether it applies equally to all people, and whether the violation occurs actively or passively. The sources of Tosafot and Rambam together require these distinctions.
An element of preventing a prohibition. Sema, seif katan 28, disagrees with Beit Yosef, who says that testimony needed merely to permit a woman to her husband does not require a Torah scholar to go to an inferior court. Sema argues that there is still a prohibition issue. If the woman later receives kiddushin from another man, those kiddushin may be treated as ineffective because she is not considered unmarried. The testimony can therefore prevent serious future prohibition. At first glance this is persuasive. But Beit Yosef's position rests on a broader principle. If every remote future consequence counts as "preventing a prohibition," then even ordinary monetary testimony would always qualify. Suppose the disputed money truly belongs to one litigant, and the other later uses it for kiddushin. The validity of the kiddushin can depend upon who owns that money. Sanhedrin explicitly says that disputes over monetary judgments can lead to questions of forbidden relations and capital liability if a person betroths a woman with money awarded through an invalid judgment. Thus, every monetary case can potentially branch into a severe prohibition. If we treated such future possibilities as sufficient, the distinction between monetary and prohibitory testimony would collapse entirely. The correct rule is that the prohibition must be presently before us or reasonably imminent. A mere speculative future development does not override the scholar's dignity. Certainty is not displaced by a remote doubt. The same applies to testimony concerning a woman's status. If there is presently no concrete prohibition about to occur, we do not require the scholar to degrade himself merely because some future kiddushin might later be attempted. However, where there are concrete grounds to expect a prohibited consequence, such as an actual kiddushin matter already developing, then everyone should agree that the testimony becomes one of preventing prohibition and the scholar must appear. This distinction is also implied by Tosafot at the beginning of Perek Kohen Gadol. The Gemara says that a Kohen Gadol testifies, and Tosafot ask that perhaps this refers to testimony in a matter of prohibition. But testimony for the oath-offering ordinarily concerns monetary evidence, because suppression of purely prohibitory testimony does not generate that offering. Therefore Tosafot must mean a monetary testimony from which an actual prohibition directly branches. In such a case the underlying testimony remains monetary, but because a concrete issur depends upon the result, the scholar must testify. This also resolves a difficulty concerning a king. A king ordinarily cannot testify because his testimony cannot be made subject to ordinary reciprocal punishment and judicial process. Yet after the rabbinic enactment that civil testimony no longer requires the same full framework of examination and reciprocal punishment, there can be monetary testimony from a king where a direct prohibitory consequence depends upon it. Rambam, who rules according to the later enactment, can therefore include a king within the laws of the oath-offering in a case where the testimony is monetary in form but a concrete prohibition branches from it. The earlier Mishnah in Horayot, stated within the older procedural framework, could exclude the king because his testimony would not have qualified under those stricter requirements. Thus the rule is precise. A remote hypothetical prohibition is not enough. A concrete prohibitory consequence that is already materially connected to the testimony is enough to require appearance.
