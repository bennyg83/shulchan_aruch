Rather, they send to him. Meaning the court sends three people, who constitute a beit din, and one beit din can transmit testimony to another. Two would not suffice, because that would be testimony from the mouth of witnesses rather than a court record, unless a communal enactment authorizes special delegates to receive testimony, in which case the enactment governs. See below and Sema seif katan 37.
An aspect of separating from prohibition. In the Perishah and Derishah I wrote that not only where beit din intends to permit a woman because it thinks her husband died and the witness knows he is alive must he testify. The same can apply in the opposite direction, because a stringent ruling may itself lead to another prohibition. For example, after beit din prohibits the woman, another man may perform kiddushin with her while people tell him she is already married and kiddushin therefore cannot take effect, and he may then marry another woman or one of the relatives forbidden because of those first kiddushin. A stringency can thus lead to leniency and violation. There are many similar cases. This is why the Tur carefully says there is an 'aspect' of preventing prohibition.
And there is evidence supporting his words. The Mechaber omitted this requirement because in Beit Yosef he understood Rambam and those who follow him to hold that once beit din knows a litigant is violent, the rule applies to all such persons without further proof. In the Derishah I argued there is no dispute: all agree there must at least be some indication that the witnesses are genuinely afraid to testify because of the person's violence.
Beit din compels his opponent to bring, etc. The Gemara says that once the witnesses come, ordinarily they will testify. It is unlikely they would do both evasive acts: first refuse to come, and after being brought falsely deny knowledge merely out of fear of the violent person.
To testify while standing. The verse says, 'the two men shall stand,' which refers to witnesses, as explained in siman 17. The Tur there notes that the custom developed to seat witnesses and litigants to avoid quarrels.
If the witness is a Torah scholar, etc. The verse 'you shall fear Hashem your God' is expounded to include honoring Torah scholars. Thus the positive commandment of witness standing meets the positive commandment of honoring Torah, and the latter has precedence in the circumstances described in the Gemara.
