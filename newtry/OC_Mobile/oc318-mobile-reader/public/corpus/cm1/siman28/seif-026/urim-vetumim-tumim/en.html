If they were leaning on the pillar, etc. See what I wrote above in siman 17. I explained there that Bach had no conclusive proof against Sema's view that where the witnesses or litigants and the judges are all leaning, one of the two groups must fail to satisfy its required posture. Upon further study I found a proof for Bach from Ran. In Shevuot 30, concerning the wife of Rav Huna, Rav Nachman arranged to rise slightly before her in order to honor the wife of a Torah scholar. The Gemara asks that judges must sit, and answers that he sat in a posture like one who is loosening his shoes. Ran infers from this that litigants are required to stand only at final judgment. Otherwise, how could Rav Nachman manage the entire proceeding? The litigants would have to stand while the judges sat, and he could not remain in that half-rising posture for the whole case. This creates a question from the incident with King Yannai. Shimon ben Shetach told Yannai, "Stand on your feet and let the witnesses testify against you." Tosafot explain that this occurred at the beginning of the proceeding. How could Shimon ben Shetach demand that Yannai stand? If the judges remained seated, they would fail the obligation to rise before a king, and Yannai was also Kohen Gadol, whose honor carries Torah force. If the judges stood, they would violate the rule that judges sit. One cannot answer that they stood in the shoe-loosening posture like Rav Nachman, because that works for the brief moment of final judgment, not for the beginning and full reception of testimony, as Ran explains. The answer must be that Shimon ben Shetach instructed Yannai to lean rather than sit fully. "Stand on your feet" need not mean completely unsupported standing. It means only that he should not sit. The judges could likewise lean. For the honor due a king and Kohen Gadol, their not sitting fully before him satisfies the required rising and deference. At the same time, for the law that judges sit, leaning can count as sitting. Why did Rav Nachman not use this solution with Rav Huna's wife? Because if he ordered her to lean rather than sit, the opposing litigant might feel disadvantaged or intimidated, as Tosafot explain regarding the concern of confusing his claims. With King Yannai, this concern does not apply in the same way. The case was a capital matter and everyone understood that the king's honor required special treatment. Thus both the king and judges could lean. But this proves Bach's point. The judges and litigant can both adopt the same physical posture of leaning, and nevertheless halachah can classify it as standing for one purpose and sitting for the other. If Sema were correct that the verse requires the litigant and judges to occupy visibly different postures, this could not work. The verse "the two men shall stand before Hashem" cannot mean that identical leaning is disqualified simply because both sides look alike. The same posture can legally count as standing for the litigants or witnesses and as sitting for the judges. Therefore Bach's view is compelled. The distinction I suggested earlier in siman 17 should be rejected.
