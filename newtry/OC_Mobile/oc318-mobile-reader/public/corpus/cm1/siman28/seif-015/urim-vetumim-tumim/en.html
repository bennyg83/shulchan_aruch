We do not receive testimony outside the litigant's presence, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah writes that if the witnesses do not recognize the litigant, their testimony is considered equivalent to testimony outside his presence. His proof is from Sanhedrin 79, concerning a murderer who became mixed among others. Since final judgment can be issued only in the defendant's presence and the judges no longer know which person is the murderer, the situation is treated as one in which the defendant is not properly before them. Shevut Yaakov, part 1, siman 148, objects. He proposes that the requirement of the defendant's presence at final judgment exists so that beit din will see him and have greater mercy upon him. On that basis he distinguishes between final judgment and receiving testimony. I am astonished by this. One should not invent reasons for Torah law and then build distinctions upon them. If seeing a defendant merely generates mercy, why should the rule change when there are two defendants? All the more so judges should be cautious before spilling additional blood. And the purpose of beit din is not sentimental mercy in the face of clear law. The judges are already commanded to search for grounds of acquittal through the ordinary judicial process. The Gemara itself distinguishes between an ox and a person because a person can present claims while an ox cannot. That indicates procedural presence, not mere emotional sympathy. The better explanation is textual. The verse says the person shall stand "before the congregation for judgment." R. Akiva also derives from that verse that a beit din which personally witnessed a murder should not itself adjudicate the capital case. There is no contradiction. The principal verse supports R. Akiva's rule, while the extra wording teaches that the litigant himself must be identifiable and standing before the court. This type of layered derivation is common throughout Chazal. Accordingly, Kenesset HaGedolah's basic principle has substantial support. Where the witnesses do not know which person is the litigant about whom they are testifying, the ordinary requirement of testimony in his presence may not be satisfied. However, one qualification is necessary. If the person can later be identified, the court should wait until proper identification is possible and then receive the testimony in the correct manner. If there is no realistic possibility of identification and the testimony will otherwise be lost, the situation is comparable to witnesses who are dangerously ill or about to depart overseas, where testimony can be received outside the litigant's presence to prevent complete loss of evidence. In such an exceptional case, the court may receive it. This appears straightforward.
Although they testified initially pursuant to the ban, etc. Sema, seif katan 48, writes that the same applies if they first testified pursuant to an oath. Because testimony given outside the litigant's presence is not examined as strictly, the witnesses can later change their statements when testifying properly before him. These words require study. Maharam's long responsum builds upon the Mishnah in Shevuot: if witnesses were adjured five times outside beit din and denied knowing testimony, they can be liable for each adjuration because they still retain the ability later to come to beit din and admit what they know. But that Mishnah does not prove that witnesses who actually swore to the truth of their earlier testimony can later reverse themselves. Suppose they swore falsely. Even if the technical laws of shevuat ha'edut do not apply outside beit din, they may still have violated an ordinary false oath and thereby become disqualified from testimony. Ran explains that when witnesses deny inside beit din and become unable to retract, the issue is not only the rule that one who has testified cannot testify differently. Their false oath can itself disqualify them. Therefore one must distinguish the exact form of the oath. If they were merely adjured by another person: "I adjure you, if you know testimony, that you come and testify," and they simply answered outside beit din, "We know nothing," without themselves pronouncing an oath or saying amen, then outside beit din there may be no ordinary oath at all. The special Torah law of the oath of testimony creates liability only under its own conditions. Their denial outside beit din does not yet fix their testimony and does not disqualify them. They can therefore later come to beit din and testify truthfully. Even if the Mishnah's case includes acceptance of the adjuration, it can be understood where they accepted an obligation to testify truthfully before beit din but then merely brushed off the claimant outside court. They did not yet violate the oath because the oath concerned proper testimony in beit din. Once they appear in court and testify correctly, there is no false oath. By contrast, if they explicitly swore, "We do not know any testimony," and that statement was false, then even outside beit din they have uttered a false oath. How can they later qualify as witnesses? One could force a distinction where there were no witnesses to the false oath, so they are believed concerning their own offering liability but cannot disqualify themselves from testimony merely through self-incrimination. But Ran's formulation concerning the in-court case and the Mishnah's broad structure do not support making that the central explanation. The cleaner reading is that the Mishnah speaks in a way that did not render them disqualified through a false oath. This also undermines Sema's generalization. If witnesses swore that their first out-of-court testimony was true, or swore to tell the truth and then knowingly gave false factual testimony, one cannot simply say they were imprecise because the testimony was outside the litigant's presence. They have implicated themselves in a false oath. Kenesset HaGedolah, citing Mishpetei Tzedek, answers that Maharam holds an oath concerning future truthful testimony does not disqualify in the same way. But according to Rama below, even an oath concerning future conduct can create disqualification when violated. Therefore this answer does not fully sustain Rama's adoption of Maharam. I found that R. Gershon Ashkenazi, in Avodat HaGershuni responsum 3, noticed the same difficulty and proposed that the oath of testimony is a special novelty. That too is strained, especially because Ran explicitly uses false-oath disqualification to explain why witnesses who denied inside beit din cannot later testify. The best explanation of Maharam is therefore narrower. They swore that when they would give legally effective testimony, they would tell the truth. Their initial statement was made outside the litigant's presence. Because such a statement does not yet have the halachic status of proper testimony, they can say: "When we spoke then, we did not yet fulfill the oath at all. We had sworn to testify truthfully in a legally valid proceeding." They may therefore correct the matter when they later testify properly before the litigant. This works because whenever possible we interpret their oath in a manner that does not make them transgressors. But if they explicitly swore that the factual statement already made outside court was true, or swore in language covering every statement whether or not it constituted valid testimony, they cannot later reverse themselves without becoming violators of the oath. Rivash, responsum 108, also implies that witnesses who testified under a ban outside the litigant's presence cannot necessarily change their statements freely where the change would make them violators of the ban. Thus the correct rule depends on the formulation. A prior out-of-court statement given under a general obligation to testify truthfully can be corrected when proper testimony is later given before the litigant. But a specific oath or ban affirming the truth of the first statement cannot simply be contradicted later. This is the proper limitation of Maharam and Rama.
