Who first testified under the ban. Ketzot HaChoshen distinguishes between one who swore, 'I do not know testimony for you,' and later witnesses establish only that he saw the event, in which case he may still have genuinely forgotten, and one who swore, 'I never knew testimony for you,' and witnesses establish that he did know, in which case he becomes suspected. His attempted explanation of Tosafot and Rashba is difficult. A statement, 'I did not know,' when the witness presently does not remember, does not itself form the classic oath of testimony, which depends on a current refusal to testify despite ability. It is closer to an ordinary oath of expression. Likewise the distinction he draws between testimony denied outside beit din and inside beit din is not persuasive. A person summoned and sworn outside beit din to come and testify can also violate a present obligation by refusing. The correct principle is that where the facts create a strong objective presumption that he did know and should remember, his contrary oath can disqualify him. For criminal punishment, however, he may still claim forgetfulness or mistake because formal warning must distinguish intentional from inadvertent conduct. For disqualification from future oaths, we follow ordinary evidentiary assessment. Therefore a witness who denies recent, memorable testimony may be treated as wicked even if lashes cannot be imposed. Likewise one who swore, 'I will not eat,' and later ate is disqualified even after time because at the moment of eating he was obligated to remember his oath. This is also the intent of Rashba: if we always accepted a plea of forgetfulness for purposes of disqualification, there would scarcely ever be a person suspected through an oath of testimony. Accordingly, Rama's case where witnesses first spoke falsely outside the litigant's presence under an oath or ban to tell the truth can be explained as Urim VeTumim does: what they said outside the litigant's presence was not formal testimony at all, only narration, and therefore the oath was not violated in the legal sense that would disqualify them from later giving proper testimony.
