They may return and testify. Maharshal rules that once witnesses testified outside the defendant's presence, they can no longer repeat the testimony validly in his presence. Taz says he found the same in Responsa Mahari ben Lev, but notes that they have no clear basis in Gemara or early authorities. On the contrary, Rif and Baal HaItur, cited by Rama below in seif 21, say that one may rely upon the witnesses themselves, implying that they may testify again properly before the party. Baal HaItur elsewhere, however, discusses witnesses who were found related and therefore disqualified, and later became unrelated. He says that once judgment was entered on the basis of their disqualification, the case is not reopened because a person tends to maintain his prior story. This could imply that repetition should not help here either. Yet Baal HaItur also allows witnesses who testified before judges lacking expertise to repeat their testimony before qualified judges. His rulings can be reconciled by saying that the related witnesses case turns on the fact that judgment had already been entered and become fixed, as I explained in siman 33, rather than on a general suspicion that repetition is worthless. There are also clear proofs that repetition can help. Bava Kamma says that testimony given by one witness today and another tomorrow can combine according to Rabbi Yehoshua ben Korcha. Tosafot adds that even according to the opinion that they cannot combine when given separately, if the first witness later returns and testifies together with the second, their testimony can combine. If a person were automatically suspected of merely maintaining his earlier story, the first witness's renewed testimony would never help. Likewise, in Sanhedrin the Gemara discusses completing a matter by bringing the witnesses in, and Rashi explains that initially the judges heard each witness separately during examination. This too shows that testimony can later be repeated in a legally effective setting. Therefore the better view is that witnesses who testified outside the party's presence may later testify again properly in his presence.
By virtue of the ban. Beit Yosef cites this law in the name of Rabbi Menachem, who brings proof from Perek Shevuat HaEdut. The Mishnah says: if witnesses were adjured five times outside beit din and later came to beit din and admitted knowledge of the testimony, they are exempt; if they denied it, they are liable for each adjuration. If they were adjured five times in beit din and denied, they are liable only once. Rabbi Shimon explains that after the first denial in beit din they can no longer return and admit. The implication is that outside beit din, even when they swore on their own initiative, they can later return and admit knowledge of the testimony. This is extremely difficult. Once they have already sworn falsely, have they not become disqualified witnesses? How can they later testify? Ran himself asks a related question. Once they initially said in beit din, "We know no testimony for you," why should repeated adjurations make them liable? They can no longer change their testimony. Ran therefore suggests that the rule that one cannot return and testify is derived from the verse, "if he does not tell," which follows the verse concerning hearing an oath, so perhaps the finality rule operates specifically after a formal adjuration. He also suggests that changing from, "We do not know," to, "We know," may not technically be the ordinary rule of changing testimony. The true obstacle may instead be that they cannot make themselves wicked by admitting that their prior sworn denial was false. Acharonim therefore attempt to explain the Mishnah as involving adjuration by others outside beit din, where the oath of testimony does not take effect in the same way. But the Mishnah and Tosafot indicate that it can also involve witnesses who swear on their own. To clarify the issue we must distinguish claims of forgetfulness from certain proof of wickedness. Ran in Perek Kol HaNishba'in asks how one becomes suspected through a false oath of testimony. Even if witnesses say he testified falsely, perhaps he forgot and was not deliberate. Ran answers that the case must involve testimony so close and memorable that forgetfulness is implausible. Rashba, by contrast, rules that one who is proven to have violated an oath cannot rehabilitate himself merely by saying, "I forgot." He proves this from the law of plotting witnesses. Such witnesses cannot be warned beforehand because they could always say, "We forgot," yet when they are later refuted they are punished. This proof at first seems backwards: the Gemara there sounds as though forgetfulness remains possible. The explanation is that the Torah's special rule of warning requires absolute clarification between inadvertence and deliberateness. Even a scholar who certainly knows the law cannot be executed without warning, because perhaps he forgot in the moment. This is a special novelty of warning. Outside that special context we ordinarily follow the presumption that a person knew and acted deliberately unless there is real evidence otherwise. Plotting witnesses need no warning. Thus their punishment shows that once wrongdoing is proven, they cannot simply excuse themselves by saying, "We forgot." This explains Rashba. Yet there is an important distinction between swearing, "I do not know now," and swearing to a definite past fact, such as, "I never knew." If a person swears that he presently does not know, it may genuinely be true even though ordinarily we would presume he does know. Courts routinely administer an oath of, "I do not know." A presumption sufficient for monetary law does not automatically transform such an oath into certain perjury. This resembles one who swears he paid a debt documented by a note. Even if the note remains in the creditor's hand and the monetary presumption is that he did not pay, he does not thereby become a suspected false swearer, because the oath might still have been true. Likewise Rashba rules that a person who swears he did not waive a claim is not disqualified merely because legal rules might treat his conduct as waiver. Therefore Rashba's rule that one cannot claim forgetfulness applies where the oath is shown certainly false, such as a definite oath about a past event. The standard oath of testimony, however, is often, "We do not know testimony for you." Such an oath can still be literally true because they may have forgotten. Accordingly, outside beit din they can later return and testify without necessarily disqualifying themselves. They can say that at the earlier moment they genuinely did not remember and now they do. In beit din, however, an additional prohibition applies: "if he does not tell, he shall bear his sin." When they deny knowing testimony in beit din, the legal presumption is that they did know, and their failure to testify is treated as a violation of that separate command. For purposes of that independent transgression, a later claim of forgetfulness does not help to erase what the law treated as nonperformance. They cannot then return and admit without making themselves wicked. This explains why repeated adjurations outside beit din can generate repeated liability, while after denial in beit din they cannot reverse course. My brother Rabbi Yehudah also addressed this question and suggested that outside beit din they can rely upon a claim of forgetfulness, though he struggled to explain why the same should not apply inside. The separate prohibition of failing to testify provides the distinction. One should add that violation of "if he does not tell" does not itself necessarily disqualify the witnesses under Torah law, because not every prohibition lacking lashes creates the status of "wicked" needed for testimonial disqualification. This explains why the Tur in siman 34, listing Torah disqualifications for witnesses, mentions false oaths but not specifically the oath of testimony, whereas in siman 92 concerning people suspected of false oaths he includes the oath of testimony. For an oath we do not entrust a person already suspected of oath violation even if that violation did not carry lashes. Yet for testimonial disqualification, Torah law ordinarily requires a transgression punishable by lashes or death, as Rambam explains. Rabbinic disqualification for lesser sins generally requires proclamation. Therefore, after an outside court denial they need not automatically have become disqualified from testimony. The broader question remains regarding Rabbi Menachem's ruling when the witnesses actually gave affirmative sworn testimony outside beit din and later wish to reverse it. There one cannot say merely that they forgot, because the two affirmative versions contradict each other. If no independent witnesses prove which version was false, however, the witnesses are not disqualified solely through their own admission that their first testimony was mistaken. We can divide their words and say the earlier error was inadvertent, rather than allowing them to make themselves wicked. This resembles the rule that when a person says, "So and so violated me willingly," we divide his statement and treat the act as involuntary where necessary to preserve testimonial competence. Tosafot in Ketubot disputes how broadly this doctrine of dividing a statement can be used where one must add an explanation of coercion or mistake. According to one approach it can be used, supporting Rabbi Menachem. According to the other, it cannot. Thus Rabbi Menachem's ruling rests upon major early authorities and is not free of dispute. Ran's own approach, which does not readily allow adding a claim of inadvertence, would explain the Mishnah differently: after a formal denial in beit din they cannot return because of the general rule that testimony cannot be changed, not because of self disqualification. Therefore, where the first testimony was given under oath or ban and then they seek to reverse it, the matter depends upon this underlying dispute. In practice, the position allowing reversal outside beit din has substantial foundation, but it should not be treated as uncontested.
