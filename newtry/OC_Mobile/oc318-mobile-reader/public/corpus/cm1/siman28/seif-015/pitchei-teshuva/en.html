He is not obligated to go and testify. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot HaTur, letter 26, writes that this language implies only that there is no obligation, but if the Torah scholar wishes to waive his honor he may go. Afterward the responsa of the Radbaz were printed, and in siman 119 he records an opinion that the scholar may not waive his honor, but he himself concludes that he may, since a sage who waives his honor has effectively waived it. At first glance this requires study in light of Chavot Yair, siman 205, who connects this question with the dispute between the Rambam and the Rosh regarding an elder for whom returning a lost object is beneath his dignity. He writes that just as a Kohen Gadol may not testify except before the king, and according to the Rosh a Torah scholar who knows testimony may not go where doing so is beneath his dignity, this is not a matter that can simply be waived. If so, the Tur's wording here, as understood by Knesset HaGedolah, would appear inconsistent with the Rosh. However, Torat Chayim on Shevuot 30 explains that although the Gemara's language could imply a prohibition, the Rambam writes only that the scholar 'should refrain,' not that he 'must refrain.' Even according to the Rosh regarding returning a lost object, that case is different, since one can go beyond the letter of the law by foregoing one's own money, as Rabbi Yishmael did. With testimony, by contrast, one can simply waive personal honor and testify. Accordingly, Knesset HaGedolah's inference is correct: he is not obligated, but if he wishes to testify he may. Birkei Yosef above, siman 6, letter 16, discusses this at length and similarly infers from the Gaon's ruling concerning one summoned before a judge inferior to him that he may voluntarily appear. He also distinguishes between testifying or litigating before one inferior to him and cases of returning a lost object or loading an animal, where the public humiliation is more apparent. See also Tumim on this matter.
