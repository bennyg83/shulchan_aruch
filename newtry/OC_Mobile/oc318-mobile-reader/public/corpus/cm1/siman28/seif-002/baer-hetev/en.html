To impose cherem. The Beit Yosef writes in the name of the Rashba that if one already imposed a general cherem upon everyone who knows testimony, he may not afterward impose a separate cherem upon a particular individual. Shach.
To make them swear. Meaning to administer an oath to them individually before beit din, requiring them to say, "We accept upon ourselves an oath and curse," etc., or to answer amen. It appears that the same applies to cherem in this manner, and they need not make a separate verbal acceptance, since oath and cherem are treated similarly in this regard. Only in the synagogue may one impose a general cherem or oath upon the community by saying, "Whoever among you knows," or upon specifically identified individuals. The Rivash writes that where witnesses come to testify concerning a transgression and there is reason to fear that intimidation will cause them to withhold their testimony, they may be required to swear. Thus the Sema.
They disagree. The Sema writes that the view stated here is that of the Or Zarua. Rabbeinu Eliezer ben Shmuel and the Mordechai write the opposite, that an oath does take effect upon relatives because beit din formulates it with the implicit condition needed to investigate the truth. Darkei Moshe cites both views and records the Mordechai's opinion last. The Sema therefore questions why Rama writes here that the opposing view is the primary one.
Primary. Although in siman 16, seif 3, the Mechaber rules without qualification that even where evidence or testimony favorable to one party is known by the opposing party, that party must disclose it, and Rama makes no gloss, one must say that there the opposing party knows who the witnesses are or where the evidence can be found. It does not concern oral testimony that the opposing party himself would be required to give. Sema.
