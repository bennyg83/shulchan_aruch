But he cannot administer an oath to them. This means that he cannot have the witnesses brought separately before beit din and compel them to say, "We accept upon ourselves by curse and oath that we know no testimony for him," or compel them to answer amen to an oath imposed upon them. It appears to me that the same applies to a cherem imposed in this individualized manner. Oath and cherem are equivalent for this purpose. Only in the synagogue may one impose a general cherem or oath upon the entire congregation, by saying, "Whoever among you knows testimony," or upon specifically identified individuals, in the manner discussed in Hagahot Darkei Moshe, seif 3. So writes the Sema. Responsa Mabit, part 2, siman 283, likewise cites a scholar who held that as a matter of law one may neither administer an oath to witnesses nor impose a cherem upon them. This is contrary to the Mabit himself, who disagrees and holds that one may administer an oath and impose a cherem against their will. He also writes this in his responsa indices. The proof he brings from Tosafot, Rashi, Ran, and Rambam, whose words imply that if the claimant administered an oath to the witnesses and they said, "We know no testimony for you," they become liable, is not decisive. Once they respond, "We know no testimony for you," they have thereby accepted the oath upon themselves that they know nothing. Likewise, what he cites from his teacher Rivash in the name of Ramban, that even if they remain silent they are liable, follows the view that silence of this kind is deemed an admission. But all agree that one cannot force them to take an oath or accept a cherem. The scholar therefore wrote well that the rule is obvious. See Beit Yosef, column 4, in the name of Rashba, that after one imposes a cherem generally upon the congregation, he cannot then impose a second cherem upon an individual.
