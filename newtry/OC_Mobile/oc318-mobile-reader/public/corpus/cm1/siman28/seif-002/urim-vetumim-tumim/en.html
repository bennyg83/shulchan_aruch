A person may impose a ban, etc. This means the litigant himself may stand and declare that anyone who knows testimony relevant to his case must come and testify. He may likewise impose an oath in an appropriate manner. But I am doubtful whether beit din itself should routinely issue such a ban in its own name against everyone who knows testimony. What business does beit din have becoming the litigant's advocate? The court must judge the evidence brought before it, not search for proofs on behalf of one side. There can be exceptions, such as orphans, where beit din acts as the guardian of their interests, or other special needs of the hour. But ordinarily the court itself should not issue a communal ban merely to help a litigant assemble evidence. This is why the language is precise: "a person may impose a ban." This also resolves Beit Yosef's difficulty. In several responsa Rashba permits a litigant to impose a ban in the synagogue upon anyone who knows testimony to come forward. Yet in responsum 900 Rashba writes, "We have not heard of imposing a ban in beit din." The distinction is exactly as above. Rashba permits the litigant to issue the public call. What he rejects as unfamiliar is the court itself issuing the ban in its own judicial capacity. That would make the judges appear like advocates. The distinction is clear.
Some disagree. This is the primary view. Sema, seif katan 6, asks why Rama decides the matter when the poskim themselves disagree. There is no difficulty. Rashba and Rosh in responsa both support this second view. From them Torah goes forth, and in their light we see light. There is also a substantial practical reason. Below, siman 36, where a group witnessed an event with intent to testify and later came to beit din, Shach holds that if valid and invalid witnesses actually appear together pursuant to a summons, the participation of the invalid witnesses can disqualify the valid testimony. As I explain there, at minimum everyone agrees that where the parties or court deliberately intend the relatives and disqualified witnesses to come together and testify, the valid testimony is invalidated because they have been made part of one evidentiary unit. Now consider the view of Rosh and Tosafot that merely seeing the event with intent to testify does not invalidate the valid witnesses so long as the relatives or invalid witnesses never actually come to beit din and testify. How then could beit din proclaim a ban that compels all relatives and disqualified people who saw the event to come and testify? Without the proclamation, those invalid witnesses might never come, and the valid testimony would remain intact. By forcing them to appear, beit din itself would create the joinder that destroys the valid evidence and thereby cause loss to the litigant. That cannot be proper. The first opinion, which includes relatives in the proclamation, can be understood according to Rambam and those who hold that the joinder already occurs at the time of seeing the event with intent to testify. Under that view, bringing the relatives later does not newly damage the valid witnesses. If they intended to testify at the time of seeing, the valid testimony was already disqualified; if they did not, their later appearance does not invalidate it. But according to Rosh and those who follow him, compelling relatives to appear can itself create the problem. Therefore the practical law should follow the view that relatives and otherwise disqualified persons are not included in the general ban compelling witnesses to come. Sema also asks from siman 16, where even a litigant himself can sometimes be compelled to provide information. There is no contradiction. A litigant's own statement does not become part of the evidentiary unit in the technical rule of "the matter shall stand by the witnesses." His participation therefore does not invalidate other witnesses in the way a relative or disqualified witness can. Here, however, compelling invalid witnesses to join the testimony can destroy otherwise valid evidence. The distinction is clear.
