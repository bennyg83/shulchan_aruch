If the witness is a Torah scholar, etc. Rambam writes that if the litigant is a Torah scholar, it is permissible for him to remind the witness of the matter, because a Torah scholar understands that the witness may testify only if he genuinely remembers the event and is not merely relying on the litigant's suggestion. From Rambam's words it appears that the concern with an ordinary litigant is suspicion. The opposing party may suspect that the witness is not testifying from his own memory but is merely echoing what the litigant told him. When the litigant is a Torah scholar, that suspicion is reduced because he is presumed to understand the proper limits. Bach asks against Rashi's explanation of the Gemara's incident involving Rav Kahana and R. Elazar. Rav Kahana reminded R. Elazar of the details. R. Elazar initially said he did not remember. Eventually he recalled and testified. Rav Kahana hesitated, and R. Elazar told him: "Do you think I am relying on you? I exerted myself and remembered independently." Bach asks: according to Rashi, if the witness himself is a Torah scholar, he may testify after being reminded. Why then did Rav Kahana hesitate? According to Rambam, the case is easier because the reason a Torah scholar litigant is permitted to remind is that he will question and investigate the witness until it is clear the witness remembers independently. That is exactly what happened. But Bach's distinction creates a difficulty of its own. Beit Yosef writes that even where neither party is a Torah scholar, if the witness appears and beit din thoroughly questions him and he says that he now remembers independently, the testimony can be accepted. Bach rejects this because no earlier authority states it. Yet if the entire reason a Torah scholar litigant may remind the witness is that he will verify whether the witness truly remembers, then surely investigation by beit din should be at least as effective. This shows the concern cannot be merely lack of inquiry. The principal concern is indeed suspicion and appearance. Even after questioning, an ordinary litigant may still suspect that the witness was influenced by the reminder. The special rule for a Torah scholar reduces that concern because he is presumed to understand the halachic requirement and not to manufacture testimony. Why then did Rav Kahana hesitate? Because R. Elazar initially said explicitly that he did not remember. Even after Rav Kahana supplied the facts, R. Elazar still had not immediately recalled them. That naturally created concern that his later statement might still rest on Rav Kahana's words. R. Elazar therefore reassured him that he subsequently exerted himself and recovered his own independent memory. The incident still proves that a Torah scholar may remind the witness. It also shows that the witness must genuinely recover his own recollection. I would add a practical distinction. The litigant himself should not ordinarily remind the witness. But if the litigant conveys the factual details to beit din and asks the judges or a court emissary to question the witness about them, that is more lenient. A third party may remind the witness, and the fact that the information ultimately originated from the litigant does not transform the judge or emissary into the litigant himself. This also explains Tur's statement that where a litigant is ill and asks beit din to receive testimony during his lifetime because he can help the witnesses recall the details, the rule is stated broadly and not only for a Torah scholar. The litigant can supply the relevant facts to beit din, and the judges can then question the witnesses until they recover their own memory. The entire restriction is rabbinic and rooted in suspicion and improper appearance. We therefore need not extend it beyond its stated scope. For practical law, this distinction appears sound.
