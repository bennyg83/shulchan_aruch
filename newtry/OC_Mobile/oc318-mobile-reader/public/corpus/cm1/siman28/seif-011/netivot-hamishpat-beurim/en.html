Unlike those who validate, etc. See Sema, citing Tosafot and Rosh that testimony may be sent in writing. Even according to Rabbeinu Tam, who validates testimony from writing, it does not become testimony before beit din until the writing actually reaches the court. The witness may therefore retract before it arrives, as Rama writes in responsum 12. If he dies, loses speech, or forgets the testimony before the writing reaches beit din, the written statement is invalid even according to Rabbeinu Tam. A formal deed differs because the testimony is treated as investigated in beit din from the moment of signature. Ordinary written testimony becomes testimony only when the court receives and reads it. Accordingly, even where testimony may not be received outside a litigant's presence, Rabbeinu Tam can allow the witness to send the writing to beit din, because the judicial act of testimony occurs only when the court reads it in the litigant's presence. The same applies today for a Torah scholar even according to our practice. Urim VeTumim disagrees, arguing that a witness is less embarrassed to write outside the litigant's presence than to testify before him. That is not compelling. The requirement of presence is a scriptural procedural rule, not merely a psychological safeguard against brazenness; even testimony concerning an ox sentenced to stoning requires the ox's presence. Moreover beit din can arrange to be present at the writing, and a witness who knows the writing will be read in the litigant's presence is not necessarily more brazen.
