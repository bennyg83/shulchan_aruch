(In the Tur: if the litigant is violent, etc.) In either event there is a difficulty. If the litigant is already known to be violent by beit din, why is it necessary to teach this rule specifically where there is proof? Even an ordinary person becomes regarded as violent once witnesses establish such conduct. If, on the other hand, he is not otherwise known as violent and becomes so only through this proof, why does the Tur later write, "otherwise, anyone who has witnesses," etc.? Why frame the difficulty in terms of a violent person when it would apply equally to an ordinary person, as the Beit Yosef writes in the name of Tosafos: otherwise anyone could say that his fellow is violent. One may answer that the case indeed concerns one who was already known to be violent. The novelty is that one might have thought that no proof at all is required once he has such a reputation. The ruling teaches that even a known violent person requires proof. It is nevertheless obvious that this proof itself can establish a person as violent even if he had not previously been known as such.
