And not like those who validate. Sema writes that Tosafot, Rosh, Mordechai, and Tur follow Rabbeinu Tam, who holds that one who is capable of testifying orally may send written testimony to beit din, based on the principle that whatever is fit for oral expression can sometimes be expressed in writing. Sema says he saw teachers who did not trouble a Torah scholar to come personally to court but instead asked him to write his testimony and send it. I am surprised. According to Rabbeinu Tam, why does the Gemara say that a Torah scholar should not go to testify before a court inferior to him? He could simply write the testimony and send it. This is especially difficult according to authorities who extend Rabbeinu Tam's rule even to matters of prohibition. Likewise, when the Gemara says there is no wisdom or understanding against Hashem in a matter of prohibition, why not solve the problem through written testimony? There is no question concerning an elderly person for whom appearing is beneath his dignity if he is intrinsically incapable of oral testimony in that setting, because then the condition of being fit for oral testimony may itself be absent. But a Torah scholar who could testify before a greater court is certainly fit for oral testimony in principle. The real limitation is different. Ordinarily, testimony must be received in the presence of the litigant. What does it help for the witness to write privately and send his statement to beit din? Even if the writing counts as testimony, it was not given in the opposing party's presence. It is strained to say that reading the writing aloud later in the litigant's presence converts it into testimony before him. The entire purpose of requiring presence is that the witness confront the party directly and be less willing to lie in his face. Writing elsewhere lacks that restraint. If reading the document later sufficed, then whenever witnesses were in a hurry to leave overseas, they could simply write their testimony, depart, and have the court read it later in the litigant's presence. The special rules allowing testimony outside the party's presence in urgent situations would become unnecessary. Therefore Rabbeinu Tam's rule applies only in circumstances where testimony need not be received in the litigant's presence, as discussed below in seif 16. In those cases written testimony from one who is otherwise fit can be valid. Where the testimony must be given in the litigant's presence, written testimony does not solve the problem. This explains the Gemara concerning the Torah scholar. Sema's report of the custom must therefore be limited to cases where the opposing party's presence is not required. His words are too abbreviated if read more broadly. This also resolves the debate whether Rabbeinu Tam's rule can apply in capital law or in prohibitory matters analogous to capital cases. In genuine capital law, testimony must certainly be given in the defendant's presence. Therefore written testimony has no practical place there. There is further proof. Sanhedrin says that false witnesses are treated as having no physical act because speech is not an act. If capital testimony could be delivered by writing, false witnesses could sometimes perform an actual physical act through the writing itself. Yet the Gemara still classifies their offense as lacking an act. Necessarily, in the capital context relevant there, written testimony is not valid. This also answers Maharsha's question in Shevuot concerning why Tosafot prove that false testimony lacks an act from another source rather than from the explicit Sanhedrin baraita. The Sanhedrin source concerns capital liability, where written testimony is invalid. The other source concerns lashes, where the rules can resemble civil law more closely and written testimony might otherwise have been possible. Therefore the additional proof was needed. The practical rule is clear. Rabbeinu Tam's validation of written testimony applies only where the ordinary requirement of testimony in the litigant's presence is absent. Where presence is required, the witness must testify orally before beit din.
