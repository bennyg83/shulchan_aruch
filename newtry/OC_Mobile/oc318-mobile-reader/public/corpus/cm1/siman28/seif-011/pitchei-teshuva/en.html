But if it can be established. Sema, seif katan 22, writes that this rule ordinarily does not arise between Jews, because Jewish law does not extract money on the testimony of one witness. Noda BiYehudah, second edition, end of siman 16, notes that this is not literal, since there are certainly cases in Jewish law where one witness can produce monetary liability, as the case of Rabbi Abba's ingot demonstrates. Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 147, discusses a Jew who sued a non-Jew in a secular court. The non-Jew denied the debt and produced one Jewish witness who testified that he owed nothing, causing the court to dismiss the claim. Later it became established, including by the witness's own admission, that he had testified falsely. Shevut Yaakov rules that the witness is not liable in human law. In Shevuot 32, one who causes the loss of a single witness is exempt because something that only causes monetary liability is not treated as money itself. Here the witness merely caused the non-Jew to be exempt from an oath, which is indirect damage and therefore exempt, all the more so when the testimony was given for a non-Jew. Rama's statement in siman 28, seif 3, that if it can be established that the witness testified falsely he must pay, refers to testimony that affirmatively imposes monetary liability. By contrast, where the witness testified to exempt someone, as in seif 4, Rama did not state liability, because payment cannot be imposed. Sema's wording likewise proves this, for he says the rule does not arise between Jews because money is not extracted by one witness. If false testimony that merely exempts another created liability, the rule would arise between Jews as well. Thus in such a case no monetary liability is imposed on the witness.
