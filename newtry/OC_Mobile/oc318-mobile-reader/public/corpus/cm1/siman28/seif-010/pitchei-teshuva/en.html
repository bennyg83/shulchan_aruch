If the Jewish litigant admits that he truly owes the non-Jew but merely wishes to evade payment, the witness is not placed under ban as he would be where two witnesses are available. If a lone witness instead seeks to testify falsely against his fellow Jew, he plainly may also be liable to pay, and this is the case addressed by Rambam, Hilchot Talmud Torah chapter 6, and Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 334. Here, however, the defendant says he does not owe the non-Jew, while one witness says, 'I know that you do.' Since the Torah beit din hearing them does not accept one witness against the defendant's denial for monetary purposes, from our legal perspective it is as though the Jew owes nothing and the witness is about to give false testimony against him. We therefore warn the witness not to testify, and if he disregards the warning we place him under ban for defying beit din, as stated in the Hagahot Mordechai. But we cannot extract money from the witness, because as against a monetary claim the defendant, who is in possession of his property, is believed that the truth is with him. This, Chatam Sofer writes, resolves several difficulties raised by later authorities.
