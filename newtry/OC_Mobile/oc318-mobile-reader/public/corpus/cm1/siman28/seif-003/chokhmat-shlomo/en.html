There, Seif 11. “Witnesses who sent their testimony in writing.” Note: Gittin 71a cites Rav Kahana in the name of Rav concerning a deaf person who can communicate through writing, and later challenges from a baraita that one tests such a person for matters including testimony. The flow of the sugya is difficult, because even without Rav Kahana’s statement the two baraitot could apparently have been set against each other. Tosafot struggle with this. It appears to me that there is room to distinguish between one who was mute from birth and one who became mute later. We find a parallel in Yevamot: a man who became a eunuch through human action may perform chalitzah, while one who was naturally a eunuch cannot, because the first had a time when he was fit and the second never did. Even where the law requires present capacity for children, prior fitness can matter. So too, perhaps the requirement that a witness be capable of oral testimony excludes only one who was never able to speak. One who once could testify orally and later became mute might still be treated differently. The baraita deriving “If he does not testify” to exclude a mute could then concern one who never had a period of fitness, while the baraita about testing him for testimony could concern one who became mute later. There would be no contradiction. Rav Kahana’s statement, however, concerns a deaf person who neither hears nor speaks. Even if he became deaf only later, prior fitness cannot readily help him, because a deaf-mute who is exempt from mitzvot is legally like a person whose status has fundamentally changed. We find in Kiddushin, regarding acts that take effect only after a slave is freed, that a change of legal identity can make the subject comparable to something not yet in existence. Here the physical and legal condition has changed even more. One cannot simply say that his earlier capacity remains legally operative. Therefore, if Rav Kahana nevertheless permits written testimony, the Gemara can properly challenge him from the exclusion of a mute. The Gemara then answers from “by their mouth and not from their writing.” The language of this derivation implies that written testimony is invalid even from witnesses who are presently capable of speaking. This supports the opinion that the exclusion is not limited to those physically unable to speak. Tosafot in Yevamot likewise understand “by their mouth and not from their writing” in this broad sense. Once that is established, the Gemara can again ask from the baraita that allows testing such a person for testimony, because prior fitness would no longer solve the problem. Thus the sugya can be read coherently, and it provides support for the Rambam and those who follow him. I later saw a commentary stating that there is no reason to distinguish between one mute from birth and one who became mute later. In my view there is a strong reason, as shown by the law of a eunuch: one who was once fit may retain a different legal status. The explanation above therefore appears correct, with Hashem’s help.
In the gloss. “Unlike those who validate written testimony where the witnesses are capable of testifying orally and are not mute.” See my responsum to Premishlan, incorporated in Even HaEzer, sixth edition siman 120, and my comments to Even HaEzer siman 119. I wrote there that even according to the permissive opinion, perhaps the writing helps only if the witnesses signed by day, when they were then fit to testify orally. If they signed at night, when they were not presently fit for oral testimony, written testimony may not be effective. The same would apply to a document: on this approach, a document signed at night would lack Torah validity. See there.
