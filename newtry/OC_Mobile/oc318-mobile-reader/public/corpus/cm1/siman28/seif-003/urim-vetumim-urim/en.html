Yam Shel Shlomo in Bava Kamma, as well as the Sema and Bach, ask from Yoreh Deah siman 334, where the Rambam's language is cited: "A Jew who testifies against a Jew in gentile court and thereby causes money to be extracted from him contrary to law is placed under cherem until he pays." The Sema answers that "he is not liable to pay" here means that beit din does not seize his property, but the cherem is nevertheless not released until he pays. He then asks why this differs from one who was obligated to take a hesset oath and refused, whose cherem is lifted after thirty days even though he still has not paid. See Tumim, where I distinguished between the cherem here and that case. But if it can be established that he testified falsely, he must pay. This law is stated where a Jew testifies for a gentile against a Jew rather than one Jew testifying against another Jew, because under Jewish law money ordinarily cannot be extracted through one witness, while with two witnesses there can generally be no later clarification that they lied, since two witnesses have the evidentiary force of one hundred. Sema. One must ask why the Gemara did not simply discuss one Jew testifying against another in secular court. Rama in a responsum, siman 52, explains that when the Gemara says, "a Jew who knows testimony for a gentile," rather than one Jew against another, it does not discuss wicked people who voluntarily submit Jewish disputes to secular law. This provides some evidence against the Sema's view above in siman 26 that if parties made a kinyan obligating themselves to litigate under secular law, and one party has greater rights there than under Jewish law, he may proceed under secular law. According to that view there are legitimate cases where two Jews go to secular court. Still, one may answer that since the party accepted the secular legal rights by kinyan, as the Sema explains, he also accepted the secular evidentiary rule that one witness suffices. In such a case one Jewish witness would therefore actually be believed under the parties' undertaking, and he would be permitted to testify. Accordingly, if a Jew initially undertook a complete obligation toward a gentile to accept all gentile laws, procedures, and details, he may also have accepted the reliability of one witness, so that even under Jewish law the single witness would be effective and permitted to testify. This requires study, for perhaps a distinction remains. In any event, the Sema's explanation is incomplete, because even under Jewish law there are many cases where one witness is effective, such as the case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba and other examples. Where a litigant has a migo against a single witness he cannot contradict the witness. Even if the litigant admits the underlying event, as in the silver ingot case where he admits that he seized it, the witness may nevertheless have lied about having been present at the seizure. Without that false testimony the litigant could have been believed to say, "I seized my own property," while because of it he loses. Similar cases are numerous. Rather, in a dispute between Jews, the other Jewish litigant who collected the money through false testimony must return it once it is established that the collection resulted from false evidence. This is even stronger than an error in an explicit rule. There is therefore no need to place the witness under cherem or collect from him. The unusual case where the adverse litigant has gone overseas or the like is too remote for the Gemara and Shulchan Aruch to formulate the general law around it. They therefore speak of testimony for a gentile, from whom a Jewish beit din lacks power to recover. See Yam Shel Shlomo, who also writes that where one testifies between two Jews we do not place the witness under cherem, though his reasoning is strained. In Tumim I left unresolved whether, when a Jew seeks to extract money from a gentile through a document or similar proof, the same cherem applies to a single Jewish witness, even though robbing a gentile is forbidden.
