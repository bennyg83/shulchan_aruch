But he is not obligated to pay, etc. See Urim for Sema's view that the witness is nevertheless placed under nidui and the nidui is not lifted until he satisfies the injured party. Sema asks why this differs from one who is obligated to take a shevuat hesset but refuses. Such a person is placed under nidui, yet after thirty days the nidui can be lifted even if he has not sworn or paid. One can defend Sema. There, perhaps the defendant truly owes nothing. Why should we use nidui indefinitely to force money from a person merely because the claimant has an unproven monetary demand? Here, however, even if the witness speaks truthfully, Tosafot ask that he still caused loss, since avoiding payment of a non-Jew's loan can sometimes be permitted. Tosafot answer that this does not create an enforceable monetary claim, but the witness nevertheless acted improperly toward his fellow. Therefore, so long as uncertainty remains whether the witness testified truthfully, the nidui is not lifted merely upon his own assertion. Even on his own version he may have acted wrongfully, and he should appease the injured party. This is possible, but I prefer a different distinction. Suppose the witness testifies in a secular court that he personally saw the Jewish defendant borrow the money, and the defendant denies ever borrowing. If the witness speaks truthfully, he has not caused unlawful loss at all. The defendant genuinely owes the money. In such a case the witness is exempt from payment, and the nidui issue is more limited. But other types of secular testimony are different. For example, the witness testifies that the defendant admitted the debt before him. Under Torah law the defendant might still have defenses such as "I was joking" or "I admitted only so as not to appear wealthy." Or the witness testifies that a loan occurred, while the defendant says he later repaid it. Under Torah law a borrower can ordinarily claim repayment even where the original loan had witnesses. Non-Jewish courts may reject those defenses and impose payment based on the witness's statement. In such a case, even if the witness says, "Everything I testified was true," he may still have caused a monetary loss contrary to Torah law if the defendant's additional defense is also true. Why is the witness not automatically required to pay? Because the damage is not certain. He can say: perhaps you never repaid; perhaps your supposed jest or self-deprecating admission was not genuine. Beit din cannot extract damages based upon an uncertain dina d'garmei. But the witness's conduct remains improper because he exposed another Jew to a system that could collect money without recognizing Torah-law defenses. Therefore nidui can remain until he satisfies the injured party. This explains Rambam's wording in Yoreh Deah, which speaks of a Jew who testified and thereby caused money to be extracted "not according to law." That phrase is exact. Even where the factual words of the witness were true, the monetary extraction could still be contrary to Torah law because the foreign court ignored valid defenses. The wording there says simply "a Jew who testified," not specifically "one witness," because this problem can arise even with two witnesses if the secular system refuses defenses that Torah law would recognize. Bach also notices the issue, but he concludes that in such a case the witness must pay full damages under dina d'garmei. That is too strong. The damage itself remains uncertain until it is established that the defendant truly had the defense he asserts. We do not extract money from the witness based on a possibility. Hagahot Mordechai, cited by Darkei Moshe, supports this distinction. It says that if one can establish that the witness lied, or there are witnesses that the debt had already been repaid, then the witness must pay. Why require proof of repayment? If Bach were correct, the witness should owe whenever his testimony deprived the defendant of the ability to claim repayment, even without proof that repayment actually occurred. The fact that Hagahot Mordechai requires proof shows that absent clarification the witness is not monetarily liable because the damage remains uncertain. Thus the correct rule is: Where wrongful loss is established, payment can be imposed. Where the witness's statement may have been true and the defendant's supposed Torah-law defense is unproven, no monetary damages are extracted from the witness, although the nidui consequences can remain because his conduct was improper. This is the coherent reading of the sources.
For he can say, "I testified truthfully." I have some doubt about the following case. A Jew holds a promissory note against a non-Jew. A single Jewish witness testifies in the secular court that the note was already paid. Under that legal system one witness is believed, and the Jewish creditor thereby loses the note. Should the witness be placed under nidui, or can he say: "I testified truthfully. I acted in order to prevent my fellow Jew from stealing from the non-Jew"? Where a non-Jew seeks to collect from a Jew and a Jewish witness testifies against the Jew, the witness can be placed under nidui even though he insists he testified truthfully. Avoiding payment of a non-Jew's loan can in some circumstances be permitted, and the witness had no right to cause collection through a forum that gives one witness excessive force. His conduct is at least suspect and improper. But in the reverse case, the Jew is seeking to collect from the non-Jew. If the witness truly knows the debt was already paid, then continued collection would be actual theft from the non-Jew, and theft from a non-Jew is prohibited. The witness can therefore say: "I acted to save my fellow Jew from a Torah prohibition of theft." How can beit din place him under nidui when he presents a definite claim of proper motive and truthful testimony? What led me to this doubt is Tosafot's question on the Gemara's statement that only one Jewish witness is punished but two are not. Tosafot ask that even two should sometimes be prohibited, since avoiding payment of a non-Jew's debt can be permitted. If the Gemara's statement were limited to cases where the Jewish defendant truly owed the money, Tosafot's question would be unnecessary. One might answer that Tosafot merely ask why the Gemara speaks categorically, since there exist some circumstances where even two witnesses should be punished. But even after Tosafot's answer, many other examples remain where two witnesses can cause improper collection, such as where they testify to an admission but Torah law would allow a claim of jest or self-deprecation, or where they establish the original loan but Torah law would allow a later claim of repayment. Therefore the strongest reading is that Tosafot assume the case concerns specifically the permitted withholding of a non-Jew's loan. That suggests that where the Jew is attempting to collect what he does not truly deserve from a non-Jew, and the witness says he testified truthfully in order to prevent theft, we do not place him under nidui. The same can apply even to a single witness. This is what inclines me toward leniency, though the matter requires further study.
