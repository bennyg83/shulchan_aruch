And some say that if he undertook upon himself. Tumim writes that in his view there is no actual dispute. The first opinion, a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 114, concerns one who undertakes to support a poor person. Such an undertaking has the character of a vow, because a verbal pledge to charity is binding, and ordinary vows are interpreted stringently. He must therefore support the poor person for his entire life if he remains poor, or for as long as he needs support. If the recipient becomes wealthy, the charitable obligation ends and there is no continuing vow. The second opinion, that of the Rosh in a responsum, concerns an ordinary monetary undertaking where the law of vows is inapplicable; there the burden of proof rests on the claimant in every respect. Tumim considers this correct in law. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 9, writes similarly and explains why the Rashba in his novellae to Gittin 75b and the Ran there were uncertain. Their case is not one who voluntarily undertook to support another, but one who receives a house on condition that he support the donor's son or father. The issue then depends on the donor's presumed intent, not merely on the recipient's own undertaking. The price cannot clarify the matter because the property was not sold for consideration, but given as a gift in exchange for the recipient's undertaking to support the relative. Chatam Sofer therefore analyzes the matter as a double doubt: perhaps the halachah follows Rava that even in gittin the phrase means for as long as needed; and even if the halachah follows Rav Ashi, perhaps monetary law differs. In addition, because the property was given conditionally, it remains in the original owner's presumptive possession until the condition is fulfilled. Thus the ordinary rule that the claimant must prove a maintenance obligation is reversed with respect to retaining the property. Chatam Sofer further discusses the Sema and Shach below at the beginning of siman 212 regarding a house 'to dwell in' and concludes: A person who vows to support a poor person without specification must support him for as long as he needs. If it is merely a contractual undertaking, not a charitable vow, the burden of proof rests on the claimant and all agree that a minimal period can suffice. If one gives 'a house to dwell in,' there is a dispute between Rav Hai Gaon and Rav Nachshon Gaon, and we rule like Rav Hai that even a short period suffices. But where the recipient did not voluntarily bind himself and instead another gave him property, whether land or even movables still under the donor's or guardians' control, on condition that he support the donor's son, there is a genuine doubt even where the beneficiary is not poor. Because of the double doubt the gift remains with the original owner, and the recipient does not acquire it unless he provides support for as long as the son needs. Responsa Mayim Chayim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 6, discusses a community that appointed a man as shochet and bodek without fixing a term. At first glance this might depend on the Rama's dispute here, but Mayim Chayim writes that the Rosh's lenient view applies to undertakings such as supporting another, allowing residence in a house, or exempting someone from communal taxes. Appointment to a communal office is different, because the custom is that so long as no disqualification is found the appointee is not removed. This is also implied by Orach Chayim, siman 53, seif 26. Therefore even the Rosh would agree that the shochet was hired for life or for as long as the community needs him.
