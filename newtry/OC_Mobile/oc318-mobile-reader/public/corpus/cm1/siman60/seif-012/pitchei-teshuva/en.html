But. See Noda BiYehudah, simanim 27 and 28. His son-in-law, Rabbi Yosef, maintained that where one sells something not yet in existence by personally obligating himself to deliver it, ordinary warranty is presumed to have been included through scribal error, even a warranty extending to after-acquired property. Even authorities who hold in siman 112 that after-acquired property is not presumed through scribal error reason there that a person ordinarily does not focus on property he may never acquire. Here, however, the very undertaking concerns what he will later acquire, and if he never acquires it there is no performance at all. It is therefore reasonable to presume that he intended the future assets to stand behind the obligation. Noda BiYehudah responds that although this is logically attractive, he does not agree and in fact holds the opposite. The reason ordinary warranty is presumed is that a lender does not ordinarily throw away his money, and a purchaser does not ordinarily buy for nothing; when the subject matter is certain, each expects security. One who contracts over something not yet in existence, however, knowingly enters uncertainty, since perhaps the seller will never acquire it. Having accepted that uncertainty, he may equally have accepted the risk of acquiring without warranty. Accordingly, warranty must be written explicitly even as to assets presently owned, all the more so as to after-acquired property. See there and see above, seif katan 10.
