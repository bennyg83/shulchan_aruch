Rambam is challenged from the Mishnah that one does not collect consumption of produce or improvement of land from encumbered property, implying that one can nevertheless create such obligations personally even though their amount is not fixed. Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharshal Chayun that Rambam invalidates an unquantified obligation only where a person undertakes it gratuitously. If consideration was given, the person fully resolves to obligate himself because of the money received. This answers the Mishnah concerning a purchaser's recovery of improvements. Yet Rambam also rules that if a donor accepts responsibility for a gift, a creditor can collect improvement as from a purchaser, even though the donor received no price. The answer is that where a donor accepts responsibility, he certainly owes the donee at least the donee's expenses. Those expenses are not an unquantified voluntary obligation, because the donee incurred them on the donor's reliance and acts effectively as the donor's agent. Once the donee has someone from whom to recover expenses, the creditor can collect additional improvement under Rif and Rambam's approach. There is also a more general rationale. Rambam's concern with an unquantified undertaking is a form of asmachta, because one may not fully resolve to accept an unknown amount. But in an ordinary sale, responsibility is such an assumed term that the Sages treat its omission as a scribal error. That demonstrates genuine intent and removes asmachta. A separate question arises from the verse of recognition concerning a firstborn son. The Gemara asks why the Torah needs the father's special credibility, since according to Rabbi Meir a person can transfer even future property. Why is that question limited to Rabbi Meir? Even according to the Sages, one can personally obligate himself in a fixed monetary amount and encumber property. The answer is that later acquired property is encumbered only rabbinically according to the Sages, because by Torah law one cannot encumber what does not yet exist, just as one cannot sell it. Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef say that the Sages strengthened liens on after acquired property to avoid locking the door before lenders. Therefore a father cannot by Torah law accomplish the equivalent of firstborn inheritance regarding property not yet acquired. Rabbi Meir, who holds one can transfer future property by Torah law, creates the Gemara's question. Kesef Mishneh's suggestion that he can undertake an enormous fixed debt and encumber present and future property works only according to Rabbi Meir on the Torah level. According to the Sages the personal obligation can arise because the obligor himself exists, but the lien on property not yet in existence does not arise biblically. Therefore after his death the heirs are not bound through those later assets absent a valid property lien. This also answers the objection that one can obligate himself to future produce. A personal obligation to deliver future wheat attaches to the person because he exists now. But a lien on specific future property is a separate matter and does not arise by Torah law according to the Sages. Baal HaTerumot's distinction concerning Rabbi Meir and unquantified obligations is also understandable. Since the quantity will eventually become known, an obligation that cannot take effect now because it is unquantified can later become effective under Rabbi Meir's broader doctrine that a transaction may take effect later even though it is ineffective now. According to the Sages, if it does not take effect now, it cannot spring into existence later merely when the amount becomes known. This is comparable to the principle that something ineffective now does not ordinarily become effective later. Thus Rambam's position can be maintained.
He is not obligated. The Tur writes that just as one cannot obligate himself in an unquantified amount, one cannot waive an unquantified right. This appears difficult from the law that a man betroths a woman on condition that she have no claim against him for food, clothing, or conjugal rights. Rabbi Yehudah says that regarding monetary matters the condition is effective. Rashba explains that the condition means she agrees to waive food and clothing, yet those obligations are unquantified. The answer is that just as a person fully resolves to accept an unquantified obligation because of marriage, for example when he undertakes support of his wife's daughter, so too the strength of the marriage transaction can cause a complete waiver of unquantified monetary rights. Tumim asks from a worker hired without a fixed amount of food, since the employer cannot excuse himself by saying the support obligation was unquantified. The answer is that wages are acquired through speech. Once labor is rendered, the employer must pay its value even regarding future or unquantified elements. The rule of Rambam concerns gratuitous undertakings, not the ordinary compensation due for labor.
And this is the practical rule. Meaning, the obligation can be collected from the debtor himself. But everyone agrees that unquantified obligations are not collected from encumbered property because of the enactment protecting purchasers. The Shach concludes this way, and Rashdam writes likewise in Even HaEzer.
