Five years. According to Rashi in Gittin 51a, an undertaking to provide food and clothing for five years is an obligation with a fixed measure, because the duration is fixed. The Ramban, in a responsum cited by the Baal HaTerumot, disagrees, relying upon the statement in Ketubot that maintenance is not fixed. The Rashba likewise disagrees in his novellae. In my view this is not a difficulty for Rashi. The passage in Ketubot says that the Sages exchanged the wife’s surplus earnings for maintenance, rather than her basic labor for maintenance, because labor has a fixed measure while maintenance varies. That does not prove that a five-year undertaking lacks a legally fixed limit. Moreover, a husband’s maintenance obligation includes medical treatment whose cost may be unlimited. This is why maintenance of a wife or daughter under the conditions of the ketubah is not fixed. But Rashi discusses one who undertakes to support his wife’s daughter. Such an undertaking does not include medical treatment, as the Yerushalmi states and as explained below. Since the years are fixed and extraordinary medical expense is excluded, the obligation can properly be called fixed. The Rashba’s other objections to Rashi are also not compelling. If one assumes that five years is not fixed, the Gemara’s question may be explained as presuming that the Mishnah concerns a written undertaking. A written obligation ordinarily reaches encumbered property, and the Gemara therefore asks why the daughter’s maintenance differs, answering that a formal acquisition was made. Such an acquisition may be stronger than writing alone, as Tosafot and the Rashba explain, or the acquisition together with the writing may be decisive, as explained below according to the Ritva. The Mishnah itself says that a man marries a woman and undertakes to maintain her daughter, a circumstance ordinarily recorded for proof. His own daughters’ maintenance, by contrast, arises automatically from the conditions of beit din and is not ordinarily written separately. Thus the Gemara could assume writing regarding the stepdaughter without assuming it regarding his daughters. The Yerushalmi also explains the Mishnah’s collection from encumbered property on the ground that he wrote her a document. Tosafot in Ketubot state expressly that according to the opinion in Gittin that a fixed obligation may be collected from encumbered property even without writing, the five-year maintenance obligation is considered fixed. Tosafot elsewhere and the Rosh similarly call it fixed because the number of years is fixed. The Baal HaTerumot also records this interpretation of Rashi. The practical ruling therefore requires study, but Rashi’s position has substantial support.

Others disagreed with him, etc. This is the ruling we accept. Some responsa nevertheless state that a person in possession may say, “I rely upon the Rambam,” who holds that one cannot obligate himself in an amount that has no fixed measure. Similar rulings appear in the responsa of Maharshal, Maharam and other later authorities, and the Gidulei Terumah records that many judges treated the matter as an unresolved dispute. In my view, however, the principal halachah is not the Rambam’s view. The Gemara in Ketubot concerning one who undertakes to support his wife’s daughter shows that a person may bind himself to an obligation whose precise future cost is unknown. The obligation is enforced because the undertaking creates a personal debt, not because the future food or clothing is presently capable of acquisition. The Raavad, Ramban, Rashba, Ritva, Ran, Rosh, and other authorities understand the sugya in this manner. The Rambam’s restriction is therefore difficult to reconcile with the Gemara, and the attempts of the Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh to harmonize his rulings are strained. The Mechaber here and at the end of siman 131, the Darkei Moshe, Ir Shushan, and Bach all rule that a person may assume such an obligation, and this is the established custom. This conclusion concerns collection from the obligor’s unencumbered property. Collection from property sold to others is a separate and more difficult question. The Tur says that if no formal acquisition was made and the obligation was not written, collection is only from unencumbered property; if there was an acquisition or written instrument, collection extends to encumbered property. This follows the view of the Rif and Rosh that the Mishnah excludes produce, improvements, and maintenance from encumbered property because those obligations were not written. The Rashba also rules this way. The Rambam, however, explains the exclusion as resting upon the absence of a fixed amount. He rules that improvements, produce, and family maintenance are collected only from unencumbered property because they have no fixed measure. The Semag, Baal HaTerumot, Bartenura, and Rashi likewise follow Rabbi Chanina’s reason. According to that view, writing alone cannot permit collection of an unlimited obligation from purchasers. The Rashba states this expressly. A responsum of the Ramban also appears to require a formal acquisition in addition to writing. The Tosafot Yom Tov questions the Bartenura for saying both that the purchase price of land is collected from encumbered property because it is evidenced by a sale document, and that produce is not collected because it lacks a fixed amount. There is no contradiction. The Gemara leaves unresolved whether Rabbi Chanina requires both writing and a fixed amount or only a fixed amount. The land price satisfies both; produce, even if mentioned in the document, is not fixed. The Bartenura’s reference to the document may also emphasize that even written produce remains excluded. The sale itself is public and can create purchaser notice even without a document, but that does not eliminate the separate problem of an unbounded amount. There are strong reasons to follow Rabbi Chanina. The Bavli discusses his formulation at length and frames the unresolved questions according to it. The Yerushalmi cites his reason without recording the competing explanation. Rashi and Tosafot were forced into several difficulties when explaining the exclusion merely as absence of writing, because standard sale documents mention improvements and produce. The view based upon lack of a fixed measure avoids those difficulties and accords with later Amoraic discussion. A difficulty arises from the Rambam’s ruling in the laws of marriage that when those who undertook to support a wife’s daughter die, if they made a formal acquisition or obligated themselves by document, she may collect from encumbered property for the promised period. If a five-year maintenance obligation is not fixed, how can a document suffice under the Rambam’s own rule? The better reading is that a student inserted the words concerning a document. The Maggid Mishneh quotes the law as depending upon a formal acquisition and says expressly that writing alone does not permit collection from encumbered property. The Rambam’s continuation also mentions only the absence of an acquisition, not the absence of a document. The Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer 114, which allows collection upon a document, did not account for these sources. Even a formal acquisition requires analysis. Tosafot and the Rashba understand the Gemara’s answer, “where they made an acquisition from him,” to mean that acquisition is stronger than ordinary writing and can support collection of an unbounded obligation. Rashi, however, understands an acquisition as functioning because it ordinarily stands for writing. On that view it should not be stronger than a document and should not overcome Rabbi Chanina’s rule. The Baal HaTerumot notes that according to Rashi a formal acquisition should not help for a truly unlimited obligation. The Ritva writes that one who undertakes by acquisition to support another for a fixed period is personally liable despite the absence of a fixed monetary amount, and that if the obligation is in a document it may be collected from encumbered property. His language may reflect the view that ordinary acquisitions do not themselves create purchaser priority, or that the Gemara requires both acquisition and writing. It may also be distinguished between an acquisition concerning an existing object and one concerning future, unmeasured support. Bava Metzia indicates that an acquisition concerning an unmeasured future obligation does not automatically function as a written lien. Accordingly, the law is clear enough as between the parties: a person may obligate himself to provide support or another benefit even though its precise cost is unknown, and the obligation may be collected from his unencumbered property. But collection from purchasers is doubtful. Many authorities follow Rabbi Chanina that an unbounded amount is not collectible from encumbered property by writing alone, and it is uncertain whether an acquisition alone is sufficient. Since numerous later authorities already allow the possessor to rely upon the Rambam even against personal collection, all the more so purchasers or possessors of encumbered property may resist collection where the amount was not fixed. The matter therefore requires study in practice, and one should not remove property from a possessor without a clear lien satisfying the stricter views.
