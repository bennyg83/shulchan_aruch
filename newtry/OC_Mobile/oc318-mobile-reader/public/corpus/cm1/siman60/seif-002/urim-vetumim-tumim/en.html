Or if he did not set a fixed number of years. Shach asks that Responsa Rashba states explicitly that such a case is still considered a fixed obligation. Sema already anticipated this in seif katan 10. It therefore appears that Rama's phrase, "or he did not set a fixed number of years," means two things together: he did not fix the number of years, and he also did not fix what he would give each year. Rama teaches that nevertheless most later authorities disagree with Rambam and hold that even in such a case, where the obligation is indefinite from several directions, a person can still obligate himself. However, Rashba's statement at the end of that responsum, that one who obligates himself to give each year a tithe of whatever produce grows from this field is also considered to have undertaken a fixed obligation, requires study. If so, then one who obligates himself to give all the produce the field produces each year should likewise be considered fixed. What difference is there between one tenth and all of it? Yet the Gemara says that one does not collect compensation for consumed produce from encumbered property, and R. Chananel explains that this is because the amount is not fixed. There too the obligation is to pay whatever produce came from the field, whether little or much. Rabbenu Yerucham does not copy this final statement of Rashba, apparently because he did not accept it. One must distinguish as follows. If a person obligates himself to pay the monetary value of whatever produce will grow from the field in a given year, the obligation is not fixed because he does not know the monetary amount for which he is assuming liability. But if he says, "I will give you the actual produce of this field that grows," whether much or little, this is considered fixed. He is not obligating himself for an unknown monetary value; he undertakes only to hand over whatever this known field itself yields. By contrast, in the case of consumed produce, the produce itself can no longer be delivered, so the obligation is to reimburse its value. That monetary liability is not fixed. This also resolves Ramban in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 64, who says that one who undertakes to give twenty-two kor of wheat each year has assumed an unfixed obligation. He must purchase those twenty-two kor from the market with his own funds, and the market price is unknown. Therefore the monetary burden is unfixed. There is no contradiction between Ramban and Rashba. The distinction is whether he undertakes to deliver a defined physical yield itself, or instead assumes an open-ended monetary burden measured by market value.
Even though they made a kinyan from him, and so forth. The early authorities strongly challenged Rambam's ruling. I discussed the matter at length in my chiddushim to Even HaEzer. For present purposes, the essential points can be stated briefly. Rambam writes in Hilchot Mechirah 11 that one who obligates himself in an amount that is not fixed does not become bound. He then asks why one who undertakes with his wife to support her daughter is nevertheless obligated, and answers that he made the commitment at the time of marriage, and the matter resembles those obligations that are acquired by mere verbal commitment. Kesef Mishneh correctly infers from Rambam's wording, "it resembles matters acquired by verbal commitment," rather than "these are themselves matters acquired by verbal commitment," that Rambam is not speaking about the stipulation made at kiddushin or erusin itself. Rather, he speaks of a commitment made at nissuin. Therefore a kinyan or a document is required, and the obligation is not acquired through mere words under Rav Gidal's rule. Nevertheless, because it is made before completion of marriage, it resembles the special marriage commitments and can bind even though the amount is not fixed. This reading of Rambam is correct. The difficulty is conceptual. If a full kinyan ordinarily does not work for an unfixed obligation, why should mere verbal commitment at erusin be stronger? One would expect the opposite. It therefore initially appeared to me that Rambam's rule applies only where the obligor receives nothing in return. If he assumes an indefinite liability gratuitously, his mind does not fully commit. But where the other party undertakes something in exchange, the obligation is commercial rather than gratuitous and can bind even if unfixed. Rambam himself describes the ordinary case as resembling a gift, where there is no known and present thing being given in return. That wording indicates his concern is a gratuitous undertaking. This approach resolves not only Ramban's challenge in Baal HaTerumot from the cases of "whatever I inherit from my father" and improvements to land, which are transactions rather than gifts, but also a major difficulty from the law of hired workers. The Mishnah says that before workers begin their labor the employer must stipulate concerning their meals, lest he become obligated to provide an unlimited feast. If, according to Rambam, every food obligation is unfixed and no kinyan or obligation could bind at all, why is there such urgency to stipulate before work begins? The answer is that workers are not receiving a gift. They perform labor for the employer, and their food is part of their compensation. Because there is an exchange, the rule of unfixed obligations does not apply. So too at kiddushin. The woman agrees to marry because of the commitments made, and the husband acquires the marital relationship. This resembles a sale or exchange, and an unfixed obligation can bind. Rambam's question arises only when the stipulation is made later at nissuin, after kiddushin has already occurred. At that stage it resembles a gratuitous undertaking. Rambam answers that because the marriage process is not fully completed until nissuin, it still resembles the special erusin commitments. Since it is not actually at erusin, however, a kinyan is required. This also answers Rivash, siman 345, who asks why Rambam requires, in the laws of gifts, that verbally acquired marriage commitments concern property already in the promisor's possession. If verbal commitment is strong enough to bind an unfixed obligation, why should it not also bind property not yet in his possession? Hagahot Maimoniyot tried to answer by saying that at erusin Rambam does not require the property to be in his possession, but that does not fit Rambam's language or Shulchan Aruch Even HaEzer siman 51. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. Chazal did not create a wholly new mode of acquisition for things that cannot be acquired at all. They merely recognized that, in the marital exchange, an unfixed obligation is sufficiently serious to bind. Property not in one's possession cannot be transferred even in an ordinary sale, so the special rule does not help there. The same resolves Raavad's objection from the sugya concerning one who says, "I owe you one maneh in a document." Reish Lakish says the statement does not create liability, and the Gemara challenges him from the Mishnah where one marries a woman and undertakes to support her daughter. The Gemara answers that the case involves marriage stipulations. Raavad asks: according to Rambam, even R. Yochanan would require the special marriage context because otherwise the obligation is unfixed. But at the initial stage the Gemara had not yet introduced Rav Gidal's rule. It could simply understand the Mishnah as a commitment at kiddushin, where the obligation is part of the marital exchange and therefore valid despite being unfixed. The challenge to Reish Lakish is that, even apart from unfixedness, he denies that such a written self-obligation creates debt. The answer then invokes the special marriage framework, either at kiddushin through verbal commitment or at nissuin with a kinyan. This reading minimizes the dispute between Rambam and the other authorities and seems compelling. Nevertheless, Ramban in Baal HaTerumot appears to understand Rambam differently, and Ritva in Ketubot rejects this distinction in the name of his teachers, arguing that marriage itself also has the character of exchange. Rambam also rules that a guarantor does not become bound for an unfixed obligation, although guaranty itself rests upon the benefit of trust extended to him. Still, that objection is not decisive, as Sema writes below in siman 131, because guaranty is structurally weaker than a direct obligation. I later found Mishneh LaMelech citing another author who proposed this same distinction, though he also brought Ritva's objection. Despite the strength of the proofs, I defer to the great authorities who understood Rambam otherwise. A second explanation therefore appears more secure. Rambam derives his rule from the sugya in Gittin concerning why support obligations are not collected from encumbered property. R. Yochanan says because they are not written; R. Chananel says because they are not fixed. The Gemara challenges from the obligation to support a wife's daughter for five years and answers that a kinyan was made. Rambam reasons as follows. If the problem with an unfixed obligation were merely lack of notice to purchasers, then a kinyan should not help. The amount remains unknown, and purchasers still cannot protect themselves. Rather, according to R. Chananel, the basic Torah law is that an unfixed self-obligation does not bind even against the obligor himself. Chazal imposed such obligations in certain areas, such as a wife's and daughters' support and compensation for improvements, as rabbinic enactments. When Chazal created those obligations, they could have extended them against purchasers as well through hefker beit din. But for the protection of commerce they left purchaser property subject to the original Torah rule and did not extend the rabbinic obligation to purchasers. This is the dispute between the first Tanna and R. Yose. The first Tanna says the failure to collect from purchasers is because of tikkun olam, implying that basic law would otherwise permit collection and Chazal removed it. R. Yose says: what tikkun olam is there? The obligation itself is fundamentally unfixed and therefore, by Torah law, does not bind property. Chazal created a personal obligation but did not extend it against purchasers. Thus the lack of purchaser collection is not a special cancellation but simply the underlying law. Once this is understood, the marriage case is different. Chazal recognized, because of the strength of marital commitment, that even an unfixed obligation can bind through a kinyan. Once the personal obligation itself is valid as a matter of the marriage framework, a kinyan can also give it purchaser-facing force. The same Torah obstacle no longer exists. This explains why the Gemara's question against Reish Lakish and R. Yochanan presents no difficulty. Their dispute concerns the view that the lack of purchaser collection is because the obligation is unwritten, meaning they do not accept R. Chananel's concept that the basic obligation is invalid because unfixed. According to them, an unfixed self-obligation can bind in general. Thus the Mishnah concerning support creates no independent problem. It also suggests that Rambam's rule concerns self-obligation, not transfer of an object. If a person says, "Whatever my trap catches I sell to you," according to the view that one can convey future property, the sale can take effect even though the amount is unknown. He is not placing an open-ended personal debt upon himself; he transfers whatever particular thing later comes into existence. This accords with Rashba's distinction, discussed above, between undertaking to deliver the actual produce of a field and undertaking to pay its unknown value. Therefore the many challenges against Rambam from "whatever my trap catches" and similar cases do not apply. Likewise, the Gemara asks why Scripture needs the rule of recognition for a firstborn if a father could simply give the extra portion as a gift, and answers that the rule is needed for property acquired only later. Ramban asks that even according to R. Meir, future-acquired property would still be an unfixed amount. According to what I have written, there is no difficulty. A gift or sale is not an unfixed personal debt. The transfer attaches to the property itself when it comes into existence. One can add another answer. R. Meir permits transfer of future property but also allows the transferor to retract before the property comes into existence. This shows that the acquisition does not actually take effect until the property exists. At that instant, the property is already objectively defined, even if the parties do not yet know its exact quantity. Therefore there is no unfixed obligation at the moment the acquisition takes effect. By contrast, a self-obligation binds immediately and cannot be retracted, and at that moment its amount is unknown. Rambam's concern therefore applies only there. These two explanations can both be true and reinforce one another.

Regarding encumbered property, however, Shach notes that Rambam rules in Hilchot Malveh chapter 21 like R. Chananel that the reason such obligations do not collect from purchasers is because they are not fixed. Rashi and Ramban in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 43, also follow R. Chananel. There is therefore room to say that even if the personal obligation is valid, it still does not collect from encumbered property. Shach further questions whether a written document helps. Rambam, Hilchot Ishut 23, says that if one undertakes to support his wife's daughter for five years and either a kinyan was made or he obligated himself in a document, the obligation is like an ordinary written debt and collects from encumbered property. But if the agreement was made at kiddushin without a kinyan, it belongs to the special category of matters that were not instituted for writing and does not collect from purchasers. Shach therefore suggests that the words "or he obligated himself in a document" were perhaps inserted by a mistaken student. Since the obligation is unfixed, he says, a document should not help. He notes that the Gemara answers only that a kinyan was made, not that a document was written, and Rambam's conclusion likewise mentions absence of a kinyan rather than absence of a document. This cannot be correct. Rambam already wrote explicitly just beforehand that outside the time of kiddushin the obligation does not bind until a kinyan is made or a document is written. One cannot dismiss both explicit statements as scribal additions. If the Gemara's answer "they made a kinyan" meant specifically kinyan and excluded writing, from where would Rambam derive that writing helps at all? The matter is clear. Rambam understands the Gemara's question whether such commitments were "given to writing" to mean whether writing creates purchaser-facing force or whether Chazal withheld documentary effectiveness from these marriage commitments even when written. This is the approach of Rashi and Rif. The Gemara attempts to prove from the Mishnah that the wife's daughter collects support from encumbered property, and answers that a kinyan was made. Now, if writing were inherently ineffective because the obligation is unfixed, the proof would make no sense. The support obligation itself, absent the marriage exception, would already require a kinyan merely to bind personally. Rather, within the marital setting Rambam holds that either kinyan or writing can supply the seriousness needed for an unfixed obligation. The remaining question is whether Chazal allowed a document in this context to have normal documentary effect against purchasers. Since at kiddushin they did not institute such writing, a written paper made then does not have that effect. A kinyan, however, creates publicity and therefore allows collection. Outside the actual kiddushin moment, such as at nissuin, there is no special rabbinic rule against writing. A kinyan or document therefore works. Rambam's wording is exact. This also explains why at the end he says only, "if they stipulated at kiddushin and there was no kinyan," rather than "no kinyan or document." At kiddushin itself, even if a document was written, it does not help because these are matters that Chazal did not authorize to be written. Therefore the only relevant exception is a kinyan. Accordingly, Rambam certainly holds that in the marital context a document can function like a kinyan for an unfixed obligation when writing is halachically available. What do we learn from this for ordinary cases according to those who reject Rambam and hold that an unfixed self-obligation binds generally? At first glance, one might infer that writing should likewise be as effective as a kinyan for collection from purchasers. Rashi says a standard kinyan is destined for writing, suggesting little distinction between them. However, there is a strong argument the other way from the Gemara's formulation concerning R. Chananel. It asks whether one needs both written and fixed obligations, or whether being fixed suffices even if unwritten. It never asks the converse, whether writing suffices even if the amount is unfixed. According to Rambam this is unsurprising, because outside the special marital framework an unfixed obligation does not bind at all. But according to those who disagree with Rambam and validate such obligations personally, the omission is telling. It suggests that even if written, an unfixed obligation does not collect from purchasers. Rashba in Gittin indeed writes that according to R. Chananel one needs specifically a kinyan; a document alone does not suffice. He likewise cites R. Chananel himself as requiring kinyan rather than writing. Although elsewhere Rashba says a standard kinyan is intended for writing, that statement concerns a different issue: whether a given transaction is one for which writing is allowed. It does not mean that writing has the same force as kinyan where the specific defect is that the obligation is unfixed. Tur in Even HaEzer appears to mediate. Regarding a document made at nissuin, because Rambam clearly recognizes its force there and because the marriage context itself strengthens the obligation, Tur inclines to allow writing. But outside marriage, the primary view follows Rashba and R. Chananel that only a kinyan, not a mere document, overcomes the purchaser-facing problem of an unfixed obligation. This explains the rulings in Even HaEzer. In siman 112, concerning daughters' support, Shulchan Aruch cites Ramah's view that if after marriage a kinyan was made concerning their support, they may collect from encumbered property. Tur there had added, "or he wrote a separate document." Shulchan Aruch omits that phrase because he generally follows Rambam and R. Chananel that an unfixed obligation requires kinyan for purchaser collection and a document alone does not suffice. But in siman 117, concerning support of a wife's daughter undertaken at nissuin, Shulchan Aruch rules that if a kinyan was made or a document written, collection from purchasers is allowed. That is the special marriage setting, where everyone agrees that the affection and seriousness of marriage can give writing the same force as kinyan. Beit Shmuel there was somewhat confused by these distinctions. He asks how a document can help when siman 51 says the commitment was not "given to writing." The answer is simple. At kiddushin itself, Chazal did not institute writing and a document does not help. After kiddushin, at nissuin, writing is permitted and can operate. There is no contradiction. This also explains the Gemara's attempted proof from the rule that engagement and marriage documents are written only with both parties' consent. The Gemara answers that the reference is to the document of kiddushin itself. What, then, is "the marriage document"? According to Rashbam, it may mean the ketubah, but Ritva asks why both parties' consent would be needed when a document can ordinarily be written for a debtor without the creditor present. He gives a strained answer involving absence of kinyan. According to what I have written, the phrase can instead refer to the financial marriage stipulations made at nissuin. Those are indeed matters for which writing is valid and therefore require the consent appropriate to their terms. This also allows a somewhat forced defense of Maggid Mishneh, who explains Rambam in Hilchot Malveh chapter 4, "engagement documents are not written," as referring to financial stipulation documents. If he means stipulations at nissuin rather than kiddushin, there is no contradiction with the rule that kiddushin commitments were not instituted for writing. The practical conclusion is: a kinyan concerning an unfixed obligation can create collection from encumbered property, as Shulchan Aruch rules in Even HaEzer siman 112. A mere document ordinarily does not. But a document made in the special context of nissuin, because of the strength of marriage commitment, can be effective, as ruled in Even HaEzer siman 114.
