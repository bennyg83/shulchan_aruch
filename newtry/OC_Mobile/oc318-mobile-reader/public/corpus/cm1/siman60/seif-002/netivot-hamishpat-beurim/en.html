According to Rambam, he did not become obligated. See Shach, who cites Raavad's objection from the beginning of chapter HaNose. Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish dispute one who says, 'I owe you one hundred in a document.' The Gemara challenges from the Mishnah concerning one who undertakes to support his wife's daughter for five years and answers that the Mishnah concerns stipulations made at marriage. Raavad infers that according to Rabbi Yochanan the Mishnah would have worked even without the special marriage-stipulation doctrine, which seems inconsistent with Rambam's rule that a person cannot obligate himself in an indefinite amount except through such stipulations. In my view Rambam can be defended, and this also resolves Maharsha's question on Tosafot. Rambam's general theory of self-obligation is connected to *odita*. When a person says, 'I owe you,' the statement can function as an admission that he previously became obligated in a legally effective manner. But where no legal mechanism could ever have created the obligation, an admission of that type is necessarily false and cannot create the liability through ordinary *odita*. A husband's undertaking to support his wife's daughter is different. Had he made that undertaking at the time of engagement, the Sages recognize it as binding through the special law of marriage stipulations. Therefore, when at the time of marriage he now says, 'I am obligated to support your daughter for five years,' his statement can be treated as an admission that he validly obligated himself during the engagement. That is why the undertaking can work through *odita* even though an indefinite obligation toward an ordinary third party would not. Why, then, do Rambam and Maggid Mishneh require a kinyan or document at the time of marriage? Because an indefinite undertaking resembles asmachta. Even where ordinary self-obligation can rest on *odita*, a conditional or asmachta-like obligation requires an additional legally effective form such as kinyan or document. The same is true here. This also answers Raavad and Maharsha. Where the kinyan is needed merely to publicize and strengthen an already valid obligation, it can create collection from encumbered property. Where the kinyan is required to create the very obligation itself, its function is more limited and does not automatically produce the same purchaser-enforcement consequences. The Mishnah can therefore concern a special marriage undertaking without proving that every indefinite self-obligation is valid.
And this is how we rule. Sema in siman 232 struggled to distinguish between price fraud and a defect. Ramban, cited by Baal HaTerumot, gives the clear reason. In ordinary bargaining everyone knows that each party tries to maximize his advantage, and the Torah nevertheless says that ordinary silence does not constitute waiver of legally cognizable overreaching within the relevant period. A hidden defect is different. A buyer ordinarily assumes that the item has no defect. If the seller expressly discloses that there is a defect, even without identifying which particular defect, there is stronger basis to infer waiver. Rambam, however, apparently includes defects within the broader prohibition of overreaching and therefore holds that so long as the buyer did not actually know the specific defect, ordinary silence is not treated as waiver. This follows all the more from the Torah's rule regarding price fraud.
