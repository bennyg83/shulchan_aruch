The correct reading is: that the witnesses should write the document in Shimon's name, but should give the document, etc.
They do not compel him. The Tur's wording is that he can say, 'I do not want your honor and I do not want your disgrace.' Meaning, this causes him embarrassment, because it appears as though he needs to sell his documents.
Even if Shimon admitted to him, etc. The Tur explains that no act occurred through which the condition could take effect. This differs from a condition made with the borrower, because the loan itself was made on that condition. It is therefore analogous to a credibility clause or a condition, 'Do not repay me except before witnesses,' which is effective when stipulated at the time of the loan, as explained in simanim 70, 71, and 207.
Even if he did not explain his words, but merely said to the witnesses, etc. The Tur likewise uses this wording. I wrote there that the same applies if he says this to the borrower, even without elaborating. The Tur merely uses the ordinary case. When one says this before witnesses, he usually does not fully explain himself, so as not to create an impression that he is wealthy. Otherwise, why would he initially want the document written in Shimon's name? When speaking directly with the borrower, however, the borrower already knows that the loan money belongs to him, and therefore he ordinarily explains and says, 'Write the other document in my name.'
A document that was written in Reuven's name, etc. The Mechaber abbreviated this responsum in a place where it deserved fuller explanation. The question was about a community whose custom was to lease out its revenue rights. When they wrote the lease document, they placed it in the name of one member of the community, and at the end of the document before the witnesses' signatures they wrote that the money belonged to the community's lease of such-and-such revenue. Reuven then produced such a document and claimed the money for himself, while the community warned the lessee not to pay Reuven despite the document being in his name, because their established custom was to write such documents in this manner. The responsum ruled that since this was the community's custom, Reuven cannot prevail merely because the document bears his name; he must prove the money is his. He also cannot say that the community transferred the document to him in payment of a debt they owed him, for if so he should possess a written transfer with delivery. Thus the ruling applies specifically where the community had an established custom of writing its revenue documents in an individual's name, perhaps to avoid the disorder of jointly owned affairs or for another known reason. Without such a custom, the individual would be believed to say the money was actually his and that the statement that it belonged to the community was written merely to avoid creating an impression of wealth. At the end of siman 65 it will likewise be explained that if the body of a debt instrument says that it belongs to a certain person, the holder may still say he wrote that merely to avoid appearing wealthy. This differs from Eir Shushan, who gave as a rationale that an individual would not normally attribute his own money to the community merely to avoid appearing wealthy. The responsum itself repeatedly makes the communal custom decisive, proving that the ruling is limited to such a case.
