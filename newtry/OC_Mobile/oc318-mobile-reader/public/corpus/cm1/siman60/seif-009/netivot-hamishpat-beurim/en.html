We do not compel him. Tur concludes that if Reuven conducted the matter without qualification and Shimon protests, saying, "Although the money was not mine, I nevertheless acquired the debt because you instructed the witnesses to write the document in my name," there is no substance to Shimon's claim, since Reuven told the witnesses to give the document to Reuven. See there. Tumim asks that this rule derives from Bava Kamma 102 concerning one who purchases a field in another person's name, explained in siman 184. Responsa Rivash, siman 250, Responsa Rosh, siman 96, Rashba's novellae there, and Shittah Mekubetzet all indicate that where a person buys a field in his friend's name and the friend protests that he acquired it because it was bought in his name and the document was written in his name, the property belongs to the protesting friend. How then is this case different, when the present ruling is learned from there? Tumim answers that a sale is different because the case concerns a deed of acquisition, and when the buyer orders a deed of acquisition to be written in Levi's name, he thereby confers the field upon Levi. A loan document, by contrast, is only evidentiary and does not itself effect acquisition. I cannot accept this at all. If the case involved a deed that itself effects acquisition, the deed would have to reach the buyer. If Reuven said, "Give it to me," neither person would acquire through the deed. Levi would not acquire because the deed did not reach him and the seller was not told to acquire it on Levi's behalf; Reuven would not acquire because the deed was not written in his name. Necessarily the case there is one where the land was acquired by money and the deed merely serves as evidence. Tumim himself ultimately leaves the matter requiring study. In my humble opinion the original question is no question. In the field case, when Reuven says to the seller, "Here is a maneh, and let the field be acquired by Shimon," Shimon acquires the field through the money given by Reuven, as explained in siman 190 concerning one who says, "Here is a maneh, and let the field be sold to so and so." This is where land is acquired by money alone without a deed. Although Rama there requires that the other person desire the acquisition, that case concerns money supplied merely as the purchase money while the field is worth much more and the other person is to be the purchaser and pay the balance. But if Reuven expressly gives his own money as a gift and directs that his friend acquire the field with it, one may confer a benefit on a person in his absence. See Shach, siman 183, in the name of Beit Yosef, that if one buys with his own money intending to acquire for his friend and tells the seller so, the friend acquires. Because this is an actual acquisition for the friend, Reuven cannot later claim that he did it merely to avoid appearing wealthy. Shach, siman 126, seif katan 54, cites Tur that in a three party assignment one cannot plead jest or avoidance of appearing wealthy, because it is an act of acquisition, and such claims are ineffective against an acquisition. There is no greater acquisition for one's friend than this. Accordingly, Rashba, Rivash, Rosh, and the other authorities rule that where one buys a field in the name of his children or another person, those persons acquire. Once the friend acquired the field at the moment the money effected acquisition, a later evidentiary deed written in his name changes nothing, even if Reuven ordered the deed delivered back to himself. A loan is different. Even if Reuven expressly says, "I give my money as a gift to Shimon, I lend you this money in Shimon's name, write the document in Shimon's name and give the document to him," Shimon does not acquire the money merely by those words. A person cannot transfer his own property to another without an effective act of acquisition through a third party. Even whether the expression "give" is equivalent to "acquire on his behalf" is disputed. Here, where he merely says, "I lend my money in Shimon's name and give it to him as a gift," this certainly does not constitute acquisition for Shimon. Writing the document in Shimon's name likewise does not confer the debt on him where Reuven says to give the document back to Reuven. Accordingly, even if Shimon says that the money was actually his because Reuven admitted as much, Reuven may explain the admission as having been made merely to avoid appearing wealthy, since no acquisition took place. This resembles a document stating that the money belongs to the communal lease fund; see Sema, seif katan 28. Although Shach in siman 81, seif katan 18, writes that where one says, "Write it," he cannot later claim that he spoke only to avoid appearing wealthy, that means where he said, "Write and give it to him." If he said, "Give it to me," he certainly may make that claim. All the more so, where Shimon admits that the money was not his but claims that he acquired the debt, his words have no substance when Reuven instructed that the document be returned to Reuven. If Reuven said, "Give it to Shimon," however, it is equivalent to acquisition according to those who hold that an instruction to give is equivalent to an instruction to acquire, because the document was written in Shimon's name. The same distinction applies to purchase of a field. If at the time of acquisition Reuven bought it without qualification for himself, and only afterward said, "Write the deed in so and so's name," he may say that he did so merely to avoid appearing wealthy, since no acquisition was effected by that admission. This is the case of siman 184, which carefully says, "I bought it for Levi," implying that he had already made the purchase without qualification and only when the document was written said that he had bought it for Levi. Therefore Levi does not acquire. Know also that Reuven's credibility to say concerning a document in his possession, written in Shimon's name, that the debt is really his and that Shimon's name was used merely for convenience applies specifically where there are witnesses that Reuven lent the money. If there are no such witnesses, the law is different. Certainly if the borrower dies and Shimon claims that the document is his and came into Reuven's hands as a deposit, Shimon is believed, because the document is written in his name. We presume that Shimon lent the money, collect from the borrower's heirs, give the money to Shimon, and require Reuven to return the document. Otherwise, the rule in siman 66, seif 11, that Reuven is not believed to claim that he purchased the document would be difficult. Why should he not be believed through a migo that he could have said, "It is mine, and I merely wrote Shimon's name for convenience"? Likewise, in the case of the bundle of documents in Ketubot 85, explained below in siman 64, where one cannot claim that documents were held as collateral through a migo of purchase because he lacks a written transfer, why is he not believed through this migo? Necessarily this claim is unavailable. Even if the document itself says before the signatures that the money came from a communal lease fund, Shach, seif katan 35, rules that the holder is not believed to contradict it. All the more so where the entire document is written in the other person's name. Even according to Sema there, his ruling applies only where the body of the document is written in the holder's name and only an internal notation says otherwise. Where the entire document is in another's name, all agree. Likewise, if the borrower is alive and admits to Reuven that Reuven lent the money but merely ordered the document written in Shimon's name for convenience, while Shimon denies this and claims that the document came into Reuven's hands as a deposit, once the document has been established before beit din neither Reuven nor the borrower is believed. Shach, siman 56, rules even concerning a third party that when the other party claims loss or deposit and the document has already been established in beit din, the third party is not believed, because it has already been established as a valid document. See also what I write below in siman 66 concerning Sema, seif katan 33. This is clear.
We compel him to do so. It is obvious that the new document is written only with the new date, even if there was a kinyan at the time of the loan. We are not writing based on the original kinyan. So long as the borrower does not instruct the witnesses, they may not write. Beit din compels the borrower to direct the witnesses to write, and his agency is required for the writing. He cannot now appoint them as agents to create a retroactive lien, as Shach writes in siman 81, seif katan 4. Even where there was a kinyan, the rule is the same. Urim VeTumim states this explicitly, and it is straightforward. "We compel him" means that beit din places him under sanction, as with anyone who refuses to obey a judicial directive. There is, however, a difficulty. Why must beit din compel him? Let the witnesses come before beit din and let beit din itself certify the matter, as at the beginning of siman 41. Since Reuven can presently collect from Levi on the strength of this document, why should they not write evidence enabling him to collect whenever necessary, stating, "It became established before us that this document belongs to Reuven and was written in Shimon's name merely for convenience"? He would then benefit by collecting from the original date, as in siman 41. It appears that siman 41 is different because the matter is clear: beit din sees a valid document in his hands. Here it is not clear that the document belongs to Reuven. His present right depends only on Shimon's failure to object, or on Shimon's inability to sustain his objection, as Tur writes, so long as Shimon brings no proof. It is possible that Shimon is withholding proof only because he wishes to prevent collection by one of his own creditors. Therefore beit din does not write an independent certification for Reuven. Rather, it compels the borrower, saying, "According to your own admission you became obligated to Reuven, so make him a document." If it is later established that the arrangement was only to evade a creditor, we follow that proof. Were beit din itself to write that the matter had been established before it, later proof might be ineffective. One cannot object that the borrower should be able to retract from making a document, as explained in simanim 39 and 243. There, no document has yet been written and he may say that he does not wish to cause additional publicity and documents. Here publicity has already arisen through the existing document, so it makes no difference whether this document or another exists. There remains a difficulty from Tosafot, Bava Kamma 102, s.v. kegon, who ask why the witnesses cannot write another document, and answer that the case is where the witnesses do not wish to do so. This implies that if they wish, they may write a second document. The reason must be that the first document is viewed as defectively written because it does not name the true creditor or seller disclosed at the time of the transaction. It is surprising that the Mechaber omitted their view. It appears that Tosafot wrote this only because their case concerns a deed of sale without warranty, as implied by Rashi there, who explains that the purchaser should have stipulated, "Write two documents in the other person's name, so that if people challenge the land I can show them that it belongs to him, and write another document for me." If there were warranty, writing two documents would be prohibited. Since Tosafot address a document without warranty, their question is sound. With no warranty, the rule that witnesses have completed their agency does not apply, as explained in Bava Batra 171 and Shach, siman 41, seif katan 10. But with a document containing warranty, the witnesses may not write again because they have completed their agency. Only where the first document is entirely invalid does Sema, siman 41, seif katan 5, reason that the principal did not instruct them to write a worthless shard, based on Gittin 63 concerning a mistaken document. Here the first document is not worthless, because collection can be made with it. Moreover, even with a completely invalid document, if the principal expressly instructed them to write that very document, they have completed their agency. Even if the creditor separately instructed them to write another document, that was not an agency from the borrower. This also explains the difference from siman 184. There it is ruled that if the purchaser informed the seller that he was buying for himself, the seller is compelled to write another deed, while here an express prior stipulation concerning another document is required. In a sale without warranty, even absent prior disclosure the Gemara initially considered compelling the seller because he knew that the purchaser would not throw away his money for nothing. The seller can answer only that he thought the purchaser wished to conduct some matter through the named person. Once the purchaser expressly disclosed the truth, he may say, "I informed you precisely because I intended that you later write me another document. If I merely wanted the named person to write me a document, why was there any need to tell you, after your sale was complete and you had no further involvement?" In a loan, however, the borrower may say, "You told me only so that I would know to repay you rather than the named person, and so that I could not say that you are not my litigant. You did not tell me for the purpose of requiring another document." Therefore an explicit stipulation concerning a second document is needed.
The law is with the community. See Shach, seif katan 28 and seif katan 38, and Urim VeTumim, seif katan 19.
