Tur adds that if Reuven arranged the matter without specifying anything, and Shimon then objects and says, "Even though the money was not mine, I nevertheless acquired it because you made the kinyan in my name and instructed the witnesses to write the document in my name," his claim has no force. Since Reuven instructed the witnesses to deliver the document to Reuven himself and not to Shimon, Reuven revealed that the property was his and that the document was being written in Shimon's name merely as a trust arrangement. Shulchan Aruch omitted this addition, and I do not know why. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 51, part 9, writes that Rivash, responsum 250, who rules that if one instructed that a deed be written in the name of his minor son the son may later claim that the property was conferred upon him, disagrees with Tur. But there is no proof from Rivash. Tur's reason is specifically that Reuven instructed the witnesses to deliver the document back to him. In Rivash's case there was no real alternative, for how could the deed be delivered to a minor? Bnei Shmuel therefore strained unnecessarily. Still, the truth is that Rivash follows Rashba's position. I found in Rashba's chiddushim to Bava Kamma, Perek HaGozel Kama, that he cites Tosafot concerning one who purchased a field and had the deed written in the name of the Exilarch. If the Exilarch later claims that he acquired the field because the deed was written in his name, and the purchaser did not tell the witnesses at the outset that he was using the name only so as not to appear wealthy, his claim is valid. Rashba agrees with that view. Thus the matter is genuinely disputed. Perhaps those authorities speak only where the purchaser did not tell the witnesses to deliver the deed to him. Where he expressly instructed them to return the deed to him, that is like saying outright that the deed belongs to him and not to the person whose name appears in it. But this is somewhat strained in Rashba's wording. This may therefore be why Shulchan Aruch omitted Tur's ruling. After writing this, I saw Rosh, responsum klal 96, siman 5. Rosh asks regarding the rule, "if he wishes to write, we do not compel him": why should we ever compel the seller to write a second deed? Perhaps the named purchaser already acquired the field through the rule that one may acquire for another in his absence. Even if that purchaser later admits the field belongs to Reuven, we may suspect collusion. Rosh therefore explains that the baraita concerns a case where the named purchaser himself came and admitted immediately that the field belonged to Reuven, with no meaningful interval in which collusion could have developed. This shows that Rosh did not accept Tur's reason that because the deed was delivered to Reuven, the named purchaser has no claim. If Tur's rule were correct, there would be no need for Rosh's explanation. It therefore requires substantial study why Tur ruled apparently contrary to his father. Perhaps Tur distinguishes between a sale deed and a loan document. Land itself can be acquired through a deed. Therefore, when one orders a deed written in Levi's name, this can operate as an acquisition for Levi. By contrast, a loan document is merely evidentiary; the essential matter is the money. To confer the loan claim, one must transfer the money or the debt itself. Merely having the note written in another's name does not show that the underlying money was given to him. This distinction is possible, but still requires study.
Even if he did not explain, and so forth. The source of this ruling is Bava Kamma 102b. If a person buys a field and has the deed written in the name of the Exilarch, the baraita says that the seller is not compelled to write another deed. If, however, the purchaser stipulated otherwise, the seller is compelled. Abaye explains that the seller cannot be compelled to write a second deed in the purchaser's true name unless there was an express stipulation. The Gemara asks: if he expressly stipulated, is that not obvious? It answers that the case is where he merely told the witnesses, "Know that I will need another deed." One might have thought the seller can say, "You meant another deed from the person whose name you used." The Gemara therefore teaches that the seller must write a new deed in the purchaser's own name. This is the source of our seif. Rambam, Hilchot Sheluchin 2:5, writes similarly that the seller is not compelled, and concludes: "If he stipulated at the outset and said, 'I bought it for myself, and the reason I am having the deed written in Levi's name is so that people should not know I am the purchaser,' he may compel the seller to write another deed in his own name." Rambam omits the Gemara's specific phrase that he told the witnesses, "I will need another deed." Kesef Mishneh says Rambam stated the matter generally and regarded the Gemara's phrase as included in his language, or perhaps Rambam did not have that reading in his text. Beit Yosef below in siman 184 leaves the matter difficult. In Shulchan Aruch, siman 184, seif 3, Mechaber cites Rambam: if the purchaser told the seller that the deed was being written in another's name only so that people would not know he was the real purchaser, the seller must write a second deed. Here, however, Mechaber follows the Gemara and the other authorities and requires that he have told the witnesses that he wanted another deed. He does not cite Rambam's broader rule here concerning a loan document, namely that merely telling the borrower that the note is written in another's name to conceal the lender's identity obligates the borrower to write a new note. Why was it omitted? Perhaps Mechaber holds that merely revealing one's intention to the borrower or seller does not itself obligate that person to create a second document. Even if one says to witnesses, "Write him a document," the instructing party may retract from the writing before it is delivered, as explained above at the beginning of siman 39. All the more so where he never explicitly said, "Write me another document." One could answer that above the concern is proliferation of documents. Here, the second document will replace the first, so there is no improper multiplication of documents. Hagahot Rosh in Perek HaSefinah answers a Tosafot question this way in the case of an erased deed. That explanation works for a sale. Witnesses who write a sale deed create publicity that a particular field was sold. Writing the deed again does not create a materially different kol, because everyone knows it is the same field and a single field cannot be sold twice. Therefore there is no meaningful concern of proliferating documents or publicity. Once the purchaser reveals any intention that the deed be rewritten, the seller can be compelled, as Maharshal says, because refusing would be an act of Sodom-like conduct. A loan note is different. If witnesses first publicize that a person borrowed one maneh and later another set of witnesses publicizes that he borrowed one maneh, outsiders may understand these as two separate loans. The borrower's property is thereby devalued. That is a real concern. Therefore, with a loan document, mere revelation of intent should not suffice; there must be an explicit stipulation before witnesses. This may explain why Mechaber did not bring Rambam's broader rule here, since Rambam's statement occurs in the context of a sale deed, not a loan note. However, I think Rambam's own reasoning can be explained more deeply according to his general view. Rambam rules like Rava in Bava Batra that one who claims ownership of a document does not need to prove the transfer itself. Regarding the lender, if a person holds a note he is generally believed to say, "I had a written sale and transfer but lost the transfer document." To collect from the borrower, however, the borrower may still say, "Bring proof that the note was transferred to you; otherwise you are not my litigant," as Rambam writes in Hilchot Mechirah 6 and as explained below in siman 66. This distinction matters in a land sale. If Reuven buys a field but has the deed written in another person's name, and Reuven remains in possession of the field and also holds the deed, then if the seller later challenges him, Reuven can say, "You already sold the field. What concern is it of yours? I am in possession." If the person named in the deed later says, "You intended to confer the field upon me by writing the deed in my name," Reuven can answer that he purchased the document rights from him and that the transfer writing was lost. Since Reuven is in possession of the field and holds the deed, he has a migo and there is little practical danger. Thus, when the Gemara says the purchaser told witnesses, "I will need another deed," what novelty remains? He does not need a new deed from the named person, because he is already protected as possessor. And if he merely wanted those witnesses to know that he never intended to confer the deed upon the named person, that too is unnecessary because his possession gives him a defense. Therefore the only sensible meaning is that he wanted the seller to write another deed in his true name. The Gemara's novelty is thus clear. Likewise, Rambam's rule that if he told the seller, "I am writing it in another's name only so that people should not know I am the purchaser," the seller must write another deed, is sensible. One might otherwise say he gave that explanation merely so the seller could later testify that he never intended to confer the deed upon the named person. But such testimony is unnecessary, because Reuven is already protected by his possession and migo. Therefore the statement must have been intended as a stipulation for a replacement deed. This reasoning depends on ruling like Rava that the holder need not prove the documentary transfer. But the Bava Kamma sugya itself is introduced through Abaye. Abaye holds in Bava Batra that a person claiming through documentary transfer must bring proof. Under Abaye, the purchaser may indeed have had a reason to tell the witnesses only so they could later testify that he never conferred the field upon the person named in the deed. That is why the Gemara needs the special teaching: despite that possible purpose, his statement can be understood as requiring a new deed in his own name. This aligns Rambam with the Gemara and explains why he formulates the law differently. Accordingly, Mechaber properly omitted Rambam's formulation in the context of a loan note. In a loan, the lender is seeking to extract money from the borrower. There the borrower can say, "You are not my litigant; prove that the note was transferred to you." The holder cannot simply say that the written transfer was lost. Therefore, if the original lender tells the borrower, "The note is in another's name only so that people should not know I am the lender," that statement may have been made merely to prevent the borrower from later denying his standing. It does not necessarily mean the borrower undertook to write a replacement note. This differs from a land sale, where the purchaser is already in possession of the field and can defend his possession against the named person. However one resolves Rambam's exact position, the practical law in loan documents seems clear: merely telling the borrower that the note was written in another's name for concealment does not obligate him to write a new note. Rambam is essentially alone in the broader formulation, and we should not extend it beyond its clearest case.
The law is with the community. Sema writes that the principal reason is local custom: communal leases were commonly written in the name of a single individual. Therefore the community is believed to say that the document belongs to them. Without such a custom, however, the person holding the document would be believed and could claim that the communal wording was written merely so as not to appear wealthy. Shach disagrees. He says the unqualified language of Shulchan Aruch implies that the ruling does not depend on local custom. Since the document itself says it arises from a communal lease, the holder cannot claim that this wording was inserted merely so as not to appear wealthy, particularly because it appears before the witnesses' signatures. In my opinion, the issue depends on the dispute discussed above in seif katan 17. According to the authorities who hold that a person may be believed to say, "The property is mine even though the document was written in another person's name," because the document is in his possession, then here too the community can prevail only because there was a local custom to write communal leases in an individual's name. Otherwise, the holder would be believed simply from possession of the document. Why should a statement inside the instrument that it is a communal lease be stronger than an entire document written in another person's name? But according to the authorities who hold that the holder is not believed and the named person can say, "It is mine; you conferred it upon me," no special custom is needed here. The community is entitled to rely on the document's own language that it concerns a communal lease. The defense that the phrase was inserted merely so as not to appear wealthy is unavailable because it was written before the witnesses signed. Shulchan Aruch omitted the earlier ruling and, as I explained, followed Rashba and those who agree with him. He therefore also omitted the need for a special local custom here. Thus this issue depends on the same underlying dispute.
