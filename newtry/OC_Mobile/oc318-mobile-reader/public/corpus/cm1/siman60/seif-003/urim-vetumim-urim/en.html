Produce. Meaning maintenance, or maintenance at the obligor's table. If the beneficiary instead demands money and does not wish to eat at his table, the law is with the beneficiary, Sema.
According to the economy of a shared household. Many people eating together do not incur expenses in direct proportion to the number of individuals, because many expenses needed for one person are also sufficient for several. He therefore need pay only the amount that would represent this individual's proportionate share if many people were eating together.
Because of the conduct of the beneficiary, etc. Such as where he or members of his household regularly provoke quarrels, and he could prevent them but does not, or similar circumstances. The matter is entrusted to the judge according to what he sees, Bach and Shach.
If the beneficiary became ill, etc. Since he is not receiving support by virtue of a beit din imposed marital condition, medical expenses are not included, even medical care with no fixed cost. This differs from a wife's maintenance, which is owed by marital enactment. See Tumim, where I concluded that many authorities disagree with this law. It is therefore a doubtful question of law.
Maintenance, etc. Even if no medical treatment is being provided and there is no medical expense at all, the obligor must nevertheless provide the monetary value of the maintenance the beneficiary would have received while healthy.
Or as long as he needs. Sema writes that the wording requires explanation. Perhaps it means that if the beneficiary was needy at the time of the vow, we interpret the promise as lasting only as long as he remains needy, whereas otherwise we interpret it as permanent. According to what I wrote in Tumim, the case concerns a vow to charity, where one's verbal undertaking is itself binding. Therefore, once the beneficiary is no longer needy, such as if he becomes wealthy, the charity obligation ends and there is no continuing vow. But as long as he remains needy, the vow continues. This is correct.
Some say that if he accepted, etc. See Tumim, where I wrote that the authorities do not actually disagree. Rashba discusses a promise made to a poor person, which becomes a charity vow through speech, and therefore he rules stringently that the words imply all the days of the beneficiary's life. Rosh, the author of the dissenting opinion, discusses an ordinary undertaking where the law of vows does not apply. In that case the claimant must produce proof. This is the correct law.
He is discharged after one year. Since he said, "One hundred gold coins per year," the statement cannot refer to less than one full year. But if he merely said, "I will support him," he may argue that he meant only a single meal, Sema. This is correct and obvious.
Beit din assesses, etc. See Rosh, chapter HaNose, that one who undertakes to support another cannot reduce the support below the standard of his own household, where they eat meat and drink wine, and give the beneficiary only seeds and water. Responsa Shevus Yaakov, siman 150, writes that one who undertakes to support his daughter in law or daughter, and she gives birth, must provide during the postpartum period the foods appropriate for her according to local custom. He disagrees with Responsa Bach. See there.
He is exempt from her maintenance. This means that even if she left an heir under Torah law, he is exempt, because he obligated himself in maintenance only during her lifetime.
He is exempt from her share. The reason is that the obligation itself certainly remains payable even after her death, because he undertook a fixed monetary amount. But a husband does not inherit property merely expected to come to his wife after her death, and this payment is considered such expected property. Therefore he receives nothing. If she had another heir, that heir would acquire the claim and the obligor would have to pay.
From her share. Taz seeks to prove from this that where one undertakes to support his son and daughter in law, or his daughter and son in law, for a number of years, and his own child dies, he must nevertheless continue supporting the surviving in law. Beis Shmuel and Chacham Tzvi agree. See Tumim, where I established that there is no proof. The present case does not concern his own daughter. Where it is his daughter and grandchild, he owes no continued maintenance, neither to the surviving spouse nor to the daughter's heirs, because we follow Rabbeinu Tam's presumption, as stated in Even HaEzer siman 53. Nevertheless, compromise is proper because a great authority already ruled otherwise. This is after the first two years. During the two year period of the dowry return enactment, it appears that everyone agrees there is no continuing maintenance obligation, because that enactment itself was made to prevent grief, and there could be no greater grief than this.
