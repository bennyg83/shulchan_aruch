But only by reason of the one who undertook the obligation, etc. The Bach writes that this appears to refer to where he does not wish to support him. The same applies to another impediment, such as where the obstruction comes from members of his household who provoke quarrel and strife with him. Needless to say, if the obligor himself shows him hatred or regularly quarrels with him, all of this is included in an impediment that comes from the obligor, and he must give him the full value of his maintenance. Even though he says verbally that he wishes to support him at his table, nevertheless his heart is not with him. This depends upon the assessment of the dayan. The Rama's words in the gloss also appear so. Regarding what he writes, that members of his household provoke quarrel and strife with him, it appears that this is specifically where it is evident that this is with the obligor's consent, or where he has the ability to protest against the members of his household and does not protest, or the like. Otherwise, it is not proper that he be held liable because of what members of his household caused. See Even HaEzer, siman 114. See Responsa Maharshal, siman 28.
He is not obligated for his medical treatment, etc. From the words of Tosafos in Perek Na'arah SheNispattah, Kesubos 50b, at the end of the passage beginning 'u'mai aliyah,' it appears that he is obligated to heal him. However, the words of the Ritva are primary, as is evident from the Yerushalmi at the beginning of Perek HaNoseh regarding one who stipulated to support his wife's daughter. The Ritva and the Rosh cite it there. The Rosh writes regarding one who undertook to support his wife's daughter that if she became ill he is not obligated to heal her. Although regarding a wife's maintenance we say that medical treatment whose cost is not fixed is included in maintenance, that applies to one who supports her by virtue of the conditions of beit din, for he is obligated to provide all her needs. But one who merely accepts upon himself to maintain another has not included medical treatment in that undertaking. So too writes the Tur in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 114, see there. This is also the accepted practice.
All the days of his life, or as long as he needs, etc. Perhaps the meaning is as follows: if he vowed this to him at a time when the recipient was in need, presumably his intent was for as long as he remained in need. If he vowed it at a time when the recipient was not in need, his intent was for all the days of his life. This requires study. These are the words of the Sema.
And some say that if he accepted upon himself, etc. These dissenting authorities disagree with the first view, as is also evident from the responsum of the Rosh there and from Darkei Moshe, contrary to the Levush. The reason that here he is obligated for one year, whereas below in siman 212, seif 1, in the gloss, he is discharged after one hour, is that here he revealed his intent for a year, unlike there, where he merely said that the person may reside there without specifying a time. Likewise, at the end of siman 163, where one is exempted from tax for one year, the case concerns a particular tax, or alternatively a place where the entire year's tax is collected at one time. All this is written by the Sema, see there at length.
He is exempt, etc. Even so, he is exempt from her share. The words of the Rama and the Levush here are obscure. At first glance, the Rama seems to imply that even where he fixed a specific sum he is entirely exempt from her share, on the ground that he undertook only to support her for as long as she required maintenance. This appears even more strongly from the Levush. But that cannot be correct. Responsa Rashba, siman 970, the source of this ruling, states explicitly that he is exempt only vis a vis her husband, because a husband does not inherit a loan owed to his wife, since a husband does not take property that is merely prospective, and a loan is considered prospective property with respect to him, as explained in Even HaEzer at the beginning of siman 90. So too the Rama writes in the gloss in Even HaEzer at the end of siman 114 that if he wrote the obligation to him and to his wife, the husband does not inherit his wife's share. The Levush there likewise concludes that he does not inherit his wife's share because it is prospective property. Accordingly, the words of the Rama and the Levush here must also be understood in this way. It follows that an undertaking to support them for a stated period and an undertaking fixing a specific sum do not have the same law. In ordinary maintenance for a period he is entirely exempt even toward her heirs, whereas if he fixed a sum for her he must pay it to her heirs and is exempt only toward her husband. This appears clear to me.
