Even the amount that accrued during the wife’s lifetime is not inherited by the husband if she died before collecting it, because it is a debt due to her and is considered expectant property; a husband does not inherit expectant property as he does property already possessed. A case arose where a father undertook to provide a fixed amount of food and housing for his son and daughter-in-law, and the son died after marriage. Some argued that the father was exempt from the daughter-in-law’s portion because he intended support only while she remained his daughter-in-law, comparing the matter to wedding dowry where an assessment may show that the father intended the gift only for his daughter’s benefit. This comparison is incorrect. The Mishnah states that one who undertakes to support his wife’s daughter for five years remains obligated even after divorcing the mother, and his heirs remain obligated after his death. Merely describing her as “my wife’s daughter” does not limit the term, since prudent people had to add expressly, “for as long as you are with me.” Likewise, writing that support is for “the couple” does not condition it on both spouses remaining alive. The Rashba’s ruling concerning support for Shimon and his wife likewise treats the undertaking as two separate debts. Once the marriage occurred, each acquired an independent right. This differs from dowry, whose language and purpose may make the daughter’s enjoyment central and where transfer may remain incomplete. Support and housing are direct gifts that vest upon marriage. Although the gloss discusses several challenges from Rabbenu Tam’s dowry doctrine, it concludes that support differs because the beneficiary acquired a complete right from the outset, the Mishnah directly supports this result, and one should not extend the exceptional dowry presumption to other gifts. The daughter-in-law therefore remains entitled to her share of support and housing.
