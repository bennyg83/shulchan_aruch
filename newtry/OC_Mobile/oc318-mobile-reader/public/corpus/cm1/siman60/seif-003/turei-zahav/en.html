It is treated like a debt owed to the wife that she had not yet collected, which is merely prospective property, and a husband does not inherit prospective property as he inherits property already in possession.
An incident occurred in our community in which a man wrote to his son and daughter-in-law that he would provide them a fixed amount for food and housing. His son died after the marriage, and the daughter-in-law claimed her share of the support and housing. Some argued that the father was exempt, because it is self-evident that he undertook to provide for her only so long as she remained his daughter-in-law. They brought proof from the law of a bride's dowry in Even HaEzer, siman 52: where the daughter dies, we follow Rabbeinu Tam and do not extract money from the father, because we presume he wrote the dowry only so that his daughter would benefit. In my humble opinion, the cases are entirely different. The present law is explicit in the Mishnah at the beginning of chapter HaNoseh: one who marries a woman and undertakes to support her daughter for five years remains obligated even if he divorces the mother, and even after his death his heirs must support the daughter. We do not say he undertook this only so long as she remained his wife's daughter. Indeed, even if he explicitly writes in the document, "I obligate myself to support my wife's daughter," he cannot later argue that the phrase "my wife's daughter" means only while her mother remains his wife. Otherwise, why does the Mishnah say that prudent men wrote expressly, "so long as you are with me"? This shows that only such an explicit limiting phrase releases him, and the mere description "my wife's daughter" does not. The same applies here. Although he wrote that he would give support "to the couple," one cannot infer that the obligation lasts only so long as the couple remains intact, because that wording is no stronger than "my wife's daughter." Nor is there any greater force in this descriptive language than simply naming two people, as Rashba writes in responsum 970. This is precisely the law cited by Rama here and in Even HaEzer, siman 117, concerning one who undertook to support Shimon and his wife. There, one person promised support to Reuven if he would marry a certain woman; the woman died and the husband sought to claim her share as well. The ruling was that the undertaking to support Shimon and his wife created separate debts to two ordinary individuals. Each therefore had an independent claim against the promisor. The wife's uncollected share became prospective property, which the husband does not inherit. This is the reason for Rama's ruling here. We clearly learn that at least the surviving spouse's own share remains due; no presumption helps us say that the promisor undertook support only while the marriage continued. Why is this not comparable to the law of a bride's dowry? Because here the promisor's obligation became independently fixed toward each beneficiary when he wrote that he would support both of them, just as with an undertaking to support a wife's daughter. Once the marriage occurred, the obligation became a complete debt. A bride's dowry is different, because the obligation is from the father to the daughter in the form "the dowry that she brings with her." The editor raises a difficulty from Tosafos in chapter Na'arah Shenispatesah, where Rabbeinu Tam explains the Toras Kohanim's statement, "Your strength will be spent in vain," as referring to one who promised his daughter much money and she died within the seven wedding days, so that he lost both his daughter and his money. Rabbeinu Tam says the case must be where the groom already possesses the money. If the Taz's distinction depended only on the particular wording "that she will bring with her from her father's house," Tosafos could have answered more simply that the Baraisa concerns a promise written without that formula. One might answer that the Baraisa's phrase "one who promises for his daughter" implies such a formula. But Mahari in siman 321 derives an even stronger rule from that Baraisa: even if the dowry was converted into a loan obligation and the document no longer mentions that the money is dowry, the husband does not acquire it, because we follow the underlying presumption that the father intended the gift only for his daughter's benefit. He proves this from Tosafos's need to establish the Baraisa where the groom already has possession rather than merely where the father converted the obligation into a loan. This indicates that the rule does not depend on the wording of the original conditions but on the nature of the dowry itself. Rama likewise writes in Even HaEzer that one who promises money to his daughter or son-in-law is subject to the dispute of Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam, implying that even a promise directly to the son-in-law may be subject to Rabbeinu Tam's presumption. It is therefore possible to uphold the Taz's ruling based on substance rather than wording: a dowry is fundamentally a gift for the daughter, not the husband, and he merely assumes responsibility for it; therefore, until it actually reaches him, he does not acquire it upon her death. Support and similar gifts, by contrast, are primarily gifts to the recipients themselves, and the right vests when the marriage occurs. There is some indication against this distinction from R' Eliyahu Mizrachi, who labored to uphold a groom's ownership of gifts that had already reached him on the ground that he was in possession; perhaps he invoked possession only as an additional argument to silence dissenters. In that responsum he too indicates a distinction between gifts and dowry along the lines stated. Rama's further ruling there, however, that even when a father promises a gift to his son we presume he intended it only for his son's benefit, would seem to undermine the distinction, because such a gift is a complete gift to the son. Chelkas Mechokek already objected, and Beis Shmuel did not solve the problem by saying Rama wrote it independently, since the cases are simply not analogous. It appears that Rama's gloss belongs later, in the discussion of the enactment of Rabbeinu Tam and the communities. In any event, the Taz's ruling is correct for two reasons. First, there is a major distinction between dowry and support. With dowry, circumstances change between the time of writing and the daughter's death: at the time of writing the groom has not acquired the actual assets, is responsible for them, and cannot freely sell them because of the wife's family interest; only at her death does he seek to take all of it as inheritance. We can therefore say that when the moment of acquisition comes and the property is not in his possession, he does not acquire it because the father never wrote with that intent. Support, gifts, and similar obligations are different: the recipient acquires the right fully from the time of writing, and there is no basis to divest him at the wife's death of property already fully acquired. Even if this precise reason does not fit a father's promise directly to his son, the conceptual distinction is still strong. Second, the Mishnah at the beginning of HaNoseh strongly supports the rule. Moreover, Rabbeinu Tam's law itself is a novelty, even in dowry, and we apply the principle that one should not extend a novel rule beyond its stated case. The presumption was stated specifically regarding dowry, which everyone has and regarding which people's intentions can reasonably be treated uniformly. A special promise of support or a gift is unusual, and we say that one who departs from the ordinary pattern gave generously. The potential loss is also much greater. Presumptions of intent are easily distinguished from one case to another, as Tosafos write in many places. Therefore the ruling of the Taz is correct. The Baraisa in Na'arah Shenispatesah likewise speaks specifically of "clothing and utensils that she will bring with her from her father's house to her husband's house." There alone do we say that the father's intent was specifically that she benefit, because the core gift is attributed to her and the husband receives it as nichsei tzon barzel. A commitment of support and housing that a father gives to the couple, or on condition that someone marry a certain woman, becomes binding once the marriage occurs. When I later merited to study the sugya of a bride's dowry, I saw that according to Rabbeinu Tam, the foundation of the Sages' view that the groom does not acquire the dowry is precisely the phrase that the assets are to come with the daughter from her father's house to her husband's house. Although the marriage has taken place, so long as the contemplated act of bringing the property from her father's house to her husband's has not occurred, the groom has not acquired it. The Sages compare that outstanding act to the unperformed chuppah in the case of R' Elazar ben Azaryah. R' Nasan distinguishes between the act of bringing the assets and entering the chuppah. According to the halacha, then, the groom fails to acquire specifically where some condition of the father's undertaking remains unperformed, namely delivery of the property into the husband's domain. An undertaking to support a wife's daughter lacks any such outstanding act; therefore the obligation continues even after the mother is divorced. This is clear and entirely correct. Accordingly, in the present case the father must pay the daughter-in-law her share of the food and housing.
