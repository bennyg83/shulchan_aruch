One. At the end of siman 163, regarding a community that exempted an individual from taxes, Rama likewise writes that they may say they exempted him for only one year. The two opinions cited by Rama here therefore disagree with one another. The first, that one who undertakes support for another 'all the days of his life' remains obligated, appears to be the Rashba's view. The second, that the obligation ends after one year, is the view of the Rosh, as is evident from Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe here and in Even HaEzer siman 114, unlike the Ir Shushan, who says they do not disagree. Rama writes in siman 212, seif 1, that if one says without specifying a term, 'So-and-so may live in my house,' even one hour is included. The reason appears to be that where no term was fixed the owner can say he intended only one hour, and the claimant bears the burden of proof. Why then does Rama rule here that he must support him for at least one year, while there one hour suffices? Here the obligor revealed an intention concerning a yearly measure by saying, 'or I will give one hundred gold coins per year.' According to the Rosh, however, where nothing at all was specified, even one hour suffices. One might then ask why Rama rules at the end of siman 163 that an exemption from tax lasts at least one year. That case must concern a tax assessed once yearly, such as a poll tax, or a place where the entire year's tax is collected in a single assessment. This appears to explain Rama's rulings here, at the end of siman 163, and at the beginning of siman 212. It remains difficult that Rama ruled without qualification in all three places and explained the distinction nowhere. See siman 212 for another resolution. This explanation, however, appears more correct to me. Thus concludes the Sema.
He fixed an amount. The Shach writes that Rama's words might appear to mean that even where a fixed sum was stipulated, the obligor becomes entirely exempt from her share. This cannot be correct. The responsum of the Rashba from which the ruling derives explicitly says otherwise, and Rama likewise rules so at the end of Even HaEzer siman 114. Rama's words here must therefore be understood as follows: where he undertook general support for a stated period, he becomes entirely exempt upon her death, even as against her heirs. But where he fixed a specific sum for her support, he must pay that amount to her heirs; he is exempt only from paying it to her husband. This appears entirely clear to me. Thus concludes the Shach. The Taz discusses a case in which a father undertook to provide his son and daughter-in-law with a fixed amount of food and lodging, the son died after marriage, and the daughter-in-law claimed her share; see there at length. Responsa Shevut Yaakov, volume 1, siman 150, rules that one who undertook to support his son and daughter-in-law must provide the daughter-in-law during childbirth with the costly and light foods customarily given to Jewish women, and he disagrees with the Bach, who ruled otherwise.
