The law is with the recipient of the gift. Beit Shmuel writes in Even HaEzer that a person obligated to support another must give money only where the recipient has food available from another source. If he has no other food, the obligor may provide actual food rather than cash. He proves this from Ketubot, where the Gemara explains that a woman has only one stomach. In my view Sema's contrary position is primary. The phrase 'to support him' ordinarily means to provide the financial means by which he can obtain the food suitable to himself. The Jerusalem Talmud explicitly rules that where one party wants produce and the recipient wants money, the recipient of the gift has the stronger claim. The same applies even to support of a daughter, as the Jerusalem Talmud derives from the Mishnah concerning payment of the value of support. The Babylonian Gemara does not contradict this. In the case of a mill rented in exchange for grinding the landlord's household grain, once the landlord became wealthy and acquired another mill, he demanded monetary rent. Ravina compared this to the support Mishnah, but Rav Avira distinguished because the landlord can tell the tenant, 'Grind and sell, grind and keep the proceeds.' Only where the tenant has no other work for the mill can coercing him to take money instead become a matter of avoiding Sodom-like conduct. The issue there is therefore not that a support obligation is inherently discharged only through actual food. It concerns whether the obligor can satisfy the agreed service through the very commodity or service originally promised. Accordingly, where a person undertakes simply 'to support' another, the recipient can ordinarily demand the monetary value needed to obtain appropriate food. Where the undertaking is expressly 'to support him at my table,' different rules apply, as explained in the following seif.
Only because of the obligor. Shach writes that where the obstacle to eating at the obligor's table comes from the obligor's own household, he must provide support elsewhere. At first this seems difficult from the law of a widow, from which Beit Yosef derived the rule that heirs may say, 'If you will not live with us, you receive no support.' Yet the Mishnah also teaches that where the widow is young and the heirs are young, she may live elsewhere and they must support her there. Likewise, if a wife cannot live with her husband because neighbors are spreading damaging rumors about her, he must support her elsewhere. This shows that the source of the obstacle need not be the obligor himself. The distinction is between support imposed by the rabbinic marital framework and support voluntarily undertaken by contract. A wife's support is a condition of beit din. The Sages imposed on the husband the normal risks and unavoidable circumstances associated with that obligation. Even if she becomes ill, he must provide the food appropriate for a sick person. Therefore, if an external circumstance recognized by beit din makes it impossible for her to eat at his table, he must support her elsewhere. A person who voluntarily undertakes to support another at his table is different. He does not ordinarily assume every unavoidable circumstance that may arise to the recipient. If the recipient becomes ill or some external circumstance prevents him from eating there, the obligor need provide only the value corresponding to the benefit actually lost, often according to the ordinary saving in household expenses. The Torah does not impose accidental risks beyond what he undertook. But if the obstacle comes from the obligor himself, the recipient may say, 'You undertook to support me, and if you refuse to do so at your table, support me separately.' Even then, the obligor ordinarily owes food, not necessarily cash. If the recipient demands money while the obligor offers actual food, the obligor has the stronger claim unless the terms imply otherwise. A simple undertaking 'to support' can mean payment sufficient for the recipient to obtain appropriate food, as explained above. But 'to support at my table' plainly describes actual food at the obligor's table, not unrestricted cash. If the deed adds 'at my table specifically,' the extra wording can be construed to mean that if the recipient chooses not to eat there, the obligor is exempt. If the obstacle comes from the obligor, however, he must provide the full value of support elsewhere. Finally, the measure differs between 'to support' and 'to support at my table.' The first is measured according to the recipient's appropriate standard. The second is measured according to the food ordinarily provided at the obligor's table.

It implies for as long as he needs. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer cites Rashba as proving that an unspecified undertaking means forever, from Nedarim, where an unqualified prohibition of all produce lasts indefinitely. This seems strong and requires explanation according to Rosh's later view, cited by Rama, that an unspecified monetary undertaking does not necessarily mean forever. The rule that documents are interpreted like vows applies where the Gemara established that a phrase has a definite meaning in ordinary human language. For example, where 'until Pesach' means until but not including Pesach, that interpretation is linguistic and therefore transfers to documents. But many stringencies in vows arise not from linguistic certainty but because a person entering a vow places himself into the doubtful scope of his own words. That principle does not govern money. Proof is the case of one who consecrates 'my animals' or 'my beasts' and thereby includes a hybrid animal because in consecration he enters into the doubtful breadth of his words. In an ordinary monetary writing saying 'my animals' or 'my beasts,' the hybrid is not necessarily included, because the burden of proof rests on the claimant. This shows that vow interpretation is not mechanically imported into money where its basis is the special stringency of vows. So too, when Nedarim says an unspecified vow lasts forever, that can be because the person accepted every stringent possible meaning of the unqualified language. In money, by contrast, where an unqualified phrase could mean forever or only a limited period, the holder of the document has the burden of proving the broader obligation. Even if most people use the phrase to mean indefinitely, money is not extracted solely by following a majority against possession. The cases in Gittin concerning a condition to nurse a child or provide support are not contrary. There the condition was imposed for the recipient's benefit and relief, or for the purpose of causing discomfort, and therefore its scope follows the purpose of the condition. Rashba and Ran use those cases specifically where the donor conditions a gift on being supported, not where a person simply undertakes an indefinite obligation to another. The two are different.
