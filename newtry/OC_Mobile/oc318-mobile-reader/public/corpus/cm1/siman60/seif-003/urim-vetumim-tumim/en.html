He is not obligated for her medical treatment. Shach writes that Tosafot Ketubot 50b appear to disagree and hold that he is obligated in support including medical needs. Shach says Ritva's view is primary, based on the Yerushalmi. I am very hesitant to decide the law this way. Rosh's entire distinction rests on his understanding of the Gittin sugya. He follows Rashi that an undertaking to support for five years is considered fixed and therefore does not pose a question against R. Chananel. Since ordinary support could otherwise vary, Rosh answers Tosafot's difficulty by distinguishing between support imposed by a condition of beit din, which includes medical expenses, and support voluntarily undertaken for a wife's daughter, which does not. But according to Rashba, Ramban, and their school, who hold that even a five-year support obligation is considered unfixed and that the Gemara's question applies to R. Chananel as well, there is no proof for Rosh's distinction at all. Indeed, the proof cuts the other way. Ramban in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 64, and Rashba in his Gittin chiddushim prove that support is unfixed from the statement in Ketubot that support has no fixed amount. If they accepted Rosh's distinction, the cases would not be comparable: the support obligation imposed by beit din includes uncapped medical expenses, while an ordinary undertaking to support another person would not. Their proof therefore implies they do not accept that distinction. There is another proof from Rambam's approach. The Gemara in Ketubot asks whether the support obligation collects from encumbered property and answers that a kinyan was made. If an ordinary undertaking to support a wife's daughter were fixed because medical treatment is excluded, while daughters' support under the marriage conditions were unfixed because medical treatment is included, then the Gemara's comparison would fail. It could simply answer that the two obligations differ in their degree of fixedness. Since it does not, we must say either both are fixed or both unfixed in the same legal sense. Ritva's proof from the Yerushalmi is also not conclusive. The Yerushalmi says that if she becomes ill, the obligation resembles one where she married. This can simply mean that he must continue giving her the same food allowance she would have received if healthy, even though the illness may reduce her food consumption. It need not mean he bears all medical expenses. The medical treatment may either be unnecessary, separately fixed, or outside the support undertaking. Rosh himself cites the Yerushalmi but does not use it as proof for his medical-expense distinction. He derives that distinction from reasoning. Ritva too does not clearly derive the exemption itself from the Yerushalmi; rather, he uses the Yerushalmi to show that the ordinary food allowance continues even during illness. Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 114 likewise does not infer from the Yerushalmi that medical expenses are excluded. He treats the exemption as otherwise established and uses the Yerushalmi only to show that the healthy person's normal support amount must still be given during illness. Therefore this law remains a genuine unresolved dispute. Tosafot, Ramban, and Rashba do not appear to accept the asserted exemption from medical treatment, and the Yerushalmi does not decide the issue. It should be treated as a safek din rather than stated categorically.
And some say that if he undertook to support him without qualification, and so forth. In my opinion there is no dispute at all. The first view, that of Rashba, appears to concern a vow to support a poor person. The obligation operates as a neder of charity, under the principle that one's verbal pledge is binding in matters of tzedakah. Ordinary unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. Therefore, if he vows to support the poor person without limitation, he must do so throughout the poor person's life so long as he remains poor, or for as long as the person remains in need. If the person becomes wealthy in the interim, the mitzvah of tzedakah no longer applies and the vow ends. This resolves Taz's question. Taz asks: even if the person no longer needs support, why should the obligation end? One who undertakes to support his wife's daughter remains obligated even after she marries. The answer is that here the liability is based on the law of a tzedakah vow. Once the beneficiary is no longer needy, the charitable vow has no object. Rashba's proof, as cited by Beit Yosef in Even HaEzer siman 109, is from Nedarim: one who says, "These fruits are forbidden to me," without specifying a period, is understood to mean forever. That proof makes sense only in the law of vows, where unspecified vows are interpreted stringently. Ran in Nedarim similarly explains that unspecified vows are not treated as fixed because they resemble consecrated property, whose sanctity endures indefinitely. See Yoreh Deah siman 218. This confirms that Rashba is discussing a vow. There is another proof. Rashba himself, in his Gittin chiddushim concerning one who says, "on condition that you serve my father" or "nurse my child," leaves unresolved whether an unspecified term in monetary matters means one month or even one day. Ran and Maggid Mishneh cite him similarly. How then could Rashba here rule definitively that an unspecified support obligation continues indefinitely? The answer is that here it is a neder, where the law is different. Rosh, in responsum klal 6, siman 18, however, plainly does not speak of a vow, despite using language about "being released from his vow." The entire responsum reasons from the principle that the holder of a document bears the burden of ambiguous language, and that is a monetary rule, not the law of vows. In vows, uncertainty is generally treated stringently. Rosh even cites the case of one who vows "twenty" and similar examples only to illustrate a general interpretive principle that one chooses the smaller measure where the larger has no natural limit. He is not applying the ordinary law that unspecified vows are stringent. Thus Rosh's case is an ordinary contractual undertaking. There, because the claimant seeks to extract money and the duration is uncertain, the narrower interpretation applies. Rashba's case is a tzedakah vow, where unspecified language is interpreted stringently. The two authorities do not disagree. This is the correct reconciliation.
Reuven who undertook, and so forth. See Urim, where I explained the case in accordance with Shach. Taz raises a difficulty. The ruling implies that after one spouse dies, the obligor must continue supporting the surviving spouse for that person's share. Why is this not governed by the law of dowry commitments in Even HaEzer siman 53, where even after writing and committing, we sometimes say there was an implied assumption that the property was given only so that the promisor's child would benefit from the marriage? Taz further asks from the Mishnah where a man marries a woman and undertakes to support her daughter for five years. Even if his wife dies, he remains obligated to support the stepdaughter. Why do we not apply the same implied assumption and say he undertook only because his wife would remain alive and enjoy the arrangement? Taz therefore distinguishes between dowry that is written as property the woman brings into the marriage and an ordinary support undertaking. R. Natan, in his glosses, offers another strained distinction: dowry fundamentally belongs to the daughter, whereas here the obligor undertook directly to support both spouses. These distinctions are difficult, especially because Rama in Even HaEzer rules that even a father who promises property to his son can invoke the dowry presumption. If so, there is no meaningful basis to distinguish the son's own benefit from the daughter-in-law's. The question, however, is not difficult. The Gemara compares R. Natan's implied-assumption rule, which according to Rabbenu Tam applies even after nissuin, to R. Elazar ben Azaryah's rule that a woman does not receive the ketubah supplement after erusin but does after nissuin. Tosafot explain in Ketubot 47b that once nissuin occurred, the husband wrote the supplement because of the affection of chuppah, and that affection has already been realized. Thus, if a husband writes his wife an enormous ketubah supplement and she dies shortly after nissuin without ever having relations, she nevertheless acquired that supplement from the moment of chuppah. The relevant implied condition is satisfied by entry into chuppah itself. If so, what difference is there between adding one thousand dinars to the ketubah and undertaking to support her daughter? That too is part of what he adds because of the marriage. Once the marriage occurs, the affection of chuppah that induced the undertaking has been realized. The obligation therefore remains even if the wife later dies. This also accords with Chelkat Mechokek, who argues against Rama's rule concerning a father's promise to his son. Once the son marries, the son's liability to his wife arises immediately, and property already placed in the father's hands for that purpose may be treated as belonging beneficially to the son. Beit Shmuel and R. Natan agree. One might still hesitate because a ketubah is inherently designed for collection upon death or divorce, while support of a stepdaughter is an ongoing obligation that could plausibly have been intended only while the wife lived. Yet the Yerushalmi forces the broader approach. Baal HaIttur explains the Yerushalmi's question on the Mishnah of supporting a wife's daughter. The Yerushalmi asks from R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish, who say that one who writes an obligation believing he already owed it and later discovers he did not owe it is exempt. Baal HaIttur understands the question to be that the husband thought he would be supporting his wife together with her daughter. Had he known his wife would die, he would never have undertaken the daughter's support. The Yerushalmi answers that he made it like a ketubah supplement. In other words, once the marriage occurred, the support undertaking became part of the marriage consideration just like the ketubah supplement and survives the wife's later death. The same Yerushalmi raises a parallel issue concerning the ketubah supplement itself and answers that it is part of the marital addition. This is exactly the principle stated above. Even according to Rif and those who interpret R. Yochanan and Reish Lakish differently, the conceptual point remains valid. The Yerushalmi teaches that the support undertaking is treated as part of the marriage supplement and is fully acquired through nissuin. This removes Taz's difficulty. There is also no difficulty from the present responsum of Rashba itself. Rashba does not follow Rabbenu Tam's dowry presumption; he follows Rif, Rambam, and Rashi. So even on Taz's assumptions, Rashba's own ruling is consistent. And even according to Rama, who does cite Rabbenu Tam, one can distinguish between a father promising property specifically to his own daughter, where his dominant intention is that she herself benefit, and a person undertaking support of another orphan or unrelated individual in connection with marriage. In the latter case the charitable or marital purpose is fulfilled once the marriage occurs, and there is no comparable personal implied condition. This distinction is supported by the debate in Yoreh Deah siman 253 concerning one who vows money for an orphan bride and she dies. The authorities discuss whether her heirs receive the money. If the ordinary dowry presumption applied to every marital contribution, there would be no dispute. Rather, the strong implied condition exists particularly where one promises for one's own child. This also answers Beit Shmuel's question against Maharshal and Binyan Ariel concerning a father who promised his own daughter property and she died. They distinguish that from a donor who promised funds for an unrelated orphan, where no such familial implied condition exists. Therefore the ruling here creates no contradiction. Still, after clarifying all this, the underlying intuition remains powerful: where one explicitly undertakes to support both spouses for a period and one dies, perhaps the support portion intended for the deceased spouse should end. The Yerushalmi strongly suggests otherwise in the marriage context, but I hesitate to impose the full burden in every practical case. Since Taz, Chacham Tzvi, and Beit Shmuel already ruled on the matter, my practical approach is to seek compromise where possible. Within the first year governed by the communal return enactment, however, there is much less room for doubt. That enactment was made because "your strength should not be spent in vain." If one spouse dies within that period, the disappointment and loss are precisely within the spirit of the enactment. There is therefore strong reason not to impose continued support, and Taz's proof does not apply because the Mishnah he cited does not concern that communal enactment.
