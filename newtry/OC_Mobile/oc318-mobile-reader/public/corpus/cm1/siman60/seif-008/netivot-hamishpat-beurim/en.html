Shach implies that where it is possible that Levi's admission was true, namely that Reuven really acted only as Levi's agent, the admission still may not transfer the document according to some authorities. I find this very difficult. If the admission may be factually true, the one who admits cannot retract. A litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. The authorities who question the force of *odita* must therefore be discussing a case where it was already established that the money was the admitter's and he now seeks to create a transfer by means of a knowingly false acknowledgment. One might say that although Levi's admission binds Levi personally, it should not force Shimon the debtor to litigate with Reuven, because Levi cannot prejudice Shimon through his own admission. But Levi can be believed through a migo. He could have assigned the debt to Reuven by writing and delivery, or under authorities who recognize transfer through acquisition with land, he could have used that form. Once his statement, 'Reuven was my agent,' is accepted, he cannot later waive the debt because an agent has no power of waiver in his own right. This resembles the law where a father says, 'This is my son.' If he is believed through a migo that he could have made a gift, his statement then fixes the son's legal status against him and cannot be casually withdrawn. So too here. Accordingly, the dispute cited by Baal HaTerumot concerns true *odita*, where everyone knows the money was the speaker's and he nevertheless says it belonged to another in order to transfer it. Mechaber and those who follow Tosafot hold that *odita* is an actual Torah mode of acquisition, not merely ordinary evidentiary admission. Otherwise it could not transfer money for purposes such as redemption of maaser. The opposing authorities treat *odita* only as the rule that a litigant is bound by his own admission and therefore do not allow it to impose a liability on the debtor where the admitted fact is known to be false.
If he does not prejudice others. Taz asks that Shimon should not have to pay so long as his debt document is not returned, as Beit Yosef writes in siman 66. Urim VeTumim answers by constructing a case involving an earlier creditor and a lien on movables together with land. In my view the difficulty does not arise. Beit Yosef's case concerns a known transfer of the document by delivery without the necessary written assignment. Even according to the view that the transferee does not acquire the underlying debt, he does acquire the physical paper. The borrower can therefore tell the original creditor, 'You placed my document in another person's hands, and you must recover it before I pay you.' Here, however, the claimant is not believed in saying that the document belonged to him only as agent. If he is not believed regarding the underlying debt, he is likewise not believed regarding ownership of the paper itself. There is therefore no established separate paper right in another person's hands that the borrower can invoke. Moreover, when the original creditor himself owes money to third-party creditors, those creditors can collect directly from the borrower under Rabbi Natan's lien and can receive a beit din receipt protecting the borrower against the outstanding document. They can say, 'Why should we lose because your creditor placed the document in another person's hands?' Even if the document were physically in the original creditor's own hand and he refused to surrender it, the borrower could still be required to pay the creditor's creditor against a judicial receipt, because the debt is now legally theirs under Rabbi Natan's rule. Urim VeTumim further writes that even a migo that the creditor could have burned the document or claimed payment does not help when his admission prejudices others. A migo from one person to another is not used in that way. Burning the document would benefit the borrower, whereas the present statement benefits the third person to whom the creditor says the document belongs. Those are different litigating interests.
