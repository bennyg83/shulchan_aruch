The questioner wished to distinguish that one who undertakes to support another for life is also obligated in medical expenses. He argued that only where a fixed number of years was specified, as with a stepdaughter, are medical expenses not included in maintenance, because the obligor did not necessarily contemplate illness during that particular period. Where he undertakes maintenance for the person's entire life, however, illness will certainly occur at some point, and medical expenses should be included. Rabbi Akiva Eiger answers that this is not implied by the Rosh, who distinguishes between a stepdaughter and a widow on the ground that the widow's maintenance is a beit din condition, without distinguishing that a widow is supported for life. This explicitly implies that even one who undertakes to support a stepdaughter for life is not obligated in medical expenses. He further discusses a man who held a debt document against his son in law. The son in law had undertaken to provide him, free of charge, the value of his maintenance for the rest of his life. After the father in law died, the son in law wished to deduct from the debt the extra cost of the light foods needed during the father in law's illness, which exceeded ordinary healthy maintenance by about forty Reichsthaler. The questioner ruled that he could not deduct it, because one who undertakes maintenance must also provide this form of sick person's food, citing Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 150. Rabbi Akiva Eiger disagrees. The plain wording of the Rama in siman 60 states that if the supported person becomes ill, the obligor is not liable for his medical treatment, 'only for the cost of maintenance when he was healthy.' Had the Rama intended merely to exclude medical treatment, he could have written simply that he is not liable for treatment. Since it is obvious that maintenance itself remains due, the added phrase implies that the obligation extends only to ordinary healthy maintenance and not to the more expensive maintenance of illness. Although in the reverse case, where the sick person's food costs less, the obligor must nevertheless provide the value of ordinary healthy maintenance, as stated in the Yerushalmi at the beginning of the chapter HaNoseh, which compares illness to marriage, and as codified in the Rama to Even HaEzer siman 114, seif 5, the wording 'only' in the Rama here still indicates that the upper limit is the cost of healthy maintenance. One cannot explain the Rama as meaning that although treatment itself is excluded, the obligor must use any savings from the sick person's reduced food toward treatment, because even where no treatment is needed he must still provide the full value of healthy maintenance. The Ritva, the source of the law, also proves this interpretation. Rabbi Akiva Eiger discusses the matter at length and concludes that in the case before him the son in law may deduct the forty Reichsthaler by which the special foods exceeded ordinary healthy maintenance, unless the original condition was specifically to support him at the son in law's table. The Bach, Responsa siman 46, writes that in such a case the obligor must provide the actual food required during illness, whether it costs more or less than healthy maintenance. According to this, the case of Shevut Yaakov cited by Baer Heitev, seif katan 14, concerning one who undertook to support his son and daughter in law, applies specifically where he undertook to support them at his table, as is customary. It would not apply where he merely undertook to pay their maintenance costs. See Even HaEzer, siman 114, seif 6, and Chelkat Mechokek and Beit Shmuel there.
