One who obligates himself, etc. If he writes, "I hereby obligate myself to give you such and such," even if the object or benefit is not presently in existence, he is obligated through personal undertaking. This is not a mere kinyan of "I will give," because the obligation attaches immediately, even though economically it is only a gift. Sema. See Tumim. If he says, "I obligate myself to give you the dates of this palm tree when they grow," and he dies before the dates come into existence, the heirs are exempt because the specific future fruits were never in existence. But if he says, "I obligate myself to give you one hundred if the dates of this palm tree grow," rather than undertaking to transfer the fruits themselves, the monetary obligation is immediate subject to the future condition. If he dies, the heirs must fulfill the obligation when the condition occurs. See there.

He is obligated. The Shach holds that even where the amount is fixed, he cannot collect from encumbered property if the subject of the obligation did not yet exist when the undertaking was made. For example, if one says, "The house that I will inherit from my father is sold to you through this personal obligation," the future house had no present existence and there was no public notice sufficient to bind purchasers. See Tumim. I conclude that with a kinyan he certainly can collect from encumbered property. Even a deed alone appears from Tur in Even HaEzer to create such collection.

The phrase "I have given" can itself be understood as an effective present gift, as Scripture says, "I have given the field's money." Where the document holder's wording can reasonably bear an effective meaning, we interpret the document in his favor rather than assume a meaningless admission formula.
