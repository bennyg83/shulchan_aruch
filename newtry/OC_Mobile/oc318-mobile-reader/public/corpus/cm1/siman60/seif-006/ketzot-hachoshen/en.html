One who obligates himself. Baal HaTerumot, gate 64, derives this from the law that one may create a lien for a debt upon property he will later acquire, as we rule that d'akni property becomes subject to the lien. Bnei Shmuel asks from the Gemara in chapter Asarah Yuchasin and chapter Mi Shemet. The Gemara asks why the verse 'he shall recognize' is necessary, since a father could simply give all his property to the son he identifies as firstborn. It answers that the verse is needed for property that falls to him only afterward. This works according to the Rabbis, but according to Rabbi Meir, who holds that one can transfer something not yet in existence, what does the verse teach? Bnei Shmuel asks that the question should exist even according to the Rabbis, since the father could obligate himself to the son, and an obligation can take effect even with respect to something not yet in existence. Even more surprising are the words of Kesef Mishneh in chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah. He writes that no objection to Rambam follows from this proof, because the father could acknowledge owing the son thousands upon thousands of gold dinarim and could subject to him movable property together with land, both what he already owns and what he will later acquire. One should not object that perhaps he will possess more property at death, for then there would be no end; since he can obligate himself in an enormous amount, the migo exists. Kesef Mishneh uses this to answer the challenge against Rambam from the Gemara's question to Rabbi Meir concerning a nonfixed obligation. But if the Gemara's question to Rabbi Meir is that he can obligate himself in a fixed amount and subject present and future property, why is there no identical question according to the Rabbis, since we rule that d'akni is subject to a lien? Mishneh LaMelech already expresses astonishment at this. There is, however, no difficulty. Our rule that after-acquired property is subject to a lien is rabbinic. By Torah law, just as one cannot transfer property that does not yet exist, one cannot create a lien upon property that does not yet exist. Rashbam writes this in Bava Batra 157b concerning the question whether d'akni can be subjected to a lien: a lien is stronger than ordinary acquisition because the Rabbis strengthened it to prevent the door from being locked before borrowers. Nimukei Yosef similarly writes that a lien on property not yet in existence works because the Sages enacted this for creditors. Thus one cannot subject future property by Torah law. Kesef Mishneh's words are therefore correct. According to Rabbi Meir, the father can obligate himself in a fixed sum of thousands and can subject all present and future property through a lien of movable property together with land, because Rabbi Meir recognizes transfer of property not yet in existence. According to the Rabbis, however, even if he can obligate himself personally in an enormous sum, the lien on property he later acquires has no Torah effect. After his death the heirs would not have to pay unless the property itself had been subjected to the debt. This also resolves Bnei Shmuel's question. True, according to the Rabbis he can create a personal obligation concerning something not yet in existence because his own person presently exists. But if his future property is not subjected to that obligation, the heirs need not pay their father's debt after his death merely from later-acquired property. The lien must attach to the property itself, and since that property does not yet exist, by Torah law the lien cannot attach to it, just as it cannot be transferred. Thus the Gemara's difficulty remains only according to Rabbi Meir. This is clear. See what I wrote in siman 279, seif 1.
And even if he gave him a pledge. The reason is that we apply the rule, 'there is no maneh here, and therefore no pledge.' It appears that a person can subject his property only after his own person first becomes obligated. Then 'a person's property serves as guarantor for him,' as Rosh writes in the first chapter of Kiddushin. Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia gives another reason: meshichah works where the object itself is fully acquired, but not to create merely a security interest. A pledge therefore becomes effective either at the time of a loan, when the lender acquires it through the money of the loan, or after the loan when beit din collects it for him. Where there is neither loan money nor beit din collection, mere meshichah does not create the pledge interest. See what I wrote in siman 87, seif katan 2.
But with language of obligation. Knesset HaGedolah cites Mahara Sasson, siman 132, that even where the undertaking is phrased as a personal obligation, if the giver dies before the fruit comes into existence the recipient nevertheless acquires. Knesset HaGedolah suggests this may depend upon the dispute whether a person who subjected after-acquired property may retract before the property comes into existence. In my opinion, a personal obligation cannot be retracted according to any opinion. Consider one who obligates himself to pay one hundred litrin even though he does not presently have that amount. Can he later retract? Certainly not. See Tosafot in chapter Af Al Pi. The same applies to one who obligates himself to deliver a fixed measure of wheat he does not yet possess, or the future fruit of a date palm. The obligation takes effect upon his person, and his person exists now. He therefore cannot retract and must deliver the wheat or date fruit. If he has no wheat, his property is subjected to pay the value of the wheat or fruit. The dispute about retraction concerns only liens on property as against purchasers. A creditor cannot collect from a purchaser unless the very property purchased was subjected to the debt. Since future property did not exist, the lien may not attach and there is room for retraction, just as Rabbi Meir allows retraction from transfer of something not yet in existence before it comes into existence. But while the debtor remains alive, his personal obligation has already attached, and we collect from whatever property he has, even the cloak upon his shoulders, to satisfy what he obligated himself to pay. He cannot retract because his person existed when he undertook the obligation. If he dies before the date fruit comes into existence, the heirs must likewise pay from his estate, because heirs do not require the special d'akni lien rule in order to pay an established debt. See Shulchan Aruch siman 108. Likewise, a groom who obligated himself to one hundred litrin and died without those litrin coming into his hand leaves an ordinary debt payable by his heirs from his other property, because the personal obligation already existed. This is clear. I later saw Tumim explain Knesset HaGedolah differently. He distinguishes between one who says, 'If I do not give you the future date fruit, I will give you such-and-such money,' where the monetary obligation takes effect immediately because the money is something that can exist and only its condition depends upon something future, and one who simply says, 'I obligate myself in the future date fruit.' In the latter case, Tumim says the obligation takes effect only when the fruit comes into existence; if the obligor died first, his obligation ended and the heirs are not bound. He derives this from a responsum of Maharam in the Mordechai, first chapter of Bava Metzia, concerning one who said, 'If my son grows up and does not marry your daughter, I obligate myself from now to give you ten zekukim.' The man died before the son grew up, and the son did not marry the daughter. Maharam initially contemplated obligating the heirs because, although the condition depended on something not yet in existence, both the recipient and obligor were already in existence. He brings proof from chapter HaMafkid, where one is treated as saying, 'When it is stolen and you choose to pay me, my cow is hereby acquired to you from now.' Tumim argues that if personal obligations always work even concerning future property, Maharam would not have needed this proof. He could simply have said that even the Rabbis agree an obligation can concern something not yet in existence. This supposedly shows that death terminates such an obligation. The principal view, however, is as I have written. Where the personal obligor exists, the obligation is not itself a future object at all. The subject of the obligation is his own person, which already exists, so death does not retroactively erase it. A conditional undertaking is different. If one says, 'If my son grows up and does not marry your daughter, I will become obligated,' and if a condition depending on something not yet in existence is governed by the law of a nonexistent object, then no obligation ever took effect upon his person. The fact that his person existed does not help, because the very condition preventing the obligation from attaching had not yet come into legal existence. This resembles an undertaking 'after I convert,' which does not attach to the person's body now. Maharam therefore had to prove that a condition concerning a future event is not itself governed by the law of something not yet in existence. Once that is established, his personal obligation can attach now. But an ordinary obligation to deliver future produce is already an obligation upon an existing person, and it certainly cannot be retracted. Examine this carefully.
For which they performed a kinyan with him. The Shach writes that the Mechaber derived the need for a kinyan from Baal HaTerumot. In the Shach's opinion, Baal HaTerumot follows his own view that one cannot obligate himself in something he did not previously owe except through a kinyan, and saying 'you are my witnesses' does not suffice. Rambam, however, rules that a person can obligate himself merely by saying, 'you are my witnesses.' If so, here too, once he says, 'you are witnesses that I obligate myself,' he should be bound even without a kinyan. The Mechaber's ruling therefore requires study. We already discussed this at length in siman 40 and concluded that there is a distinction. If he says, 'Be witnesses that I owe so-and-so a maneh,' this operates through the law of acknowledgment, which itself functions as a mode of acquisition. But if he says, 'Be witnesses that I undertake to become obligated,' using future language, he cannot create the obligation merely through speech without a kinyan. See there. This is clear.
