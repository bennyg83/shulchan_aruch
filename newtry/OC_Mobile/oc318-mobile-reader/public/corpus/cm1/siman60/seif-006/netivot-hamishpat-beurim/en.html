One who obligates himself in something not yet in existence. Knesset HaGedolah writes that according to authorities who allow retraction from a lien on future property before the property comes into existence, the same applies to a personal undertaking derived from such a lien. If the obligor dies before the thing comes into existence, the heirs are exempt according to all authorities. Urim VeTumim agrees. Ketzot HaChoshen objects from Tosafot in Ketubot, who write that a groom can obligate himself in one hundred litra even though he does not presently possess that amount, and the heirs remain bound because his body exists and the obligation takes effect immediately. But these cases are not comparable. Where one obligates himself to pay money, or even fungible produce commonly available in the market, the personal debt attaches to him immediately. The fact that he does not yet own the particular funds merely postpones payment. He cannot remove the obligation from himself. If, however, one says, 'When I buy this field, I obligate myself to give it to you,' or, 'I obligate myself to give you the fruit this palm will produce,' no present debt rests on his body before the property comes into existence. He can avoid the obligation simply by never acquiring the field, and with the palm's fruit his obligation begins only when the fruit appears. Such a personal undertaking is no stronger than the corresponding future-property lien from which the law is derived. If he dies first, the heirs acquire the later fruit before any personal obligation attaches to the deceased, and they are exempt. Similarly, if one says, 'I obligate myself to buy for you a particular house that does not belong to me,' the obligation is ineffective where the house is wholly outside his control. Beit din does not take enormous sums from him merely because the present owner refuses to sell. He can, however, say, 'If I do not buy that house for you, I obligate myself in one hundred,' provided the formulation avoids asmachta. Rivash and Rabbi Eliyahu Mizrachi support this distinction. The core rule is that a personal obligation can bind now only where the debt itself can presently attach to the obligor, even if performance is deferred. It does not create control over a specific future object wholly outside his power.
Or he accepted responsibility. Shach writes that the ordinary responsibility clause written in documents, by itself, does not help where there was no valid acquisition. If there is no acquisition, there is no responsibility, unless he separately and expressly assumes a personal obligation to maintain the property in the recipient's hands. Urim VeTumim asks from the case of one who sells land that is not his while the buyer knows this, where an express responsibility can still obligate the seller. The answer is that in the stolen-land case the sale is effective between buyer and seller so long as the true owner does not appear. If the seller later buys the field from its owner, the first buyer acquires it through the original transaction because the buyer relied on him. Thus the sale has real legal existence between the parties, and responsibility can attach to it. A sale of something not yet in existence is different. No acquisition occurs at all, and either party can retract. The seller may return the money and keep the thing after it later comes into existence. Where there was no transaction at all, a generic responsibility clause has nothing to support. Only an independent personal undertaking can create liability. This also explains why a buyer of stolen land may not be able to cancel merely by proving the seller lacked title; until the true owner actually asserts his claim, the original sale relationship remains operative between the parties.
He gave him a sum. The reference is to a case where the donor fixed a specific amount for support. If the recipient dies before the period is completed, the unpaid amount does not automatically pass to the heirs unless the wording created an independent debt rather than merely measuring the support obligation. This is the distinction explained in the earlier seif and in Even HaEzer.
And this is how we rule. Even where the undertaking is expressed through a condition referring to a thing not yet in existence, the condition can be effective when the body of the transaction itself presently exists. There is no greater example than conditioning a present act on a future event. The condition is distinct from the acquisition itself. Therefore one can make a present kinyan dependent on an event or thing that will exist only later, so long as the legal act itself can presently take effect subject to that condition. This differs from attempting to transfer the future object itself before it exists.
The claimant must bring proof. Sema attempted to reconcile this with siman 250 and siman 112 by distinguishing a living obligor who claims with certainty from heirs who claim uncertainty. I do not accept that distinction. We make a full defense for heirs, and Even HaEzer explicitly requires the claimant to prove that the movables existed at the time of the gift even against heirs. The cases are instead distinguished by the kind of property and by the evidentiary presumption, 'Here it is found, here it was.' If a person gave all his movables from now until after death and the same household movables are later found in his possession, there is strong reason to presume they were already his at the time of the gift. This applies especially to ordinary household items that tend to remain in a person's possession for years. But if the gift concerned only part of his movables, or fungible money, grain, merchandise, or commercial stock that regularly changes, that presumption is much weaker. The giver can say that the specific gifted property was lost and that what is now present is different property later acquired. This also explains the distinction between a person who transferred all his assets and one who transferred only a share. Where all assets were transferred and it became established that whatever he handled belonged to the recipients, later property in his possession continues to stand in that same presumption. Maharit writes similarly: once a person is established to own nothing independently and merely manages the recipient's assets, what remains under his hand is presumptively theirs. That rationale does not apply where he gave only a fraction and continued to own property of his own. Even where a person expressly accepted responsibility for accidental loss, the burden can remain on the recipient where the present dispute is whether the personal obligation ever arose. If the donor says with certainty that the gifted one hundred gold coins were never in his possession at the time of the gift, the recipient seeks to establish the very creation of a personal debt. A possession presumption therefore supports the donor. Even if the donor is uncertain whether these coins were present at the time, if he says there may have been another hundred that was lost, the recipient still must prove that the personal obligation attached. In a true doubt whether the debt ever arose, existing possession is a strong presumption. Accordingly, the rule of siman 250 concerning identifiable household movables does not govern fungible gold coins or commercial inventory, and the apparent contradictions among the Shulchan Aruch's rulings disappear. Shach's attempt to make the rule of siman 250 depend on the position in siman 112 is therefore not correct.
For anyone who says, 'I gave.' Urim VeTumim asked that Rama elsewhere rules that our ordinary documents are constitutive instruments, and the Gemara concerning one who says, 'I gave such-and-such a field to so-and-so,' says that these cases require a document. If 'I gave' were itself an admission of a prior gift, why would any document be needed? Responsa Rama already answers this. The phrase 'I gave' can bear two meanings. It can be an admission about the past, or it can function as present language of transfer in a written instrument. Where it is known, or the recipient admits, that no prior transfer occurred, the words cannot be treated as an admission and therefore operate prospectively as a constitutive formula. That is the case in the Gemara, and it proves that our documents can function as acquisition deeds. Where the circumstances allow us to understand 'I gave' as an admission that the transfer already occurred, we prefer that reading because past tense naturally suggests acknowledgment. But if such an acknowledgment would contradict known facts or reliable testimony, we interpret the same words prospectively so that the witnesses or admitted facts are not contradicted. A deeper question remains. Even if everyone admits there was no earlier transfer, why should the acknowledgment not itself operate as an *odita*, which is an independent mode of acquisition? The answer is that where the wording can be reconciled with the known facts by treating it as a present transfer, we do so rather than declare the speaker's acknowledgment false. *Odita* has its independent force when the acknowledgment cannot be reconciled with reality in another natural way. This also explains the case of Issur the convert. If Rav Mari had expressly denied ownership of the money, one would have to analyze the conflicting admissions. But where the recipient is silent, Issur's acknowledgment can transfer the money through *odita*. Rama further writes that if a document uses both expressions, one of past acknowledgment and one of present transfer, the later expression governs in the same way that later documentary wording ordinarily controls.
