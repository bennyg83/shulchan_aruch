A person who obligates himself regarding something not yet in existence or not presently in his possession is liable. Although one cannot transfer something not yet in existence, that applies when he uses language of sale or gift. Even if he writes an encumbrance over a field, accepts liability over all his property, or gives collateral, nothing is effected. If he uses language of personal obligation, such as, 'Be witnesses that I obligate myself to give so-and-so such-and-such,' he is liable, provided a formal act of acquisition was made. If Reuven produces against Shimon a document of acquisition stating, 'Because I gave him one hundred gold coins, I encumbered all my property for their collection,' but it did not personally obligate Shimon, and Shimon claims he did not possess the coins when he wrote it and one cannot transfer something not yet in existence, the rule depends on the currency. Coins in ordinary circulation are not acquired by exchange; coins not used in that country may be acquired by exchange, but they must have been in his possession at the time. If it is unknown whether they were, the claimant bears the burden of proof.
