One who obligates himself in something not yet in existence. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 60, writes that one who obligates himself to give everything a palm tree will produce is bound by his words, but if the obligor dies before the fruit comes into existence, the heirs are exempt according to all opinions, even according to the view that with after acquired property he cannot retract before acquiring the land. His words require explanation. Tosafos and the Rosh at the beginning of Perek Af Al Pi write that a ketubah is an obligation dependent upon something not yet in existence, because the obligation is not collectible until she is widowed or divorced, and at present he has no immediate liability. Even if he has no property at the time, we still collect later. They bring proof from a paid custodian who obligates himself to pay if the item is stolen or lost, even though the future event has not yet occurred and he may presently have no property. It is therefore clear that a person can create an obligation tied to a future event, and his heirs may later be required to pay. The distinction is as follows. If he says, "If I do not give you the fruit that this palm tree will produce, I will pay you such and such money," he obligates himself immediately in money, which is something presently in existence, while merely suspending enforcement upon a future condition. Such an obligation is fully binding, and the heirs are liable. A ketubah and the liability of a paid custodian are of this form. He says in effect, "If I die, I am obligated to you from today," or, "If it is stolen or lost, I will be liable." The obligation arises now, with the future event serving only as a condition. But if he simply says, "I obligate myself to give you the fruit this palm tree will produce," then when does that specific obligation arise? Only when the fruit comes into existence. If he has already died by then, his personal obligation has ended and the heirs are exempt. This distinction is explicit from the responsum of Maharam cited by Mordechai at the beginning of Bava Metzia. One person said, "If my son grows up and does not marry your daughter, I obligate myself from now to give you ten zekukim." He died before the son grew up, and the son did not marry the daughter. Maharam initially considered obligating the heirs. Although the condition depended upon something not yet in existence, the obligor and recipient were already in existence, and only the condition concerned a future matter. He brings proof from Perek HaMafkid, where we treat a custodian as though he said, "When it is stolen and you choose to pay me, my cow is acquired to you from now." This works even according to the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir, demonstrating that where all parties and subject matter are presently in existence and only the condition is future, the arrangement is valid. Maharam ultimately exempted the heirs only because the son was prevented by circumstances beyond his control. It follows that without that separate factor the heirs would have been liable even though the condition involved a future event. Why did Maharam need to prove his point from Perek HaMafkid? Why not simply say that everyone agrees a person can create a monetary obligation even concerning something not yet in existence? Necessarily, because if the obligor dies before the future object comes into existence, a bare obligation relating directly to that future object ends with him. Maharam therefore relied upon the fact that the person obligated himself presently in ten zekukim and merely made the obligation conditional upon a future event. In that form the obligation arises now and survives against the heirs. Accordingly, Maharam does not contradict the authorities who say that one can obligate oneself with respect to something not yet in existence. Knesset HaGedolah's ruling follows directly from this distinction. The difference is whether the person creates an immediate monetary obligation and merely ties it to a future condition, or whether the obligation itself is stated only with respect to the future object. In the latter case, death before the object comes into existence ends the obligation. This accords fully with Tosafos in Perek Af Al Pi and with Tosafos at the beginning of Perek Na'arah Shenitpatah.
He is liable. Knesset HaGedolah brings a difficulty, which I have also seen in other works. In Bava Batra and Kiddushin the Gemara asks why Scripture needs the rule of "he shall acknowledge" concerning a firstborn son. If a father wishes to give a particular son a double portion, he can simply make him a gift. The Gemara answers that the verse is needed for property that comes to the father when he is already dying. But why can he not say through the language of obligation, "I hereby obligate myself to give you a double portion according to whatever property will reach me one moment before my death," and stipulate that the heirs will be exempt from unavoidable accidents as though the recipient were an heir? Knesset HaGedolah struggles with the question. There is no difficulty. If the father owns only movable property and speaks through the language of obligation, the son merely becomes an ordinary creditor. If the father then dies, the heirs need not satisfy that debt from inherited movable property, because orphaned movable property is not subject to an ordinary creditor. By contrast, if the father identifies him as the firstborn, he takes as an heir from both land and movable property. The Gemara's question from a gift is equally applicable to land and movable property, and therefore the answer based on property acquired while dying remains necessary. One cannot object that the father could subject movable property together with land, because Torah law speaks to every person, including one who owns no land. The Gemara uses this principle in many places. Perhaps their question follows Rashba in Kiddushin, who holds that by Torah law even inherited movable property is subject to creditors like land. But according to Rashba there is still no difficulty. Rashba also holds that with respect to after acquired property a person can retract before the property comes into existence. Ramban below in siman 111 further holds that even as against heirs, a clause covering after acquired property is needed. Therefore, if Scripture had not written that the father is believed to identify the firstborn, and his credibility rested only on a migo that he could obligate himself toward that son, then when he presently owns no property and later property comes to him, he could retract from the future property clause before acquisition. The heirs would therefore not be bound. If he were likewise allowed to retract from the statement, "This is my firstborn," because its entire force rested only upon the migo of self obligation, the verse would still be needed to teach that once he identifies him as firstborn he cannot retract and say, "I lied." Thus the verse remains necessary. It does, however, appear that Knesset HaGedolah has a proof that one must accept Tosafos's principle that every person is presumed to have some land. Otherwise, what does the Gemara mean when it says he could simply give a gift? Perhaps he has only coins deposited with another person. All the more so in the later question concerning property that comes to him afterward: perhaps the future property consists of coins. Through what act could he transfer them? Meshichah is impossible if they are not in his possession, coins are not acquired by ordinary chalipin, and agav requires land. Even according to the view that coins can be acquired through chalipin, if his son is a minor without legal capacity to transfer the sudar to his father, and another person cannot act as agent for a minor, how would the acquisition occur? Certainly according to Rabbi Yochanan, who holds that meshichah does not acquire by Torah law even for a gift, there would be a difficulty. One must therefore say, as Tosafos does in several places, that every person is presumed to have some land. Yet even without this, the original question can be answered differently. According to Rabbi Yochanan ben Berokah, who permits a person to say concerning one who is eligible to inherit that he should receive a double portion even though he is not the firstborn, why is the verse "he shall acknowledge" needed at all? The father already has a migo to arrange the inheritance. We must say that the verse is needed where the person is not already established as his son. This appears from Rashbam. The rule that a person is believed to say, "This is my son," is itself ultimately derived from the firstborn verse. Without that verse he would not be believed even regarding an ordinary son. Accordingly, suppose Reuven's father Yaakov is still alive, and Reuven dies after saying that Levi is his son. Reuven cannot give Yaakov's future estate to Levi as a gift, because we rule that a son who sells his father's future inheritance does not bind his own son, who may later remove it from the purchaser. But once Reuven is believed to identify Levi as his son, Levi inherits property that was merely expected at Reuven's death just as property already in Reuven's possession. This must be included in the Gemara's answer of property that falls to him while he is dying, and certainly property that arrives afterward. According to this, Knesset HaGedolah's question disappears completely. No number of obligations that Reuven creates can entitle Levi to take Yaakov's later estate ahead of other heirs, because those heirs can say, "We come directly in the place of our grandfather," as explained below at the end of siman 104. Only the verse allowing recognition of the son can accomplish that.
Or he accepted responsibility, etc. The Shach, seif katan 20, understands this to mean that he merely accepted ordinary warranty. If he expressly assumed an independent complete obligation to maintain the transaction, then he is liable by force of that obligation. But ordinary warranty does not help, because where there is no acquisition there is no warranty either. This is compelled by the Gemara in Bava Metzia 16 concerning one who sold property that was not his and later bought it from the original owner. The Gemara asks through what act the buyer acquired it, since the original document was a mere shard. The case there involved warranty, as is clear from the continuation of the passage. If ordinary warranty itself constituted an independent complete obligation to establish the sale, then once the seller later acquired the property it should obviously belong to the buyer by force of that warranty. Why would the Gemara need the reasons that the seller wanted to maintain his credibility or avoid being called a robber? He would already be obligated to maintain the sale. Necessarily, the sale being ineffective also prevents the ordinary warranty clause from creating an independent transfer. Thus, where there is no valid sale, ordinary warranty falls with it. This does create a difficulty. If ordinary wording of warranty, such as, "I will arise and remove all claims, losses, and improvements," does not create an independent obligation and merely follows the validity of the transaction, why is the situation different where one sells a field for one hundred and assumes warranty for the improvement up to two hundred? The Ramah and Ritva hold that even if the field is later found not to have been his, and even where the buyer knew it was not his, the seller must pay the improvement amount. Why should the extra one hundred differ from an ordinary gift? If the underlying acquisition was void because the property was not his, why should the warranty survive? Even those who disagree with the Ramah do so because payment of improvement appears like interest, not because the warranty is ineffective when the underlying transfer fails. Perhaps the distinction is as follows. If a person says, "I give you these one hundred, and I accept responsibility for them," the warranty is not an independent obligation. It is secondary to the gift, and if there is no gift there is no warranty. But where he transfers property worth one hundred and separately assumes responsibility for improvement up to two hundred, the additional hundred was never part of the transferred property. It arises only through the warranty clause. It therefore constitutes an independent obligation. The same may be true of improvement. The sale itself concerns the field, while responsibility for improvement is a separate undertaking. Since it is independent, the seller can be liable for it even though the underlying field was not his. Rivash, responsum 250, expressly compares the case of one who gives away his son's land to the case of one who expressly undertakes responsibility for improvement. It must be that in Rivash's case the warranty was formulated as a complete independent obligation, as is clear there. His comparison means only that just as ordinary warranty for improvement can create liability even though the field was not the seller's, so too an express independent obligation attached to a gift can survive even though the donor had no title to the land. This requires further study.
He is liable. The Shach writes that even though the subject is quantified, for example, "I obligate myself to give you this house that I will inherit from my father," he does not collect from encumbered property according to the view that support for a wife and daughters is not collected from encumbered property because the obligation is not written, which Rashi and Tosafos explain to mean that since the obligation does not yet exist at the time of writing, it is not considered written. The Shach writes this only according to his own view that the phrase "they made a kinyan from him" is not essential. But according to the Shulchan Aruch, which expressly requires a kinyan, it is clear that collection is made from encumbered property. The Gemara asks why support obligations are not collected from encumbered property and answers that the case is without a kinyan. Tosafos writes that even for daughters, were it not for the separate concern that their father may have set aside funds, a kinyan would make the obligation collectible from encumbered property, because a kinyan is stronger than writing. The Tur in Even HaEzer, siman 112, also says that if he wrote a document for the daughters, they collect from encumbered property, apparently even if the daughters did not yet exist when the document was written. This demonstrates that writing can be effective for a future obligation. One must distinguish that improvement has no separate document of its own, since the primary document is for the principal, and therefore the improvement clause does not create the same public notice. But with daughters, if he writes a separate document for them, it has publicity. The Shulchan Aruch in siman 112 omits the rule concerning a separate written instrument and cites only the case of a kinyan. I have already written that it omitted the written case because the amount is not fixed. In any event, we learn that the Shulchan Aruch holds that a kinyan gives the obligation sufficient force to collect from encumbered property. Therefore, where there is a kinyan, collection certainly extends to encumbered property. With writing alone, the matter requires study. The Tur and Ramah cited above imply that a separate document may suffice even in such a case. See Even HaEzer, siman 114, and Beit Shmuel there.
Provided that a kinyan was made from him. The Shach asks why a kinyan is necessary. The Mechaber rules in siman 40 like Rambam that a person can obligate himself even though he did not previously owe anything, as in the case, "I owe you one hundred by document." Baal HaTerumot, he says, follows his own view at the end of gate 64 that one cannot create such an obligation without a kinyan, and therefore requires a kinyan here, but according to the Mechaber the requirement is difficult. The Shach leaves the matter requiring study. I am astonished. Where did the Shach find that Baal HaTerumot requires a kinyan for "I owe you one hundred by document"? Baal HaTerumot explicitly writes in gate 64, part 4, that the halachah follows Rabbi Yochanan, who says that one who says, "I owe you one hundred by document," is liable even without saying, "I accept liability upon myself," and certainly if he expressly accepts liability, and all the more so if a kinyan was made. Thus he clearly holds that even without a kinyan one can become obligated according to Rabbi Yochanan, whose view we follow. Perhaps a corrupted printed version misled the Shach. The correct reading is that if he accepts liability upon himself, he becomes obligated as though a kinyan had been made. This is straightforward. As to the substantive question why a kinyan is needed here, I am surprised because the Shach himself, in siman 40, accepted the standard resolution of the authorities concerning the rule that an unpaid custodian who wishes to assume the liability of a borrower requires a kinyan. The reason is that this is not an absolute present obligation, but a conditional one: "If it is stolen or lost, I will be liable." Such a conditional obligation requires a kinyan. I have already explained above that this is clear and fundamental to this entire approach. Here too, the person's obligation is conditional: "If the object later comes into my possession, I will be obligated to give it to you." This resembles the custodian case and therefore requires a kinyan. The Rosh and Tosafos in Perek Af Al Pi also imply that this type of obligation requires a kinyan, and they labor to explain why it is not merely a kinyan on words. The matter is explicit.
For anyone who says, I gave, etc. The Rama's ruling requires study. He cites a view that an expression such as "I gave" is construed as an acknowledgment, and therefore can validate the arrangement. But below, siman 190, the Beit Yosef cites the Ran that the documents used in our time are acquisition documents, not merely evidentiary documents. Although they use wording such as "I gave" and "I sold," this is not merely the language of acknowledgment. It is present dispositive language, as in the verse, "I have given the field." Ran proves this from Gittin 40, where one who says, "I gave the field to so and so," is held by Rabbi Meir to have effected the gift, and Rabbi Yochanan explains that all those cases concern a written instrument. If "I gave" merely meant acknowledgment, why would a document be necessary? The entire force of Ran's proof is that the language is an operative grant. Therefore, the Mechaber below rules that our documents are acquisition documents, and the Rama does not object or cite a dissenting opinion. If so, the present ruling cannot stand. Once the expression is treated as operative language rather than acknowledgment, all the conditions stated by the Mechaber concerning the effectiveness of the acquisition must be satisfied. Raavad, who disagrees, follows the view of Raah and others that our documents are evidentiary instruments. As to the Gittin passage requiring a document, those authorities must say either, as Maharam writes in responsum 92, that it was known there that the speaker intended an acquisition rather than an acknowledgment, or that without a document the speaker could later say he was joking or that he made the admission only to avoid appearing wealthy, as explained below in siman 81. One cannot answer that because the language can also be construed as acknowledgment we should apply the rule that the holder of a document has the weaker hand. That rule does not operate where doing so would nullify the instrument entirely merely because ordinary people may not know the technical law. Above it is explained that where there is reason to think the drafter did not know the law, we interpret the instrument so that it is not void. Here, not everyone knows that coins cannot be acquired by ordinary chalipin. See what I wrote above in siman 42, seif katan 6. Therefore, this ruling requires study and appears clearly not to be the practical halachah. Bnei Yaakov also noticed this difficulty and left it unresolved.
