He may seize from land. Even if the document does not expressly include a warranty clause, we rule that omission of warranty is a scribal error. Whether an after acquired property clause is likewise supplied automatically, see below in siman 112.
And property that I will acquire, etc. Whether he may retract this obligation before actually acquiring the property is also disputed among the great authorities. See below in siman 112, where I discuss it at length.
For a limited time. Meaning that even if the sale was not permanent, the creditor still may not collect from that property, Shach. See below in siman 114.
The custom is that he does not seize, etc. Shach questions this custom at length. He writes that in a place where it is not customary to write a movable property with land lien in all documents, but only in some, one should not rely on Rosh's custom because other authorities did not cite it. Nevertheless, the established practice in all batei din is to rely on Rosh in every case, and we have never heard of or seen collection from sold movable property merely because the document contained such a lien. This is especially so because according to Rosh, nowadays all movable property is in any event included in the lien, yet we still do not collect it from purchasers because we rely on the market protection enactment. One should not depart from this practice. Likewise one should disregard Nachalas Shivah's statement that when a woman's ketubah includes a movable property with land clause, market protection does not apply. His words are incorrect. There could be no greater market protection concern, for if buyers could not safely purchase any item from a married man because his wife might later collect it through her ketubah, ordinary commerce would be impossible.
That he sold, etc. Even if the movable property later comes into the creditor's possession after the sale, the purchaser has already acquired it, Shach. Maharshal writes that if a woman borrowed and then married, although we rule that the husband has the legal status of a purchaser, market protection does not apply and the creditor may collect from him, Shach. Responsa Maharam Alshich writes that if movable property was transferred not through ordinary meshichah or the like, but through maamad sheloshtan while the items were held by another and the buyer was directed to collect them from that custodian, then because this mode of acquisition is uncommon, market protection was not enacted. Shach below at the end of siman 126 objects. His objection is not compelling against so great an authority, especially because maamad sheloshtan is itself a rabbinic enactment made for commercial convenience, and we do not ordinarily enact one enactment merely to support another. We should not extend the market protection enactment beyond its established scope.
Or gave as a gift. The same applies if he consecrated the property for the poor. This concerns a gift by a healthy person. A gift of a person on his deathbed was treated by the Sages like inheritance, and even an oral creditor without a movable property with land lien may collect from it, Maharit, Yoreh Deah part, siman 54. See there.
Or mortgaged. But if a later creditor collected through beit din before an earlier creditor whose document includes a movable property with land lien, the earlier creditor may seize it from him. If, however, the debtor himself voluntarily transferred the property to the later creditor without beit din collection, it is like a sale or gift, and the earlier creditor cannot collect it. See Tumim at length. If the later creditor merely seized the property, the earlier creditor certainly may collect it from him. This is so even if the earlier debt had not yet matured when the later creditor seized, such as where the later creditor seized during the husband's lifetime and the husband then died, and the widow now comes by virtue of her ketubah. The law favors the woman. See Tumim.
