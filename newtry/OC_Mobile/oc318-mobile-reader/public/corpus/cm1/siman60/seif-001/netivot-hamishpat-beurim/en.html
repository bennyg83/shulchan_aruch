The custom is that he does not collect from purchasers. See Sema, seif katan 6, through his statement that if the debtor transferred all his property to another and retained nothing for himself, and so forth. At first glance this is difficult. Since the transfer is presumed to have been made only to evade creditors, as the Mechaber concludes, then even if the document did not contain a clause subordinating movable property together with land, the creditor should collect, as explained in siman 99, seif 6, where even an oral creditor collects in such a case. Sema, seif katan 8, resolves this. His intent there is to answer the difficulty against the Mechaber, who writes that the enactment of the market does not apply, although in any event the creditor should collect because the transfer was merely evasive. Rama therefore refers to siman 99, where it is explained that if beit din sees the transfer as a genuine gift, the recipient acquires. The novelty here is that even so the market enactment does not apply, because the principal purpose of the transfer was evasion, and such a case is also uncommon.
A debt document. See Shach, seif katan 9, through his statement concerning one who first sold the debt document. In my humble opinion there is much room to question his ruling. What basis is there to compare this case to the market enactment, which is rooted in the law of the Gemara? Wherever the Gemara itself does not distinguish, we should not create distinctions by our own reasoning. Here, by contrast, the rule is merely an enactment of the Geonim.
