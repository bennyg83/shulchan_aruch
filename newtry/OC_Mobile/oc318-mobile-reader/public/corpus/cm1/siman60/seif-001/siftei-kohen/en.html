[" He may collect from encumbered property, etc. This applies even if the instrument does not expressly state liability against encumbered property, because omission of such liability is treated as a scribal error, as explained at the beginning of siman 39. Concerning after acquired property and whether omission of that clause is likewise a scribal error, see what I write at the beginning of siman 112. The principal laws of collection from encumbered property are explained later from siman 111 through the end of siman 114."]
[" And property that I will acquire in the future, etc. The Sema, seif katan 2, cites a dispute between the Rashba and Ramban whether one who created a lien on after acquired property may retract that lien before acquiring the property. At the beginning of siman 112 I conclude like the Ramban that he cannot retract. See there."]
[" For a limited time. The Sema explains this as meaning either while the debtor still owed him or when the debtor needed to sell or give the property to this person. This is strained, for if so there was no need to write 'for a limited time.' Rather, the Rambam immediately afterward, in the law of an apotiki, distinguishes between a sale for a limited term and an absolute sale, and the Mechaber copies this language at the beginning of siman 117. The point here is that even if the debtor sold the property only temporarily rather than absolutely, the creditor does not collect from the buyer until the term ends. The Tur likewise writes in siman 111, seif 19, that if property was given as a gift for a fixed period, the creditor cannot collect it until that period expires."]
[" This is the law of the Gemara. The statement that nowadays movable property is not collected from purchasers is difficult, because neither the Rambam nor the early and later authorities mention such a universal custom. On the contrary, the Rashba, Rivash, and other later authorities repeatedly ruled in practice that movable property subject to a lien could be collected, and Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri likewise indicate that this occurred in their time. The Rosh mentions such a custom only in one responsum concerning the particular country in which he lived, and even there he was uncertain of the practice and wrote that if the custom could not be clarified one should follow Torah law and allow the creditor to collect the movables. It therefore appears that the Rosh spoke specifically of a country where every instrument customarily contained a lien on movable property together with land. His own words show this. He writes that by strict Talmudic law one may collect movable property, but after arriving in that country he was surprised to see that scribes routinely wrote a lien on movables together with land, because if such clauses were effective no one could safely purchase any object from another. He wished to abolish the practice, but was told that despite writing the clause, the established custom throughout the country was not to collect from movables that the debtor had sold or given away, because of market protection. The implication is clear. Under the original Talmudic law, such clauses were uncommon, so buyers did not need to worry about an unusual hidden lien. In that country, however, the clause appeared in every instrument and therefore became common, creating an intolerable burden on commerce, since every buyer would have to fear that the seller had previously borrowed by an instrument that encumbered these movables. It follows from the Rosh and Tur that only where the universal practice is to write such liens in all instruments did they adopt the contrary custom of not collecting movables from purchasers. Elsewhere, movable property remains collectible. The Mechaber likewise refers specifically to places where the settled custom was not to collect movables, which itself arose because those places routinely wrote such liens in every instrument. In any place where the custom is not clearly established, one should not depart from Torah law. Certainly, where the practice is not to include a movable property lien with land in every instrument, but only in some instruments where the debtor expressly undertakes it, market protection does not apply, and one need not investigate any supposed contrary custom."]
[" The custom is that he does not collect, etc. See Maharshal, responsum 189, concerning a woman who borrowed money and then married. The creditor may collect from her husband from the assets she brought into the marriage, and market protection does not apply."]
[" That he sold or gave away, etc. See what I write at the end of siman 156, that even if one of these movables later comes into the possession of the creditor to whom the debtor had granted a lien on movables together with land, the creditor must return it to the purchaser or donee, because that person already acquired it through the applicable act of acquisition."]
[" Or gave away, etc. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe understand the Rosh to hold that market protection applies even to a gift, whereas they take the Rashba to disagree, as discussed in siman 356. There I challenge this reading and show that the Rashba and Rosh do not disagree. Darkei Moshe further writes that a responsum of the Rosh, klal 79, siman 5, appears to support the Rashba. In my view he misunderstood the responsum. The Rosh writes that the reason one does not collect movables that the debtor sold is market protection, but that with a gift of a promissory note market protection does not apply because such a transfer is uncommon. Darkei Moshe understood this to mean that a gift in general is uncommon. That is incorrect, for immediately beforehand the Rosh writes that even movables given as a gift are not recovered because of market protection, as the Tur cites here in seif 4. Rather, the Rosh means that the gift of a promissory note is uncommon. The Tur says this explicitly at the end of siman 111: although ordinary movables given by Reuven could not be collected because of market protection, a gifted promissory note is different because such a transfer is uncommon. The Tur here, seif 7, likewise cites this responsum. Accordingly, even where a promissory note was sold, the same law applies, as both the Tur and Mechaber rule."]
[" Because of market protection. The concern is that otherwise no one would safely purchase an object from another. See the responsa of Mahari ben Lev, volume 2, siman 55, and Mabit, volume 1, siman 218, and volume 2, simanim 177 and 230. Beit Yosef writes that market protection concerns movables that the debtor sold or gave away, but where a later creditor collected before an earlier creditor, there is no such market concern, so the ordinary priority rules of siman 104 apply. He expressly wrote this to reject the ruling of a judge whom he considered mistaken. The Sema likewise follows Beit Yosef and rejects Maharshal di Medina, who held that where there are two creditors and the later creditor first collected movables, they are not taken from him. Mahari ben Lev and Mahara ben Chaim likewise support Beit Yosef, and Maharshal Kohen calls the matter obvious, reasoning that otherwise the clause encumbering movables together with land would serve no purpose. I do not find that reasoning compelling, because the clause still benefits the earlier creditor while the movables remain in the debtor's possession, and it can also have effect with respect to promissory notes and similar property. Moreover, the judge whom Beit Yosef called mistaken was no lightweight, for Mabit himself ruled that way, and Maharit records it as a dispute among the leading authorities and sides with Mabit. After seeing Beit Yosef, Mabit argued that a later creditor is no worse than a donee. Beit Yosef in Bedek HaBayit responded that a gift differs because the donor presumably received some prior benefit from the donee, making it comparable to a sale. This answer is strained. A later creditor likewise gave the debtor the benefit of the earlier loan. More fundamentally, as I show later in siman 356, even the Rosh agrees that ordinary market protection does not apply to a pure gift in the law of stolen property, since a donee is no better than one who received stolen goods in payment of a debt. Here, however, the Rosh nevertheless treats both sale and gift as protected. This indicates that the basis here is not the ordinary market regulation of siman 356. Rather, the local practice treated the clause encumbering movables together with land as ineffective for collection from third parties, though effective while the movables remained with the debtor. Once the clause is treated as unwritten for purposes of collection from third parties, it follows equally that movables given as a gift are not recovered, and likewise that movables already collected by a later creditor are not taken from him. This is strengthened by the comparison to the law of stolen goods, where a donee ordinarily has less protection than one paid a debt, yet here the Rosh protects a donee. It is therefore all the more logical to protect a creditor who received payment of his debt. I see no sound basis to distinguish between voluntary repayment and collection by the creditor himself. Accordingly, where a later creditor first collected the movables, they are not removed from him, and this is how one should rule."]
[" A promissory note, etc., and he sold it, etc. It follows from the Rosh, Tur, Mechaber, and later authorities that promissory notes can be subjected to a lien through agav. It is surprising that Beit Yosef does not mention the dispute, because the Rashba, responsum 914, holds that documents cannot be encumbered through agav at all, and Beit Yosef himself cites this briefly later in siman 104. According to the Rashba, a creditor plainly cannot collect from a promissory note that the debtor sold, even under strict Talmudic law. In siman 66, seif katan 25, I write that the Rashba's view appears correct, although the later authorities state the Rosh and Tur's position without qualification, so the matter still requires study. Even according to the Rashba, however, the Rosh's statement that market protection does not apply to promissory notes has practical effect where the first transfer of the note was accomplished through a valid agav sale or another effective acquisition, and the seller then sold it again to a second purchaser. The first purchaser may recover it from the second without reimbursing him, because the special market protection does not apply to documents, such transactions being uncommon."]
