He may collect from real property, etc. Even if the document does not expressly state a warranty, because omission of a warranty is a scribal error, as explained above at the beginning of siman 39. See below siman 112, where Rama writes that some say the same applies to a clause encumbering after-acquired property, treating its omission as a scribal error. I wrote there, however, that this applies specifically where the document contains no warranty language at all. If it expressly contains a warranty but omits after-acquired property, we say that what was not written was not acquired. The Mechaber here speaks of the latter case when he writes that an after-acquired-property clause must be included.
'That which I have acquired and that which I will acquire.' In chapter Mi Shemet, Bava Batra 157, the Gemara concludes that even the Sages who disagree with Rabbi Meir and hold that a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence nevertheless agree that he can encumber something not yet in existence. Rashbam and Nimukei Yosef explain that the Sages strengthened a lien beyond ordinary acquisition so that lenders would not be deterred from extending credit. Tosafot likewise distinguish between acquisition and lien. Rama cites a dispute about the extent of this rule in Darkei Moshe to simanim 104, 112, and 209. Certainly, if a person created the lien and did not retract until the property came into existence, all agree that he can no longer retract and that the creditor acquires retroactively from the time of the lien, even though the property did not yet exist then. In an ordinary acquisition we rule like the Sages that even after the object comes into existence he can still retract, unless the transferee has already seized it, as Rav Nachman states in chapter Eizehu Neshech regarding one who sells the fruits of a palm tree. In that sense a lien is stronger than an ordinary acquisition, as the Gemara concludes in chapter Mi Shemet. The dispute concerns whether he can retract from the lien before the property comes into existence. Ramban, cited by Nimukei Yosef in chapter Chazakat HaBatim in the discussion of one who sells a cow or garment, holds that he cannot retract. Rashba, also cited there, holds that he may retract, explaining that a lien according to the Sages cannot be stronger than an acquisition according to Rabbi Meir, who allows transfer of an object not yet in existence but, according to Rav Huna's interpretation, only once it actually comes into existence. Rama briefly cites this dispute in his gloss at the beginning of siman 112 and in siman 209. See what I write further in Sema there, seif katan 12.
But movable property has no warranty. The term 'warranty' here refers to the ability to collect from later purchasers, as the conclusion explains: if the borrower sold or gave the movable property to another, the creditor cannot take it from that person. This terminology appears in many places. Its source is the Mishnah at the beginning of Kiddushin 26a: 'Property that has responsibility is acquired by money,' etc. Arukh, under the entry for achrayut, explains it as landed property. One explanation connects the word with 'after' or 'the remainder,' meaning one is secure in land because it remains and cannot be spirited away to avoid collection. Another explains it from responsibility or guaranty, because landed property serves as security and guaranty for the debt, as Chazal say that a person's property serves as his guarantor.
And what it says, 'or gave them at the relevant time.' This is Rambam's wording in chapter 18 of Hilchot Malveh, as in many places. Here it can mean when the borrower still owed the creditor, or when the borrower needed to sell or give the items to that person.
'Not as an asmachta,' etc. This language must be included when movable property is transferred together with land. The reason is explained in the Tur below at the beginning of siman 113, and I discuss it there in Sema, seif 1.
The custom is that he does not collect. Nevertheless, they continue to write such a clause because of the two cases the Mechaber concludes with: where the debtor transfers all his assets to another and leaves nothing for himself, and where he sells a promissory note. There is also a practical difference regarding a later creditor, as I write immediately below in the name of Beit Yosef.
Which he sold, gave, or pledged, etc. The market enactment applies specifically to these cases, for otherwise no one would purchase movable property, as the Tur explains. But if this debtor has two creditors whose loans occurred one after another, and he encumbered movable property together with land to each, and the later creditor first collected the movable property, the earlier creditor removes it from him. Beit Yosef writes this as obvious and says that any judge who does not rule this way is no judge and should be removed from judicial office. The Mechaber likewise rules so below at the end of siman 113. See there, where I explain why that ruling speaks of both creditors having an after-acquired-property clause. This is also why the custom is to write in documents that movable property is encumbered together with land. This is unlike Rabbi Shmuel de Medina in responsa, part 3, siman 108, who holds that even between two lenders, if the later creditor first collected movable property, the earlier creditor cannot remove it from him. In Derishah there I wrote that in my opinion Beit Yosef's position is correct.
See below siman 99, seif 6. There Rama writes that if it appears to beit din that the transfer was not intended as a scheme but as a genuine gift, the recipient acquires even if the donor's purpose was thereby to prevent collection of his debt.
