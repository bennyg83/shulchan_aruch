Future. See below, siman 112, where Rama writes that some say an obligation regarding after-acquired property is likewise presumed to have been omitted only by scribal error. This applies specifically when the document contains no warranty language at all. If it expressly contains warranty but omits after-acquired property, we say that whatever was not written was not acquired. That is the case the Mechaber addresses here when he writes that the after-acquired-property clause must be stated expressly. Thus the Sema.
From them. The Sema writes that the conclusion of the Gemara in Mi SheMet is that although a person cannot convey an item not yet in existence, he can subject it to a lien, because Chazal strengthened liens in order not to close the door before borrowers. Tosafot likewise distinguish between acquisition and lien. Rama records this in Darkei Moshe in simanim 104, 112, and 219, and in those three places he notes a dispute. Certainly, if the debtor created the lien and did not retract until the property came into existence, everyone agrees he can no longer retract and the creditor acquires retroactively from the time of the lien. The dispute concerns only whether he may retract before the property comes into existence. See siman 112 for further discussion. The Shach writes there that he concluded the debtor cannot retract. See there.
Temporarily. The Sema explains this as meaning either while the debtor still owed him, or at the time he needed to sell or give the property to this person. The Shach calls this strained. He explains instead that even if the debtor sold the property only temporarily rather than permanently, the creditor cannot seize it from the buyer, in contrast to the distinction made in siman 111, seif 19. See there.
The market. The Sema writes that takanat hashuk applies specifically to ordinary purchasers of movable property, for otherwise no one would buy movables. If, however, the debtor had two creditors, borrowed from them successively, encumbered movables to each together with land, and the later creditor collected movables first, the earlier creditor may remove them from him. The Beit Yosef states this plainly and even writes that any judge who fails to rule so deserves removal from judicial office. The Mechaber likewise rules this way at the end of siman 113. This is also why people customarily write in documents that movables are subjected together with land, contrary to Responsa Rashdam, part 3, siman 108. The Shach writes that no such general custom appears in the early or later poskim. On the contrary, the Rashba, Rivash, and other later authorities repeatedly rule in practice that movables can be seized. The Rosh does write in a responsum that in his region the custom was otherwise, but his words imply that this was specifically where every document routinely contained a lien on movables together with land. Since such clauses were ubiquitous, takanat hashuk was needed or no one would buy movable property. In other places, where no clear custom exists, and especially where such a lien is written only when expressly requested, movables may certainly be seized under strict law. There is then no basis for takanat hashuk, and no investigation of local custom is needed because we do not presume a custom contrary to Torah law. Responsa Rashdam writes concerning a woman who borrowed money and then married that the creditor may collect from her husband out of property she brought into the marriage, and takanat hashuk does not apply. The Shach further disagrees with the Beit Yosef regarding a later creditor who collected first and holds that takanat hashuk applies even there, so what he collected remains his. He concludes that this is the proper practical ruling; see his lengthy discussion.
Documents. The Shach writes that this passage proves documents can be subjected through agav. He wonders why the Beit Yosef did not mention the dispute, since the Rashba in a responsum holds that documents cannot be subjected through agav at all. According to the Rashba, therefore, a creditor could not collect a debt document that the debtor sold, even under Talmudic law. Still, even according to the Rashba there is a practical application, because takanat hashuk does not apply to documents. If the debtor first sold the debt document through agav or another valid mode, as explained in siman 67, and then sold it again, the first purchaser can recover it from the second without reimbursing him, because trade in documents is not common enough for takanat hashuk. Shach.
