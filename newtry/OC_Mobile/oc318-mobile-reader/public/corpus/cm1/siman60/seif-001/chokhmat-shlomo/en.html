Seif 6. The claimant must bring proof. See the lengthy discussions of the Sema and Shach. Rashi in Bava Kamma 14b explains that movable property of orphans is not subject to collection because one can say that the orphans acquired it only after their father’s death. Rashi’s wording is itself difficult, for even if witnesses testify that their father acquired the movables during his lifetime, ordinarily a creditor does not collect from the heirs’ movables. Nevertheless, Rashi’s formulation proves at least one point: with movable property we do not say, merely because the property is found here now, that it must already have been here previously. Even where the heirs themselves are uncertain, the principle “here it is found, here it was” is not applied to movables according to Rashi. Conversely, no proof can be drawn from Rashi regarding land, because land ordinarily has publicity and witnesses can know when it was acquired. For present purposes, however, his language is significant regarding movables. There, at the beginning of the seif: one who obligates himself in something not yet in existence or not presently available to him. In my opinion this applies only where the obligor can actually obtain the item. For example, one sells wheat to another and says, “I sell you the wheat belonging to a certain person at such and such a price per measure,” and undertakes a complete personal obligation to supply it. If he can obtain that wheat at the same price or for a modest additional amount, he must purchase and deliver it, because he validly obligated himself. But if he can obtain it only at an extraordinarily greater expense than the price for which he sold it, the rule of ones applies. A person who undertook to perform for another and is prevented by circumstances beyond his control is exempt, as appears in simanim 21 and 55 and elsewhere. Yoreh Deah 232 also rules that a very large financial loss can constitute ones where there is a clear assessment that he never undertook the obligation on such terms. Accordingly, if the seller would suffer such an exceptional loss that it is evident he never accepted an obligation requiring that sacrifice, he is exempt. The existence in the market of other wheat of the same type does not change this. His undertaking related to this particular supply, and once that obligation is excused by ones, we treat it as not binding him to purchase other wheat instead. This seems clear and correct as practical law. There, seif 6, in the gloss: many authorities disagree. See Urim VeTumim and my addenda to Kuntres Kinnuyei Nedarim, where I offered several resolutions.
