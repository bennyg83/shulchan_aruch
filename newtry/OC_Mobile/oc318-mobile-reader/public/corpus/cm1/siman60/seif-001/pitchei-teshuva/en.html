And see below, siman 99, seif 6. The Sema explains that there the Rama writes that if beit din sees that the transfer was not a sham but a genuine gift, the recipient acquires it even though the donor intended thereby to prevent his creditor from collecting. Dagul Merevavah comments that the Sema's words are puzzling. In siman 99 the case concerns movable property from which the creditor would not otherwise collect by law, such as an oral loan, or even a written loan without a clause binding movables together with land. Even there the Mechaber rules that if the transfer was merely a sham the creditor may collect, and the Rama adds that if the donor truly intended a gift, the recipient acquires even though the purpose was to defeat collection. Here, however, the debtor conveyed movables together with land, so the creditor's lien reaches them by strict law, and the only reason not to collect from a purchaser or recipient is the commercial protection of market transactions. Where the gift was made specifically to defeat the creditor, that market enactment does not apply, and the creditor collects by law. Dagul Merevavah suggests that this may in fact be the Sema's intent. The Sema was troubled why the Shulchan Aruch needed to state this law here concerning movables bound to land, since even an oral creditor can collect from such a sham gift under siman 99. He therefore points out that siman 99 also teaches that a genuine gift is effective. Here, by contrast, the market enactment is inapplicable, so even though the recipient validly acquired the gift, the creditor may collect from it by virtue of his lien. Netivot HaMishpat likewise explains the Sema in this straightforward manner.
