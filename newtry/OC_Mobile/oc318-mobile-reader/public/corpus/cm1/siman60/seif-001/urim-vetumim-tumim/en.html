Or he gave. If a later creditor collected first, the earlier creditor who holds a lien on movable property together with land may collect from him, because in this uncommon situation the Sages did not enact the market protection. This was decisively ruled by the Beit Yosef in his work and in Bedek HaBayit, to the point that he called Mabit, who disagreed, greatly mistaken. Many sages of that generation agreed with him, as the Shach himself records. Nevertheless, the Shach ruled against the Beit Yosef and did not even treat the matter as an unresolved question of law. Yet none of his arguments supplies a clear proof. He argues that because we see that the special lien does not help against a recipient of a gift, it follows that the clause concerning movable property together with land is treated as though it had not been written at all, and therefore should not help against a later creditor either. This is incorrect. If so, it should also be ineffective against other documents. The correct principle is that the Sages enacted protection in common situations and did not enact it in uncommon situations. The Shach further cites the law below in siman 356 that for market protection a gift is weaker than property transferred by a thief in payment of a debt, and he argues from this that a later creditor should also be protected. There is an obvious distinction. If the debtor himself gives property in payment to a later creditor, that is very common. A later creditor comes and takes an item from the debtor in payment. But where beit din itself assesses and transfers the property to a later creditor, the act of beit din is public and it is very uncommon that an earlier creditor would later come to seize it. The Sages did not legislate for an uncommon case. The Shach calls this forced, but it is not forced at all. It is plainly the Beit Yosef's intent to speak of property formally awarded by beit din, not property the debtor himself transferred, which is equivalent to a sale or gift. In an assessment by beit din, there is not an absolute transfer in the same manner, to the point that many authorities hold that even an assessment of movable property is returned if circumstances change, as explained below in siman 103. The debtor's hand therefore remains involved to some extent, and an earlier creditor may seize it. It is true that Tosafos at the beginning of Perek Mi SheHayah Nasui explains the question whether a later creditor who collected first keeps the property. Tosafos says the case must concern land, because movable property is not ordinarily subject to a creditor's lien. They ask: if it is land, why would we ever think that the later creditor keeps it? Even if the debtor had sold it outright, the earlier creditor could seize from the purchaser. Tosafos answers that although one seizes from a purchaser, one might have thought not to seize from a later creditor because preventing lending is more serious than preventing purchasing. A person may be more reluctant to lend if a later collection can be taken away than a purchaser would be reluctant to buy. If that reasoning were the primary one, one could indeed argue similarly concerning market protection: if the Sages protected purchasers, why should they not protect later creditors, where discouraging lending is an even stronger concern? Knesset HaGedolah also regards this passage as a proof, though he tries to reject it by saying that lenders will not refrain from lending for this reason because not every document contains a clause subjecting movable property together with land. That rejection is unconvincing. The same could be said of purchasers. In any event, a later creditor is at least comparable to a purchaser. But the Tosafos answer there is strained. The primary explanation is that of Raah, Ritva, and Ran. They explain that the question whether a later creditor who collected first keeps the property depends upon the dispute whether property liens are of Torah law. If liens are of Torah law, certainly he does not keep it. But if liens are not of Torah law, then strictly speaking the earlier creditor should not even collect from a purchaser. The Sages enacted collection from purchasers only to protect lending. Where there are two creditors, why prefer the enactment protecting the earlier one over the enactment protecting the later one? See there. Accordingly, in our case strict law is that the special lien upon movable property together with land should permit collection. The Sages protected purchasers through a market enactment. But where the competing party is a later creditor, why should we follow the enactment for that creditor rather than the enactment for the earlier creditor? We should leave the matter under strict law. This is especially so according to Raah's further reasoning that collection from a purchaser had to be enacted because otherwise every debtor could simply sell all his assets and leave the creditor with nothing. But there was no comparable need to protect a later creditor. Who would lend to a person who already has an earlier creditor in reliance upon the hope that the earlier creditor will not come and prevent beit din from transferring the property? The Sages had no reason to protect such an uncommon reliance. If so, how can we nullify an express clause subjecting movable property together with land in order to protect a later creditor against the possibility that he might refrain from lending, when this situation is uncommon and no meaningful protection is achieved? The words of the Beit Yosef are therefore clear and correct. Even if the matter remained doubtful, in a doubt concerning a rabbinic enactment we leave the matter under Torah law.
Or he pledged. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 202, rules that if a person seized property from a husband during the husband's lifetime, and after the husband's death the wife sought to collect the seized property through the strength of her ketubah, which had a lien upon movable property together with land, the wife cannot remove it from the possessor because her time for collection had not yet arrived when he seized it. I do not know the source for this distinction. Whether or not her time had arrived, her lien had already attached. If the seizure is to be compared to a pledge, then even after the due date the market protection always applies to a pledge. In truth, however, this is not a pledge. How is such seizure weaker than the case of a later creditor who collected first? There is no greater seizure than that. Yet we have already established the Beit Yosef's position, and even Mabit, who disagrees in general, concedes regarding simple seizure. What difference does it make whether her time for collection had arrived? Does she not collect from land that her husband sold during his lifetime? Since the property was subject through the movable property together with land clause, the same lien applies here. Chavot Yair's words therefore require study and appear to have been written in passing.
For the market enactment does not apply to documents, etc. The Shach proves from here that the Mechaber follows the Rosh and not Rashba, who holds that documents can be subjected through an agav acquisition. It is possible, however, that even if documents themselves are not subject, where the second creditor collected land the first creditor can collect from him through Rabbi Natan's rule that one may extract from the person who owes one's debtor. Had the market enactment also applied to documents, then even Rabbi Natan's rule would not enable the earlier creditor to collect, because the same concern of market protection would remain. The Mechaber therefore says only that documents are uncommon and the market enactment was not made for them. See below at the end of siman 111, where I discuss this at length.
