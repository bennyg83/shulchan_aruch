(Rama, seif 2: Any item acquired, etc.) The Mordechai's gloss in the chapter Yesh Nochalin states: where the obligation is based on the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased, the beneficiary acquires nothing. The heirs are merely compelled to fulfill their father's instruction. Thus, if he said, "Give this object to so and so," and the heirs gave or sold it to another, the intended recipient has no claim against the purchaser or donee, even if the object still exists, because he never acquired that object. But where the rule is based on the principle that the words of a deathly ill person are treated as written and delivered, the recipient acquires the object completely. If the heirs sold or gave it away, he may proceed against the purchaser or donee, because the object was acquired by him wherever it is. If it remains in existence, he takes it; if not, the one who disposed of it must repay its value, for this is like stolen property whose owners did not despair, and the owner may collect from either party. These are precisely the Rama's words here. Property acquired through a deathly ill person's gift belongs to the recipient itself, and one who buys it from the heir purchases stolen property. By contrast, where it is not acquired as a deathly ill person's gift because the donor was healthy, and the only basis is the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words, the beneficiary has no proprietary right in the object. The duty rests on the heirs, not on the purchaser. The Sema's words in seif katan 5, limiting this to land, are therefore astonishing, for the Mordechai's gloss expressly concerns movable property, as shown by its statement, "If it remains in existence." Nor is its comparison to the passage in Rabbeinu Yerucham cited by the Beit Yosef at all apt. Rabbeinu Yerucham writes that a creditor and a wife's maintenance claim do not collect from a gift made by a healthy person, even from property the orphans themselves gave away. There the claimant has no ownership in the movables, only the force of a debt lien. Since collection of a debt from movables is rabbinic, it does not displace a gift by a healthy person, which is effective by Torah law, whether the father gave it or the orphans gave it. In the Mordechai's case and the Rama's ruling here, however, the recipient acquires the object itself through the deathly ill person's gift, and the heirs are thieves when they sell it. The dispossessed owner may therefore recover his property from the purchaser. The market overt protection does not apply, because that protection was enacted only for common cases. The Tur in siman 60 writes that it does not apply to promissory notes because such transactions are uncommon. This case is even less common, where a deathly ill person orders a gift to another and the heir disregards the instruction and sells or gives it away. People do not commonly refrain from buying because of such a remote concern. I am also greatly surprised by the Beit Yosef, who challenged Rabbeinu Yerucham: once the husband died, the property fell before the widow, so how can a gift by the heirs be effective? He therefore limits Rabbeinu Yerucham to the period before the enactment concerning collection from movables. Yet Tosafot in Ketubot 49 write like Rabbeinu Yerucham and give the reason just stated: a rabbinic right to collect a debt from movables cannot displace a gift by a healthy person that is effective by Torah law. It follows that they discuss even the period after the enactment. In my humble opinion, this is clear law: a creditor, ketubah claimant, or maintenance claimant does not collect movables of orphans that the orphans gave to another as a gift by a healthy person. Although the Darkei Moshe strained to explain Rabbeinu Yerucham as referring only to a gift made by the father during his lifetime but delivered to the recipient by the orphans, this is exceedingly forced. On that reading the Beit Yosef's objection remains: once the father died, the property fell before the widow, so how can the orphans' delivery validate their father's gift, when it was a gift by a healthy person without any kinyan or delivery and was therefore void? The correct explanation is that even if the father gave nothing and the orphans themselves gave the movables to another, the creditor cannot collect from the recipient. Nor can one say that the orphans must pay as one who damages another's lien, for siman 107 already establishes that orphans who sell movables need not pay. This matter appears to me clear and settled.
