For at his death the assets became obligated. Rambam explains that support of the widow and daughters, and related obligations such as the sons' ketubah inheritance, become a charge upon the estate with the completion of death, whereas an ordinary deathbed gift takes effect only after death. The family obligations therefore precede the deathbed gifts. Rif writes somewhat differently. He says the widow's maintenance obligation and the deathbed gift arrive together after the completion of death, but the rabbinic enactment of maintenance is not displaced by the donor's private deathbed disposition. The timing language of Rambam is difficult. The sons' ketubah inheritance is expressly described as inheritance, and inheritance ordinarily arises only after death, not during the completion of death. Rashba likewise says elsewhere that if heirs' inheritance necessarily vested at the completion of death, every delayed deathbed gift would fail because inheritance has no interruption. This suggests Rif's formulation is more accurate: the claims arise together. Why, then, does maintenance prevail if both arise simultaneously? Nimukei Yosef says the maintenance enactment was earlier in rabbinic priority, but that alone is not entirely satisfying if neither right takes effect before the other. A useful analogy appears in Temurah. When a Torah sanctity and a person's declaration are set to take effect simultaneously, the Gemara says, "Whose words are heard, the master's or the student's?" The Torah rule prevails over the individual's declaration. So too here, if the Sages' mandatory maintenance obligation and the testator's discretionary deathbed gift arise together, the rabbinic obligation created by the Sages precedes the private disposition. A responsum applied Rambam's language to a case where the sick person physically handed twenty nine gold coins to a third woman and directed that if he recovered she return them, but if he died she divide them between his daughter and second wife. His widow claimed the entire amount for her ketubah. In my view that case differs. Where the donor himself physically removes the gift from his possession and transfers it during life subject to a condition of death, the gift is a present healthy person's transfer whose condition, once fulfilled, relates back. That transfer should precede obligations attaching only at death. The ordinary rule preferring maintenance concerns a true deathbed gift that takes effect only through the special deathbed enactment, not property already validly delivered during life. The source text repeats the opening lemma and the same basic distinction. The central conclusion remains: ordinary deathbed gifts yield to obligations arising from the Sages' estate rules at death, but a genuinely present conditional transfer made during life can stand on an earlier footing. For ketubah and maintenance. Beit Yosef cites Rashbatz concerning a widow who paid part of her husband's charitable legacies and then died. Her heirs refused to complete the payments. Rashbatz ruled that movables she had seized after her husband's death are in the hands of her heirs as payment of her ketubah, while land for which she had not yet sworn on her ketubah should be sold to complete the charitable dispositions. Beit Yosef expressed surprise at the first ruling, arguing that if the widow died before taking the required oath, her seizure should not benefit her heirs. The surprise is unwarranted. Baal HaTerumot rules that where the debtor died before the creditor and the creditor's heirs seized property, we do not remove it from them, because a judge who ruled according to Rabbi Eliezer would not have his ruling reversed, and a person may in appropriate circumstances execute his own lawful claim. Most authorities accept this, and the Shulchan Aruch itself rules that if the creditor's heirs seized, the property is not removed. Accordingly, once the widow had actually seized the movables for her ketubah during her lifetime, her heirs retain that possession even though she died without completing the ordinary oath process. Rashbatz's ruling therefore has a clear basis in the accepted law.
