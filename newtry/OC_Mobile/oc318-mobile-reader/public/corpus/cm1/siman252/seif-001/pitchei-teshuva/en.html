Shevut Yaakov, part I, siman 168, discusses a woman who directed her children before her death that if disputes arose among them, a particular relative should adjudicate. At her urging they all gave that relative a formal pledge. One son later refused to submit to him, claiming that he gave the pledge only to calm his mother so that her mind would not become disturbed. Shevut Yaakov explains that apart from the pledge, the rule “it is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased” applies to the deceased's property and inheritance, not generally to other matters. Even regarding property, the accepted law requires that it have been delivered to a third party for that purpose, as in siman 252. There is no basis to extend the rule to unrelated personal directives. Nevertheless, as a matter of kindness beyond the strict law, one should honor a parent's other dying wishes as well, though the court ordinarily compels such conduct only by moral persuasion rather than coercive process. Here, however, the formal pledge provides a basis to compel the son to submit to the named relative. The son's argument that he pledged only to calm his sick mother initially seems substantial. Yoreh Deah siman 232 seif 17 rules that a vow made at a sick person's importuning merely so that the patient not become distraught is treated as a vow made under duress and needs no release. Shevut Yaakov notes reservations about that rule but accepts the codified halachah. He nevertheless distinguishes a parent's testament. Where the promisor had no duty to comply apart from his oath, he can plausibly say the oath was made solely to prevent distress. But a child is already morally obligated, at least beyond the strict law and subject to verbal pressure, to honor a parent's final directive. If he then adds a formal pledge or oath, how can that pledge be dismissed as merely calming the parent when he was independently expected to comply? Yaakov's requiring Yosef to swear while ill supports this distinction, as does Mordechai cited by Ramah, siman 81 seif 2, where even a son's silence cannot simply be explained as an effort not to upset his mother. Certainly one who actually gave a formal pledge cannot avoid it on that ground. Shevut Yaakov therefore concludes that in this case there is no release from the obligation.
