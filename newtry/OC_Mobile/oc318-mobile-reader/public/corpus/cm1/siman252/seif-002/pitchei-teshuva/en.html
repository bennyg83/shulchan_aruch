Or vowed to give. Rabbi Akiva Eiger, responsum 108, understands this to mean a vow to give to the poor. Ramah, responsum 48, question 3, discussing Mordechai in the chapter Mi SheMet in the name of Maharam, writes that one who says “I will give after my death” does not create a binding vow, because when the obligation would take effect he is already free of mitzvot. Ramah adds that even one who says “I will give after thirty days,” or simply “I will give” and then dies, does not bind his heirs, just as Rivash rules concerning an oath. One might object from the case of a person who says “an olah is incumbent upon me,” whose heirs must bring the offering. Rabbi Akiva Eiger answers that Ramah follows his own view in Yoreh Deah siman 258 seif 7 that dedicating a debt document to charity requires writing and delivery, in accordance with Rashba, because charity is not governed by the rule that a declaration to the Most High is equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person. Thus a vow “I will give to the poor” is not itself a proprietary obligation created by kinyan; it merely obligates the promisor personally to keep his word, like an oath, and that personal duty does not pass to heirs. Rabbi Akiva Eiger notes, however, that Ramah above, siman 212 seif 7, says that if one vows, “When the fruits of this palm come into existence I will give them to the poor,” and dies, the statement is ineffective. Sema implies that this is specifically where he died before the fruit came into existence; if the fruit grew during his lifetime and he then died, it should be given after death. All the more so might this apply to one who says “I will give one hundred.” This appears inconsistent and requires study. See Ketzot HaChoshen here and below, siman 290 seif katan 2, and what I wrote above, siman 212 seif 7 seif katan 9. Rabbi Akiva Eiger further discusses a paper found among the deceased's effects stating that a certain sum “shall be for good causes after my death.” There is considerable doubt whether the heirs must fulfill it. Even if “shall be” can function like “I will give,” it is itself highly doubtful whether a simple “I will give” binds heirs, and here the language resembles “I will give after death” even more closely. Elsewhere Rabbi Akiva Eiger likewise questions whether a dedication of a Sefer Torah “after death” has any effect, since there is no dedication after death. An additional difficulty is that even an unqualified dedication, where no living witnesses testify to it and only a writing is found among the donor's papers, is disputed by major authorities. See above, siman 250 seif 25. Ketzot HaChoshen records a case where a man vowed during life to give a tithe of his money to charity, distributed part of it, and before death directed that the remaining tithe be given pursuant to his earlier vow. The poor of the city sought to compel the orphan administrators to fulfill the will. Ketzot concluded that the direction had no effect. The original vow did not bind the heirs after death, and the later statement could not be treated as an ordinary deathbed gift because the dying man expressly said to give it on account of his existing vow. Tosafot distinguish a true gift, for which the Sages enacted that the words of a dying person are like a written and delivered instrument to prevent distress, from an instruction to pay an existing debt, where the recipient has only the original debt-law rights. So too, Ketzot argued, the poor had only the original vow, which died with the promisor. Netivot HaMishpat disagrees. In the debt case, the orphans at least have a mitzvah to pay their father's debt, though they may not be judicially compelled; the dying man's instruction can therefore be read as directing fulfillment of that existing obligation. In the case of a vow that otherwise imposes no duty at all on the heirs, however, when the dying man says “give,” the natural meaning is that he now grants a deathbed gift. Netivot says this is evident from the authorities who allow collection from heirs in such circumstances. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat end of siman 115, likewise questions Ketzot and suggests the principle that a person knows his original vow will not bind after death and therefore, by his new deathbed direction, intends to correct it and give now as a valid deathbed gift, analogous to the Talmudic presumption that one knows an earlier defective act is ineffective and performs the later act with the legally effective intent.
