It is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. Tosafos in chapter Mi Shemet, Bava Batra 149, s.v. 'that they teach him a claim,' initially cite Rabbeinu Yitzchak that this rule applies only when the deceased gave the instruction orally face to face. Rabbeinu Yitzchak later retracted because of chapter HaSholeach, Gittin 40, where Rabbi Yochanan interprets the statement, 'I made so-and-so my slave a free man,' as written in a document. The baraita then teaches regarding, 'I will make him a free man,' that Rabbi says the slave acquired while the Sages say he did not acquire but the heirs are compelled, which Tosafos understood as a written statement as well. Our printed Gemara does not contain the words, 'the heirs are compelled.' Maharsha therefore writes that Tosafos had a version of the baraita reading, 'the Sages say he did not acquire, and the heirs are compelled.' It appears that Tosafos refer to the Tosefta, Bava Batra chapter 9: one who says, 'I gave such-and-such field to so-and-so,' or, 'it shall be given to him,' Rabbi says he acquired while the Sages say he did not acquire and the heirs are compelled to fulfill the words of the deceased. Likewise, one who says, 'I made so-and-so my slave a free man,' or, 'he shall be made free,' is subject to the same dispute. This is evidently the baraita underlying Gittin 40. Although the Gemara's wording and the Tosefta's wording differ slightly, both use future language according to the Sages, and the Tosefta explicitly states that the heirs are compelled. A difficulty remains, because the Tosefta says the same concerning a field. Tosafos hold that the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased applies only where the property was initially deposited with a third party for that purpose. In the case of a slave they explain that the slave is considered in possession of himself, and therefore no outside deposit is needed. But how can the heirs be compelled concerning the field? Ramban indeed proves from this Tosefta that the rule applies even without prior deposit, as Ran cites in Gittin. But Tosafos themselves evidently had this Tosefta in mind, so how do they explain the field? According to Maggid Mishneh, chapter 22 of Hilchot Mechirah, the matter can be resolved. He writes that even in the case of a shechiv mera's vow, although the one who made the vow has died, the heirs are compelled through the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased just as he himself would have been obligated had he lived. Rambam ordinarily requires prior deposit, but a vow is different. Since the father was personally obligated while alive, that preexisting obligation is no worse than prior deposit. See what I wrote in siman 212, seif katan 7. We also wrote in siman 245, seif katan 1 that according to those who hold a kinyan of 'I will give' is effective, the baraita's case of a field written as, 'I will give it,' means that the recipient has not yet acquired the field, but the donor is personally compelled to fulfill what he wrote. This resembles kinyan 'I will give': the kinyan does not itself transfer the property immediately, but it obligates the promisor to make the transfer. Since the donor would have been compelled during life to give the field as promised in the document, that living obligation is no worse than prior deposit, and the heirs are compelled through the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased, just as Maggid Mishneh says regarding a vow. The slave case is different. We explained in siman 245 that where he writes, 'I will make him free,' the slave has not acquired freedom and remains the master's slave. Nor does the document create a personal obligation upon the master to free him, because the slave's hand is legally the master's hand. The principle that the get and the slave's hand arrive simultaneously cannot help where this particular document does not itself free him. Thus the master could not be compelled during life merely because of this document. The heirs' obligation must therefore rest on the special consideration that the slave is regarded as in possession of himself, as Tosafos explain. Although the Tosefta uses future language, 'it shall be given' and 'he shall be made free,' all are expressions of future undertaking and are equivalent to a kinyan of 'I will give.'
Provided that he now gave it to a third party for this purpose. Tosafos in chapter Mi Shemet ask from Gittin 40, where one says, 'My maidservant so-and-so gave me satisfaction,' and the heirs are compelled by reason of the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words even though nothing was deposited with a third party. Tosafos answer that emancipation is different, and the rule is applied there more readily. Ran in the first chapter of Gittin explains that Heaven has acquired a right in the slave and the slave also has a right in himself, making this stronger than removing an object from one's possession and placing it with another. Baal HaItur likewise explains that Heaven has acquired the right. Yet the concept that Heaven has acquired a right appears to apply only when the deceased instructed actual emancipation, for after emancipation the slave enters the sanctity of Israel. What if he did not instruct emancipation, but only that the slave not be required to perform hard labor? There is no direct acquisition for Heaven, yet Gittin says that if he declared that his maidservant had given him satisfaction, the heirs are compelled to give her satisfaction. Rif explains that if she says, 'I do not wish to perform this task,' they may not compel her. She remains a maidservant, so where is the heavenly acquisition? Perhaps this is why Rashi explains that 'they give her satisfaction' means that even if she cannot receive satisfaction without emancipation, they must free her. Since the command is broad enough to entitle her to freedom if she chooses it, a heavenly right exists in the instruction. Consequently the mitzvah to fulfill the deceased's words governs all aspects of the instruction, even if she chooses only favorable treatment rather than actual freedom. Rosh in chapter Metziat HaIshah gives a different explanation: the case is like property placed with a third party because the slave is in possession of herself. Although we wrote in siman 241, seif katan 3 that a slave is not considered in possession against the master, since legally the slave is like the master's ox or donkey and the slave's hand is the master's hand, for the limited purpose of this rule the slave's possession suffices. It is no worse than property deposited for the intended recipient, even though formal ownership has not passed from the original owner.
One who swore or vowed to give to a particular person. An oath does not itself create an acquisition, and a vow to give to the poor likewise does not create an acquisition. The rule that a statement to Heaven is equivalent to delivery to an ordinary person applies to inherent sanctity or Temple property, but a vow for the poor merely creates a personal obligation through the verse that treats charity as an obligation of one's speech. When the donor dies, the heirs therefore need not fulfill the father's oath or vow. This applies even if he said, 'This is for the poor.' A case came before me involving a person who, during life, vowed to give a tithe of his money from his assets. He distributed some during life and, before death, instructed that the remaining tithe be given from his estate in accordance with his earlier vow. The city's poor asked me to compel the guardians of his minor heirs to fulfill the will. After consideration I did not find myself able to obligate them. Since one who vows and dies does not obligate his heirs, the earlier vow itself could not bind them. Nor does the later deathbed instruction necessarily create a shechiv mera gift. In Bava Batra 175, Rav and Shmuel say that where a shechiv mera says, 'I owe one hundred to so-and-so,' if he adds, 'Give it,' they give, but if not they do not. The Gemara asks: if he holds no document, how can 'give it' suffice? It is an oral loan, and Rav and Shmuel themselves hold that an oral loan is not collected from heirs. Tosafos ask: are not the words of a shechiv mera treated as written and delivered? If a written and delivered gift would work, why should this verbal direction fail? Tosafos answer that the special enactment applies where he gives by way of gift, for otherwise his mind might become distressed. But where he directs payment specifically because of an alleged loan and not by way of gift, the claimant has only the original loan basis. If that original claim cannot be collected from heirs, the deathbed words do not transform it into a gift. The same applies here. The deceased instructed payment specifically because of the vow he had undertaken during life. The poor therefore have only the original vow basis, and a vow does not bind heirs after the vower's death. I recognize that the general implication of responsa literature is not obviously so. Responsa Ran, siman 1, discusses a similar direction to give what had previously been vowed for Torah study and rules that the heirs must give it. Yet the responsum appears to ground the obligation in the original vow. Rama, by contrast, clearly states that heirs are exempt from the father's vow itself. See also what I wrote in siman 250, seif katan 9: one who says on his deathbed, 'I owe one hundred to so-and-so,' speaks as one seeking to pay an already existing debt and therefore effectively intends a transfer from today. An instruction effective from today is outside the special shechiv mera enactment unless an ordinary kinyan exists, as Rama rules in siman 250, seif 7. The same reasoning applies here. Since he vowed during life and now says to give the poor according to that earlier vow, his intent is to satisfy a present obligation. That points toward a transfer from today, not a special after-death gift, and thus the shechiv mera enactment may not apply. See what I wrote there, seif katan 6. The matter still requires study. In siman 290, seif katan 2, however, I wrote an explanation preserving Beit Yosef's responsum that heirs are obligated where one vowed and then died. Moreover, according to what I wrote above in seif katan 3 from Maggid Mishneh, whenever the father was personally obligated during life, the heirs are compelled through the mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. If so, the same should apply here.
