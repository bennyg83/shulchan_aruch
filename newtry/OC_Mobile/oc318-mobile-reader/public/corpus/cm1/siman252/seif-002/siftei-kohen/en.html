The recipient may recover it, etc. See the Sema, seif katan 6. Everything he writes there is incorrect. His statement that the case appears to concern land is not persuasive, because the plain wording indicates that even movable property may be recovered from them. This is explicit in the long responsum of Ritzba cited in the Hagahot Mordechai to Bava Batra, which is the source of this ruling and is quoted in the Darchei Moshe. He distinguishes sharply between the rule that the words of a dangerously ill person are as though written and delivered, and the rule that it is a mitzvah to fulfill the words of the deceased. By virtue of the first rule, the recipient acquires the specific object completely. If the heirs sell it or give it away, he may recover it from the purchaser or recipient, because the object was already acquired by him and the owners did not despair. He may collect from whichever party he chooses. The Sema's further claim that Rabbeinu Yerucham's reason is the market regulation is also incorrect. Rabbeinu Yerucham mentions no market regulation. He writes only that a ketubah and support are not collected from movable property that the father gave away, or that the heirs gave away after his death. Indeed, the Beit Yosef and Darchei Moshe in siman 60 are difficult, because the market regulation has no application to a gift. The words of the Rosh and Tur in siman 60 concern a different matter, as I explain below in siman 356. The correct law is that where movable property was acquired by one person through the gift of a dangerously ill person, he may recover it from a later recipient, as stated by Ritzba in the Hagahot Mordechai, and no authority disputes this. Rabbeinu Yerucham's case is different because the woman has only a lien on the assets, even after the enactment of the Geonim. There is plainly no priority among movable property, as explained above in siman 104. This also resolves the Beit Yosef's and Darchei Moshe's objection to Rabbeinu Yerucham that once the father died the movables became subject to the widow under the enactment of the Geonim, so how could the heirs' gift help? His words are clear. The Rosh likewise states explicitly in Kiddushin, in the chapter HaOmer, that even after the enactment of the Geonim a ketubah is not collected from movable property given away by the heirs. Although the Perisha and Bach in Even HaEzer, beginning of siman 100, write that the Rosh discusses support only, any careful reader will see that this is untenable, for the Rosh expressly discusses the ketubah. The reason is that even after the enactment of the Geonim the woman has only a lien, and that enactment plainly applied only to movables still in the heirs' possession, not to movables they sold, because of the purchasers' loss. The same applies to movables given away, since a gift is treated like a sale for this purpose, as is clear in Gittin 50b and the poskim, and as explained above at the beginning of siman 113. Since a ketubah and an ordinary creditor are not collected from movable property purchased from the heirs, because there is no priority among movables, they are likewise not collected from those who received them as gifts. The Nimukei Yosef states this explicitly in the chapter Mi SheMet. He writes that the rabbinic enactment allowing a creditor and a wife to collect from the heirs' movable property applies only while the movables remain in the heirs' possession. If the heirs sold or gave them away, the creditor may not seize them, because the enactment of the later authorities cannot be stronger than an express lien on movables, which does not bind purchasers unless the document states that the movables are included together with land. The Beit Yosef himself cites this above, at the end of siman 107, seif 16. There is no doubt that the Beit Yosef, Darchei Moshe, and those who followed them did not correctly understand Rabbeinu Yerucham here. His words are simple and are explicitly supported by the Rosh and Nimukei Yosef. Here, however, the body of the movable property was fully acquired by the recipient through the gift of a dangerously ill person, by strict law, and therefore it is his wherever it is found. It is obvious that according to all authorities he recovers it from the purchaser or recipient, because they sold or gave away property that was not theirs. If it was sold in circumstances where the purchaser did not know of the prior gift and the market regulation applies, the original recipient must reimburse the purchaser for the price he paid, as explained below in siman 357. All this is clear. See also what I wrote above at the beginning of siman 107.
