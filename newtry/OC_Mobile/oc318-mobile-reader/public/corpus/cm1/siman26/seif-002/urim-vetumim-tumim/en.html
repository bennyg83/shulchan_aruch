And to testify that this person is liable. Sema cites R. Sherira Gaon that this permission applies only where the non-Jewish court is known not to take bribes. In such a court a Jew may testify, where permitted, concerning another Jew's liability. Sema asks why Rama omits this condition. Kenesset HaGedolah, citing Mahari ben Lev, asks more fundamentally: why should bribery matter? If the witness testifies truthfully that the defendant owes the money, what concern is there? The concern is clear. Below, siman 28, we rule that where non-Jewish courts extract money on the basis of one witness, a Jew may not testify before them alone, because his testimony will cause collection in a manner not recognized by Torah law. The same applies to corrupt judges who take bribes. Even if the witness truthfully testifies that the defendant owes one hundred, corrupt judges may exploit the proceeding to impose an additional fine, excessive costs, or other monetary burdens beyond the lawful debt. The witness then becomes the cause of money being extracted improperly. R. Sherira Gaon's responsum itself proves the comparison, because he brings support from the rule concerning a Jew who knows testimony relevant to a secular court. Therefore the bribery condition is entirely relevant. Sema's question concerning Rama can also be answered. Certainly, if we know clearly that the judges accept bribes, they have no status as lawful judges of the king. They are robbers and perverters of justice. No Jew may hand another Jew over to such men. R. Sherira's novelty is that one should know they do not take bribes before testifying. This parallels Rif's position below, siman 68, that secular judges are not presumed honest unless their integrity is known. Rosh disagrees there and holds that absent evidence of corruption, we do not presume secular judges to take bribes. Rama follows Rosh in siman 68. Therefore Rama need not repeat R. Sherira's formulation here. According to his general rule, so long as we have no specific knowledge that these judges are corrupt, they are not presumed bribed. But if it is known that they do take bribes, the prohibition is obvious and requires no special statement. This resolves the matter.
