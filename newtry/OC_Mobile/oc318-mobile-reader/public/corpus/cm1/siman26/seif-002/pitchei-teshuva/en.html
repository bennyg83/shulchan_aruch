Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 155, discusses a handwritten obligation stating that if the debtor does not pay by a certain date he will owe a penalty of one hundred reichsthaler to the government. By law, the creditor may not sue the debtor in a secular court, as stated in siman 26, unless the debtor is violent or refuses compliance, in which case one first obtains permission from beit din. With such a document, beit din must proceed cautiously and warn the debtor of the additional damage that may result beyond the debt itself. If he ignores the warning, and the creditor then seeks only what is due while the authorities impose their penalty, the debtor has no complaint against the creditor. Without permission of beit din, however, the contractual condition does not help, even if it expressly says that upon default the creditor may report the penalty to the government and will not be treated as an informer. The condition cannot authorize suit in secular court or reporting the penalty alone. Even if the clause waiving the law of an informer were viewed as a stipulation of exemption, like 'tear my garment on condition that you be exempt,' there is still room to distinguish, because there the person granting the exemption derives no benefit and therefore clearly waives liability, whereas here the circumstances differ; see the responsum.
For the beit din to go. Birkei Yosef writes that Baal HaTerumot, in the name of Rav Sherira Gaon, states that elders and students have permission to go to the secular authorities. Mekor Baruch, siman 42, and Mahara Sasson, siman 52, understood the mention of sages and elders rather than ordinary people as a limitation enacted for public order: if ordinary people were allowed to testify there, they might learn to bring all their disputes before secular authorities even without permission of beit din. Mekor Baruch therefore maintained that the dispensation applies specifically to scholars and students, comparing this to Rambam's instruction that desecration of Shabbat for a dangerously ill person should be performed by leading Jews and sages. Birkei Yosef notes that they overlooked Ittur, at the end of the section on arbitration, which shows that the wording is not exclusive. In my view, Rav Sherira may have mentioned elders and students as a greater novelty: even they may go, despite the possibility that a distinguished person should be treated differently, as in Ketubot 52b and 85b.

Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 3, explains Rav Sherira's requirement that the secular judges not accept bribes. A Torah scholar is ordinarily not required to testify before a beit din beneath his dignity, and there is a question whether he may waive his honor where he has no obligation to do so. One might therefore think that a Torah scholar should certainly not testify before a secular tribunal. Rav Sherira teaches the opposite: it is an honor to Torah judges that, after they have ruled a person liable but lack power to rescue the oppressed party, they go and testify before secular authorities that this person was found liable for a specified amount. Rav Sherira limits this to tribunals that do not take bribes and will enforce the Torah court's decision. If they take bribes, the scholar may degrade himself by testifying and yet achieve nothing; he may not waive the honor of Torah on a mere possibility. Chatam Sofer further discusses a deathbed declaration made before witnesses that the dying man owed a sum to someone. After his death he left minor heirs, so the creditor feared the witnesses would forget before the heirs reached adulthood. Recording their testimony formally in beit din was difficult because beit din was concerned for the opinion that testimony should not be received in the presence of minor heirs. The creditor therefore wished to have the witnesses' statements entered into the official protocol of the secular authorities merely for preservation. Chatam Sofer concludes that this does not make the secular tribunal a judicial authority at all; it merely deposits the information for a future day, when it can be brought before Torah judges. He sees no prohibition in doing so even without permission of beit din, all the more with permission. When the heirs mature and the record is brought before a proper beit din, whether the witnesses may alter their earlier statements because those were not made before beit din, or whether adherence to them is suspect as maintaining a prior falsehood, will be decided by the competent judge of that time. In any event, because an official record will likely lead the heirs, once grown, to accept the debt and pay voluntarily, preservation of the testimony is legitimate.
