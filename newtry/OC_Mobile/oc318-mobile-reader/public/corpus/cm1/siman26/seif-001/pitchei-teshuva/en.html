He obtains permission from beit din. See Responsa Chavos Yair, siman 155, concerning a handwritten obligation stating that if the debtor did not pay by a specified time he would become liable for a penalty of one hundred Reichsthaler to the government. By strict law, it is certainly forbidden for the creditor to sue the debtor in a non Jewish court, as stated in Choshen Mishpat siman 26. If, however, the opposing party is violent and recalcitrant, we rule that the creditor may obtain permission from beit din and proceed in a non Jewish court. With respect to such an obligation, beit din must be especially deliberate and must warn the debtor of the loss that may befall him beyond the debt itself. If the debtor disregards the warning, and the creditor then claims what is owed to him while the authorities demand the penalty, the debtor has no complaint against the creditor. Without permission from beit din, however, the debtor's stipulation is certainly ineffective, even if it explicitly states that immediately upon delay the creditor may report the penalty to the authorities and shall not have the status of an informer who delivers another to coercive authorities. All of this does not permit the creditor to sue him in a non Jewish court or even merely to report the penalty to the authorities. Even if we were to say that the statement that he shall not have the status of an informer is like a condition that exempts him from liability, comparable to 'tear my garment on condition that you be exempt' in siman 380, seif 1, there is still room to distinguish. There, the one who gives permission receives no benefit, so he certainly waives liability wholeheartedly, for otherwise why would he say it. Here, however, the circumstances are different.
