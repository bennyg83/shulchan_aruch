Even if both litigants agreed, etc. Kenesset HaGedolah questions Beit Yosef's citation of R. Yerucham, who writes that if one litigant wishes to go before non-Jewish courts, we do not listen to him, and cites a responsum of Rif that he is placed under nidui. Kenesset HaGedolah infers that if both litigants agree, perhaps they may go. That inference is incorrect. R. Yerucham cites Rif precisely because the case is not the obvious one where a person simply chooses a non-Jewish tribunal in defiance of Torah law. The relevant responsum is the one cited by Baal HaTerumot and later by Mechaber in seif 3. There, one litigant had accepted upon himself adjudication in a non-Jewish court, and the other wished to proceed there because of that prior acceptance. Rif nevertheless ruled that doing so is prohibited. R. Yerucham's phrase "if one of the litigants wishes" means that one says, in effect: "My opponent already brought or accepted the matter there, so I too wish to go and litigate there." The law is that we do not listen to him. His opponent's wrongful resort to that forum does not permit him to participate. That is why R. Yerucham specifically cites Rif's responsum. It is the very case under discussion. His terse wording is somewhat compressed, as is often his style, and therefore requires careful reading. I have seen in Metz, and similarly in other Ashkenazic communities, communal enactments concerning debt instruments that permit litigants to adjudicate certain disputes before secular courts. I do not know who permitted this. One might say that because the enactment was accepted by the entire community, it is as though all townspeople consented in advance. But even unanimous consent does not permit Jews to give prestige to non-Jewish law and courts. Rashba writes explicitly that even where both litigants voluntarily wish to adjudicate before a non-Jewish court in a monetary matter, it is forbidden. Torah did not leave the people of Hashem free to abandon its judicial system in order to honor foreign law. This remains prohibited even where the secular court would reach the same substantive result as a Jewish beit din. One might further argue that the special law of negotiable instruments reflects dina d'malchuta, because it concerns commercial regulations enacted for the order of the kingdom. That can indeed affect what a Jewish beit din rules. If the king establishes a commercial regulation for the country's benefit, a Jewish court may be required to recognize that law where dina d'malchuta applies. Likewise, parties who drafted the instrument subject to that commercial legal framework can become obligated accordingly. But none of this authorizes the parties to go before the non-Jewish judges themselves. The king's commercial enactment may influence the substantive rights. It does not transform a forbidden tribunal into a permitted one. The king's law is certainly no greater than the law of the King of the universe, the holy Torah, and yet even when the secular judges happen to rule in the same manner as Torah law, appearing before them remains prohibited. Therefore, because of our sins, this communal practice is deeply troubling to me. I lack the power to uproot a practice that has become firmly established and to which no one listens when it is challenged. But anyone with sufficient authority to abolish such an enactment will receive reward from the God of justice.
The first view appears primary. Rama decides that once a litigant chose a non-Jewish court and lost there, beit din need not reopen the same claim for him. In my humble opinion this requires great study. If the secular tribunal awarded one party possession of another Jew's money contrary to Torah law, who permitted him to retain it? What source is there for allowing him to keep another person's property merely because of a forbidden judgment? The only foundation appears to be Maharik, shoresh 188, quoting R. Yitzchak bar Peretz. He writes that one who brought his claim before royal law and afterward wishes to sue again in Jewish court on the same matter is not heard, because having abandoned Jewish law and chosen secular law, he cannot later reverse himself. Maharik connects this to dina d'malchuta. But Maharik himself writes there that R. Yitzchak bar Peretz held a particularly broad view of dina d'malchuta. Maharik states explicitly that we do not follow that view in every matter, and most authorities disagree. Even Rama's broad ruling below, siman 369, that dina d'malchuta applies extensively must be understood, as Rashba writes, to laws enacted by the king for the welfare and order of the country. If every procedural and substantive rule of non-Jewish courts automatically became binding through dina d'malchuta, all Torah monetary law would disappear. Instead of studying the Talmud and poskim, we would merely inspect royal statutes. That cannot be. R. Yitzchak bar Peretz must therefore mean something more limited. Certainly, if the same dispute is originally brought to a Jewish beit din, the judges do not apply non-Jewish law merely because the king's courts would rule differently. Rather, where the litigant himself chose the foreign forum, his conduct can reveal consent to be bound by that system for this dispute. Maharik's language indeed suggests this: by leaving Jewish law and choosing the secular tribunal, he demonstrated that he wished to be judged there. The principle can apply even without an actual appearance in court where the contract itself was deliberately drafted according to secular forms and law. In that circumstance the party may have accepted those legal consequences in advance. But we do not accept that broad theory as stated. If a person formally makes a kinyan agreeing to litigate before non-Jewish judges, Mechaber rules in seif 3 that he can still retract. The agreement is invalid because it is an undertaking to violate Torah law. If even a formal kinyan does not bind him to the forbidden forum, why should mere conduct of having gone there once be stronger? One must therefore say that R. Yitzchak bar Peretz follows Baal HaTerumot and those who hold that a kinyan to submit to a non-Jewish court can be effective. Then actual conduct in adopting their legal forms can likewise create binding acceptance. But our halachah follows Rosh and Mechaber that such a kinyan does not validate the prohibited arrangement. Accordingly, the entire basis for refusing to reopen the case becomes doubtful. One might defend Rama by referring to his ruling above, siman 22, seif 2. There he says that although a person who accepted a non-Jewish judge can retract before judgment, once the non-Jew has already ruled he can no longer retract. Here too, Rama speaks after the foreign judgment. But Sema, and I as well above, explain that the siman 22 ruling itself concerns a case involving a kinyan. Without a kinyan, why should completion of the foreign judgment suddenly give it legal validity? Indeed Shach attacks Rama there and argues that the supposed source in Mordechai does not support the ruling. I explained above that Beit Yosef's interpretation of Mordechai is correct and that the source concerned accepting a non-Jew as a witness rather than as a judge. Therefore Rama's foundation remains uncertain. At first I considered a different rationale. Perhaps Jewish beit din refuses to hear the case as a penalty. The litigant deliberately walked in darkness, despised Torah adjudication, and gave honor to idolatrous legal institutions. Why should beit din now protect his interests after he rejected its authority? I found support in Bava Kamma 92: "Call your fellow, and if he does not answer, cast a great wall upon him," derived from the verse, "Because I purified you and you were not purified, you shall no longer be purified." The commentators apply this to a person who refuses to attend Jewish court. After he persists, the claimant may proceed against him in a secular forum. Why choose that particular verse, when Tanakh contains many passages describing how refusal to heed Israel's judges leads to punishment by foreign powers? Perhaps the lesson is stronger. Call him to Torah judgment. If he refuses, create a barrier against him. Even if he later says, "Now I wish to return to Torah law," perhaps his prior refusal has consequences: "you shall no longer be purified." On this approach, once a person chose secular judgment, beit din could decline to assist him as a penalty. This may be what Beit Yosef understood when he cites Maharam's responsum concerning R. Yoel and R. Ephraim. There one party wrongly went to secular authorities, and the other retaliated by informing on him and causing financial loss. Maharam ruled that despite the first party's misconduct, the informer must reimburse the loss. Even the property of a complete informer is not automatically ownerless. If refusing renewed Jewish adjudication were based on a punitive forfeiture of the wrongdoer's property, Maharam's case would contradict it. Beit Yosef therefore writes that Maharam's responsum appears contrary to R. Yitzchak bar Peretz. Rama responds that Maharam's case is different. The first litigant never consented to have his money confiscated through denunciation merely because he used a secular court. That suggests Rama does not understand the rule as a penalty forfeiting property. He understands it as consent to the foreign adjudicatory result. If so, we return to the earlier difficulty: why should such consent be stronger than a kinyan that Torah law allows him to retract? For that reason the practical law requires great caution. If the money has already been physically extracted through the secular judgment and is now held by the other party, it is difficult for Jewish beit din to reverse possession where the holder can claim kim li according to Rama and the authorities supporting the binding effect of the completed foreign adjudication. But where the secular court merely entered a judgment and the money remains in the original possessor's hands, I find it very difficult to authorize extraction contrary to Torah law. What basis is there to remove money from its possessor when the supposed obligation arose through a forum Torah law forbids and through an acceptance which, according to our general rule, was retractable? That would approach outright theft. Thus, so long as the foreign judgment has not yet resulted in possession, a Jewish beit din should be extremely reluctant to enforce the foreign result against Torah law. Even according to Rama, one further limitation is obvious. His rule can apply only where the foreign judges decided the case according to the secular law that the litigant chose. If the judges themselves corrupted the proceeding through bribery, manipulation, or obvious violation of their own law, then the litigant never accepted such injustice. He accepted judgment according to that legal system, not judicial corruption. This is analogous to accepting a relative or otherwise disqualified judge and later discovering that the accepted tribunal acted outside the basis of the acceptance. Where the supposed judge did not even follow the accepted standard, the acceptance fails. Accordingly, if the foreign court distorted its own law through bribery or similar misconduct, Jewish beit din must hear the case and protect the victim from wrongful loss. That point is clear. Sema adds another ruling. Even according to authorities who would ordinarily reopen the claim, if the foreign court has already transferred the disputed money, Jewish beit din need not intervene. I do not know the source for this. Seizure obtained through non-Jewish coercion is generally not considered valid halachic tefisah. The poskim state this clearly. Why then should possession acquired through a prohibited foreign judgment automatically prevent beit din from determining whether the holder possesses stolen money? The mere fact that foreign authorities placed the money in his hands should not transform wrongful possession into lawful ownership. Therefore Sema's extension also does not appear correct.
