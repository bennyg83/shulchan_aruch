It is forbidden to adjudicate, etc. Even where a person is permitted to enforce his own right without first going to beit din, he may not do so through gentile authorities. Sema. See what I wrote above in siman 4.
And in their courts, etc. The practice is to compel payment of taxes and communal levies through gentile authorities. Maharik, shoresh 1 and 6. See there.
Even if their law is the same. Even where they apply the same substantive rule as Jewish law, the Torah says, "These are the judgments that you shall place before them," which excludes gentile courts. The verse indicates that even these very judgments, meaning rules identical to Jewish law, must be brought before Jewish judges.
And he has raised his hand against the Torah of Moshe. Because by doing so he makes it appear, Heaven forbid, as though the Torah of Moshe is not true or is insufficient to bring the law to clear resolution, and therefore he goes before gentiles who are supposedly more skilled in the royal laws fashioned according to human reasoning.
Only that he compelled him through gentiles, etc. This means without permission of beit din. But if he first went to beit din and established that the other party is violent and refuses to comply, it is explained below that beit din may authorize him to use gentile enforcement. This is clear, and Sema writes so. Kenesset HaGedolah cites Rashach that the prohibition concerns adjudicating before gentiles, but if he already has a judgment from a Jewish beit din he may compel performance of that judgment through gentiles. This requires study in light of what I wrote above, that even one who may otherwise enforce his own right may not rescue his property through gentiles unless the debtor is violent. See my discussion there.
The first view appears primary, etc. See Tumim, where I wrote that this ruling is very open to question and that one must distinguish whether the claimant has already collected. At the least, before he has been finally obligated under gentile law, where proceedings have merely begun, he certainly may withdraw and sue instead under Jewish law, and the other party must submit to Jewish law, since one may retract before final judgment. Likewise, even after he was held liable under gentile law, if beit din knows that the secular court distorted its own legal system and that the obligation does not follow the law and statutes established for it by the king, the extraction is plainly theft.
