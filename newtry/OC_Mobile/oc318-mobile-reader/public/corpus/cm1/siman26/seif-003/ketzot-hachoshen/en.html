There is an authority who says. The Sema writes that he found no one who disputes this ruling and suggests that it may relate to the principle that beit din is not warned to coerce performance of a positive command whose reward is stated alongside it. Ramban explains that beit din is not obligated to punish such a person, although if they wish they may still compel. The Sema ultimately says that perhaps this is simply the Mechaber's style of writing "there is an authority who says" even when no disagreement is known. Taz cites Bach, who explains that there is no practical compulsion here because the person can always say, "I will give, but I will give whenever I choose." Taz objects. Even according to authorities who do not compel ordinary charity because its reward is written beside it, where a person says, "I will give charity," the statement is a vow, and vows are certainly enforced. The baraita in Rosh HaShanah derives from "you shall observe what leaves your lips" both the prohibition and the warning to beit din to compel him. Charity is included in that verse. Bach elsewhere distinguishes that ordinary charity can be compelled, but a vow to the poor is not coerced because the person can seek release from the vow. Mordechai, however, indicates clearly that a charity vow is indeed subject to coercion. Rav Hai Gaon is the main authority supporting the Sema: Ran cites him as holding that even one who violates a vow after three festivals is subject to the prohibition but beit din need not compel him. Ran rejects this from the explicit baraita concerning "you shall fulfill." Therefore the primary law follows Taz, though the Sema had a significant authority to rely upon. There remains an underlying question: why should a charity vow differ from ordinary charity? If ordinary charity is a positive mitzvah whose reward is written beside it, the same divine blessing should apply to the person who vowed. Does his prior vow somehow remove the reward? If the answer is that the vow also creates the prohibition of violating one's word and beit din coerces because of that prohibition, ordinary charity itself also has prohibitions such as, "Do not harden your heart and do not close your hand," yet some authorities still say it is not coerced. Perhaps the true distinction is that an oral pledge to the poor has the force of delivery according to those who say a statement to the poor is like delivery to an ordinary person. In that case the poor actually acquire a property right and collection follows naturally. But according to those who deny such acquisition and treat the statement only as a vow, the question remains. Tumim attempts to explain Rif's commentary to the Mishnah concerning greater stringency of vows over oaths by applying it to a charity vow made if one fails to litigate before a gentile court. He builds this on Rabbeinu Tam and Rosh concerning one who says, "Sleep is forbidden to me for three days if I do such and such," which is ineffective because the body of the vow, three days without sleep, is impossible and therefore a vain vow. Tumim extends this to the reverse structure, saying that if he says, "If I sleep within three days this object shall be forbidden," it too should be void because the condition refers to impossible sleep. This analogy is incorrect. In Rabbeinu Tam's case the body of the prohibition itself is impossible to observe. Where the prohibition is entirely possible and only the condition refers to an impossible or inevitable occurrence, the vow can take effect unless the condition is clearly stated as exaggeration. Thus Tumim's construction does not resolve Rif.
