One who accepted by kinyan to litigate, etc. This refers specifically to accepting foreign adjudication. Acceptance of a disqualified witness can be effective according to all views, as explained in siman 22, seif 2.
It is nothing and is prohibited, etc. Responsa Rosh, cited by the Tur, says that even if a contract provides for litigation under non-Jewish law, the clause does not mean that the debtor cannot invoke Shemitah or other Torah law. Its purpose is that if he is violent and refuses Jewish jurisdiction he may be compelled through foreign courts, not that the parties intended to violate Torah law. This seems to apply where the document generally says they will litigate there. If it explicitly says he obligates himself to accept their law in all circumstances and the creditor has a concrete monetary advantage there, Rif and Baal HaTerumot indicate that the kinyan may attach to the debtor's property and create a monetary obligation, not to the abstract promise to litigate before the foreign court. I found no authority clearly disagreeing. Ran's general statement that acceptance of foreign adjudication is ineffective can be understood where no additional monetary right exists under that system. The Mechaber's unqualified ruling here and in siman 22 therefore requires study.
And he is obligated to give, etc. A vow can take effect in such a circumstance, and by undertaking a mitzvah obligation he can become bound to give what he accepted.
Some say beit din does not, etc. Baal HaTerumot cites this in the name of Rif, and the Tur brings it. I found no opinion that expressly says beit din forcibly extracts it. Perhaps this relates to the rule that with a positive commandment whose reward is written alongside it, beit din is not commanded to punish, though Ramban explains that beit din may still choose to enforce it. All the more so where the person accepted an obligation in the form of a vow. More likely, however, the Mechaber sometimes writes 'some say' even when there is no known dispute.
