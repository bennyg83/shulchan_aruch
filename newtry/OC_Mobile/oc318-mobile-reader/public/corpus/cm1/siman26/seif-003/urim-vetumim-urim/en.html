One who accepts, etc. Sema understands that if the parties explicitly agreed to be judged according to secular law, and the agreement cannot be interpreted in another way, a formal kinyan can obligate the monetary result where that law gives one party rights beyond Torah law. Without a kinyan, a mere condition is ineffective because it attempts to override Torah law. In Tumim, based on Beit Yosef's text of Baal HaTerumot, I argue that no kinyan can authorize actual litigation before secular courts. At most, where the wording necessarily refers to a monetary right available under secular law, a Jewish beit din can interpret the parties' agreement and award that right itself. Heaven forbid that the agreement should enhance the standing of idolatrous courts. Even according to Tur's version, there is reason to distinguish. If such a condition was made at the time of the loan, the kinyan can attach because it is not certain that the condition will ever be used; perhaps the debtor will voluntarily pay the additional benefit. But if the parties make the agreement only after a dispute has already arisen, its purpose is plainly to bypass Torah law, and the condition has no force. This explains why the Shulchan Aruch states broadly that even where both parties consent to gentile judgment it is prohibited and warrants excommunication.
He must give, etc. Although the undertaking was connected with conduct forbidden by Torah law, a vow can nevertheless take effect in this form. Sema. See Tumim for the reconciliation of Rif's explanation of the Mishnah in Nedarim.
Beit din does not extract it from him. Sema explains this by the rule that beit din does not coerce fulfillment of a positive command whose reward is stated alongside it. Bach says instead that the person may claim that he will give the charity to other poor people at a later time. In Tumim I show that neither explanation fully accounts for the law. A more plausible basis is asmachta. Rif holds that even a pledge to charity may be ineffective where it was made merely as an asmachta. Therefore the matter depends upon the person's actual intention. If the undertaking was a serious charitable commitment, it should be fulfilled; if it was merely an exaggerated deterrent premised on the assumption that he would never violate the condition, it can be asmachta. Beit din cannot readily know the thoughts of his heart and therefore does not extract the money. The matter remains for his conscience.
