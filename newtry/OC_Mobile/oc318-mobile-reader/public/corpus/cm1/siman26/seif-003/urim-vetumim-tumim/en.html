It is of no effect, etc. Sema, seif katan 11, reads Rosh's responsum as follows. Rosh writes that even though the document said the parties would litigate under non-Jewish law, the language could be interpreted as meaning only that if the debtor became violent or refused Jewish judgment, the creditor could use secular enforcement to compel him to submit to Torah law. Rosh therefore avoided interpreting the document as an absolute agreement to violate Torah law. Sema infers that where the document explicitly says that the parties are unconditionally obligated to litigate before non-Jewish judges, and the creditor would gain substantive rights there that he would not have under Torah law, a kinyan could make that agreement effective. According to Sema, Mechaber's unqualified ruling would then be difficult. I do not understand this. If Sema admits that an agreement to litigate before non-Jewish courts is a condition to violate Torah law and is therefore void, what can a kinyan add? We have never heard that a kinyan validates a condition that is invalid because it contradicts Torah law. Bava Metzia 94 proves the opposite. The Gemara asks how a shomer chinam can stipulate to alter his Torah obligations, since that is a condition contrary to what is written in Torah. It does not answer that the Mishnah can be understood according to Shmuel where a kinyan was made. If kinyan cured the problem of conditioning against Torah law, the Gemara would have distinguished the Amoraic views accordingly. Likewise Rambam in Hilchot Ishut, as explained in his responsum, maintains the rule that a condition contrary to Torah is void even where a kinyan exists. Thus there is no general distinction between with and without kinyan for this purpose. Sema's conclusion therefore requires study. Still, Sema's textual observation about Rosh is correct. Rosh repeatedly labors to interpret the disputed contractual wording in a way that avoids saying the parties truly intended to accept non-Jewish adjudication. This implies that if the language absolutely and unmistakably expressed such an intention, Rosh would recognize some legal consequence. Gidulei Terumah, Shaar 61, says so explicitly. But what consequence? The better explanation is not that the secular forum itself becomes permitted. Rather, the parties may have created a valid monetary condition concerning the substantive result. For example, if at the time of the original contract the debtor agreed that even after Shemitah he would still pay because that is how the secular commercial system treats the obligation, or if a deposit arrangement allocated risk according to a particular secular rule, that can be a monetary condition between the parties. A Jewish beit din can enforce such a private condition where halachically valid. There is no honor given to non-Jewish courts. The beit din simply enforces the parties' own financial undertaking. Rosh therefore seeks to determine whether the contract actually created such a substantive obligation or merely used secular language for enforcement. But it does not follow that the parties can authorize themselves to go before the foreign judges. This explains why Mechaber rules without qualification that a kinyan to litigate there is ineffective. There is another way to reconcile Tur's text. Tur apparently had a version of Baal HaTerumot implying that where the agreement to follow secular law was embedded in the original contract, the condition might carry legal force. The printed Baal HaTerumot before us says the opposite, and Beit Yosef notes the difference. Even according to Tur's version, a distinction can be made. If the condition was written from the outset when the debt instrument itself was created, the parties may have intended merely to define their substantive monetary obligations according to a commercial standard. For example: "Even if Shemitah passes, I will pay you," or "I accept liability for this deposit under the rule used by the kingdom." Such a condition can be performed voluntarily and enforced by a Jewish court. It does not inherently require secular litigation. If later, after a dispute already arose, the parties make a kinyan specifically that non-Jewish judges shall decide between them, that agreement is directly an undertaking to violate Torah law and is void. Thus even Tur's version can be explained consistently. Mechaber in siman 22 and here speaks of an agreement made in the context of an existing dispute to submit the case itself to non-Jewish adjudication. He properly rules that it is entirely ineffective. This is the cleanest resolution. And according to the printed Baal HaTerumot, which Beit Yosef follows, even less remains of Sema's difficulty. The kinyan and condition are simply void without further distinction.
Some say that beit din does not extract it, etc. Beit Yosef already writes in Bedek HaBayit that the reason for this rule is unclear. Sema suggests that the obligation is merely a positive mitzvah whose reward is stated alongside it, and Jewish beit din does not compel such a mitzvah. This does not appear correct. First, beit din does compel charity. The Gemara says that property can be seized for charity. Second, even if ordinary voluntary charity were treated differently, a person who vows to give charity creates an actual obligation. Scripture treats "what leaves your lips" as including charity, and where poor people are present, every delay in payment can violate bal te'acher, as stated in Rosh Hashanah and Yoreh Deah siman 257. Beit din certainly has authority to prevent a person from continuously violating such a prohibition. This case is even further removed from an ordinary reward-bearing mitzvah, because the person did not undertake the payment merely out of compassion for the poor. He used charity as a penalty or undertaking connected to his commitment not to litigate before non-Jewish courts. Sema's explanation is therefore difficult. Bach says the person can answer, "I will give the money to poor people in another city whenever I wish." That too is not correct. If he did not designate particular poor people, then the first eligible poor people who come to collect become the immediate recipients of his obligation. If he refuses while they are present, bal te'acher applies. There is no unlimited right to postpone indefinitely or redirect at whim. Bedek HaBayit's question is therefore strong. At first I thought Rif's phrase "beit din does not extract from him" might mean only that the court does not descend directly upon his property. In an ordinary vow, one could say that the assets themselves are not subject to a Torah lien, and since the enactments concerning creditor collection do not necessarily apply to such a charitable obligation, the court might compel the person personally without issuing an adrachta against his property. This would explain why Rif says "does not extract" rather than "does not compel." But I retracted this. The verse "and you shall perform" is interpreted as an instruction to beit din to compel performance in the laws of offerings, and charity is included in the same broad category of verbal obligations. Those who delay bringing sacrifices can have property seized from them, as stated in Shekalim and Rambam. It is therefore difficult to say charitable vows are categorically beyond property enforcement. Moreover, Rif concludes that the court merely informs him of the obligation. If personal coercion or nidui were proper, Rif should have said so. The best explanation is different. Rif follows authorities who hold that asmachta applies even to charity, unlike the ruling adopted in Yoreh Deah siman 258 and below in Choshen Mishpat siman 207. Here the undertaking is naturally structured as an asmachta. The person said, in effect: "If I refrain from going to the secular court, I will give such-and-such amount to charity." His actual expectation may have been that he would go to the secular court and would never have to pay the charitable amount. He did not necessarily intend an absolute present obligation. If so, the commitment may be an unenforceable asmachta. Everything depends on his true intention. If at the time of the undertaking he genuinely intended to make an unconditional charitable vow and used the condition merely as a framework or as reassurance to the plaintiff, then the vow is real and he must pay. But if his genuine intent was only to create a penalty that he never expected to incur, then the obligation can fall under asmachta. Because this is a matter entrusted to the heart and beit din cannot know his original intention with certainty, the court does not compel payment. Instead, beit din informs him that if in truth he intended a binding vow, he is obligated before Heaven to give the money. This explains Rif's exact wording that they tell him to give "of your own intention" what he imposed upon himself. The phrase means: examine what your intention actually was when you undertook it. If you intended a true charitable vow, the obligation rests upon you. This also explains why Mechaber presents the rule as "some say." Our general halachah below, siman 207, is that charitable commitments are not ordinarily invalidated by asmachta. Accordingly, Rif's rule is not universally accepted. One might still distinguish this case because the undertaking was made in a context of pressure or compulsion, but at minimum the doubt justifies the qualified presentation. Rif then connects this to the Mishnah in Nedarim: "Konam that I sit in a sukkah," where a neder can take effect even though a shevuah to violate a mitzvah cannot. Rif's explanation is initially difficult. According to the usual reading of the Mishnah, the person directly forbids sitting in the sukkah upon himself through a neder. But Rif appears to compare it to our case, where the person says that if he does or does not perform an act, a separate sacrificial or charitable obligation will arise. If so, why should neder differ from shevuah? A person could also swear, "If I sit in the sukkah, I will bring an offering." He would still sit and merely incur the secondary obligation. The Gemara itself explains the Mishnah differently: a neder attaches to the object, while a shevuah is a personal prohibition. Rif's comparison therefore needs explanation. Rambam, in his Commentary to the Mishnah and in Hilchot Nedarim, writes that one who says, "Konam that sitting in the sukkah is upon me," becomes prohibited, and he also writes that if one says, "If I sit in the sukkah, an offering shall be upon me," he sits and brings the offering. Tosafot Yom Tov asks why Rambam mentions the latter formulation in connection with the Mishnah, since a similar conditional undertaking could seemingly exist with a shevuah. The answer may lie in a principle of Rabbeinu Tam and Rosh. A person who makes a neder dependent upon an impossible or inherently false condition can fail to create a meaningful neder. For example, formulations involving sleeping three days continuously can be classified as impossible nonsense rather than a valid conditional undertaking. By analogy, if a shevuah is explicitly attached to violating a mitzvah, the very condition may be structurally invalid because the person cannot create a binding shevuah to negate Torah. With a neder, however, the mitzvah-act itself can be made the object of a neder. A person can say, "Konam is the sitting in the sukkah upon me," because neder can prohibit the object of the mitzvah. Therefore, when he says, "If I sit in the sukkah, an offering is upon me," the condition is not anchored in an inherently impossible legal framework. There exists a form of neder that can directly affect sitting in the sukkah. The conditional neder can therefore take effect. A shevuah does not have that structure. Since a shevuah cannot prohibit the mitzvah-act itself, a conditional formulation built on violating the mitzvah lacks the same legal foundation. This explains why the Gemara distinguishes neder from shevuah and why Rambam mentions the conditional offering specifically with neder. It also illuminates Rif's comparison to our case. The person linked his charitable obligation to conduct involving a prohibited secular forum. The fact that the condition touches an improper act does not automatically prevent the neder from taking effect, just as a neder can be structured around an act involving a mitzvah. The real question is whether the undertaking was intended as a genuine vow or merely as an asmachta. If genuine, the charity obligation can be binding. If merely a penalty never seriously accepted, beit din cannot compel it under Rif's approach. This reading harmonizes Rif and Rambam. I would have been fully comfortable with this interpretation had I found even a hint of it in an earlier commentator. It appears correct, but I hesitate to create a wholly new reading without precedent.
