See also Responsa Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 3, who writes that the intent of Rav Sherira's statement that the non Jewish courts must be courts that do not accept bribes is as follows. We rule that a Torah scholar is not obligated to testify before a beit din of lesser stature than himself, as stated in siman 28, seif 5, and see the end of siman 263. One might therefore have thought that a Torah scholar is certainly forbidden to demean himself by testifying before a non Jewish court, in a place where he is not obligated to do so. Rav Sherira therefore teaches the opposite: it is an honor and glory for the judges of Israel, who ruled that a certain person is liable but lack the power to rescue the oppressed party, to go and testify before the non Jewish court that this person was found liable before them for a certain amount. Rav Sherira conditioned this permission on the court not accepting bribes, so that it will enforce the judgment as the Jewish judges ruled. If, however, they accept bribes, then after Torah scholars have demeaned themselves by testifying in a setting beneath their dignity, the outcome may still be corrupted. In such a case, the effort may be for nothing, and they have no right to waive the honor of Torah on the basis of uncertainty. See there further concerning a deathbed will in which a dying person declared before witnesses that he owed a certain person a specified sum. After his death, he left minor orphans. The creditor feared that because he could not collect until the orphans reached majority, the witnesses might forget what the deceased had instructed before them. Formally recording their testimony through beit din was difficult, because the beit din was concerned for the latter opinion in siman 110, seif 9, that even testimony concerning a will should not be accepted by beit din while there are minor orphans. The creditor therefore wished to have the witnesses' statements recorded in writing in the official protocol of a non Jewish court, so that they would be preserved until the orphans reached majority. The question was whether this is permitted, and whether doing so gives them the status of judges. Chatam Sofer discusses at length the sugya in Gittin 44a concerning one who sells his house to a non Jew, and concludes: In our case, the non Jewish court is not being made into judges at all. One is merely depositing the information with them so that, when needed, it can later be produced and brought before Jewish judges. I therefore see no trace of prohibition in this, even without permission from beit din, and certainly with such permission. When the orphans grow up and the matter comes before a valid beit din, whether the witnesses may retract or alter what they previously said, since the first statement was not before beit din, or whether, if they maintain their first statement, they are believed because people tend to maintain their earlier falsehoods, is not a question I decide here. The judge who will serve at that time will bring a just judgment to light. Nevertheless, recording their statement in the non Jewish court will be useful, because it is very likely that when the orphans grow up they will believe the record, will not contest it, and will pay voluntarily, similar to Rashi's wording in Avodah Zarah 13b.
