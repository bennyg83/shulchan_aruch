The refuse of the courtyard, etc., but lodging troops, etc. The entire passage is the Rambam's language. In the Perishah and Derishah I demonstrated from the contrast, "but lodging troops," that in his view the term refers to the expenses of providing provisions for those lodged there. The contrast is then precise. The "refuse" at the beginning means the expense of removing refuse from the courtyard, where the residents do not wish it left and trampled into a manure heap for their fields. That expense is assessed according to entrances, because the refuse is thrown out through the entrances. The expense of feeding lodged troops, by contrast, is not assessed according to entrances but according to the people residing there. I brought several proofs that this is how the Rambam and Tur understand the matter. Others explain "lodging troops" as the refuse left behind by royal troops when they depart from their encampment. The Rif and Rosh explain that this includes not only animal manure but also earthenware jars and their lees. Such items are divided according to the number of residents. According to that explanation, the refuse at the beginning likewise means refuse that has already become a manure heap useful for fertilizing fields, and it is divided according to entrances, while the manure and jars left by troops are divided according to residents. Either way, the second clause contrasts with the first. Rama's gloss can be understood accordingly. His first explanation of "the refuse left in the courtyard" follows the second interpretation, meaning the manure heap left by the troops after their stay. His later "some say that lodging troops means what must be provided to them" refers to the expense of their food. Under that interpretation the initial refuse means the expense of removing ordinary refuse from the courtyard. Both readings maintain the contrast introduced by the word "but." This excludes the Ir Shushan's explanation, which treats the opening clause as manure for fields and the latter clause as an assessment imposed by the king, without preserving the contrast. Perhaps for that reason he omitted the word "but," but that is not correct.
