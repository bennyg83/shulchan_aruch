Lodging is assessed according to the people. See Tzemach Tzedek, siman 19. He explains that this does not mean that the poor and wealthy pay equal amounts. Since ordinary military billeting does not involve danger to life, the expense is assessed according to wealth. The phrase "according to the people" excludes assessment according to doorways; it means according to the householders and their means. Just as the expenses of lodging troops are apportioned according to wealth, so too any income or benefit generated by them, such as manure or discarded vessels, is apportioned on the same basis. The wording "according to the people," rather than "according to the souls," supports this understanding. Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 10, explains that this interpretation applies according to those who understand the obligation as the monetary cost of military lodging. According to the view that each householder must actually provide quarters in his home rather than contribute money, "according to the people" may simply mean according to householders, and Tzemach Tzedek would agree. See what I wrote above in siman 163:3. Nachalat Shivah, part II, siman 10, discusses cases in which some Jews receive two or three soldiers while others receive one or none. He distinguishes how the soldiers were assigned. If they were billeted directly by the commanding officer, the burden falls upon the household that received them. If the municipal officials distributed quarters according to official allotments, that distribution is binding even if it does not track the community's usual tax assessment. If municipal officials assigned soldiers to some Jews arbitrarily and not pursuant to fixed allotments, those who received none must contribute to those who bore the lodging burden, provided the exempt householders are not separately required to pay the municipality for their exemption. If the officials allot a stated total number of soldiers to the Jewish community, then the Jewish householders distribute the burden among themselves according to financial assessment. His wording is difficult in places and his exact conclusion is not entirely clear. A responsum of the Bach, siman 153, cited earlier, rules that when soldiers travel from town to town and lodge in some Jewish homes but not others, all contribute according to wealth where the soldiers take by force beyond lawful governmental rules. Where the burden is imposed by valid law of the kingdom, the governing law determines the allocation. A later authority adds that if the communal shamash himself assigned quarters, the other neighbors must contribute to the legitimate burden, though they need not compensate a householder for excess amounts unlawfully extorted beyond the soldiers' entitlement.
