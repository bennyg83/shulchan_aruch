Billeting is assessed according to the people. See Responsa Tzemach Tzedek, siman 19, who writes that this does not mean, as one might initially suppose, that the charge is collected equally from everyone, poor and rich alike. That certainly is not so, for since billeting does not involve danger to life, the charge is collected only according to wealth. Rather, the phrase "according to the people" excludes assessment according to doorways; it does not mean that all pay equally, but that the wealthy person pays according to his wealth, and so forth. Just as the expense of billeting is collected according to wealth, so too the income derived from the soldiers, namely the manure and empty casks, is divided according to wealth. It appears that the wording is precise, for the Mishnah says "according to the people" and does not say "according to the souls." See there. See also Responsa Shav Yaakov, Choshen Mishpat, siman 10, who explains his words. He writes that the Tzemach Tzedek's forced interpretation, that "according to the people" means according to wealth, is needed only according to those authorities who explain billeting as the collection of the expenses of lodging, rather than an obligation to provide lodging in one's own house. According to those who explain that each person must provide actual lodging, and no money is paid, but the soldiers themselves are quartered in his house, there is no need for that forced interpretation. "According to the people" then means according to the householders, and the Tzemach Tzedek himself agrees to this. See what I wrote above, siman 163, seif 3, second gloss, seif katan 7. See also Responsa Nachalat Shivah, part 2, siman 10, concerning whether one must provide actual lodging in his house. At times one person has two or three soldiers quartered with him, while another has only one, or none at all, and the one who has soldiers demands that the one who has none contribute toward his burden. He writes that he ruled as appeared proper to him, sometimes on the basis of proof and sometimes by assessment: if the soldiers were quartered with Jews through the official billeting authority, the loss falls upon the person to whom they were assigned. Likewise, if the townsmen distributed them among themselves by billets and assigned billets to the Jews as well, their distribution is legally effective even if they did not divide according to the community's tax assessment. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 345, likewise ruled in his case. But where the townsmen gave soldiers to some Jews and not to others, meaning not by billets but according to their own choice, then those who were not burdened must contribute to those who were. I do not know why he does not say that even in this case the loss simply fell upon the one to whom the soldiers were assigned. This applies only if those without soldiers are thereafter exempt from paying the townsmen. If they must later pay money to the townsmen because no soldiers were quartered with them, they are exempt from contributing to those who had soldiers. If the municipal body or the quartermaster assigns to the Jews a total number of soldiers corresponding to the Jewish portion, then the Jews divide that burden among themselves according to wealth. This is not consistent with the Shav Yaakov and Tzemach Tzedek cited above. See there further. His language is somewhat confused, and it is difficult to determine his precise view. Concerning whether one may exert influence with the billeting official in this matter, see below, siman 388, seif 2, in the gloss, and what I wrote there. In Responsa Bach, siman 153, cited by Misgeret HaShulchan and Atzei Levonah above, siman 163, seif katan 13, he writes: If soldiers travel from city to city and are quartered in some houses but not others, everyone must contribute to those houses, and the assessment is according to wealth. This applies when the soldiers take by plunder and robbery contrary to the law of the kingdom, and the residents are compelled to give whatever the soldiers demand. But what they take pursuant to the law of the kingdom is governed by dina d'malchuta dina, and one must determine what the king's law is; whatever that law requires stands. Atzei Levonah comments that in his view there is a distinction: if the community attendant assigned the billeting, the other neighbors must contribute, but if the soldiers themselves extort more than what the law allows, the others are exempt from contributing toward that excess.
