Rabbeinu wrote at the end of the siman: It appears necessary to distinguish. His meaning is as follows. He was troubled by the Rosh's statement, cited by the Beit Yosef, that the courtyard must contain six units of four by four cubits. If the Rosh speaks of a case involving loss, such as ownerless property where the owner of two entrances acquired eight cubits, it is indeed true that he cannot be compelled to divide. Since the Rosh follows the Geonim that only in ownerless property does each entrance receive four cubits, whereas this is not so in inheritance or purchase, the Rosh could not have stated that five units of four by four suffice. In ownerless property, five such units are genuinely required, since the owner of two entrances may not be deprived. The Rosh must therefore be speaking of inheritance or purchase. There, while the courtyard is jointly owned, the owner of two entrances has no advantage over the owner of one entrance. Nevertheless, one might think that he can say, “Until now we used the courtyard jointly, but if you divide, you will certainly divide equally, while I need four cubits for each entrance.” He might therefore seek to prevent division. The Rosh teaches that he is not heard, since he suffers no loss. Four cubits for one entrance, plus another four cubits in the courtyard, suffice. The same applies to ownerless property where the entrances are equal, for there too one may compel division. By contrast, where the entrances are unequal in ownerless property, division may be prevented. This explains Rabbeinu's distinction. The Rosh's case of unequal entrances concerns inheritance or purchase, where no actual loss exists. His essential point is that where division causes loss, there can be no division unless the loss is avoided. Thus, in ownerless property where one owner has two entrances and the other one entrance, he cannot be compelled to divide until there are five units of four by four cubits. One might otherwise have thought that in inheritance or purchase, since each entrance ordinarily requires four cubits, an owner with two entrances needs eight cubits for his entrances, and since the other must receive an equal amount, the whole courtyard would need six units of four by four cubits. The same thought might arise in ownerless property where the entrances are equal, because there too there is no loss. This is incorrect. Since division causes him no loss, he may be compelled once there are four cubits for one entrance and an additional four cubits in the courtyard. Rabbi Yosef Halevi speaks of this case. There is therefore no disagreement at all. Rabbeinu merely explains Rabbi Yosef Halevi and the Rosh, unlike the Beit Yosef, who raised two objections. Each objection is resolved by the other according to the explanation given here.
