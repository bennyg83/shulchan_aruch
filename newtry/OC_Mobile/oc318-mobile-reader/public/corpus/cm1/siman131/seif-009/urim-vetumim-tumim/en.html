Some have written, etc., Levi did not become obligated, etc. The Beit Yosef explains why this is an asmachta and not like an ordinary guarantor for a loan. A loan is given for expenditure, and from the outset the borrower enters the transaction expecting repayment. With a guarantor for title to land, however, when he sees no challenge to the field at the time of sale, he relies on the assumption that the purchaser will never lose it, and his undertaking is therefore an asmachta. Gidulei Terumah, gate 35, part 1, law 5, asks why ordinary guaranty differs, for the guarantor there likewise enters believing the borrower is wealthy and will pay from his own resources. In truth, there is no difficulty. In ordinary guaranty there is only one uncertainty, whether the borrower will pay or become impoverished so that the creditor turns to the guarantor. With a field there are two uncertainties: perhaps no challenge will ever arise against the title, and even if one does arise, perhaps the seller will have sufficient assets to reimburse the purchaser. In the face of this compounded uncertainty, the guarantor does not truly commit himself. Yet if so, a difficulty remains. Why do we say that a guarantor for a ketubah does not become obligated because the woman suffered no monetary loss, or because he performs a mitzvah, rather than simply saying that there too are two uncertainties: perhaps she will die during the husband's lifetime and never collect, and even if she survives him or is divorced, perhaps the husband will have sufficient assets to pay the ketubah. It is difficult to distinguish.
