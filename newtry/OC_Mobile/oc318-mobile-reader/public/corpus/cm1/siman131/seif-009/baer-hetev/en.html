Field. The Tur explains that with an ordinary guarantor we say that because of the benefit of the creditor's reliance, the guarantor resolves to obligate himself. Here that rationale does not apply, because the purchaser did not buy the field in reliance on the guarantor, since a person may buy land even for a single day. The Taz notes that Bava Metzia rules against Shmuel's use of this principle in another context. One must therefore distinguish: with respect to the seller's own warranty to the buyer we do not say that a person buys land for one day, but with respect to a third-party guarantor we do. This distinction requires study. If, however, a kinyan was made from the guarantor that he would pay the purchase money whenever demanded, meaning whenever the buyer wishes to rescind even without any claimant challenging title, this shows that the essential purpose of the guarantee was to secure the buyer's money. He is then liable like any other guarantor. For this reason the Tur and Mechaber say, “If a kinyan was made from him that he would pay the purchase money,” rather than merely the value of the field under ordinary warranty law. Sema.
And he becomes obligated. The reason is that the Gemara teaches that although every guarantee contains an element of asmachta, a guarantor at the time the money is given nevertheless becomes obligated without a kinyan because he fully resolves to bind himself. Once this principle was established, the law did not distinguish among different forms of asmachta. This is the basis of the various opinions Rama cites afterward. Sema.
