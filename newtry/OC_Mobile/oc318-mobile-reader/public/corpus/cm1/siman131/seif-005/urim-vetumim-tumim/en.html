For you already saw my refusal, etc.; the law is with the guarantor, since the creditor should have specified the terms of his guaranty, etc. The Taz holds that even if the creditor did not previously see his refusal, but the guarantor merely said, I remain as I was, this wording does not create a new obligation. He may explain that he meant he would remain guarantor under the prior circumstances, when the creditor still held the collateral. This is correct. We must then explain why the Tur and Mechaber elaborate that otherwise perhaps he would be called wicked for speaking ambiguously in a manner that causes another Jew loss against a non Jew. The second Jew might not have examined his words so carefully, and perhaps this amounts to actionable causation. In the present case, however, the second Jew should in any event have understood that something was amiss. This also explains Maharik, responsum 133, who infers from this responsum of the Rif that with any doubtful language of guaranty the burden of proof rests upon the claimant. If the ruling applied only where the guarantor had initially refused, there would be no proof regarding other doubtful formulations, for here there are special indications that he intended merely to put the creditor off with words. Rather, Maharik considered even here that there was reason to estimate that he intended a full guaranty, since he plainly saw that the creditor would not return the property except upon his guaranty.
