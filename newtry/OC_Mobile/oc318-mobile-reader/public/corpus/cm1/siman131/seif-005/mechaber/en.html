A Jew became guarantor to his fellow Jew on behalf of a non-Jew. The guarantor took collateral from the non-Jew and gave it to the lender. At twilight before Shabbat, the non-Jew came to the guarantor and said, 'Become guarantor for me to this lender for what I owe him, so that he will return my collateral, which I need.' The guarantor replied, 'Shabbat has already begun, and I will undertake no guarantee.' The non-Jew continued to persuade him until the guarantor told the lender, 'I am as I was before,' and the lender returned the collateral to the non-Jew. When the lender later sued the guarantor, the guarantor claimed, 'When I said, I am as I was before, I intended only my status after the collateral had come into your possession. My earlier refusal to guarantee for the non-Jew, which I attributed to the prohibition of Shabbat, proves this.' The law follows the guarantor, because the lender should have clarified the meaning of his guarantee. Nevertheless, the guarantor must assist the lender in collecting the debt from the non-Jew.
