For there are those who disagree with this. See the Sema, Shach, and Taz, who explain who these dissenters are. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi, siman 51, who writes that a party in possession may say that he relies upon the dissenting view. Responsa Ra'anach, part 1, siman 60, says the same, and Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 2, concludes likewise as practical halachah. All this concerns an ordinary guarantor. With a kablan, however, since the creditor may collect first from whichever party he wishes, the kablan is like the borrower himself, and everyone agrees that even if the creditor was warned to collect at the due date and did not do so, the kablan remains liable. However, the customary wording in engagement agreements, "guarantor and kablan on behalf of the groom, so and so, and on behalf of the bride, so and so," does not give those persons the law of a true kablan but only of ordinary guarantors. Therefore, if the parties to the engagement miss the appointed date, these guarantors are not automatically obligated to pay the penalty. Even according to the Rosh and those who follow him, cited in the Rama, siman 129, seif 18, that a document calling someone a kablan is understood to use legally effective language, engagement guarantees are different. We are witnesses from daily practice that when engagement agreements are written, the scribes simply ask, "Who are the guarantors?" and the parties provide names. We never hear the precise formula required to create a true kablan. This is especially so according to the Sema and Shach, who distinguish engagement guarantees from other forms of guaranty. Chacham Tzvi further writes that according to the dissenting authorities, in his view the guarantor need not actually warn the creditor to collect at the due date in order to become exempt. Since they understand the guarantor's basic obligation as extending only until the stated time, and the obligation is bound to that date in such a way that he may demand that the creditor collect then, it follows that the guarantor is exempt even without warning. He was never obligated beyond that date. The burden rests on the creditor to notify the guarantor if he wishes the guaranty extended and to obtain his agreement. Perhaps even express verbal consent is insufficient and a new kinyan is required, like a guaranty undertaken after the loan was already made, because the original lien accepted at the beginning has already expired. If the original obligation truly continued after the stated date, how could the guarantor compel the creditor to collect precisely then? He writes that this is also evident from the Ra'anach. See there. Knesset HaGedolah, Hagahot Beit Yosef, letter 5, however, cites Maharshach as holding that even according to the dissenters the guarantor is exempt only if he actually warned the creditor to demand payment and the creditor then extended the time. If no warning was given, the guaranty continues. This also appears from the Vilna Gaon's explanation, seif katan 5. Nevertheless, it appears to me that even in such a case money should not be extracted from the guarantor who is in possession. Although this creates a double doubt involving two disputes, even a double doubt does not allow extraction from a party in possession, as I wrote in Pitchei Teshuva, Even HaEzer, siman 57, seif katan 7. This requires study.
