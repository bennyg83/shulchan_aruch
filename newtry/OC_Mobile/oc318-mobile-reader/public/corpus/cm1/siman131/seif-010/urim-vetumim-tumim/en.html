Or a guarantor or kablan who obligated themselves conditionally. It appears that this limitation applies specifically where the borrower obligated himself unconditionally but the guarantor entered the guarantee only on a condition. If the borrower himself undertook an obligation on a condition, while the guarantor simply guaranteed whatever amount the borrower would ultimately be legally obligated to pay, then the guarantor is bound. The proof is the guarantor of a ketubah. The husband is not obligated to pay the ketubah unless the condition occurs, namely that he dies during the wife's life or divorces her. Yet the guarantor can still become bound. Necessarily, when the condition defines the principal obligation and the guarantee itself is unconditional, everyone agrees that the guarantee can attach. Accordingly, this ruling does not necessarily contradict what was written above in siman 129, seif 8, in the Rama's gloss citing Rashba. The Rama there referred the reader to the end of siman 131, seif 12, implying that he saw the matter as connected to the Rambam and Raavad dispute. How that implication fits the case of a ketubah still requires explanation, though the distinction just stated goes far toward resolving it. See the Sema, seif katan 24.
And some say there is no asmachta in guaranty. This refers to the latter case, where the guarantor expressly stipulated that if he does not produce the debtor he will pay. In the first case, everyone agrees he is exempt, because he never obligated himself to pay if he failed to produce the debtor. Raavad suggests that perhaps beit din may take "a life for a life" and pressure the guarantor until a compromise is reached. Maggid Mishneh already writes that these words are difficult to understand. Perhaps Raavad means that just as a recalcitrant debtor can be placed under a ban, the guarantor can also be put under social pressure until a settlement is reached. Even this explanation is strained.
