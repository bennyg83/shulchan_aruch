For there are those who disagree, etc. See Sema seif katan 7, who understands the ruling of Mahari Weil cited by the Rama, that if the guarantor sent word to the creditor, Send me the document and come receive your money, and the creditor failed to come, the guarantor is discharged, as disagreeing with Ramban's rule stated by the Mechaber. The Sema understands Mahari Weil to mean that even if the borrower did not actually have the money ready, but merely said, Give me the document so I may collect the debt, the guarantor is nevertheless discharged. I hear this but do not understand it. Where does the Sema see this in Mahari Weil? Mahari Weil explicitly requires the guarantor to swear that he knew clearly that the borrower was ready and willing to pay the creditor and that only the creditor's delay prevented payment. Thus the borrower must have been standing ready to pay. Otherwise the guarantor is not discharged. Mahari Weil required this oath precisely because the guarantor's exemption depends upon that fact. This plainly differs from the Sema's understanding. Yet the substantive ruling of Mahari Weil, which the Rama cites without dissent and which the Sema also accepts, itself requires serious study. Mahari Weil's entire basis for discharging the guarantor is that where the borrower wishes to pay and the creditor refuses to accept, the borrower is exempt from responsibility for the money, as Or Zarua writes in chapter HaGozel. Mahari Weil then says the guarantor is analogous to the borrower. Thus his ruling follows only the opinion of Or Zarua that when the debtor says, Take your money, he is discharged, which is the first view cited by the Mechaber above in siman 120 seif 2. But the Rama there cites dissenters, and in siman 74 seif 3 rules without qualification that the debtor remains liable. See Sema, Shach, and what I wrote in siman 74. According to that opinion, the guarantor too should remain liable, for Mahari Weil derived his exemption only by analogy to the borrower, and there is no basis to distinguish them. The law therefore requires study. If the creditor already holds property of the guarantor, he can certainly plead kim li in accordance with the opinion imposing liability, unless we say that the Rama independently concluded that even though there are two opinions regarding the borrower, in the case of a guarantor everyone agrees that he is discharged. Since the Mechaber already writes that the law concerning a guarantor requires deliberation, and Baal HaTerumot calls the matter difficult in monetary law, perhaps in this circumstance all agree to exemption even though Mahari Weil analogized it to the borrower. Further study is required.
And the guarantor can, etc. He may compel him at the due date to release him from the guaranty. See Shach seif katan 6, who understands this to follow only the opinion that disagrees with Ramban concerning the law stated by the Mechaber. According to the Mechaber, who rules that the guarantor cannot compel the creditor, the Shach says he likewise cannot compel the borrower. This does not appear correct from the Rama's unqualified language. The truth is clear. Ramban indeed holds both that the guarantor cannot compel the creditor and that he cannot compel the borrower. The Tur, however, appears to distinguish the two. He holds that the guarantor may compel the borrower to release him, for Ramban has no Gemara proof on that point, and reason supports it because the borrower is servant to the lender. But concerning compelling the creditor, who is under no obligation to obey the guarantor, Ramban has proof from the rule that the creditor may collect from purchasers even if he delayed demanding payment for a long period until the borrower's free assets were lost. The Tur agrees with Ramban on that point, and we find no authority who disputes it. The Tur's wording likewise indicates that his disagreement with Ramban concerns only whether the guarantor may compel the borrower. This is also reasonable. If the Tur held that the guarantor could compel the creditor to demand payment from the borrower, why would he need to compel the borrower to release him from the guaranty? It would be enough to compel the creditor to sue and press the borrower; if the creditor refused, the guarantor would thereby be discharged. Necessarily, the Tur agrees with Ramban that the guarantor cannot compel the creditor. This also appears from the Perishah. Likewise, when the Raavad disputes Baal HaTerumot, gate 35, part 1, law 44, regarding a kablan, his dispute concerns only compelling the borrower. He holds that a kablan may compel the borrower, but we have not heard that he may compel the creditor. The Rama therefore properly states the rule without qualification, for all agree. Know further that from the Raavad's words we learn that the guarantor's ability at maturity to compel the borrower to release him applies while the borrower is alive. If the borrower died, even in a case where there is no concern that he left money for payment, such as where he admitted the debt, the guarantor cannot compel minor heirs. The Raavad explains the baraita stating that a loan document found in the possession of a guarantor does not permit collection from orphans as referring to a case where the guarantor demands, Release me from the guaranty, and he understands the case as one of a kablan. From the borrower himself the kablan could collect, meaning compel him to obtain his release, but not from minor heirs unless the kablan already paid and the creditor wrote to him, I have received, in which case he may collect where the debtor admitted the obligation, as explained in the Gemara. Although other decisors do not interpret the baraita this way, they may nevertheless agree with the Raavad as a matter of law. We find no one who expressly disputes him, and we should rule accordingly.
