A guarantor who came to the creditor, etc., the guarantor is not discharged thereby. The practice of taking a new kinyan from guarantors when the time of a marriage arrangement is extended cannot be explained merely as a concern that the guarantor may compel the bride's father either to proceed with the marriage or to release him from the guaranty, for the guarantor can indeed do this, as the Tur concludes and Rama likewise writes in the gloss. According to that explanation, if each side trusts that its own guarantor will continue guaranteeing the obligation and not pressure it, no new kinyan should be needed, yet that is not the practice. Perhaps the reason is that some authorities disagree with this rule, as Rama concludes, and the Mechaber himself writes that the matter requires consideration. It is also possible that collection of the penalty from the guarantor is not an ordinary monetary obligation but arises because of the embarrassment caused, and in such a case even Ramban may agree that the guarantor can say, 'I agreed to be your guarantor only until the time to which you consented, and no longer.'
And in the meantime the properties were blighted. The case of blight is stated; all the more so if the property was robbed from him, since violent men are liable to fall, as the Mechaber, from Rashba, writes in siman 111, seif 13; see there.
This law requires consideration, for some disagree. I have not found any authority who expressly disagrees. Darkei Moshe writes that Responsa Mahari Weil, siman 80, appears to disagree; see there where he cites his words. Although Rama concludes there that one can distinguish, that Mahari Weil speaks only where the borrower is ready to pay, but if he is not ready he agrees with Ramban, which is the ruling of the Mechaber here and as Rama concludes here in the gloss, Rama nevertheless writes here that there are dissenters because Mahari Weil further writes there that if the guarantor said to the creditor, 'Give me the promissory note so that I can sue for the debt,' and the creditor did not do so, the guarantor is exempt. The expression 'to sue for the debt' implies even where the money was not ready in the borrower's hand. Therefore Rama writes here that some disagree. What he writes here in the gloss, that if the guarantor says, 'Give me the promissory note so I may sue for the debt,' all agree the guarantor is exempt, must likewise concern a case where the guarantor says the borrower has the money ready to pay. He did not need to spell this out because it follows the immediately preceding discussion. That is strained. Independently, however, it appears to me that there is no dispute. Ramban speaks only where the guarantor does not wish to exert himself to collect from the borrower and pay the creditor, but merely tells the creditor, 'You go litigate with the borrower and collect from him.' There he rules that the guarantor cannot force the creditor to undertake litigation. Mahari Weil, by contrast, speaks where the guarantor himself is prepared to exert himself to collect the money and pay. This distinction appears clear.
And he cannot say, perhaps the non-Jew will remember, etc., and he trusted him, so he must continue to trust him. The Tur writes this below at the end of siman 183, and the Mechaber likewise writes it there in seif 5. Rama cites in his glosses a responsum of Maharam Mintz: Reuven guaranteed Shimon to a non-Jew to produce Shimon for judgment within eight days, so that Shimon could exempt himself by an oath or otherwise Reuven would pay ten zekukim for him. Shimon gave Reuven ten zekukim as collateral. The eight days passed and the non-Jew did not demand Shimon, and afterward Shimon died. His widow then claimed the ten zekukim from Reuven for her ketubah, while Reuven replied that perhaps the non-Jew would still bring a claim against him because of the guaranty. Maharam Mintz ruled that since Reuven had not yet suffered any loss, the eight days had passed without demand, and it was very likely that no loss would ever occur, why should the guarantor retain Shimon's money and defeat the widow's right because of a mere fear that the non-Jew may make an improper claim? The wording implies that even though Reuven did not trust Shimon to guarantee him without first receiving the ten zekukim as collateral, he nevertheless had to return them. One can distinguish that case. Even if the non-Jew later sued Reuven, the guarantor could prevail in law by saying: 'I guaranteed only that if you came within eight days and asked me to produce Shimon, I would do so. Since you did not ask within eight days, I am discharged.' That is proper both under their law and under Jewish law where the guaranty does not itself involve a definite monetary obligation, as explained above regarding marriage guaranties and in seif 12 regarding guaranty to produce a person. The only remaining concern is that the non-Jew might pressure him through improper tactics, and for such a possibility we are not concerned. Here, however, the discussion is of an ordinary guaranty to pay on another's behalf, and there is certainly reason for concern unless the guarantor already trusted the borrower; this is clear.
He must trust him even more. This applies specifically where the non-Jew forgot the matter and it is doubtful whether he will ever claim the money. It does not apply to an ordinary guarantor. Therefore the Tur and Mechaber immediately above write that a guarantor can compel the borrower to release him from the guaranty when the time arrives. Darkei Moshe writes the same. This is obvious, unlike what is written in Ir Shushan, whose presentation makes Rama's rulings contradict one another. In truth, Ir Shushan's own rulings are contradictory, for he himself rules likewise below at the end of siman 183.
