Through the note of the non Jew it is recognizable, etc. The borrower's duty to indemnify the guarantor for every loss imposed upon him by the non Jew because of the guaranty appears to apply only while the guaranty continues because the borrower has not paid. But if the borrower already paid, thereby terminating the guaranty, and the non Jew later maliciously makes a false claim against the guarantor, the guaranty has already ended and the ordinary rule returns that one person is not seized for another. This resolves the question of several authorities, including Panim Meirot, part 2, responsum 37, on the responsum of the Rosh. The Rosh writes that even though the borrower has a receipt from the non Jew, this does not help so long as he left the original document in the non Jew's hands. They ask why this should matter, since the borrower must indemnify the guarantor for all losses, and although he paid the debt, the guarantor was still forced to pay again. According to what I wrote, it is clear. Once the borrower paid, the guaranty and all its liens ended. The borrower is therefore no longer automatically obligated to indemnify the guarantor for a later malicious claim. The Rosh accordingly bases liability on the borrower's negligence in leaving the document with the non Jew. If he obtained a receipt and left without placing it in the guarantor's hands, what more could the guarantor have done? Nevertheless, it appears that the non Jew's note is sufficient proof of payment of the debt itself, for it is as though he wrote, I have received, and the borrower must reimburse the guarantor. Thus even a notation written between the lines allows collection although it is not in the non Jew's handwriting. There is no need for the Sema's explanation in seif katan 15 that since the loan document is in Shimon's possession one might otherwise suspect him of forging the notation. The very fact that the original document is in his possession creates a presumption that he paid, for why else would the non Jew have delivered the document to him? We therefore do not suspect forgery. But if the Jew claims that he paid more than the amount properly due, a writing from the non Jew certainly does not establish that excess. The Jew may have appeased him to write such a statement. Could one imagine extracting money on the testimony of a non Jew? Certainly not. Proper evidence from a reliable non Jewish court is required. This is clear and correct, and the Bach writes similarly, though his language implies that such formal evidence is necessary for every matter. That is not so. To prove payment of the principal debt itself, no such evidence is needed, for the creditor effectively wrote to the guarantor, I have received. But to establish an excess amount unlawfully extracted, reliable court evidence is plainly required.
But not for an uncommon accident. The Rama writes this to reconcile the responsa of the Rosh and Maharam cited by the Mechaber, which imply that the borrower must reimburse the guarantor even for amounts the non Jew extorted from him, with Maharik's responsum in the name of Rabbenu Yaakov that exempts him. The Rama distinguishes between common and uncommon losses. Maharshal in Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter HaGozel Batra, law 25, however, treats Maharik's responsum as disagreeing and rules like the Rosh and Maharam. Indeed, from the proofs of Maharam and the Rosh from the case of a Jew who sells property adjoining another Jew to a non Jew, where the seller must remove every loss caused by the non Jew, one could infer liability even for uncommon losses. There the seller must remove every type of damage, common or uncommon, and all the more so one who directly placed another into the position of guarantor. Yet the Rosh and Yam Shel Shlomo follow their own view. The Rosh holds regarding one who sells adjoining property to a non Jew that even before any ban is imposed, if damage occurs to the neighboring Jew, the seller is legally obligated to remove it; the ban serves only to make him formally undertake the obligation so that his assets become bound. Thus by law he is liable for every loss. It follows naturally, all the more so, that a borrower must indemnify his guarantor. According to the Raavad and those who agree with him, however, absent the ban and the seller's undertaking, if the neighboring Jew suffers damage from the non Jew the seller has no monetary liability at all, because the damage is merely indirect. The ban is imposed because of the wrongful act of exposing another Jew to harm, and if he wishes to escape the ban he accepts responsibility for all damage. If so, there is no proof to our guarantor case. There too, by strict law, the seller is exempt, and any damage that occurred before the ban is not reimbursable. One can distinguish that in the adjoining property case the seller acted with his own property and is therefore exempt, whereas here he acted with another's position, a distinction stated by the Raavad in chapter 7 of Hilchot Chovel U'Mazik. The Maggid Mishneh disagrees only regarding destroying another's lien. In our case the distinction may still apply, and Mahari Ben Lev writes similarly. In any event, no proof can be drawn from the adjoining property case. Below, siman 175 seif 40, the Rama rules like the Raavad, so that proof falls away. The Rosh in his responsum gives another reason for requiring the borrower to indemnify the guarantor: it is as though the borrower expressly stipulated so. This reason can support liability independently. But it suffices only for common losses that the parties could anticipate, making the implied condition plausible. It does not extend to entirely uncommon accidents, as Maharik writes, and as is demonstrated in chapter Mi SheMet that a condition is not construed to cover wholly unexpected events. Thus the Rama's ruling is correct as practical law. Yam Shel Shlomo likewise follows his own position, for in the adjoining property case he rules like the Rosh and not the Raavad. It further appears that since the Rosh's whole basis for requiring indemnification against every malicious exaction is the analogy to selling adjoining property to a non Jew, and because one who agreed to perform the mitzvah of placing himself before the lion should not thereby lose his right to reimbursement, a further distinction follows. If the guarantor undertook the guaranty solely for his fellow's benefit, Maharam and the Rosh are correct that although he voluntarily exposed himself to danger, his monetary rights are not thereby diminished with respect to losses he never accepted. But if he became guarantor for his own benefit because he was a partner in the transaction, his fellow did not place a lion upon him; he exposed himself for his own sake as a partner. His partner therefore need not indemnify him for malicious exactions, and the ordinary principle that one person is not seized for another applies. This resolves the Sema's question in seif katan 14 from siman 93 above and siman 176 below, which imply that a partner who became guarantor to a non Jew and later suffered a malicious exaction is reimbursed only for what he was legally and properly required to pay. The distinction is between a partner, upon whom the matter already rested and who acted for himself, and our case where the guarantor had no interest in the transaction and entered the guaranty solely for his fellow's benefit. This distinction is clear and sound. There is no difficulty from seif 8 concerning two persons who jointly accepted a tax farm, where all losses are shared. There neither one became guarantor to the non Jew more than the other; the non Jew merely chose to demand from one of them. Plainly every partnership loss belongs to both. See below siman 176 seif 12, which distinguishes between a malicious claim arising before division of the partnership and one arising afterward. Before division, it is plainly a partnership loss because both were equally exposed. This distinction is more satisfactory and universally applicable than the distinction suggested by the Sema.
