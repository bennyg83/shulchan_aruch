And he coerced him. There is no difficulty from the Tur and Mechaber in siman 93, seif 15, where a similar case appears to require proof that the principal debtor did not pay before reimbursement is due. The cases can be distinguished. There, the gentile seller's principal dealings were with Levi, and he merely obtained an additional commitment from Reuven that if Levi did not pay, Reuven would. Since the gentile initially agreed to look to Levi, Reuven did not anticipate that the gentile would personally proceed against him, and such a claim was unusual. Therefore the ruling there is that Shimon need not indemnify him merely on that basis. Here, however, Reuven expressly placed himself as guarantor toward the gentile, and under their law they proceed first against the guarantor. Therefore Reuven must be indemnified in every such case. So explains the Sema. The Taz objects that in the earlier case too the first partner entered a guaranty for Levi. He therefore explains that there the claim is: perhaps Levi already paid the gentile and the gentile released you in Levi's presence, but later proceeded against you through an unjust exaction. In such a case it is your own misfortune, as the Rama writes immediately below. See there. See above my lengthy discussion in Urim and Tumim, siman 93, and here in Tumim, seif katan 6, where I gave a correct resolution.
To give him. See Tumim, where I concluded that the borrower's duty to indemnify the guarantor for coercive losses caused by the gentile by reason of the guaranty applies only so long as the borrower has not paid. Once the borrower has paid, the guaranty has terminated. If the gentile thereafter brings a false claim against the guarantor, we return to the general rule that one person is not held liable merely because another was seized on his account. This explains the Shulchan Aruch's wording. Otherwise, why would it need the reason that Shimon says, 'I had to pay because I did not leave him a receipt,' when in any event the borrower must indemnify the guarantor for all losses caused by the guaranty, as was asked in a responsum of Panim Meirot? According to my explanation, the wording is clear.
The gentile's handwriting is recognizable. The Taz holds that it must be evident that the writing was made while the document was still in the gentile's possession and before any receipt existed, for otherwise how could a gentile's statement be used to extract money from a Jew? See what I wrote in the following seif katan.
That is written. The Sema, seif katan 15, writes that the text should read 'that he wrote,' without the conjunction, referring back to the gentile. The meaning is either that the gentile wrote a separate note, or wrote between the lines in his own hand how much he had received. If the text reads, 'and it is written,' it would imply merely that such writing is found in the document even if we do not know who wrote it. That cannot suffice, because the debt instrument is in Shimon's possession and we must fear that he forged an amount larger than what he actually paid. The Bach disagrees, because perhaps Shimon colluded with the gentile, so he should not be believed merely with an oath. He therefore explains that the note or interlineation must have been made in their official court records. The Taz likewise disagrees with the Sema and retains the reading, 'and it is written,' referring to a writing made while the document was still in the gentile's possession, as above. The Shach agrees against the Sema and adds that the Sema's assumption that the debt instrument must already be in Shimon's hands is not necessary. Perhaps Shimon paid only part of the debt and therefore needed a notation of payment without taking back the instrument. In Tumim I concluded that a separate note certainly suffices, because it is like a written receipt. An interlineation also suffices even if written in another hand, because the guarantor now holds the debt instrument, and why would the gentile have surrendered it to him unless payment had been made? Nevertheless, if the guarantor claims more than would ordinarily be due, I agree with the Bach that formal evidence from their court is required for the excess. For the principal debt itself, however, no formal testimony beyond this gentile's indication is necessary, because why would he have surrendered the note if he had not been paid? This is correct. Released him. The Mordechai writes: even if he has witnesses that the judge released him, since the gentile himself did not release him, the borrower remains obligated to remove the liability from him. The Darchei Moshe and Sema cite this.
He collects. The reason is that because the borrower caused him to become guarantor to a gentile, and gentiles commonly bring coercive claims, the borrower must indemnify him. This is comparable to one who incurs an expense on another's behalf with authorization, where it is established in many places that he swears and collects. See Sema; this is straightforward.
With a common coercive loss, etc. See Tumim. The Rama wrote this distinction in order to reconcile the authorities. A responsum of the Rosh requires reimbursement even for amounts taken by coercion, whereas Maharik, roots 7 and 131, cites Rabbeinu Yaakov as exempting the borrower. The Rama distinguishes between a common and an uncommon coercive loss. Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma, chapter HaGozel Batra, understands the authorities as disputing and rules like the Rosh that all coercive losses must be reimbursed. In truth, the Rosh's proof there from one who sells property to a gentile adjoining a Jew appears to show no distinction between common and uncommon losses, and certainly would seem to apply here to a guarantor. Nevertheless, I concluded that the Rama is correct. The Rosh and Yam Shel Shlomo follow their own approach that in the adjoining gentile case the seller is legally obligated to remove all resulting coercive losses. According to the majority of authorities, however, including the Raavad and those with him, whose view the Rama adopts below in siman 175, seif 40, if the Jew was not first placed under ban he need not reimburse all subsequent coercive losses. On that approach, there is good reason to distinguish between common and uncommon coercion. Even according to the Rosh's second reason for liability, namely that because the borrower must indemnify him it is as though he stipulated for indemnification, I wrote that the Rama remains correct. A presumed stipulation extends only to common risks, not unusual ones, as Maharik writes. Therefore the Rama's ruling is entirely sound. See there. I also wrote in Tumim that if the guarantor was himself a partner in the underlying matter and was coerced, the borrower need not indemnify him, because we return to the general rule that one person is not held for another merely because he was seized. See there for the reasoning.
