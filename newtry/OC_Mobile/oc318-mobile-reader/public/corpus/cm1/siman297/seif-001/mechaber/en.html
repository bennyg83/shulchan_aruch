One who denies a deposit even when the depositor provides identifying marks. It contains one seif.
If a deposit was made without witnesses, the alleged bailee may deny the transaction even if he is not wealthy enough to own such an object and the depositor gives identifying marks. Even when deposited before witnesses, if the object is not currently seen in his possession, he may say, 'I returned it,' or, 'You gave it to me.' Even detailed knowledge of the container does not defeat his claim that the present goods are others. If witnesses testify that this very object was deposited and now see it in his possession, he cannot claim that he returned and repurchased it or received it as a gift. It is removed from him, and after his death from his heirs, without an oath. Rama: If the depositor identifies an object before witnesses and a matching object is found with the heirs, it is shown to the witnesses and returned if they identify it. If the heirs say their father told them it was his, they are believed with an oath. A judge who personally knows that the deceased was not likely to own such property may, under strict conditions and based on clear identifying marks, award it to the claimant, unless the claimant regularly entered the deceased's premises and may have learned another's marks. Other witnesses' estimates of wealth do not compel removal, because the judge relies on his own firmly grounded assessment. Since unqualified courts became common, most Jewish courts agreed not to remove property from heirs based solely on judicial estimation. A bailee who returned a deposit by order of beit din and later learns that the court erred is exempt.
