Alef) This is the language of the Tur. The Beit Yosef writes that the rule is straightforward, except that the defendant takes a heset oath.
There, seif 2. In my humble opinion this is learned from Tosafot in the ninth chapter of Ketubot at the beginning of 85b, from the incidents discussed there.
The Sema, citing the Ran, explains that a person can appear impoverished while possessing great wealth, or may have found a valuable object.
Bet) In the third chapter of Bava Batra 45b, the Gemara concludes that one who deposited property with his fellow before witnesses need not have witnesses when it is returned.
Gimel) From the incident involving sesame seeds in the fifteenth chapter of Yevamot 116b, and Rava's statement to Rav Chisda there. Rambam likewise rules in chapter 6 of Hilkhot She'eilah U'Pikadon, halachah 6.
Dalet) Rambam writes that he must take an oath while holding a sacred object. The Maggid Mishneh suggests that perhaps this is because the claimant provided identifying signs, and leaves it requiring study. The Kesef Mishneh writes that Rambam's reason is entirely sound.
Heh) Tosafot write this there in the name of Rabbenu Yitzchak.
Vav) The Tur, seif 3. Rambam likewise rules there, halachah 6. The rule is explained in the Rif and Rosh in the third chapter of Bava Batra concerning Rabbah's statement that this was taught only where the deposit was delivered before witnesses, etc., 45a.
Zayin) From the incident involving seven pearls before Rabbi Ami in the ninth chapter of Ketubot 85b. Even if their father was known to be wealthy, once the property had been deposited before witnesses the heirs cannot simply claim it as his.
Meaning, at the time the claimant demanded that the orphans show the item so that witnesses could identify it. Tur. In my humble opinion it is clear that they cannot make this claim after the item was shown to witnesses and identified, because even their father himself would not have been believed then, as explained above.
Chet) The Sema writes that although heirs ordinarily swear only in order to collect, not merely to be exempt, as explained at the end of simanim 69 and 71, this case is different. Since the owner gives identifying signs and says that he has witnesses who can identify the object visually, the heirs are considered like parties seeking to collect the disputed property.
Tet) This is Rambam's language there. The Maggid Mishneh explains the incident in HaKotev according to Rambam as involving no witnesses to the original deposit. He suggests that if the father were alive and claimed, "I purchased it," then because there were no deposit witnesses and the object was seen, it might not be removed from him. But we do not advance that claim for the heirs where all these suspicious circumstances are present. This requires study. Ramban and Rashba follow another approach, and this is not the place to elaborate.
Yod) The Maggid Mishneh writes that Rambam derived this from the Gemara's wording, where Rav Nachman says, "I know that this person is not wealthy enough for such property." This implies that the judge himself must possess reliable knowledge of the person's means.
Kaf) Rambam likewise writes this in chapter 24 of Hilkhot Sanhedrin, halachah 2. The Mechaber cites it above in siman 15, seif 5.
