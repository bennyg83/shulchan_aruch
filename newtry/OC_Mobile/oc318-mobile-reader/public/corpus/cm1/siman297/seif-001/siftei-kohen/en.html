And the depositor provides an identifying mark. This means a conclusive identifying mark. Because I have seen several statements by the authorities in these laws that do not appear correct to me, as well as matters requiring clarification and several inaccurate formulations among the later authorities, I must discuss the subject at length and establish the law. The Tur rules that when an article was deposited without witnesses, the custodian may deny that any deposit occurred. Even when the deposit was made before witnesses, if witnesses did not subsequently see the article in his possession, he may say that he returned it, that it was given to him as a gift, or that he purchased it. This is so even if he is not known to possess such an article and the depositor can describe it. The Tur deliberately says that, even where the depositor gives an identifying mark, the custodian may deny the deposit. It does not state without qualification that the custodian is believed, because where the depositor gives a truly conclusive mark the custodian is not believed to admit the deposit and then say, “I returned yours and this is another article.” Such a claim is a migo against compelling evidence. If witnesses have now seen the article with that identifying mark in his possession, he cannot deny that it is present. Even without such witnesses, once he admits the deposit and the claimant gives an identifying mark that establishes the article’s identity, he cannot say that the deposited article was returned and an identical one later came into his hands. He may, however, claim that this particular article was never deposited but came to him by purchase or gift, whenever the ordinary rules of possession and migo permit that claim. This explains the Tur’s later example of wheat or sesame. A quantity and container are not necessarily conclusive marks. Even if witnesses saw the stated quantity in a particular barrel, the custodian may say, “I returned your produce; this is other produce,” because such circumstances can recur. Tosafot likewise state that where the custodian admits the deposit but claims that the same unusual mark happened to occur in his own article, a truly decisive mark defeats the migo, for the identification is treated as testimony before us. This is also the position of the Rosh. The sesame case is different because the quantity and barrel were not utterly unique. The Nimukei Yosef, citing the Ritva, states that even without witnesses to the original deposit, if the custodian admits the deposit, the claimant gives a conclusive mark, and witnesses establish that an article bearing that mark is presently with the custodian, no migo can help him. The Rashba similarly indicates that where there are witnesses to the deposit, a lesser mark can suffice when joined to the other evidence. The Hagahot Ashri states that if witnesses saw an article bearing the same conclusive mark deposited and later saw such an article with the custodian, he cannot say, “I returned yours and this is another,” even though the witnesses do not recognize it by visual familiarity. This may refer either to an absolutely unique mark, or to a strong but repeatable mark that becomes decisive when joined with witnesses to the deposit and present possession. The Maggid Mishneh, citing the Ramban and Rashba regarding the sesame case, explains that a mere count is not fully conclusive. Had there been a conclusive identifying mark, the depositor could recover even if the witnesses could not testify that the present goods were the identical goods deposited. Rabbeinu Yerucham, citing the Ramah, similarly rules that with a conclusive mark the custodian cannot say that he returned the article and later purchased it. These statements must be understood where there are both witnesses to the deposit and witnesses who saw the article presently in the custodian’s possession, although they identify it only through the mark. Without present-possession testimony, the custodian may say that he returned the article. Without evidence that this article entered his hand as a deposit, he may ordinarily say that he purchased it. The Ran’s language also indicates that the witnesses who saw the identifying mark are additional witnesses who observed the article before litigation. Otherwise, the beit din itself can see the mark and there would be no need to mention other witnesses. Tosafot in Ketubot and Yevamot, the Mordechai, and the Agudah likewise speak of a case containing both witnesses to the deposit and witnesses who later saw the article in the custodian’s possession. When the Gemara or Rif says merely that an article was deposited before witnesses, the accepted meaning in these possessory disputes is that there are also witnesses of present possession, for otherwise the custodian retains the claim that he returned it. There is nevertheless an apparent conflict. Tosafot in Bava Metzia and the Rosh imply that an absolutely unique mark defeats the claim “I returned yours and this is another” even without witnesses, whereas Tosafot elsewhere require witnesses to the deposit. The distinction is that a mark capable of recurring, though highly unusual, does not by itself make the custodian’s claim impossible; witnesses are therefore needed to remove his migo. A mark that cannot realistically recur, such as the owner’s name or another feature unique in the world, makes the claim that this is a different article equivalent to a claim against witnesses. Even then, if the custodian has no witnesses establishing that this very article came to him as a deposit, he may claim that he purchased it or received it as a gift, since the identifying mark proves ownership at an earlier time but does not itself prove the legal character by which it entered his possession. I find difficult the broader explanation attributed to the Ramban and Rashba, according to which witnesses to a deposit of one article, witnesses now seeing another article bearing the same mark, and the claimant’s identification suffice even though no witness can say that the present article is the one deposited. The possessor can answer that he returned the deposited article and later purchased the one now seen. A purchaser is believed where there is no proof that the present article entered his hand as a deposit. It appears that the Ramban and Rashba advanced this reading principally to reconcile the Rif. The Rif himself can instead be read in the ordinary manner, where the witnesses recognize the article itself. The Rambam’s omission of such a novel rule also supports the narrower interpretation. The episode in Ketubot concerning property found among the estate of the deceased must likewise be analyzed through the ordinary rules governing heirs. Where the deceased could have claimed purchase, the beit din generally advances that claim for the heirs. The presence of identifying marks does not by itself eliminate the purchase claim. Tosafot and several authorities say that an uncommon claim is not advanced for heirs, but the Ramban, Ritva, Baal HaIttur, Rif, and other authorities maintain that whatever the father could have claimed is ordinarily claimed for his heirs, even where the event is uncommon. This is especially true regarding a deposit. Thus, if there were witnesses to the deposit but none who saw the article after the father’s death, the heirs may be protected by the father’s migo that he returned it and later acquired it. If witnesses saw it after his death, the heirs cannot rely on a migo based on a return they do not know occurred. If the heirs themselves make a definite claim that their father purchased it, they are believed where they could say that they returned it, but they must take a hesset oath because their claim is definite. The judicial-estimation cases in Ketubot are not necessarily categorical rules of evidence. They show that Rabbi Ami, Rav Nachman, and Rabbi Abba acted on a compelling assessment that the property belonged to the claimant. The Rambam therefore writes that if the judge knows that the deceased was not financially capable of owning such an article and is convinced that it was not his, he may remove it from the heirs. This is a law dependent upon judicial assessment. Since contemporary courts do not remove property from heirs solely through such estimation, possession ordinarily remains with them when the legal evidence is incomplete. Nevertheless, if the claimant seized the property in a case where a strong judicial assessment would formerly have justified recovery, it is not removed from him. The practical rules emerging from the discussion are these. If an article was deposited without witnesses, then even if witnesses now see it and recognize it as formerly belonging to the claimant, the possessor may claim that he purchased it from the claimant, received it as a gift, or acquired it from another who purchased it. This applies even where he is not known to possess such property and the claimant gives a conclusive identifying mark. If the deposit was made before witnesses but no witness saw the article in his hand before litigation, he may say that he returned it, and may therefore also claim purchase or gift through that migo. If the claim concerns a stated quantity of wheat or sesame in a particular barrel, and witnesses saw a matching quantity and container, the custodian may say that he returned the claimant’s produce and that the present produce is different, because the description is not conclusive. However, where he says that the present goods themselves were once the claimant’s but were later purchased from him, the migo of return may be weak if witnesses saw goods matching the deposit, since a person may fear making the facially implausible return claim. If there were no witnesses to the deposit, he may say directly that the goods originally came to him by purchase. A conclusive identifying mark has the force of visual recognition. If witnesses establish by that mark both that this article was deposited and that the same article is presently in the custodian’s possession, he cannot claim return, purchase, or gift. If there are no witnesses of present possession, he may claim that he returned it and that no such article remains with him, and he may use that claim as a migo for purchase. Where the mark is absolutely unique and the article is produced before the court, he cannot say, “I returned yours and this is another,” for that is a migo against evidence. He may still claim purchase if there is no proof that this article entered his possession as a deposit. If the witnesses recognize neither the article by sight nor by a conclusive mark, then even when there are witnesses to a deposit and to present possession and the claimant provides strong marks, the possessor may say that the deposited article was returned and that the present article was purchased. The claimant’s knowledge of the marks can be explained by his former ownership. Wherever the father would have been believed, the same purchase claim is advanced for the heirs. If witnesses saw the article after death, the heirs cannot be given the father’s migo of return, though heirs who make a definite purchase claim may rely on their own migo that they returned it. If witnesses establish that the particular article was deposited and later saw it after the custodian’s death, or the heirs admit that it remains a deposit but do not know who deposited it, a claimant who supplies a conclusive mark is believed, provided he was not regularly present in the custodian’s home in a manner that would explain his knowledge. The deceased’s wealth does not defeat that identification. If, however, the heirs do not know whether their father later purchased the article, and there is no proof that this particular article entered his hand as the claimant’s deposit, the purchase claim may still be advanced for them. Finally, where the deceased was plainly unable to own such an article, and the claimant either proves a deposit without later sight testimony, or supplies a conclusive mark and was not accustomed to enter the deceased’s house, a judge possessing the former power of strong judicial assessment could award it to the claimant. Since such assessment is not ordinarily used today to remove property from heirs, the property is not extracted from them solely on that basis. If the claimant has already seized it in either of these strong cases, it is not removed from him. See also the responsa of Maharit cited here.
