The depositor gave an identifying sign. The Shach rules that an especially distinctive sign can defeat a custodian's migo and is like testimony. I wrote in siman 259 that even highly distinctive signs do not ordinarily extract money from a present possessor. Ritva in Ketubot supports this. In the case of seven pearls found among an estate's effects, the combination of the deceased's known poverty and the claimant's signs worked because there were witnesses to the original deposit. The signs did not independently extract money. They identified the objects as the same property previously deposited in the witnesses' presence. Thus signs are useful to connect present property to an established deposit, but they do not by themselves establish ownership against a person who says, "These were never yours." This also explains the Shulchan Aruch. If a person deposited without witnesses, the possessor can say, "No such matter ever occurred," meaning these objects were never yours. Even if the claimant supplies signs, that does not extract the property. This remains true even for articles commonly lent or rented, where there is no ordinary migo of purchase. Likewise, in siman 134, where witnesses saw an article in the artisan's hand but identify it only through signs, the artisan can still say, "You never gave it to me." Only once the original deposit itself was witnessed do the signs identify this object with the deposited one. Ordinary weight is only a weak sign and can be insufficient even with deposit witnesses. This is clear from the cases concerning unrefined gold.
The judge knew that the deceased had not been wealthy. The rule depends upon the judge actually knowing that the deceased lacked the means to own such valuables. Mere absence of knowledge that he was wealthy creates no presumption at all. Rambam therefore says, "If the judge knows that he was not wealthy." Even testimony by witnesses concerning the deceased's ordinary means does not itself become the judge's personal estimate in the same manner, as the Shulchan Aruch explains. I mention this because Maharsha appears to imply that it is enough that we do not know the deceased to have been wealthy. That cannot be correct. The entire force of the ruling comes from a positive judicial estimate that ownership was improbable. Where the judge simply lacks knowledge, there is no adverse presumption against the estate.
