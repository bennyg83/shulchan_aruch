The depositor gives an identifying sign. Shach's lengthy discussion can be summarized as follows. If there are witnesses to the deposit and the object is seen in the custodian's possession with a truly unique identifying sign, then even if the witnesses themselves lack visual recognition, the custodian cannot say, "I returned yours; this is another object." A uniquely identifying sign can create a powerful contradiction. With an intermediate sign, the custodian can make that claim if he has a migo that the item was purchased. With a weak sign, he can say "I returned yours; this is another" even without such a migo. However, signs do not generally extract money from a possessor in the same manner that they return a lost object. Intermediate identifying signs are at most rabbinic for lost property. Therefore, where witnesses have only a sign and no visual recognition, the principal use of the sign is to compel the possessor to show the object to the witnesses so they can identify it visually. This follows Tosafot in Bava Metzia: even if signs are only rabbinic, they can obligate a person to display the item before witnesses because the signs make it appear to be the claimant's. Accordingly, with heirs who plead only doubtfully, beit din does not invent a claim of "I returned it" strong enough to prevent the object from being shown where there are witnesses to the deposit and identifying signs. But against the custodian himself, if he definitely says, "I returned yours and this is another," and has the ordinary presumption that a person does not brazenly deny a true claim, the court need not compel display unless the sign is exceptionally strong. Shach's attempt to make a full distinction between unique and intermediate signs for actual extraction from the possessor is difficult in light of Tosafot in Chullin, who imply that even a very strong sign does not automatically extract an object from a possessor when the sign is public and could coincidentally match. The better rule is therefore that signs ordinarily support inspection and identification, not direct monetary extraction. A claimant who himself provides a uniquely identifying sign presents a stronger case because knowledge of the hidden sign is itself evidence of ownership. In such a case we are less concerned that he merely happened to see or hear the sign. But where the witnesses or beit din already know the sign from elsewhere, coincidence remains a concern in monetary law. The discussion of a blind witness likewise proves only that a distinctive measurement or weight can serve where the litigants' own claims remove the concern of coincidence or where no one is presently in possession. It does not establish a general rule that signs alone extract from a possessor. The practical conclusion is that the court should use signs to compel production of the item for visual identification where appropriate, but should not ordinarily use non-unique signs alone to extract property from a possessor.
