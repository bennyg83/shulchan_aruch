We remove it from the heirs. Meaning, we do not plead for the heirs the uncommon defense that had their father been alive he might have produced witnesses proving he bought the object from the depositor. Since this claim is unusual, beit din does not advance it for heirs. Therefore, certainly if witnesses saw the object in the heirs' possession before litigation, giving a case of witnesses and present observation, even their father would not have been believed to claim ownership by purchase, and we remove it from the heirs. More than this, even if witnesses had not seen it before the case, but the depositor claims that he deposited a certain object with their father, the heirs must show it to the witnesses. Their father, who could have made a definite claim, might not have had to do so, but beit din does not formulate for heirs an unusual defense. Once the object is shown and witnesses identify it as the depositor's, it is returned unless the heirs themselves make a definite contrary claim. The Tur states this, and this is how Rama's gloss fits with the Mechaber's words.

We return it. This applies even if the heirs' father was wealthy enough to own an object of this kind. Since the depositor has witnesses that he deposited it with him, the item is removed from the heirs when the depositor gives identifying signs. The later requirement that the father not have been wealthy enough to own such a thing and not have been accustomed to possessing one applies only where there are no witnesses of the deposit. The Ran says this explicitly, and it is evident from the Rosh and Tur. Therefore the Tur and Rama do not mention lack of wealth in the opening clause. The Ir Shushan mistakenly combined the two distinct cases.
If the orphans say, "Our father told us it was his," they are believed with an oath. Without an oath they are not believed. Although we ordinarily rule that heirs swear only in order to collect and not merely to exempt themselves, as explained at the ends of simanim 69 and 71, this case is different because the owner provides identifying signs and says he has witnesses who can recognize the object as his. The heirs are therefore treated as though they are coming to take from the owner, and must swear.
We do not remove it from the orphans, etc. This appears to refer only to the latter case, which depends upon an assessment of whether the father was wealthy enough or ordinarily possessed such objects. In the earlier case of witnesses and present identification, which does not depend upon an assessment, we remove the object from the orphans even in our time.
