If one made a mound ten handbreadths high in the public domain and an animal was injured by it, etc. This teaches that even though there is no harmful air, he is liable when the animal dies from the impact. It is somewhat difficult that if the animal strikes the mound itself, ten handbreadths should not be necessary at all. Even if an animal trips over ordinary ground and is injured by a stone, liability can arise, as explained in the next siman. The greater novelty would have been to say that even if the animal did not strike the mound itself but fell from it onto ordinary ground, liability applies. The Tur indeed writes: If one made a mound ten handbreadths high in the public domain, an ox climbed upon it, fell to the ground, and died, he is liable. That formulation is clear, because a mound resembles a pit. For a fatal fall from above downward, a height of ten handbreadths is required even though the final impact is upon ordinary ground.
