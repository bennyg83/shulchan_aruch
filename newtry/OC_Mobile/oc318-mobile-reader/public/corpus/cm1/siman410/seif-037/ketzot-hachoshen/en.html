Sema initially explains Tur's qualification as applying only where each participant was independently capable of causing the entire damage. That appears to be the correct reading. In the case of an ox that pushes another ox into a pit, Rabbi Natan can make the pit owner bear the uncompensated portion. The Gemara gives two formulations. One treats each cause as capable of the entire damage. The other says each caused only half, but the pit completed the injury and therefore resembles the one who completed the whole damage. Tosafot indicates that where one actor caused only half and did not complete the injury, Rabbi Natan's rule does not ordinarily require him to pay the other's unavailable share. Hagahot Maimoniyot and Maharam likewise apply the broader recovery only where each guarantor or tortfeasor could independently have produced the entire loss. Ramban in Milchamot is broader. He says that even if each actor caused only half, where one cannot pay the injured party can collect the whole from the other because the other can be told, "You caused my loss." Yet Ramban also distinguishes meat and sacrificial portions where neither part independently creates even half the damage without the other. There the broader collection does not apply. This distinction helps answer a difficulty I raised earlier concerning two judges who, together with a third judge, issued a wrongful judgment. The two cannot complete any judgment without the third. Their role therefore resembles the meat and sacrificial portions rather than two independent oxen. According to Rashi and Tosafot, however, there are passages implying Rabbi Natan's rule even where neither component could cause damage without the other. On their view the problem remains and requires further study.
If one has no means to pay, the other pays. This follows Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia and Maharam, who apply Rabbi Natan's rule even where the second tortfeasor is legally liable but has no assets or has fled. At first this seems inconsistent with the law of an innocuous ox and its fetus. If the cow and fetus belong to different owners and only one can presently be collected from, the cow owner does not automatically bear the fetus's share. The answer is that horn damage by an innocuous ox is paid only from the body of the damaging ox itself. Under Rabbi Akiva, the injured party and owner become partners in that ox. If the ox is lost, depreciates, or flees, the loss falls upon the injured party's already designated share as though collection had occurred. Rabbi Natan's rule therefore does not shift that unavailable share to the other innocuous ox. This explains why Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia's broader rule can still stand for ordinary personal liabilities while failing in the special regime of half-damages from the body of an innocuous ox. The passage where an ox and a person together push another into a pit supplies a further indication. For kofer the ox can be required to pay the full amount where the person is exempt. Ramban explains this through Rabbi Natan. If the person's monetary exemption were merely a case where the court cannot collect but an underlying liability remained, the broader rule still applies according to Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia because inability to collect is enough. Tur rejects that broad application and limits Rabbi Natan to a co-tortfeasor who is legally exempt, not merely unable to pay. The passage can be reconciled with Tur because a person is substantively excluded from kofer by the verse "upon him," not merely procedurally protected by the more-severe-punishment doctrine. Rashi's explanation invoking the more-severe-punishment doctrine is difficult for this reason. One possible defense is to distinguish kofer itself from ordinary damages: the verse excludes kofer from a person absolutely, while other monetary damages can sometimes remain subject to seizure where the only reason for exemption is the more severe punishment. The broader discussion also clarifies the dispute in Sanhedrin concerning "life for life." Where the Sages deny monetary payment altogether, seizure cannot help, because no monetary liability exists. Where a monetary debt exists but collection is blocked only by the more-severe-punishment doctrine, seizure can sometimes be effective in our present judicial setting. This reconciles the various sources.
