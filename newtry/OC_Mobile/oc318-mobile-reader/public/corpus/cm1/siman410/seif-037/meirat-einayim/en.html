Up to the amount for which he himself would have been liable, etc. The Tur states this more clearly: specifically up to the amount he would have been liable for this damage had he caused it alone, but no more. At first glance one might explain that sometimes the injured party suffers greater damage when pushed by two actors than by one, and the rule teaches that the remaining tortfeasor need not pay the entire damage jointly produced if he could not have caused so much damage alone. This is difficult, however, because the same reasoning would apply where a person and a pit together caused utensils to break. Without the pit, the person's push would not have broken them, yet the preceding law requires the person to pay the entire damage to the utensils. It therefore appears that the ruling excludes only a case discussed above, where a person and an ox together pushed a person and injured him. They share liability for the bodily damage, while the person alone is liable for the other four categories. The present rule teaches that if the person flees, the owner of the ox must pay the entire bodily damage as though his ox had injured the victim alone, but he need not pay the four additional categories on behalf of the fleeing person. Although the ox owner bears the remaining payment in place of the other tortfeasor, we do not say that he steps into the latter's place for every form of liability, because even had the ox caused the injury alone it would not have been liable for those additional categories.
