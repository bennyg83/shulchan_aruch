For he was negligent with respect to camels. Meaning, if camels had passed there, they would have caused oxen to fall through the covering. Camels are common, so initially he was negligent because perhaps camels would pass and damage the cover. Nevertheless, since no camels actually passed and the animal fell only because the covering decayed internally, the final event was an accident unrelated to the initial negligence, and he is exempt. Rama then cites authorities who distinguish and impose liability even in that case.
He is liable even where it decayed internally. The Tur gives the reason more fully. Since his initial negligence related to this very ox, for the ox that ultimately fell because of decay would also have fallen had camels passed and weakened the covering, he is liable. This differs from a case where a sighted ox falls into a pit. Although there may have been negligence regarding the possible fall of a deaf or blind ox, there was no aspect of negligence regarding the sighted ox itself. Once a person was initially negligent regarding the very object that was ultimately harmed, anything that can plausibly be linked to that negligence is attributed to it, even if the connection is remote, particularly because internal decay itself is uncommon. We therefore say that had he covered the pit with a sufficiently strong cover capable of supporting camels, it might not have decayed.
