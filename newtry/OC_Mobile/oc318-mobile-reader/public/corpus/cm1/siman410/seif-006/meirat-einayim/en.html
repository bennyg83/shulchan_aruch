And he opened it toward the public domain. The Tur says that he opened it within his own property but within four cubits of the public domain. The reason is that the public frequently presses into the four cubits adjoining the public domain. If he opens it toward another person's property, however, it must be directly adjacent to that property. Therefore the Tur specifies four cubits only with respect to the public domain. See Perishah.
Or he opened it toward another person's property, etc. The Tur writes that this applies specifically before the courtyard owner becomes aware of it. Once he knows, the digger is exempt, because the courtyard owner, who can recover the cost of the damage from the digger, should immediately fill the pit and then collect the expense from him. If he fails to fill it, it is as though he himself dug it. Although the pit is not actually in the injured courtyard owner's property but remains in the damager's courtyard and merely opens into the injured party's property, since it directly abuts the injured party's courtyard, it is treated as though it were in his courtyard. He must close it to distance the danger from himself and then recover the cost of his effort from the person who dug it.
Or he abandoned a pit located in his own property, etc. The reason is as I wrote above: since the injured party had no right to enter the property and approach the pit, the owner of the pit is not responsible to guard it against him.
