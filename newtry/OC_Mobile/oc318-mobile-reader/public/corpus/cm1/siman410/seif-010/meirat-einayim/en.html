A pit or a trench, etc. The Mishnah also lists a cave, a ditch, and a narrow excavation. It teaches the progression as increasing novelty: not only a pit, which is small and round and plainly contains harmful air, but even a trench, which is long; even a cave, which is square; even ditches that are not roofed; and even narrow excavations that are wider above than below and contain only a small amount of harmful air. Nevertheless, liability applies. The Mishnah concludes, "and any similar thing." Shmuel explains that this includes one who made a mound ten handbreadths high. In Perishah I explained that according to Shmuel the Mishnah means: not only the listed excavations, which contain both harmful air and impact, but even something with no harmful air at all, such as a mound against which an animal stumbles or from which it falls, generates liability. According to Rav the passage is resolved differently, as I explained in Derishah.
Why then did the Torah say "pit"? Meaning, why did the Torah specifically write, "If a man shall dig a pit," to impose liability, when liability applies to these other hazards as well? The answer is that the Torah chose a pit because its depth has a known measure. It is as though the verse stated the pit as a novelty of exemption: even a pit, if it is not ten handbreadths deep and an ox or donkey falls into it and dies, the digger is exempt, because death in a fall of less than ten handbreadths is considered an unavoidable occurrence, since animals do not ordinarily die from such a fall. All the more so a trench or cave less than ten handbreadths deep.
