The person acted unintentionally. Tosafot says that if a person intentionally pushed another's ox into a pit, the owner of the pit is exempt and the person alone is liable. An intentional human act supersedes the pit, just as one who intentionally places another's garment into someone else's fire cannot shift liability to the owner of the fire. An ox's intentional conduct, however, is halachically comparable to a person's unintentional act. This creates a difficult apportionment question where a person intentionally and an ox together push a third ox into a pit. If the person alone had pushed it, he would bear all liability and the pit owner would be exempt. If only the ox had pushed it, the ox owner and pit owner would divide liability. With all three involved, how is payment divided? It seems that the person should pay half, because his only joint tortfeasor is the pushing ox. The remaining half is borne jointly by the ox and the pit, one quarter each. This can explain Rava's statement that person, ox, and pit are all liable without necessarily meaning they divide equally. Still, Tosafot's language appears to imply that whenever the human actor acted intentionally, the pit owner is entirely exempt. If so, the proposed division cannot stand. The matter therefore requires study.
