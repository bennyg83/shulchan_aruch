Even if the animal died only from its harmful air. All the more so, Rambam likewise writes, if it died from the impact. This wording does not fit smoothly with the dispute of Rav and Shmuel. Rav holds that liability for a pit applies only if the animal dies from the pit's harmful air and not from the impact, because the ground itself belongs to the world, unless the pit is on his property adjacent to the public domain, in which case the bottom of the pit belongs to him. Shmuel also imposes liability when the animal dies from the impact, and the halacha follows him. According to Rav, the wording "even if" is inappropriate because there is exemption for impact. According to Shmuel, one might have expected the reverse wording: even if it died from impact, and all the more so from harmful air. One must say that according to Shmuel, once the Torah imposes liability for impact even though the earth itself is ownerless ground, there is actually stronger reason to impose liability for impact, which involves a more concrete physical effect, than for death through the pit's air, which resembles indirect causation.
Therefore, if the depth of the pit equaled its width, etc. He is exempt because the animal died in an abnormal accident and there was neither harmful air nor the sort of impact contemplated by the law. The word "therefore" follows the second clause: since harmful air alone can create liability, therefore if the pit is deeper relative to its width and produces harmful air, liability applies.
If the animal was not injured by impact in it, etc. The Tur explains a case where soft sponges were inside the pit, so that the animal was not injured by the impact when it landed on them.
