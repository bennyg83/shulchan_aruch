Something that has not yet come into the world. Even if a revenue lease or estate concession is given legal effect by the law of the kingdom, it is not stronger than an ordinary sale. A partnership in future revenue that has not yet come into existence may therefore be revocable before the subject comes into existence. If the lease includes a house or other tangible property, as is customary, the partnership can be acquired through that property, and governmental law may also give an exclusive right to sell beverages there, making retraction more difficult. A mill is similar. A customs concession is less clear, because the concessionaire does not truly prevent travel but merely collects a toll; perhaps, however, the right to exclude unpaid passage gives it a sufficient real-property character. A liquor concession without any leased house or location may remain revocable. If the parties made a kinyan sudar and deposited money upon an agreement that whatever one of them later purchases will belong to both, but one party had already made a declaration that he was agreeing only to prevent the other from bidding up the price and that his promises were merely outward, such prior disclosure may defeat the kinyan. Just as a declaration before witnesses can show that one who said he would acquire for another is actually withdrawing, so too a partnership kinyan in property not yet acquired may be undone by a clear prior declaration. The accepted middle view in partnership law is that one may retract prospectively from a partnership concerning future property, though not retroactively from what has already entered the partnership. Thus a declaration made before the property enters his possession should be effective no less than a retraction made after the kinyan but before the purchase. Perhaps where a formal moda'ah ordinarily requires proof of coercion, the desire to protect oneself from having the price raised is itself known coercion. If the other party could lawfully raise the price, the benefit of partnership, including the advantage that the fortune of two partners is better, may make the arrangement resemble a sale entered under pressure, in which recognizable coercion is needed. If a penalty sum was deposited and the agreement was made 'from now' before an important beit din, a moda'ah can still invalidate it, though here too one may need evidence of coercion because the other partner's ability to buy alone and confer a profitable share gives the transaction genuine value. As to the fixed customary fee for matchmaking, this is unlike the exorbitant ferryman case of siman 264. A matchmaker may earn a substantial established fee even for a very small communication, because the payment is for bringing the match about. If two people formed a partnership in matchmaking and one agreed to write the introductory letter in return for half the fee, once he wrote the letter he completed the act assigned to him and the other partner may not necessarily retract, even though the eventual fee has not yet come into existence because the match may fail. The fee, if the match is later completed, relates back to that completed act. If the other partner destroys the letter before it reaches either side, the writer's claim is not ordinary damages, because preventing delivery is not necessarily actionable direct damage. Rather, his claim arises from the partnership itself, much as partners who mix produce or money acquire reciprocal rights. If the other party announces withdrawal before the match is completed, the result may depend upon the dispute in siman 176 concerning partnership in future property. Under the view that one may retract from what has not yet come into existence, a matchmaking fee not yet earned remains future property despite the earlier letter. Even where the event has begun to develop, it resembles fruits not yet grown or a fetus to be acquired only upon birth. Still, where no withdrawal was clearly declared before witnesses, the original undertaking may remain operative, as in siman 183. There is another limitation. If the person who wrote the letter could not have brought about the match independently, was unknown to the parties, or would not have been listened to, while his partner alone could have completed it, then the writer may be only an insubstantial helper. A large promised payment may then resemble the ferryman case rather than ordinary matchmaking compensation. Communal matchmaking regulations granting fees to one who begins the process likely refer to a contributor who provides meaningful assistance, not merely one who prevents obstruction. Whether partnership law nevertheless gives him rights when he did not expressly withdraw remains uncertain. If at formation of the partnership he said that he was undertaking the work because he would embarrass himself by going personally to demand the matchmaking fee, but in the end he did not go and nevertheless received payment, that change may not create a decisive implied condition because he ultimately suffered no loss from the departure. Yoreh Deah 236 itself records a dispute whether statements of circumstance are treated as formal conditions. Regarding the three views at the beginning of siman 176, the intermediate view, permitting withdrawal only as to future property, may be the accepted law, though a party in possession may perhaps plead that he relies upon the opposing view. This requires further study.
