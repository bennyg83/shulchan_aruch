Mishneh LaMelech at the beginning of chapter 5 of the laws of partners questions Maharit, who wrote in Choshen Mishpat 112 that consent given after the act is complete does not help. Rambam's wording appears to indicate that later consent can be effective, and Maharit certainly was aware of the primary halachah. In Maharit's case, however, reason itself supports the conclusion that later consent should not help because a debt had already been sold in a manner that left no realistic possibility of altering the transaction, and the seller only reported the matter after everything was irrevocably done. By contrast, in Rambam's case the act, though legally difficult to reverse, might still have been adjusted through a new partnership, recovery of merchandise sold on credit, or another commercial arrangement had the other partner objected promptly. Some authorities hold that silence itself can amount to consent. Perhaps this applies mainly when the parties are partners in all their business, so that silence reveals that the general partnership included the disputed transaction. If they had expressly formed a partnership only for one defined venture, silence afterward, even where it could function as a waiver, may not amount to admission that the venture was included. We generally do not multiply disputes among authorities without necessity. Kesef Mishneh and later authorities explain that later consent is effective because waiver does not require a kinyan. This suggests that where the issue is not merely waiver of a claim but affirmative transfer of money or property, mere consent without an act of acquisition is insufficient. According to those who nevertheless treat silence as effective, perhaps their reasoning is that the silence merely reveals the original breadth of the partnership, as merchants commonly authorize one another to act according to business judgment. Yet because we do not unnecessarily broaden the dispute, it is reasonable to say that all agree later express consent helps only where the matter can operate as waiver. If one party merely possesses identifiable movable property rather than money subject to a claim, waiver may not suffice, because rights in an existing object, and certainly in land, require an act of transfer. If the partner who made the purchase was far away and reasonably expected that his fellow would not approve a transaction outside the normal partnership course, he may perhaps say that he bought for himself and never intended to include his partner. If the effectiveness of later consent rests only on waiver, that consent would not retroactively transfer the merchandise itself to the other partner, and the purchasing partner may therefore still be able to retract from sharing it.
