But if he divided, etc. The Rama adds that if one partner bought stolen property, the resulting loss is also shared. Meil Tzedakah, responsum 15, strongly questions this. At the end of siman 177 the Beit Yosef cites Rashba that if a partner tells the other, "Steal, and I will pay any loss that reaches you," the promise is ineffective because there is no agency for a transgression. Even if the promisor benefited from the theft he can say that he wanted the benefit only through a permitted act. Meil Tzedakah rejects the Sema's attempt to say that Rashba exempts only from the entire loss but requires payment of the promisor's ordinary partnership share. He also rejects the Levush's distinction that Rashba speaks before the partners had divided. In the responsum from which the Rama derived his rule, the purchaser apparently did not know the goods were stolen, and the subsequent danger could have reached the other partners as well. That does not establish liability where a partner knowingly steals or knowingly purchases stolen goods. Such conduct is a clear deviation from the partnership, and the transgressing partner bears the loss, while the other may take his share of any gain so long as the illegality remains undiscovered. He adds that when a partner deviates by buying wheat instead of barley and at first the other partner expects profit, the latter may still say, once a preexisting loss becomes known, that the deviator bears that loss, unless he had actually accepted the transaction itself. If the merchandise came into the other partner's possession and only afterward fell in value, that later depreciation is borne by him because the property had already entered his domain. But a loss that is later discovered to have existed from the outset is different. Agreement cannot create agency for a prohibited act. Therefore one who did not authorize the theft, but later shared the proceeds, need not share the resulting penalty or loss, though he must return whatever part of the stolen property or proceeds he received. The same is certainly true of a wife who bought stolen property without her husband's knowledge; his automatic acquisition of her gains does not make him a partner in the transgression.
