And they still have not spent them, etc. At first glance, the language of the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch implies that if the partners already invested the money, sold the merchandise for cash, and the currency then rose or fell because the sovereign altered its value, the gain or loss is divided equally even when the change occurred only after the sale. The Tur, citing the Ramah, appears to state this expressly. Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 29, questions this at length, as does Netivot HaMishpat, and explains that the Ramah cannot mean that the currency changed only after the sale. Once the merchandise has been sold and the partners are standing ready to divide, money is regarded as already divided, and there is no reason to attribute a later currency fluctuation to the partnership. Rather, the Ramah means that at the time the merchandise was sold the currency had already changed. For example, the partners purchased fruit jointly for three hundred zuz; the currency later depreciated, yet when they sold the fruit they received only the same nominal sum. That loss is shared equally, because it is really a loss in the partnership merchandise: had the fruit retained its relative value, they should have received more coins after the depreciation. Conversely, if the currency appreciated and they still sold the fruit for the same nominal sum, the gain is attributable to the merchandise having risen in value relative to the currency. In that sense the gain or loss arose from the partnership itself. On this reading, the Rambam's phrase that they had not yet engaged in trade excludes a case where the currency rose or fell before the merchandise was sold, for then the circumstances demonstrate that the gain or loss came through the partnership. A fluctuation only after the sale is not addressed. Thus, where two partners invested unequal sums and the currency changed only after the merchandise had been sold, the Rambam and Ramah do not clearly establish that the result is divided equally. Rashi, Tosafot, Ritva, and Riaz as cited by Shiltei Gibborim indicate that it follows the capital contributions. Beit Ephraim adds that this is the settled commercial practice in Israel. Where one partner put in one hundred and another two hundred, each first takes back the capital he contributed, without adjusting for a later rise or fall in the currency. One additional explanation is that the excess capital advanced by one partner is treated as though lent to the partnership, and a lender is repaid in the same currency unless the coinage itself has been replaced. Maharshal likewise treats unequal contributions as creating a creditor relationship between the partners. The Tur's statement that an express condition governs indicates that even currency gain or loss follows the parties' stipulation. Therefore, if their partnership agreement states that each first recovers what he contributed and only then is the balance divided, each takes back his original capital in the stipulated currency without regard to later appreciation or depreciation. Beit Ephraim further distinguishes foreign currency received in another country for partnership merchandise. If the capital currency itself did not change, but another currency received abroad rose or fell, everyone agrees that the partners must value that foreign currency in terms of the capital currency. The foreign coin is like merchandise in relation to their base currency, and each partner first recovers his capital in the agreed base currency; only the remainder is profit to be divided. He then applies these principles to Reuven and Shimon, who traded together in Warsaw. Merchants there customarily did not bring home the Prussian currency received for merchandise, but left it with an exchange broker and converted it into bank currency. Reuven needed money for himself and, with Shimon present and consenting, took bank currency from the broker at the current rate, on the understanding that Shimon could later take the same when he wished. Shimon did not do so until they were ready to depart, by which time the exchange rate had moved in favor of the Prussian currency. Shimon claimed that the exchange gain corresponding to the amount Reuven had earlier withdrawn belonged to him alone, while Reuven claimed it remained partnership profit. Beit Ephraim rules for Reuven. Reuven's withdrawal was not a formal division of the partnership. It was as though the parties had originally contributed unequal sums with an understanding that one might later withdraw a stated amount. Since Shimon saw the withdrawal and consented to leave his own funds in the partnership until he needed them, the continuing profits remained shared. Their written agreement also stated that everything was to be shared, and if Shimon intended this withdrawal to alter that rule he should have expressly stipulated so. Even if one were to regard Reuven's withdrawal as a partial division, Shimon's corresponding share was not understood to remain in Prussian currency, because their normal accounting and division were conducted in bank currency. The Prussian currency held by the broker was therefore merely an intermediate asset of the partnership. When Reuven received bank currency, Shimon was correspondingly credited with bank currency in the partnership account, and any subsequent gain in the Prussian currency remained joint profit. Shimon could not demand a division in Prussian coin contrary to the parties' established method of accounting. A judge sought support from a responsum of Penei Moshe, cited by Ateret Tzvi, concerning a partner who withdraws part of the partnership capital for a separate venture. There, profit from the separate venture is shared while a loss is borne by the withdrawing partner, and until he restores the capital the remaining partnership profits are divided according to the unequal capital then outstanding. Beit Ephraim rejects the comparison. That case involved capital needed for the partnership's trade, which the partner had no right to withdraw. Here, the sale proceeds simply lay idle with the exchange broker and produced no partnership profit. Withdrawal of idle funds, especially with the other partner's consent, did not reduce the withdrawing partner's share in profits earned by the partnership merchandise. Partners commonly take temporary withdrawals for personal use without recalculating their percentage of profits during the interval. Had Shimon intended otherwise, he needed to stipulate it. Beit Ephraim concludes that at final division each partner first receives the remaining amount of his capital in the currency in which it was contributed, and the balance is divided equally. This is even clearer if the Prussian currency appreciated before the remaining merchandise was sold, because in that event it can be said that there was no independent currency gain at all: the merchandise had fallen correspondingly and otherwise would have sold for more. But even if all the merchandise had already been sold at the earlier rate and only afterward did the Prussian currency appreciate, Shimon can demand repayment from the partnership only in the bank currency used by the parties for their accounting, and the exchange gain remains partnership property.
