The Shulchan Aruch elsewhere says a partner is not believed by oath alone to collect damages from his partner, which implies that if the loss is known he may sometimes collect from the partner's separate property. Taz explains that this follows the authorities who require such payment, while here the primary ruling follows Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia that a partner did not undertake to pay losses from his separate property unless expressly stipulated. Shev Yaakov asked why a partner who spent with permission is not like any authorized person who spends for another and can swear and collect. Radbaz answers that when partners contribute a fixed capital, each authorizes the other to act only with the partnership fund. He does not thereby agree that the acting partner can spend unlimited amounts from his private property and later collect them by oath. Tumim rejected that rationale. In his view partners never contemplate loss at all. Their whole purpose is profit. The agreement to share losses means only losses that can later be proved, not an unlimited power for one partner to swear and extract whatever he claims to have spent. For practical law I agree with Radbaz and Shev Yaakov, but for a different reason. Where one person gives all the capital on an ordinary half profit arrangement, the relationship is an iska, half loan and half deposit. The recipient is clearly a borrower as to the loan half and is liable for all accidents there. The business assets themselves function like security. A lender holding security who says the security was lost through unavoidable accident can swear and collect the established debt. Thus the investor can swear that the iska assets were lost and collect the loan half because the debt is certain and the only uncertainty is whether it was paid or extinguished. Ordinary partners are different. If one contributes two hundred and the other four hundred, neither is borrower of the other's capital. Business losses may be shared equally because that is how profits and losses from the enterprise were allocated, but theft or loss unrelated to trade is borne proportionally to capital. Currency depreciation likewise follows capital proportions. Since no personal loan debt exists, a claim that one partner suffered additional partnership expenses from his own property is merely, "Perhaps you became obligated to reimburse me." That is a doubtful obligation, and he cannot swear and collect. Accordingly, in a true heter iska document stating that the arrangement is an iska according to Rama's form, the investor can swear concerning loss of the pledged business assets and collect the loan half. But if he claims losses beyond the amount of the loan half, such as extraordinary expenses generated by the venture, the recipient can say, "I do not know whether I became obligated," and is exempt absent proof.
