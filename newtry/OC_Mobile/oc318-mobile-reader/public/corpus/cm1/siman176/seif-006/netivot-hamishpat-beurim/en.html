We do not say that he must pay from his own property. See Shach, seif katan 13. There is a scribal error there, and it should read, "not arising from the business transaction." In siman 93 the loss arose through the business transaction, and therefore if the partner knew of it the law differs. Taz has already written that he saw someone make this distinction and that he found no basis for it. In my view the matter can be explained as follows. Even where an ox purchased for slaughter was sold alive and the incremental profit from selling it alive is shared equally, any appreciation that had already accrued in the animal before the live sale is certainly divided according to capital. Suppose the ox appreciated for slaughter by one hundred. One partner owns only one tenth, so his share of that appreciation is ten. A merchant then appears and offers one additional dinar if they sell the ox alive, and they agree so as not to lose the extra dinar. It cannot be that merely because they sold it alive to obtain that extra dinar, the earlier one hundred of appreciation is suddenly divided fifty-fifty. Rather, the preexisting appreciation remains proportional to capital, and only the additional profit generated by the joined live sale is shared equally. Likewise, where Rama says that after the partners change their original plan profit follows capital, the profit that arose before the change remains shared according to the arrangement then governing. Why should the later change undo earlier profit? There is another point. If they bought an ox for plowing and earned ten gold pieces from plowing, which they divided equally although one partner had only one tenth of the capital, and afterward they slaughtered the ox and suffered a ten-gold-piece loss to principal, which under the law of slaughter follows capital so that the smaller investor bears only one gold piece of principal loss, it would be inequitable for him to keep a five-gold-piece profit while bearing only one of the later principal loss. Profit is subordinate to principal. Their original understanding in dividing profit was certainly subject to replenishing the principal if it later suffered loss. This is the case discussed in siman 93. They had already divided profit, and one partner later claimed that there had been a loss to principal, so that the other must return the excess profit previously received. Mechaber rules there that the claimant is not among those who swear and collect.
