And he is obligated to pay his fellow; see Netivot HaMishpat. I heard an explanation of the difficulty raised by Ketzot HaChoshen that distinguishes a custodian from a partner. A partner who is responsible for conducting the business may resemble a contractor and may therefore be liable even for lost profit. I have not now reviewed the passage. Perhaps liability for lost profit applies principally to merchandise designated for a particular market day, where the commercial value is tied specifically to that occasion. Just as property in a village may be appraised by the city market when that is its only real market, so merchandise prepared specifically for market day may be appraised according to the profit ordinarily obtainable then. Even so, lost-profit valuation should rest only on a known and concrete commercial connection, not speculation. Accordingly, even on this approach, special lost-profit liability applies only to the partner charged with selling the goods. In a case where one partner was responsible for small-change transactions and the capital owner himself sold in an unauthorized manner, the owner may owe actual damage but not necessarily speculative lost profit, because he was not acting as a hired commercial operator. He resembles an ordinary converter of another's property who pays its value at the time of conversion. Even where all partners participate in business, if the partner who sold contrary to instructions would not otherwise have been obligated personally to make the sale at all, the contractor analogy may not apply. If a partner failed to sell at the known proper time and an actual buyer then stood ready to pay substantially above ordinary value, the later decline in the ordinary value may be treated like a person who undertook, 'if I leave the field idle and do not work it, I will pay according to the expected produce.' But the extraordinary amount offered above market may not be fully attributable to the merchandise itself. Perhaps an excess of less than one-sixth, which merchants commonly tolerate without objection, still falls within ordinary value. If the bidder knew the prevailing market and nevertheless deliberately offered more, that strengthens the argument that the offer represented a real attainable value. Conversely, even where several merchants predicted that the goods would soon rise further, failure to sell at today's value may not be negligent if the partner reasonably expected additional profit. The Gemara itself recognizes that sellers may regret selling too soon. This requires careful commercial assessment.
