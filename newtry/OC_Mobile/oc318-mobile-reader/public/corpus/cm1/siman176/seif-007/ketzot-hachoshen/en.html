Beit Yosef rejected the view that one who was promised one third of whatever he collected on a debt thereby contributed capital to the later business. He reasoned that he never placed anything into the principal. At most he is entitled to compensation for his work. Darkei Moshe objected that he immediately acquired one third of the collected debt and therefore that third became his capital. If the promise itself were ineffective because the subject was future property, Beit Yosef would certainly be correct. Yet I wrote in siman 122 that a promise of one third of the collection can bind as wages, because wages can become enforceable by speech even concerning future proceeds. Still, the worker does not acquire one third of the actual debt itself. Since the debt is future property, he acquires only a monetary claim equal to one third. Ran says similarly that where one promised to sell a kor of wheat without a completed mode of acquisition, the other party has only a monetary claim, not ownership in the wheat. Rama rules likewise. Therefore, even after Reuven collects the debt, Shimon has no ownership in the actual collected coins. Reuven can pay him the equivalent value from his house. Even if Reuven intended while collecting to acquire one third for Shimon, one cannot acquire one's own money for another through unilateral intent in this manner. Moreover, where the coins came directly from a gentile debtor, acquisition for another Jew encounters the agency problem explained in siman 163. If Reuven says he had no intention to acquire for Shimon, the matter is clearer still. Shimon has a wage claim equal to one third of the collection, but he contributed nothing to the business principal. Beit Yosef is therefore correct.
