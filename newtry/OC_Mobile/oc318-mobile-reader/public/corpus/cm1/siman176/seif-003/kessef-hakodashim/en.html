Bach in Choshen Mishpat 176, cited by Shach, distinguishes between a loss caused through the partnership's business, for which a partner may have to make good the loss even beyond what he originally contributed, and a loss not caused by the business, for which he need not pay from his own funds beyond his contribution. If coins declined in value because they were retained for the purpose of the business, or goods were transported and an unavoidable accident occurred only because of that transport, perhaps these are losses caused by the business. The same may apply to merchandise whose normal profit depends on secretly crossing customs borders: if customs officers seize it, that may be regarded as a business loss. Perhaps such a loss resembles an animal that died because of its work for purposes of a bailee's liability. Partners are paid custodians for one another, and if a partner holds partnership money that he is permitted to use, liability for unavoidable accidents may arise. Yet where the very business consists in evading customs, seizure may be considered a loss resulting from the work itself. If one partner withdrew more than he originally contributed, then even if his share of losses is measured according to the original contribution, where the amount of the loss is unknown the case may be one of 'I do not know whether I ever became obligated,' as in siman 93. If one individual is appointed over the partnership funds, the fact that people call him the trusted person does not itself make his testimony equivalent even to that of one neutral witness, because he is interested in the matter. Perhaps even an express agreement that he be believed as two witnesses or as one valid witness requires a kinyan sudar, especially where he is to be believed as two, which is doubly detrimental to the others. However, if he had no authority to hand money to any partner and nevertheless gave it to one because he trusted that partner to repay on demand, the recipient can be sued. The case may resemble the rule in siman 91 that in a matter known to one party and not to the other, the knowledgeable party may swear and collect. According to several explanations of that rule, this should apply here because the recipient took the money by permission and for his own benefit. Ordinary partners are less clearly agents for one another where the business duties were not specifically assigned, but an individual specifically appointed to conduct all the business may be treated differently. This requires study. If, at the very beginning of the partnership, the partners expressly stipulated that a particular person would be believed as two witnesses or as one, a separate kinyan sudar is unnecessary because they contributed their funds on that condition. Perhaps such a stipulation even governs losses beyond the amount originally contributed, if the substantive law itself imposes such excess liability, because the partnership agreement is capable of creating obligations beyond each person's initial contribution. That additional obligation may arise through the compensation each partner receives for his business activity. Partnership commerce is unlike the ferryman case, since people commonly pay substantial compensation for genuine commercial activity, much as for medical services. This may be the reason Bach holds that a partner can be liable beyond his contribution for losses arising directly from the business. Yet it is difficult to say that the partners contemplated unlimited liability for every excess loss merely because of the value of their labor. The natural relationship is between the work and the profits and losses generated by the business. If one partner said that additional funds should be borrowed for the partnership, he is responsible for repayment. If the transaction produces a loss beyond the original contributions and the amount attributable to each partner is uncertain, the claimant may face the rule of 'I do not know whether you owe me.' If the partners waived partnership oaths, that waiver may prevent an oath as well. Where a lender advanced funds only in reliance upon one partner, if that partner had been authorized to borrow on behalf of the others the debt falls upon them. Subsequent uncertainty whether it was repaid is then 'I do not know whether I repaid you,' even if the factual doubt arose before the loan was formally accounted for, because there was no prior settlement offsetting the accounts. Silence, however, may not always count as consent to a loan that imposes liability beyond one's contribution, even if silence can amount to consent regarding ordinary partnership operations. Siman 77 contains distinctions concerning one partner's liability for a loan undertaken by his fellows. Likewise, even express consent given later may not create liability beyond the original investment in the same way as a stipulation made at formation. If the partners jointly consented to appoint a third person as custodian and later suspect him, their own assent to entrust him may have implications akin to guarantor liability for the resulting loss.
