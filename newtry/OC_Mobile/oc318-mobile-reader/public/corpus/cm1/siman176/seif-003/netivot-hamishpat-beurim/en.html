For even a kinyan is unnecessary. See Shach, seif katan 6, who writes that it is obvious that this also applies to the beginning of the siman, regarding other partners. His words imply that, according to this view, with every partnership, even where the parties join movable property, such as one having honey and the other wine, as soon as they agree verbally that everything shall be held in partnership no kinyan is required, and each immediately acquires half of the other's property. This is very difficult. The principal rationale of these poskim is that because each wishes to acquire, he fully resolves to transfer, as explained in siman 207, seif 13, in the gloss. If that reasoning applied even to ordinary movable property, then in an exchange of an ox for a cow we should likewise say that because each wishes to acquire, he fully resolves to transfer, and no kinyan would be necessary. Yet the Gemara expressly says that once one party performs meshichah on his item, the other becomes obligated in the exchange, which proves that before one party's meshichah there is no acquisition at all. We find this principle, that because one wishes to acquire he fully resolves to transfer, only regarding a davar shelo ba la'olam, where ordinary acquisition cannot take hold at all. Since one wishes to acquire and cannot make a complete present kinyan, he transfers to his fellow without a kinyan so that he in turn can acquire from his fellow without a kinyan, as Sema writes in siman 209, seif katan 30. The same applies to asmachta where the person does not now wish to transfer outright, such as gambling in siman 207, seif 13, and in siman 173 regarding a lottery, and similar cases in which he does not presently know what he will transfer. But where a person intends to transfer a presently existing item immediately and can do so through a proper kinyan or meshichah, the principle does not apply. On the contrary, because meshichah is lacking, we say that had he truly completed his intent to transfer he would have made a full kinyan. See what I wrote in siman 207, seif 13, for proof. Accordingly, with partners, if they stipulate regarding a davar shelo ba la'olam, such as two tailors agreeing that all work that comes to them shall be shared, the agreement can be completed verbally without a kinyan. Likewise, if they stipulate that every profit that comes into either one's hands, whether through my business or yours, shall be divided, or that each will transact with his own money and divide the profit, verbal agreement can suffice because each becomes like a hired worker for the other. The same applies to a personal obligation, because a person can bind himself through a complete personal undertaking. Even then, however, it appears that they must have already begun performing the partnership, for they are no better than workers who may retract before beginning work. Likewise, if they agree that every purchase that comes to town shall belong jointly to all of them, either may retract before the merchandise arrives, just as in siman 173, according to the view that a lottery acquires, the parties can certainly retract before the lottery is drawn. The same applies to two tailors, who must begin work, because they are no better than workers. But if they wish to transfer the body of existing wine or oil so that it shall presently belong jointly to both for all purposes, including liability for ones and the ability to use it to acquire slaves or land, a full kinyan is required. Were it not that Maharik appears to hold that even regarding an existing object this partnership principle works, I would say otherwise. We must therefore conclude that an actual partnership is different: because the partners rely upon one another, each fully resolves to transfer even an existing object.
Regarding what they will earn. I am troubled by this rule, because it creates an inequitable result. Suppose they form a partnership for one year. In the first month Reuven happens to receive work and earns one hundred, while Shimon receives no work. In the second month Shimon is about to receive work that will earn one hundred, but before earning it he says that he retracts as to future earnings. Reuven would then have to divide what he already earned, because he cannot retract from past earnings, while Shimon could keep his own future profit because he may retract prospectively. Moreover, since Shimon does not wish to give Reuven any part of what he will earn at that time, why should Reuven be required to give Shimon what he earned earlier? Reuven never intended to join with him and grant him even one day's profit except in exchange for Shimon likewise giving him the profits of the entire year. Maharik, root 181, writes this explicitly, saying that even Rambam agrees that a person cannot enjoy the partnership at its beginning while the others receive no benefit from him during the period the parties intended. Although one could distinguish that there the later value increase was known, whereas here each day's earnings are uncertain, it nevertheless appears that the same principle applies. If one hires a worker for a day and promises as his wage whatever found objects he will lift that day, the employer can certainly retract before the worker lifts a particular found object and say that he is lifting it for himself. Nevertheless, the employer must pay the worker the value of his labor. Siman 332 states that if one promises a particular object as wages, even though the worker does not acquire that object, the employer must pay its value. Likewise, if he promises a davar shelo ba la'olam as wages, he must pay its value. Here too, each partner is both employer and worker with respect to the other. When Reuven receives the benefit of Shimon's labor, Reuven is like Shimon's employer and must pay for that labor by giving him whatever Reuven agreed would constitute the wage, namely all the profit Reuven earns in his own work during that year. It makes no difference that Shimon can retract from performing future labor. Even where the promised wage is a particular object or a davar shelo ba la'olam from which the employer may retract as to the object itself, he still owes its full value. If he promised all the fruit that a particular palm would produce as wages, and the palm was later blessed with abundant fruit, he owes the entire amount as wages. Therefore, the meaning of the rule must be this. When it says that they cannot retract regarding what they have earned, it means that if they formed a partnership for a fixed period, when the time arrives they must divide whatever each earned during the binding period. One cannot then refuse to give his fellow the share of profit already earned. The very objects each acquired as compensation for his labor become jointly owned as agreed. By contrast, when it says that they can retract regarding what they will earn, it means that a party may withdraw before receiving any future profit and before the corresponding future labor is performed.
Regarding what they have earned. See Shach, seif katan 8. I will first set out the positions of the poskim. Rambam holds that in a partnership involving presently existing property, meshichah of the movable property is effective even prospectively, so that the partners cannot retract for the future. This is not like an ordinary worker, who can retract, for the reason Sema gives in seif katan 44: since both are working in the enterprise, neither has the status of a servant, and after beginning to transact they are bound as partners. But where the partnership concerns a davar shelo ba la'olam, such as artisans joining their future handiwork, even a kinyan does not help, and they may retract even from what was already earned, because this resembles the fruit of a palm, from which one may retract even after the fruit came into existence, as Shach writes in seif katan 5. Raavad holds that a kinyan sudar is effective even regarding a davar shelo ba la'olam, because the kinyan attaches to the workers' hands for their future labor, like transferring a palm for its fruit, so that everything their hands produce shall be jointly owned. This resembles one who says, "My hands are consecrated to what they produce." Therefore it is not treated like an ordinary worker and they cannot retract even prospectively. Raavad's view without a kinyan is not explicit. Rashba, where there is a kinyan, follows Raavad. Without a kinyan he disagrees with Rambam in two respects. In an ordinary partnership Rambam holds that once they begin transacting they cannot retract even prospectively, because they are not ordinary workers. Rashba disagrees and holds that they are comparable to workers and may retract prospectively. Although Rashba says this specifically concerning artisans, the reason he gives, that a worker may retract, rejects Sema's rationale in seif katan 44 and therefore applies equally to partners dealing in a davar shelo ba la'olam. Concerning artisans, Rambam allows retraction even from what has already been earned, whereas Rashba disagrees and holds that regarding what was already earned they cannot retract even without a kinyan, because it resembles one who lifts a found object for another. Ramban regarding a davar shelo ba la'olam follows Rambam that a kinyan is ineffective, as Tur cites in his name, while regarding presently existing property he follows Rashba. Maharik, root 181, writes that Raavad too holds that without a kinyan one cannot retract from what has already been earned, but may retract from future earnings, like Ramban and Rashba. Accordingly, Shach's statement that one cannot retract from what has already been earned only where there was a kinyan, together with his conclusion that there is no distinction between artisans and ordinary partners, runs contrary to all these approaches. According to all poskim, one cannot retract from what was already earned even without a kinyan, except that Rambam, concerning artisans, allows retraction, and according to Rambam even a kinyan would not help. Shach also contradicts himself, because in seif katan 9 he writes that regarding the past all agree no kinyan is necessary. It therefore appears that there is a scribal error in Shach, and it should read: "But not regarding what they already earned, even without a kinyan." Shach's further statement that they may retract from what they will earn even where there was a kinyan, followed by the statement that there is no distinction between artisans and partners, is likewise very difficult. Mechaber at the end of seif 15 rules without qualification that partners cannot divide during the fixed term, and Darkei Moshe explains that partners cannot retract even where there was no kinyan beyond meshichah of the movable property, because unlike ordinary workers, their money became bound for the enterprise until the agreed term. Even if one says that this was written only with respect to partners rather than artisans, and that Shach's statement that there is no distinction refers only to past earnings, his ruling remains only the position of Rambam and Ramban. I have not found that the other poskim reject Raavad's view that a kinyan does help. Maharik, Maharam Alashkar, and Maharit, part 1, siman 140, indicate otherwise. As a matter of halachah, therefore, where partners join in presently existing property and make a kinyan sudar, or even perform meshichah on the movable property, which is a complete kinyan as Maharit writes, they cannot retract even prospectively. If there was no kinyan and they merely began to transact, a party can say kim li like Ramban and Rashba that retraction is possible prospectively. But concerning what was already earned, all agree that retraction is impossible. As for artisans whose partnership concerns a davar shelo ba la'olam, where there was a kinyan there is strong basis to rule that they cannot retract even prospectively, because Rambam and Ramban are individual opinions against the other authorities. Regarding what was already earned, it appears that even without a kinyan they cannot retract.
And everything they will earn. See Shach, seif katan 9, through the words, "But regarding the past he agrees that a kinyan is unnecessary." I do not understand his intention. Even prospectively, according to Rambam, no kinyan is needed once they began to transact. If so, why does Shach single out the past as not requiring a kinyan? His wording also cannot readily be reconciled, and the responsum of Maharik, root 181, that he cites does not contain the proposition in this form. It therefore appears that an evident omission occurred in Shach. The text should read: "For the reason Sema writes in seif katan 11, this is according to Rambam. But according to Raavad, whom Rama cites in the gloss, without a kinyan it is ineffective to prevent prospective retraction; regarding the past, however, he agrees that a kinyan is unnecessary. See Maharik, root 181." This is indeed the substance of Maharik.
