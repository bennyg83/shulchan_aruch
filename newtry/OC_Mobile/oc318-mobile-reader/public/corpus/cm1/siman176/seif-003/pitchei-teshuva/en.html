After reviewing the Rishonim concerning the binding force of a partnership, Chatam Sofer writes that all of those rules concern partners who entered into partnership by agreement. Matters established by mercantile custom are different. Even where the custom does not match the strict law of Torah it may not be altered, for mercantile custom itself is binding, as with donkey drivers and sailors who may not deviate from established practice. This applies not only to collection of money but to all matters governed by their commercial usage. Chatam Sofer qualifies this where both partners are themselves engaged in the partnership's business. If one person is separately occupied with his own trade, he need not surrender a collateral profit simply because custom makes that type of gain common property, unless he receives compensation for the labor and opportunity he lost while producing that gain. It is unjust that one partner do the work and the other simply share the profit. He compares this to the Rosh's responsum concerning cobblers who agreed to be partners in everything except their manual labor: even if tanning work were included, the one who did it would first retain what he could have earned at his ordinary trade. So too the Rama rules that one who performs government work first receives ordinary wages for his labor before the balance is shared. The case before Chatam Sofer involved Reuven and Shimon, who jointly hired a wagon to take their wool to the Pest fair. On the road Reuven found an opportunity to buy additional wool and bought it for himself. Shimon then claimed that local commercial practice required all opportunities arising during a joint journey to be shared, and Reuven accepted that. At the fair they decided that hides should be purchased for the return trip. Reuven told Shimon to buy them and said they would be jointly owned, just as Reuven's wool had become joint property. Shimon bought the hides but then said he would share them only if Reuven paid ten gold coins for his labor. Reuven was silent, and on returning home Shimon refused to give Reuven any share. Chatam Sofer ruled for Shimon. Inquiry among merchants established that when merchants travel together to a fair, incidental gains that arise on the road are shared even if they were never formal partners, and both parties themselves understood this usage. Reuven's additional wool was therefore joint property. But once they reached the market, each attended to his own principal business, and no accepted custom required every incidental profit at the fair to be shared. Thus Reuven's claim to the hides depended only on Shimon's verbal assent. Even express assent did not bind Shimon because the parties had not become mutually obligated with respect to this new transaction, and Reuven had supplied no reciprocal undertaking that would create the mutual benefit by which one party's acquisition binds the other. If, however, local custom could be proven that merchants at the fair also share incidental gains, the custom would bind, but Reuven could not simply work at his own merchandise while taking a share of Shimon's hide transaction without allowing Shimon compensation for his lost market time. Experts would first assess Shimon's lost earning opportunity, and the remainder would then be shared. Possession cannot alter these rules, because Chatam Sofer regarded the governing law as clear.
