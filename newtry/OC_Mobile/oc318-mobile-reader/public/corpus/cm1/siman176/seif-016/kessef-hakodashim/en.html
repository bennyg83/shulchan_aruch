Partners who operate a shop containing merchandise that by its nature is sold gradually, piece by piece, so that the shop must maintain a broad assortment, cannot ordinarily demand immediate division merely because many identical units exist. Nor can one insist upon immediate liquidation through 'buy or I will buy' or competitive bidding between the partners. The commercial nature of the goods itself creates an understood selling period, even without a fixed calendar date, because time is needed to sell them gradually. The shop premises likewise cannot necessarily be divided so that each partner separately sells his own merchandise. If they sell the same kind of goods it is obviously impractical, and even if each owns a different line, one may say that he cannot be responsible for customers entering for the other's goods while also protecting his own. After the underlying goods are properly divided, however, the partners may be able to invoke 'buy or I will buy' regarding the leasehold of the shop. All of this requires further study. If partners sold liquor on condition that the buyer come on a certain day, pay, and take delivery, but the buyer failed to appear, and one partner wishes immediately to divide the goods and transport his share elsewhere while the other is willing to wait a few days, the controlling factor is commercial practice. If merchants ordinarily do not treat a short delay as cancellation, the sale remains effective despite possible price fluctuation or deterioration of the barrel, and the disagreement between Taz and Nekudot HaKesef in Yoreh Deah 236 does not require otherwise. If merchants ordinarily are strict, the sale may be cancelled as against the buyer, but that does not automatically give one partner a right to force immediate division where the partners never stipulated such a right. Even where prompt division would have been commercially advantageous because buyers are presently available or because the goods are sitting on hired wagons that will continue to incur charges, if it is genuinely uncertain whether merchants treat the buyer's delay as material, neither partner can compel the other, since each is in possession of his own partnership share. If the buyer delays beyond ordinary commercial tolerance so that the sale is clearly cancelled, and the delay creates wagon charges, liability depends upon who dealt with the wagon owner. If the buyer himself arranged that the wagons would wait, the waiting charge is his. If the goods were already on hired wagons awaiting prompt transport and the sale merely caused them to remain there without any express new rental agreement, the wagon owner may nevertheless deserve compensation. Although there is no ordinary Sabbath-loss liability for an animal and unauthorized use of another's animal can sometimes be exempt, where the use was intended as a rental rather than as theft the law differs. If the original hire was by days, siman 310 indicates payment for each day. Even where the hire was calculated by distance, ordinary trade usage may include payment for an unreasonable extra day or two of waiting. The buyer, however, who never hired the wagon owner is generally only an indirect cause of that expense unless he expressly undertook the waiting charge.
