And some disagree. Baer Heitev cites the Taz, who rules like the dissenting opinion. Maayan Ganim writes that if the principal himself redeemed the imprisoned agent, the agent need not reimburse him, because everyone agrees that the imprisonment arose through the principal's conduct. This is especially so where the agent can plead kim li. Even according to the view that one who pays another's debt to a gentile without authorization may recover because he conferred a benefit, here the principal may be viewed as acting to cure a harm he himself caused. Maayan Ganim further suggests that even where the principal used money belonging to the agent without the agent's instruction, the agent may object that the principal had no right to use his funds, especially where the agent protested and maintained that he could have escaped without payment. In that event the principal's payment may be treated as made for his own sake because he had caused the imprisonment. This is even stronger where the authorities had some independent claim against the principal as well. He nevertheless leaves the practical rule requiring further consideration. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 52, argues that there may be no real dispute. The source that makes the agent liable as a borrower speaks of imprisonment that did not result from the principal's mission. Ramban, by contrast, discusses imprisonment caused by the mission itself, which resembles an object that died because of the work for which it was borrowed. Thus, any bodily loss not caused by the mission may require the principal to redeem the agent, while an injury inherent in the mission may be treated differently. Chatam Sofer also addresses the argument of Chacham Tzvi that if liability is derived by a fortiori reasoning from monetary borrowing, the rule of "it is enough for the conclusion to be like the premise" should import the exemption of borrowing while the owner is employed. Chatam Sofer answers that the bodily case is not structurally identical to ordinary borrowing: in monetary borrowing there are two separate bodies, owner and object, whereas here the person is in a sense both owner and subject of the service. Because the exemption of "its owner is with him" is itself a special scriptural rule, one need not extend it beyond its precise form. He therefore adds that if an employer hires a father while also borrowing the services of the father's minor daughter without pay, and the daughter suffers bodily loss even without the employer's fault, the employer may be exempt because the father, who has legal rights in the daughter's labor and may sell her as a maidservant, is simultaneously employed by him. Chatam Sofer develops the point further in his discussion of the Mordechai.
