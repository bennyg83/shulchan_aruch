It appears to me that the same rule applies, etc. The words of Rama are astonishing in my humble opinion. It is wholly unreasonable that a Jew should be able to seize on behalf of another Jew where the loss will also fall upon the second partner. How can this person seize and thereby cause loss to his fellow, who owes him nothing? This is exactly analogous to the ruling of Rivash, siman 393: Leah can say to Rachel, 'Since I am liable to Reuven for this damage, you are causing me this loss, and it is as though you are seizing from my property rather than Reuven's.' Rivash further writes that even though Rachel has a migo, she must return the property to Leah, for Leah must return it to Reuven, and Rachel is now robbing Leah and seizing Leah's property. See there. Rama cites Rivash's view above, siman 58, in the gloss. Even the dissenting opinion cited there would seemingly agree here. There, because it was known that the money belonged exclusively to the principal, the claimant could seize it, and the agent was negligent in failing to take a document and was therefore liable. Here that reasoning does not apply. This is certainly so according to what I wrote above in siman 58, that Rivash and those who agree with him represent the primary ruling. At first sight one could defend Rama by explaining that he speaks where the Jewish possessor fled, traveled overseas, or lacks means to pay. Yet Rama's words in Darkei Moshe do not imply this. He writes that even when the possessor is Jewish, Reuven need only swear that he owes him nothing; the borrower swears that Reuven does owe him, does not pay, and the partners sustain the loss. The Ir Shushan similarly writes that if the debtor is Jewish, he must take a shevuat heset by virtue of a migo that he paid or that no debt ever existed, thereby also causing the claimant to swear against his partner. Their words therefore require study. I later found in Responsa Divrei Rivot, siman 276, that movable property may not be seized from a partnership except for one half, while the other half remains presumptively the property of the other partner wherever it is, because appraisal is required. Money, however, may be seized because it is treated as already divided; we assign the seized portion to the partner against whom the claim lies, and the seizure is effective for all of it, while the other partner takes an equivalent amount. His words explicitly establish that the innocent partner may not suffer loss. Whenever he would suffer loss, the property cannot be seized, as I wrote. Even regarding money, however, his ruling requires study. Although money is treated as divided when a partner wishes to divide it, who has heard that an outside claimant may compel such a division? This therefore requires practical study. In my humble opinion, Rama in Darkei Moshe and the Ir Shushan must be understood as discussing property not known to belong to the partnership. The possessor says, 'I do not believe that this is partnership property,' while the partners admit to each other that it is partnership property and maintain that no debt is owed. In that case the law is as Rama states. This appears correct to me. See Responsa Rashdam, siman 167, though the matter requires study.
