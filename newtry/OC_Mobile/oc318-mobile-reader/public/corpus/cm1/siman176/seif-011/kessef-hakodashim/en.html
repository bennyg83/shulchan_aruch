The profit is divided equally. If a partner settles a claim he had against someone in partnership business, and through the settlement the defendant undertakes an obligation that is likely to produce profit for that partner, and eventually profit results, while the second partner had initially objected to pursuing the claim, the law requires distinction. If the defendant had also asserted a counterclaim arising from that very dispute and summoned the partner to judgment, the partner was compelled to appear, and the partner who objected to going to court is a refuser. If the defendant would settle only all claims together, the pressure to settle is even stronger. This is not necessarily like a partner who trades in prohibited goods or otherwise deviates, where profit belongs to the partnership, because the acting partner can say: since my settlement does not bind your share, it is as though I acquired the resulting right with my own property. Yet according to the Shach in siman 77, a partner's legal act is not void as to his fellow; rather, if he caused a wrong he must reimburse the other partner. According to that approach the settlement may affect both unless he expressly limited it to his own share. If the defendant did not know of the partnership and dealt only with one partner, and the other partner had expressly protested against bringing the claim, perhaps the settling partner's undertaking is more like an independent acquisition with his own funds. If the defendant assumed a penalty obligation to the settling partner and profit later arose, even if the partner transfers that right to the second partner by kinyan sudar it may not be effective until the right embodied in the promissory instrument is transferred through the form required by Chazal. This requires further study. If a partner negligently fails to sell animals in an expensive market and instead takes them to a cheaper place, the resulting loss may fall on him while any profit remains shared, as with other partnership negligence. Although one who physically takes another's animal from an expensive market to a cheaper one, or locks up another's movable property until prices fall, may be exempt in human courts and liable only in Heaven, a partner may bear stricter responsibility because of his partnership undertaking. If he says he did not sell because of a dispute with his partner and feared wrongful seizure, this excuse depends on whether the dispute was to be adjudicated in the cheaper market and whether his fear had reasonable basis. If only an agreed messenger was there and that messenger refused to sell, there may be no liability on the messenger beyond ordinary law. Partners who agreed that each must pay at a fixed time with a penalty if one fails: the penalty falls only on the defaulting partner, though the creditor may sue both, and afterward the partner who paid may recover from his fellow. If a partner deviates and brings goods to a cheaper place, the loss is his; if he did so with permission, the accounting may follow the place of sale or perhaps the place where the deviation occurred. The matter becomes more severe where he deviated before transport. Similar questions arise with an agent whose deviation caused loss. If partners are engaged in several businesses and one buys merchandise, then after purchase declares he does not want a certain partner to share in it, and the venture later loses money, while he claims he intended partnership at the time of purchase and the other says his later declaration proves the opposite, the partner seeking contribution is like one who says, 'I do not know whether you became obligated,' and cannot extract the loss. If he is in possession, however, the other cannot impose an oath based on a merely uncertain claim, though a general ban may be possible. Where there is an ongoing partnership oath because the accounts remain unsettled and there is room for suspicion, an oath may still be relevant. If one partner was charged with buying goods in a cheaper place but negligently bought in an expensive place, perhaps this is not merely lost-profit causation. It may resemble an obligation to supply the partnership a certain amount of merchandise at an agreed price. Yet if the agreement was only that he would travel to buy, perhaps he is like a worker who may withdraw. According to those who hold that a partner cannot withdraw from future partnership obligations, perhaps partnership law limits the ordinary worker's right of withdrawal. According to those who allow withdrawal as to the future, perhaps he may terminate the partnership rather than travel. In any event, the extent of damages is uncertain because no one knows whether the journey would have succeeded. If the other partner observed the neglect and did not protest, he may have waived the travel condition, especially where conditions in the wine market were unusual and there was no certainty that buying at the production site would be cheaper.
