Testimony. The Shach writes that the Rama's wording is imprecise. The conclusion from the Mordechai is as follows. Where Shimon has testimony that these debts are still owed to him, even if he has no testimony that Reuven released the debtors, Reuven is not believed to say, "I released them with your permission," by virtue of a migo that he could have said, "I did not release them." This is a migo against a strong presumption, because we are effectively witnesses that a person does not deliberately destroy his own property. If there is no testimony that the debts remain unpaid, however, Reuven is believed to say, "I released them with your permission," by virtue of the migo that he could have said, "I did not release them." In that situation the migo is not against witnesses, because perhaps the debts had already been paid and that is why Shimon authorized their release. This is stated more explicitly in the Hagahot Maimoniyot responsa to Sefer Nezikin, siman 12, and the Agudah cites the same in the name of Maharam. Maharshal likewise rules so in Bava Kamma, chapter 8, siman 61. The Rama himself writes in siman 382 that where it is certain that a person caused damage, he is not believed to say that he acted with permission even when he has a migo. The Rama's words here therefore require study. It appears that even if Reuven does not know whether the debts were paid, so long as there are no witnesses that they remain unpaid, he is believed to say, "I released them with his permission," by virtue of a migo that he could have denied releasing them, where there are no witnesses that he released them. But if Reuven admits that the debts remain unpaid, he appears liable, because the migo is then against the presumption that a person does not knowingly destroy his own property. Maharshal further writes that Reuven may say, "Swear to me that you did not instruct me to release him," no worse than one holding a promissory note who may be required to swear that the debt was not paid. The Shach disputes him on this point. He concludes, however, that where there are no witnesses that the non-Jew paid this debt but there are witnesses that Reuven released him, Maharshal's view appears correct that Reuven may demand an oath. Since the damage is not certain, perhaps Shimon was not damaged at all. Shimon must therefore swear that the debts had not been paid and that he did not authorize Reuven to release them, after which Reuven pays according to what the claims were worth if sold. This is the ruling in Maharam's responsum cited by the Mordechai, the Agudah, and Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Maharshal likewise rules so. The Rama did not need to explain it here because he relied on what he wrote in siman 66, seif 32. The same must be understood below in siman 386, seif 2. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 44. Shach.
