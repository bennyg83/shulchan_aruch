A debt owed by a certain nobleman. Rama's gloss that the partner himself made the loan must be understood as a loan made with his partner's knowledge. If he made it without his partner's knowledge, he is plainly liable, as explained earlier in this seif. In my view the case also must be like the Rosh's responsum: after making the loan, the partner went to live under that nobleman's authority, and thus was not negligent at the time of the loan. Alternatively, he may already have lived there but the fear arose only after the loan. But if at the time he made the loan he already lived under the nobleman's control and was afraid to demand payment from him, there can be no greater negligence. He should have informed his partner at the time of the loan that he himself would be unable to demand payment. If he did not, he directly endangered the debt, because neither he nor his partner could practically collect it. The case must therefore be one where he moved under the nobleman's authority only after making the loan, and that move was effectively compelled because he could not reside elsewhere.
Reuven went and released them. See Shach, seif katan 25. Shach first notes that Rama's phrase, "and he has witnesses that the debts belonged to him," is imprecise, because even if witnesses prove that the debts were his, perhaps they had already been paid. This seems at first surprising. Shach himself later in this seif writes that although Reuven does not know whether the debts had been paid, he is believed to say, "I released them with your permission," through a migo that he could have denied releasing them. This implies that without that migo he would not be believed. If Rama speaks where witnesses establish both the debt and Reuven's release, why should Reuven be believed at all? Shach's second objection should therefore be understood differently. Even if Rama means that the witnesses establish only that the debts originally belonged to the claimant, while Reuven himself admits that he released them, the wording remains inaccurate. What does proof of their original existence accomplish if perhaps they had already been paid? Rama should have said that there are witnesses the debts were still unpaid when Reuven released them. Shach's conclusion is thus: where witnesses establish that the debts were still outstanding, Reuven is not believed to say he released them with authorization, even if he has a migo. Where there are no witnesses that they remained unpaid, Reuven is believed to say he acted with authorization only if he has a migo. Without a migo he is not believed, although the creditor must swear that he was not paid and did not authorize the release. The case must therefore concern written debts, where the outstanding instrument creates a chazakah that the debt remains unpaid. A migo can prevail against that chazakah, because it derives from the document rather than independent witnesses. The same framework explains Shach in siman 382, seif katan 2, where he says one is believed to claim, "I did not damage you," meaning that although he released debts, the debtors would not have paid anyway. At first glance this claim should be believed without a migo, because the injured party must prove the amount of his loss. Nor can we say that the injured party swears and collects under an enactment for one whose property was damaged, because Mechaber does not rule that way in siman 386, and in cases of informing to authorities even the enactment is doubtful. Furthermore, where such an enactment applies, a migo does not ordinarily help if the defendant admits being a damager. Rather, Shach there was answering Sema, who explained Rama's words, "he is believed to say I did not damage," as meaning, "I acted with your permission." That is difficult, because Rama had already ruled that where there are no witnesses to the release the defendant is believed through a migo, and Sema's explanation would make the later statement redundant. Shach therefore explains, "I did not damage" as meaning: "I released the debts, but in any event they would not have paid because they had defenses against you." In such a case, where there are no witnesses proving the debts remained collectible, the defendant can further say, "Because you knew they had valid defenses, you authorized me to release them." If witnesses establish that the debts were unquestionably unpaid and collectible, he cannot say he caused no damage, and then he likewise cannot claim authorization against the chazakah that a person does not ordinarily destroy his own property. But where collectibility is known only from the outstanding document, the migo can help against the document's chazakah. The basic rule, however, remains very difficult. If the debt is owed by a Jew and witnesses establish that it remains unpaid, then even if Reuven invalidated the original instrument the debtor still owes the money and a replacement instrument can sometimes be written, as explained in siman 386. If the debt is owed by a non Jew, the difficulty also remains. Why should this be compared to a case where one claims, "You killed my ox," in which there is a chazakah that a person does not destroy his own property? Suppose Shimon holds an object belonging to Reuven and says, "Reuven instructed me to give this object as a gift to Levi." Surely Shimon can be believed through an appropriate migo. Is there a chazakah that a person never gives gifts? Release of a debt resembles a gift. A person may release a debt because he received some other benefit. This is why gift law often parallels sale law. Shach in siman 56, seif katan 5, likewise writes that if Levi holds a debt instrument belonging to Reuven against Shimon and says, "Reuven instructed me to return it to Shimon," he is believed as a third-party holder even when the parties deny the underlying deposit arrangement. We do not say there is a chazakah that a person does not waive his debt. Likewise, a borrower who claims, "You waived my debt," can be believed with a migo; the migo is not treated as standing against a chazakah that no one waives debts. Why then should one who says, "You instructed me to waive your debtor's obligation," face such a chazakah? Mechaber also speaks broadly of "debts," which can include oral debts. In such a case, if Reuven testified before a non-Jewish tribunal that the debtor was released and the debtor therefore escaped payment, why should Reuven be worse than a witness who testifies in a non-Jewish court and later says, "I testified truthfully," as in siman 28? It therefore appears that the present law concerns partners. The non Jew recognizes only the partner who made the debt, such as where the instrument is written in his name or where he made the oral loan himself. That partner then releases the debtor explicitly in his own name, representing the debt as entirely his, and afterward says that his fellow partner authorized him to do so. This is true damage to the partner's property. The releasing partner himself receives no apparent benefit from the alleged release and cannot say he merely conveyed another person's gift. The same can apply with a Jewish debtor where the instrument is written only in the releasing partner's name and he gives a binding personal release that cannot later be contradicted. In that setting he is treated as one who directly damaged the partnership asset, and the special rules above apply.
