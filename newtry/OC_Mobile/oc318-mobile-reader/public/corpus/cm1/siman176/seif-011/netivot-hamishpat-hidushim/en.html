The Shach concludes that if witnesses establish that the debts had not yet been paid, Reuven is not believed even where he has a migo, because the claim stands against the presumption that a person does not gratuitously release his own collectible debts and cause himself loss. If there are no witnesses that the debts remain unpaid, then where he has a migo, such as where there are no witnesses that he released them, he is believed. If witnesses establish that he released them and he therefore lacks a migo, he is not believed, although the lender must swear that the gentile did not pay and that he never instructed Reuven to release the debt. See Beurim: this applies specifically to a documented loan, which carries a presumption of nonpayment. With an oral loan, Reuven can be believed. Also, the case of instruction to release concerns where he says he was told to release the debt in the agent's own name, for example because the debtor knew that the agent had been a partner when the debt arose. There is then no reason for the debtor to feel gratitude toward the true creditor, since he believes the release came from the agent. But if the agent says he was instructed to waive the debt in the creditor's name, he is believed where he has a migo, because regarding waiver or gift there is no presumption that a person would never choose to give a gift.
