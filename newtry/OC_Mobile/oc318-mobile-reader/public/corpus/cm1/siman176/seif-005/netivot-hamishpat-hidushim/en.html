According to their numbers. See Beurim, from which many distinctions emerge. First, if they formed a partnership to trade in merchandise and both are equally obligated to exert themselves in selling, the profit is divided equally, because the partner who invested less must work just as much as the partner who invested more. The same applies where they bought an ox to fatten, improve, and slaughter, or any similar venture where the labor burden is equal. Second, profit arising after the agreed time for division is allocated according to capital, because neither partner can then compel the other to continue working on the partnership merchandise. Third, if they began trading under an agreed term and later mutually decided to abandon that business and engage in another, but had not yet begun the new business, later profit on the first business is allocated according to capital because the original labor obligation ended. Once they begin the new agreed business, its profits are again divided equally. Fourth, profit or loss, even through theft, accruing to the original money or merchandise still physically intact from what each contributed is allocated according to capital. Fifth, where the components are inseparable and one cannot realize profit on his share without selling the other's share too, such as one ox for plowing, a single pearl, a single utensil, or any item that must be sold as one unit, profit from the joint sale is divided equally even after the agreed division date or after they changed plans, and even where the underlying increase in value of the intact contributed property would otherwise follow capital. Sixth, even if they bought an ox for slaughter and immediate division into limbs, or another item where neither partner is obligated to labor for the other, or even an ox for plowing where they agreed that one would use it two days and the other one day so that ordinary profit follows capital, nevertheless if they instead sell the live ox and realize a profit that could not be obtained without selling both shares together, that sale profit is divided equally. Seventh, if substantial profit accrued while allocation followed capital, and afterward a buyer offers a small additional premium only if both shares are sold together, the owner of the larger share does not lose the profit already attributable to his greater capital merely because of the later joint sale. Eighth, if they bought an ox for plowing, earned profit and divided that profit equally, and later decided to slaughter the ox and suffered a loss, although the loss on the ox itself follows capital, they first offset the loss against the previous partnership profit, because profit is pledged to restore principal, and only afterward divide the remaining profit. Ninth, if they jointly bought land not subject to compulsory division for their own use and later sold it at a profit, the profit follows capital so long as the gain did not arise through a partnership activity requiring equal labor. Equal division applies specifically where the partnership concerns a business requiring exertion and the smaller investor became equally obligated to work. Tenth, all these rules concern a voluntary partnership. If they became partners without deliberate agreement, such as a firstborn and ordinary heir, or where one had a partnership with his father in an ox and the father died so that one child inherited three quarters and another one quarter, profit follows capital even where the shares are inseparable. These are the principal rules derived from this seif.
