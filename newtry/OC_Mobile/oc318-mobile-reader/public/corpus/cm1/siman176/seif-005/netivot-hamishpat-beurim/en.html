Equally, according to their number. I find these laws very difficult.
First, the Sema writes in seif katan 15 that any merchandise fit for division is comparable to an ox intended for slaughter, and the profit is divided according to the money invested. Beit Yosef explicitly explains that according to this view, even if they bought an ox for plowing that was standing for slaughter and they slaughtered it, they divide according to their respective investments. If so, it is difficult why the Mechaber concludes that if the money itself remains and they have not yet spent it, any depreciation or appreciation is allocated according to the money invested. The Sema in seif katan 16 explains that even had each partner retained his own coins, the gain or loss would have reached him. This implies that if they already spent the money, bought merchandise with the partnership funds, sold the merchandise for money, and the money then appreciated because the king changed the currency after the sale, the profit is divided equally. The Tur explicitly says so. But every sum of money is like divisible produce, and it should be like an ox that was slaughtered, where the profit follows the investment. This is not difficult according to the Tur, because the Tur rules that even with produce fit for division the profit is divided equally, but it is difficult according to the Rambam.
Second, the Rosh challenges this approach from the fact that Rav Hamnuna does not explain the Mishnah of three people who put money into a common purse as a case where they became partners in produce fit for division. The Rosh answers that if so, what novelty would the Mishnah teach? Rav Hamnuna therefore explains it as a case of new coins, while the case of an ox for plowing remains otherwise. It is thus explicit that according to their approach, even after the original money has been spent and replaced by other money, the profit is according to the investments. Yet the Rambam and the Mechaber say this only where the original money has not been spent, which is the opposite of what the Rosh's reasoning requires according to the Rambam's approach.
Third, merchandise not fit for division must mean that they purchased one utensil or one pearl, as the Ittur cited by Beit Yosef explains, while merchandise fit for division means that they bought two or three units. If so, it is difficult that the Gemara says that where each partner bought with his own money and the goods then became mixed, each takes according to his investment. According to the Rambam, this is because of the Sema's reason in seif katan 16, that even had each person's property remained in his own house, the gain would have reached him. The Rambam and the Rama state this law where they partnered in produce and the produce remains extant. But according to the foregoing, why does the Gemara specify that each bought with his own money? Even had they jointly purchased three oxen, those oxen would be fit for division and the profit should be according to the money invested.
Fourth, the Mechaber writes in seif 6 that if a profit arose after the time for division arrived, they take according to their investments. This must seemingly refer to merchandise that cannot be sold one part without the other, such as an ox for plowing. The Sema in seif katan 15 explains that the reason is that the respective shares are joined and one cannot function without the other. If so, there is no logical distinction between profit arising before or after the time of division, since the shares remain joined. Moreover, this contradicts seif 8 concerning an ox purchased for slaughter, meaning for division into its limbs, as is clear from the Rif's language, where nevertheless, if they sell it alive, they divide the gain equally. This appears to apply even if the gain arose after they agreed to slaughter and divide it, which is comparable to profit arising after the time for division.
Fifth, the Rama writes that if they deviated from what they originally stipulated, they divide according to their respective investments. The Shach in seif katan 14 says that this applies even if they sold the ox alive. This contradicts the law in seif 5 that if they sold it alive, the profit is divided equally. Beit Yosef already raised this difficulty on the Tur, as did the Perishah. The Perishah cites the Darchei Moshe's answer, and in the longer Darchei Moshe the Rama writes that although the law follows the Rif and Rambam, this ruling can nevertheless be true on logical grounds, because when they deviated from their agreement the original consent was nullified and the case becomes like one in which the time for division has arrived. This is very difficult. How can that law be true when seif 5 rules that if they sell it alive the profit is divided equally even though they changed their original plan?
Sixth, the Rif in the She'arim, cited by Beit Yosef, states the view that if they changed the arrangement the profit follows the investments. He explains Rabbah's statement concerning an ox bought for plowing but standing for slaughter that was sold alive as requiring division according to the investments. The Perishah explains that this is because they changed their plan. But we rule like Rav Hamnuna, that even with an ox bought for plowing and standing for slaughter the profit is divided equally. This demonstrates that even after a change the profit is divided equally.
Seventh, there is the Rosh's question: how could the Rif and Rambam disagree with the Yerushalmi? Even according to our Gemara, the Rosh's explanation appears compelling.
Eighth, there is the Taz's question: how could the Mechaber and Rama omit the view of Tosafot, the Rosh, and all the authorities cited by the Tur? This is indeed a major difficulty, because those authorities do not accept the Rif and Rambam's position. The Ran writes that even the Rif and Rambam agree with the Rosh regarding merchandise, and disagree only where the ox was slaughtered. Yet here their view is omitted entirely.
Ninth, there is the Sema's question in seif katan 15 against the Levush. It therefore appears to me that the matter should be explained in a way that resolves all these questions. Our explanation will also resolve several additional difficulties and yield a number of legal consequences. The Bavli in Ketubot and the Yerushalmi cited by the Rosh discuss two different types of cases. The case in Ketubot concerns partners who purchased an ox for plowing which was standing for slaughter, meaning that they later decided to slaughter it, or that they originally bought it for slaughter and division into its limbs, but instead sold it alive.
Tenth, this accords with the Rif. In that case the Yerushalmi's reason, that the partners did not stipulate otherwise, certainly does not apply. They bought the ox with the intention of immediately dividing it into its limbs. What condition would they have been expected to make, when they did not purchase it for resale and profit but for division among themselves? Likewise, if they purchased an ox for plowing, not as a business venture but for each partner to use it for his own plowing or to rent it out, the Rosh's reason of their failure to stipulate does not apply, because they bought it intending to divide its use by days. Therefore, where an ox in such a case is sold alive and the profit is divided equally, the reason is solely that stated by the Sema in seif katan 15: the partners' portions are joined, and the larger share cannot produce this gain unless the smaller share accompanies it. Accordingly, if they slaughter it and sell the meat, so that the larger share can generate profit independently, they divide according to their investments even if the meat is sold together. The law regarding appreciation that arose in an ox intended for slaughter before it was sold alive will be explained below. The Yerushalmi, by contrast, concerns partners who contributed money to buy divisible merchandise for a business venture and to sell it over a period of time. The Sema's reason does not apply there because the shares in the merchandise are not physically joined. The Yerushalmi's reason does apply, however, because by entering the partnership they obligated themselves to one another to work with the entire merchandise for the term. Even without a fixed term, where they partnered to buy and sell, each became obligated to exert himself in buying and selling, and one cannot compel division before the merchandise is sold, for they became like workers for one another. The rule in seif 16 that an unspecified partnership may be divided whenever one partner wishes applies only in the circumstances explained there. If they expressly undertook to work with and sell the merchandise, it is as though they fixed a term during which both must remain partners and engage in the sale until the merchandise is sold, as is also implied by seif 22. They partnered on the understanding that both would work equally. In this setting the Yerushalmi's reason is fully applicable. One partner can say: "Since I obligated myself to work equally, I certainly entered the partnership on the understanding that I would receive half. Had that not been the arrangement, I surely would not have obligated myself to be bound together with you. Since I did not stipulate otherwise, my intention was surely to receive half, and since you too failed to stipulate the reverse, your intention must have been the same." But where they jointly purchase merchandise for immediate division, or an ox for slaughter and immediate division into its limbs, and then slaughter it so that the shares are no longer joined, if someone offers a profit for all of it at once, the gain is according to the investments. The owner of the smaller share never obligated himself to work with the larger share, and there is no basis to say that he intended to transfer to the other less than what is due him or to receive more than his own share. Accordingly, the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch can be understood straightforwardly. In seif 5, when they say that partners contributed to a common purse, one one hundred and the other two hundred, and all engaged in business, and the venture gained or lost, they divide the gain according to their number, they refer to a commercial partnership. This is the Yerushalmi's law and is based on its reason that they failed to stipulate otherwise. The Mechaber then adds that even if they bought an ox for slaughter, meaning for immediate division into its limbs, where the Yerushalmi's reason is inapplicable, nevertheless, if they are offered a profit for selling it alive, the profit is divided equally, because the shares are joined, as the Sema explains. This latter law derives from our Bavli in Ketubot 93. The Mechaber then says, "When is this said?" This refers back to the opening case of partners conducting a commercial venture. The same principle can apply even to an ox for slaughter where they bought it to work with and improve it until a specified time, for there the profit is equal because of the Yerushalmi's reasoning. The qualification, however, is where they conducted business with the money they contributed. If the money itself still exists, even though the partnership term has not ended and they remain obligated to use the money to purchase merchandise, so that neither can divide independently and the situation might resemble merchandise that cannot be divided, nevertheless appreciation of the coins is allocated according to the investments. The reason is that of the Sema in seif katan 16: had each person's coins remained in his own possession, the same appreciation or depreciation would have occurred. But if, after they have conducted business, money comes into their hands before the time for division and a later gain arises in a manner to which the Sema's reason does not apply, the gain is divided equally. This resolves the first question. The second is also resolved, because the Rif and Rambam agree with the Yerushalmi. Rav Hamnuna therefore answers that the Mishnah concerns new coins: although the reason of their failure to stipulate would ordinarily apply, the appreciation is nonetheless allocated according to the money invested for the Sema's reason, since the gain would have occurred even had each person's coins remained at home. If the Mishnah instead concerned an ox intended for slaughter that they actually slaughtered, the ruling would be obvious. The earlier question concerning the case where each partner bought with his own money is also resolved. Even if they bought goods for a commercial venture requiring effort, so that the Yerushalmi's reasoning would ordinarily apply, where each bought his own portion before the goods were mixed, the gain follows the investments for the Sema's reason. Where, however, they first contributed money jointly and then bought the goods, the Sema's reason does not apply, and the gain is equal. Seif 6 then states that if a profit arose after the time for division, the gain follows the investments. This refers only to a commercial partnership. One might have thought that since the original partnership was for a fixed term, and during that term the profit was to be equal, their continuation after the term should be viewed as a continuation on the original terms. The Mechaber therefore teaches that once the profit arose after the partners were no longer mutually obligated, it follows the investments. This does not refer to the case of an ox sold alive. There, even after the time for division, the gain remains equal because the shares are physically joined, as explained. The fourth question is thus resolved. The Rama then writes that the same is true if they changed what they originally agreed upon, and they then divide according to their investments. This too refers only to a commercial venture, not to an ox sold alive, contrary to the Shach's explanation. The law of an ox sold alive is already stated in seif 5: its gain is equal even when they changed their plan. The Rama means a case where they originally formed a partnership for a stated term, or to transport merchandise to a particular place, and later decided not to conduct that venture but to sell the merchandise immediately, after which a profit arose. We will discuss below the status of gain that arose before they changed the plan. The novelty is that one should not say that only in the case of an ox bought for plowing which they later decide to slaughter and divide is the gain according to investments, because there the only basis for equal division was the physical joining of the shares, and once they slaughtered it that reason disappeared. With ordinary merchandise, by contrast, since the reason for equal division was that the parties certainly entered the partnership intending equal profit, perhaps their later agreement to sell immediately would not remove an already vested equal share in the profit. Nor is this entirely comparable to a case where the partnership term expired, since there the parties originally undertook equal sharing only until that time, whereas here they are still within the originally fixed term. The Rama therefore writes, based on the Darchei Moshe, that once they cancel the original arrangement it is comparable to the arrival of the time for division. This is also the meaning of the Rif in the She'arim, who derives this law from Rabbah even though the halachah follows Rav Hamnuna. His point is only to compare the commercial case to the ox. With an ox bought for plowing but designated for slaughter, if they do not sell it alive, even Rav Hamnuna agrees that once the original basis for equal sharing disappears, the division follows the investments. So too with merchandise, once the original basis is nullified, the gain follows the investments. But if they merely cancel the first undertaking and then agree to conduct a different venture for another fixed term, the new venture certainly has equal profit, no less than a newly formed partnership for a term. This resolves the fifth and sixth questions. According to this, the authorities do not truly disagree. Where they bought an ox for slaughter in order to divide its limbs and actually slaughtered it in the manner described by the Rif, even the Rosh agrees that the profit follows the investments, because the partners did not enter the arrangement in order to conduct a joint sale. When the Rosh says that even after slaughter the gain is equal, he is speaking where they bought it as an investment, to work with it and improve it until a time, so that the reason based on their failure to stipulate does apply. In such a case the Rif agrees as well, as explained above. This is the reasoning of the Ran, who writes that the Rif and Rambam differ only regarding a slaughtered ox. The Rif rules as he does specifically where it was bought from the outset for slaughter and division, and in that case the Rosh also agrees. The seventh, eighth, and ninth questions are thereby resolved.
Not according to the money invested. See Sema, seif katan 15, where he writes that this refers to merchandise that is not intended for division but for sale together. The Sema appears to explain "merchandise not fit for division" as an item that cannot be divided except through a claim of gud o agud, such as a single pearl. This can resolve Rashi's wording in Ketubot regarding the case where each bought with his own money. Rashi explains that one bought lean oxen and the other fat oxen. Maharam Schiff asks why Rashi did not simply say that one bought two oxen and the other one. According to the foregoing, the matter is clear. The baraita implies that only where each bought with his own money is the gain according to the investments, whereas if they bought jointly the gain would be equal. If one bought two oxen for one hundred and the other one for fifty, these are separate items of the same use and corresponding values and are subject to division, so they are divisible merchandise, for which the gain would follow the investments even if they bought jointly. Rashi therefore explains that one bought lean animals and the other fat animals, following the view cited above in siman 171 seif 13 that superior and inferior property are considered different types. Such merchandise is treated as not fit for division, and if bought jointly the gain would ordinarily be equal. The baraita therefore specifies that each bought with his own money, in which case the gain follows the investments even though the goods are not fit for division, for the Sema's reason in seif katan 16. Nevertheless, the principal explanation appears to me as I wrote above. Where the Yerushalmi's reason based on the absence of a stipulation applies, the gain is equal. Where the merchandise was bought for immediate division and not for a joint commercial effort, the result depends on whether the gain arises specifically because the goods are sold together and could not be obtained from either share independently. If so, as with an ox for slaughter sold alive, the gain is equal because the shares are joined. The Sema's phrase, "merchandise that is not intended for division but to be sold together," can also be understood this way: if the same gain could not be obtained after division, the shares are joined for purposes of that gain. By contrast, any increase in value that could have been realized even after division is treated like profit on merchandise fit for division.
And all the more so if they became partners in produce. See Sema, seif katan 16, who explains the law of coins by saying that even had the coins remained in each person's possession, the gain or loss would have reached him, and he applies the same reasoning to produce. This is difficult. If they partnered in unequal coins or produce, and the appreciation occurred only in the particular coins or produce contributed by the partner who invested less, then according to the Sema's formulation that partner should take the entire gain, since had the property remained with him he would have received it all. Yet the Mechaber says without qualification that they take according to their investments, implying that even if the appreciation occurred only in the property of the smaller investor, the larger investor receives his proportional share. Likewise, if the loss occurred only in the larger investor's property, the smaller investor must bear his proportional part. Otherwise, what effect did their partnership have? Moreover, if the assets were placed in the common shop and were stolen there, the Sema's reason that the same would have happened had each kept his property at home does not apply, yet the Mechaber still rules that theft is borne according to the investments and makes no distinction. It therefore appears that the Sema also agrees that even where the gain arose only from one partner's property, the allocation is according to the investments. As soon as one partner performed meshichah or lifting on the other's money, each acquired a proportional interest in the other's property according to the money each contributed, and it became his for both profit and loss. Equal division can be justified only where the gain arose through the partners' labor, because the smaller investor can say that he obligated himself to labor equally with the larger investor only on the understanding of equal profit. By contrast, an increase arising in the extant property without their labor is certainly understood to follow their proportional ownership, since such gain would have arisen even in their homes. Each acquired a proportional share in the other's property; otherwise there would be no partnership while the property remained extant. However, even where gain or loss arises while the property remains extant, if that particular gain could not have been earned from either share alone, such as where a buyer offers the profit only because all the property is being sold together, the gain is equal because the shares are joined. The Sema's formulation that the gain would have arisen had each kept his property at home does not apply there, because this gain exists only by virtue of the combined sale. If they bought land without specifying a purpose, the land is not subject to division, and one contributed more, it appears that there is a distinction. If they bought it to live there themselves and later sold it at a profit, the gain follows the investments. The reason of joined shares does not apply to land, as stated in Teshuvot Maimoniyot, Hilchot Kinyan, siman 40, and the Rosh's reason that one partner relied on the other's business acumen also does not apply because they did not purchase it for profit. But if they purchased the land specifically to resell it at a profit, the gain is divided equally. There remains a difficulty with the explanation that equal profit follows from the physical joining of the shares. A firstborn and an ordinary son who inherit a slave or a nonkosher animal use it, according to the Gemara, one day for the ordinary son and two days for the firstborn. Tosafot explain that this differs from partners in an ox for plowing because there the parties entered the partnership on that basis, whereas inheritance comes automatically. According to the reason of joined shares, however, the difficulty returns, because the interests are physically joined and neither can generate the profit without the other. In my opinion, Tosafot's difficulty can be resolved differently, and their answer is somewhat strained. The Gemara's question is as follows. A firstborn does not receive a double portion of appreciation that accrues before division. He does receive a double portion of produce from property leased to others, but if the heirs themselves work the property he does not receive a double portion, as Rashbam explains in Bava Batra 124, unless the firstborn protested and demanded division while the ordinary son refused. In that case the latter is treated as one who wrongfully withholds the firstborn's share, as explained in Bava Batra 126. Therefore the Gemara properly asks according to the view that gud o agud does not apply. If the inherited asset has no form of division, the firstborn cannot protest by demanding division, and he should not receive a double portion of the appreciation produced by the heirs before division. The Gemara answers that I say the asset works one day for this heir and two days for the other, meaning that division by time is possible, as explained above in siman 171 seif 8. Since a form of division exists, the firstborn can protest and demand it, and once he does so, even if the heirs themselves continue to work the asset he receives a double portion, as a firstborn who protested. Accordingly, if the firstborn did not protest, he does not receive a double portion of the appreciation. This limitation is specific to the firstborn because he does not receive a double portion of ordinary appreciation before division. With ordinary partners who initially purchased an asset to divide its use by days, it is clear that gain attributable to ownership follows their respective investments, while in an ordinary commercial partnership the profit is equal. It follows further that if one brother already owned half of an animal in partnership with his father, the father then died, and the son inherited the father's half together with another brother, so that the first son now owns three quarters and the other one quarter, income from plowing is divided equally if they continue to work it together without stipulation. Their continuation is equivalent to partnering for the plowing until they divide. However, the language of the authorities appears to follow Tosafot, that as long as the parties did not enter the partnership voluntarily, the case is like one in which each bought with his own money, as the Mordechai writes.
