The profit or loss is divided equally, etc. This applies specifically where they purchased an ox in partnership and sold it alive, as the Mechaber concludes. While the ox remains alive, the partners' respective shares are joined together and one cannot function without the other, and therefore they divide equally. This differs from a case where they slaughtered it and sold the meat, or divided the meat between themselves. Since the pieces no longer require one another and are ready for division, they divide according to the amounts of money each invested. This applies not only where they originally purchased the ox in partnership with the intention of selling it alive and in fact sold it alive, so that profit and loss are plainly divided equally. Even if their original intention had been to slaughter it, we do not follow the initial thought but the final act, since in fact they sold it alive. The reference to an ox sold alive is not exclusive. The same law applies to any comparable merchandise that is not intended to be divided but to be sold as one unit. In such a case the smaller investment becomes blended with the larger and is treated equally with it. This appears to be the Mechaber's intent, following the Rif and Rambam according to one of the explanations and approaches that I set out in the Derishah. See there, where I wrote that there are five approaches to this law, each dependent upon its interpretation of the Gemara. This is unlike the Ir Shushan, who explains that they divide equally because they should have stipulated that profit and loss would be divided according to their respective investments, and since they did not stipulate we assume that the one who contributed more knew that the one who contributed less was more capable and therefore intended to give him half the profit despite investing less, and conversely with respect to loss. That explanation is the reason given by Tosafot and the Rosh only according to their own approach, for they hold that even if the ox was bought for slaughter and was in fact slaughtered, profit and loss are still divided equally, and likewise if they bought produce and sold it, even though it was divisible from beginning to end and its parts did not depend upon one another. That reasoning does not apply to the Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber, who apply equal division specifically where they sold the ox alive.
Reduce or leave the quaint. The taste would be if the coin remained in the hand of anyone who came to them or lost:
And all the more so if they entered into partnership with produce, etc. Meaning that the produce itself remained in existence and either rose or fell in value. The reason the Mechaber says this is all the more so is as follows. When they entered into partnership with money, their intention was to buy whatever profitable opportunity arose. Had an ox suitable for plowing come before them, they could have bought it and sold it alive. It merely happened that they bought nothing, and meanwhile the king altered the currency. One might therefore have argued that we should follow their underlying intention and divide equally. All the more so where they entered into partnership with produce, which from beginning to end is naturally intended for division, we say that each takes profit or loss according to his investment. This is straightforward.
