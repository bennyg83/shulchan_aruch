There the Mechaber, following the Rambam, implies that when the partnership loss is known the partner may have to reimburse it from his own property. That seems contrary to the Ramah here. Bach distinguishes: here the loss did not arise through the partnership's commercial activity, whereas siman 93 concerns a loss caused through business dealings, in which case a known loss must be paid even from the partner's separate property. Pitchei Teshuva notes that the Taz does not accept this distinction. The Taz says expressly that the Ramah and Rambam differ. Siman 93 merely teaches that an oath alone cannot extract money when the amount of loss is unknown; the principal substantive rule is stated here, following the Ramah, that a partner is not obligated to pay a partnership loss from his own separate property, even where the loss is known, because he did not enter the partnership on the understanding that his personal assets would be exposed. If the parties expressly stipulated that losses would be shared equally, then even the Ramah would require performance of the condition; siman 93 then teaches that where the amount of loss is unknown the claimant cannot recover merely by his own oath. Shevut Yaakov, III, 167, was asked about Reuven and Shimon, where Reuven supplied all the capital and they stipulated that profits would be divided equally but said nothing about losses. He notes the apparent contradiction between simanim 93 and 176. Bach resolves it by distinguishing commercial losses from external accidents. Shevut Yaakov discusses the competing readings and the practical difficulty of extracting money from a partner's private property where leading authorities deny such liability. Netivot HaMishpat further distinguishes an ordinary partnership from an iska in which one party gives funds to another on the standard half-loan, half-deposit basis. In an iska, the recipient is a borrower as to the loan half. If the giver claims that the entrusted property was lost by accident, he may swear and recover the loan half because the debt itself is certain, comparable to a creditor whose collateral was lost. By contrast, where two partners simply contributed unequal sums, the smaller investor never became a borrower for the larger investor's excess contribution; he merely undertook whatever partnership liability the law imposes. A claim that he must now pay a loss from his own property is therefore a claim of uncertain original liability, and the ordinary partner cannot recover by swearing. Likewise, even in an iska, if the giver claims additional consequential expenses exceeding the amount of the original capital, those are new liabilities and cannot be collected on his oath alone. Noda BiYehuda, Tinyana, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 38, discusses partners in a honey venture. Reuven claimed a loss of 120 gold coins and demanded that the others pay their shares. One partner admitted that some loss occurred but disputed its amount and alleged that Reuven himself caused it by failing to sell when a merchant had offered a favorable price. The supposed witness knew this only because Reuven himself had told him so, which Reuven denied. The lower court sought to impose liability on the partners as ones who admitted part of a claim but could not state the amount of the loss. Noda BiYehuda disagreed. If Reuven sought payment from their private property, even clear witnesses to the loss would not necessarily permit recovery, because the Raavad and Ramah hold that a partner is not personally liable from his own property, and the Perishah and Taz read the Ramah that way. Money cannot readily be extracted against those authorities. Even if partnership funds still remain in the other partners' hands, so that an established loss would be shared from those funds, they are not liable here merely because they admit that some loss occurred. Their lack of knowledge of the amount does not create the rule that one who is obligated to swear but cannot do so must pay, because most authorities do not apply that doctrine to a fact the defendant had no reason to know. Here Reuven himself says the honey was sold without their knowledge. Moreover, according to their own claim Reuven caused the loss, so their admission that a loss occurred is not an admission of any amount due. At most, liability could be constructed only by combining several disputed stringencies of the Shach, and defendants cannot be charged by aggregating doubtful positions in that manner, for they may plead kim li. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 156, appears to accept Bach and Shach that a commercial loss may obligate a partner even from his own property, but Pitchei Teshuva concludes that it remains difficult to rely on that view to extract money against the Perishah, Taz, and Noda BiYehuda.
