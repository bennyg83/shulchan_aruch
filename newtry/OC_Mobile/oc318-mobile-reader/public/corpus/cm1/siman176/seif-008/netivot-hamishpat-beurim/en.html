And he is only an unpaid custodian. See Shach, seif katan 16. His words at first appear to contradict themselves. Initially he writes that the partner is not a custodian at all, based on the Rosh concerning an ox seized by the injured party, cited by Shach and likewise found in Shitah Mekubetzet on Bava Kamma. The reason is straightforward. If one expressly accepts custody he certainly becomes a custodian. The case is where one partner brings the joint property into his domain without expressly accepting custody. Ordinarily, one who brings another's property into his house is presumed to have done so in order to guard it. But partners are different. He can say that he brought it in only to guard his own share and never accepted custody of his fellow's share. He is therefore not a custodian at all and does not even take a custodian's oath. Shach then cites Maharam Alashkar, whose reason for exemption is that it is shemirah b'baalim. That implies that the partner is at least a custodian. Otherwise Maharam could simply have said he was not a custodian at all. Bava Batra 43 likewise asks why a partner should be liable, since it is shemirah b'baalim, which also implies that he is otherwise a paid custodian. The resolution is that where each partner actively handles and guards his half, as Rashbam explains there, partners are hired workers for one another. Each is obligated to work with and guard both his own and his fellow's share. In that situation each is certainly a custodian, and indeed appears to have the law of a paid custodian, because they are hired to one another even without explicitly saying, "Guard for me and I will guard for you tomorrow." Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 177, seif katan 9, also gives a reason that makes an iska recipient a paid custodian. The Gemara's question of shemirah b'baalim, rather than asking why the partner should be a paid custodian, likewise proves this. Maharam Alashkar speaks of that type of ordinary joint activity. Mechaber here, however, discusses a case where one partner brought the entire property into his own house. In that case he did not accept any new custody of his fellow's part, because he can say, "You too should have come to guard it. Since you are not guarding my share, I am not obligated to guard yours." In that case Shach says he is not a custodian at all. Shach's purpose in citing Maharam Alashkar is therefore not to prove the exact basis of his own exemption. He cites the phrase, "He is never a paid custodian," to reject the view of those authorities who would treat ordinary partners as though they had implicitly said, "Guard for me today and I will guard for you tomorrow," merely because one partner happened to guard during one period and can later force the other to guard. According to Maharam, the Gemara's special paid-custodian rule applies only where they expressly made that arrangement. Shach cites this to challenge the other authorities, not as a direct proof for his own case.
They began to transact together. It appears that if at the outset, in Nisan, each took half the merchandise into his own house to deal with and guard it, and afterward in Iyar they stipulated that each would handle all the merchandise during alternating months, the later custody of the second half is not shemirah b'baalim. When a partner takes into his house the half that had previously been in the other's house, that is a new meshichah and a new custody not in the owners' presence. He therefore bears liability on that newly received half. If one quarter of the total amount represents the fellow's ownership in that half, he must pay that quarter upon a covered loss. The present ruling must therefore concern a case where all the partnership merchandise remained in a jointly owned shop and both partners guarded it together in Nisan. They later stipulated that one would sit in the shop during one month and the other the next. There is then no new meshichah or newly accepted custody. Each had already been obligated from the beginning to guard the entire joint property as a partner. Nor should one say that when one partner ceases guarding for a month and the other later resumes in his assigned month, this constitutes a new custody without the owners. If a person accepts custody intermittently, such as Nisan, Sivan, and Av, and the initial acceptance was in the owner's presence, the exemption of shemirah b'baalim continues throughout the agreed periods. Likewise, if one accepts custody for a year while the initial custody was in the owner's presence, and during the year the object is deposited temporarily with the owner for a month, the original custody is not terminated. Shach in siman 72, seif katan 25, writes that a paid custodian who deposits the object with the owner can remain liable as custodian even while it is physically with the owner. Thus, when the object later returns, there is no new custody. Similarly, if the owner temporarily exempts the custodian from guarding during part of the agreed term, once the exemption period ends the original duty resumes even without new meshichah. The original shemirah b'baalim therefore governs the entire partnership term.
The first one is liable. See Sema, seif katan 26, who writes that if the property was stolen while both partners were working together, the first must pay only one quarter. Taz objects that where both partners are guarding together, one should owe the other nothing. If an adult and a minor are partners and jointly guard, the adult would not become liable to the minor merely because a minor is exempt from custodian liability. The same should apply here. Taz therefore explains the clause as referring to a later period in which they again began handling the property one after another. Then, if it is stolen while the first alone is working, he is liable, whereas when the second later works alone he is exempt. On that explanation, the case must be one in which they work in a jointly owned shop. If each initially handled his own half in his own house and later one takes the entire property into his own house, that later transfer is a new meshichah and would create new liability, as I wrote above. It is nevertheless possible to sustain Sema in a different case. Suppose they originally agreed that each would handle the business in his own month. In Nisan Reuven's month begins, but Shimon voluntarily lends his services and works together with him. Reuven in his own month is a paid custodian, because their original arrangement was effectively "Guard for me today and I will guard for you tomorrow." Shimon, who assists gratuitously, is only an unpaid custodian. In such a case Reuven remains responsible. Indeed, if Reuven had entrusted the object entirely to Shimon, Reuven could remain liable as a paid custodian who entrusted it to an owner. The same would be true if an adult who had accepted paid custody then received gratuitous help from a minor. Yet Reuven does not pay the whole value to Shimon. Half the property belongs to Reuven himself and half to Shimon. As to Shimon's half, Reuven is a paid custodian, but Shimon's own assistance is not exempt through shemirah b'baalim merely because Reuven, who owns only the other half, is present. Siman 346, seif 17, makes clear that the owner must be owner of the property being guarded. Therefore Reuven's net liability to Shimon is one quarter of the total amount. The same applies if Reuven hires Shimon to help during Reuven's month. Although Shimon is then a paid custodian too, Reuven remains responsible for the difference in their legal positions. Conversely, if initially each guarded alone in Nisan and Iyar, so both acceptances were without the owners, and later in Tammuz Shimon's month Shimon hires Reuven to help, then if theft occurs Shimon may have to pay Reuven one quarter. Reuven's newly undertaken custody alongside Shimon is shemirah b'baalim because Shimon is simultaneously guarding Reuven's share. This can also explain Tur's language, "If from the beginning each worked alone," which Sema found difficult. "The first is liable" means that in each month, the partner whose duty to guard arose first for that month remains the principal custodian compared with the second partner who only later joined him. He can therefore owe one quarter in the circumstances described.
They completely divided the partnership. Taz questions this. Maharik's reason that the person retaining the money is a paid custodian is that partners are paid custodians for one another. Once the partnership has ended, what benefit remains? Taz concludes that the later custody must be connected to the earlier partnership custody. If so, however, the earlier custody was also shemirah b'baalim because the other partner was present. Taz therefore suggests that the person who retained the money was the one who had begun working before the other partner, so that his original custody had not been in the owner's presence, and he leaves the matter difficult. In truth, this is still very strained. A partner is a paid custodian only where the arrangement is effectively "Guard for me today and I will guard for you tomorrow," because his compensation is the other's future guarding. Once they divide and the other will no longer have to guard later, the basis for paid custody disappears. This is no better than a paid custodian whose term has expired, who thereafter is only an unpaid custodian, as explained at the end of siman 304. It therefore appears that the case can involve a new custody created after division. If the partnership property had been in a jointly owned shop and, after division, one partner took the other's money into his own house to hold until payment to a non Jew, that is a new meshichah and new custody not in the owner's presence. Alternatively, if each previously kept his own half at home, after division one transfers some of his money to the other to pay the non Jew, again creating new custody. All the more so, if the property remained with the person who had the current duty to guard under their alternating arrangement, he can remain a paid custodian for the balance of that duty. Even if they had originally guarded together, when after division one partner alone receives and retains the money there is no shemirah b'baalim, because this is a new meshichah. The reason he is a paid custodian where the retained property is money is that he has permission to use the funds, like one who deposits unsealed money, who becomes a paid custodian because of the right of use under siman 292, seif 4. Here even an ordinary householder has the status of a money changer in this respect, because as a former partner he is permitted to use partnership funds while they remain in his hands, and an opportunity to use them in a profitable deal benefits him. This is also the rationale for Taz in Yoreh Deah, siman 177, regarding an iska recipient. If a profitable transaction arises that requires more capital than his personal share, the joint money allows him to undertake it, and this benefit makes him a paid custodian. Only while both actively guard equally are they exempt from one another because of shemirah b'baalim. If the retained item is not money, however, he would not automatically be a paid custodian unless the item remained with the partner who was still obligated for that month's paid custody, or unless he retained it because he was personally liable to the non Jew and therefore held it as security against that obligation. In the latter case, he receives the same benefit as a creditor holding a pledge, and is a paid custodian. Ketzot HaChoshen discusses two people who rented a customs franchise and agreed that each would operate it in his own month, where the partnership began under circumstances of shemirah b'baalim although the customs revenues had not yet come into their hands. Ketzot says the later revenues remain subject to the original exemption because the custody obligation already existed at the beginning of the partnership. This is difficult. So long as there was nothing at all to guard, what does it help that there was an abstract future obligation? In my view, shemirah b'baalim applies only if the actual custody began while the owner was working with the custodian. The proof Ketzot cites from Maharit is not clear to me. Even as he reports it, that case involved a spouse who from the beginning of the marriage was already guarding the house and the vessels inside it. Here, by contrast, there was no object under custody at all at the beginning.
