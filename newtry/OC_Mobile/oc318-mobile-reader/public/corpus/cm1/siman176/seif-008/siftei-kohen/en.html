And he is only an unpaid custodian, etc. This ruling is incorrect. The Rama overlooked Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of the chapter Shor Shenagach Arbaah VaChamishah, Rabbeinu Ephraim, Baal HaMaor, Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ramah, and the Bartenura there. Concerning a damaged party who seized the ox, Rabbi Akiva holds that the parties become partners, yet the damaged party is liable for subsequent damage only in proportion to his share. The Agudah, the Tur below in siman 401 in the name of Rabbi Yitzchak and the Rosh, and Maharshal rule likewise, and the Rama himself cites that position at the end of siman 401. Even those who disagree there may concede here, because there the injured party seized the ox against the tortfeasor's will and may therefore be presumed to have accepted responsibility for the whole animal, unlike here. The Sema's distinction between damage and loss is untenable. The principal rule is that a partner is not even an unpaid custodian unless one expressly says, 'Guard for me and I will guard for you.' Baal HaMaor and the Mordechai state this explicitly, proving it from Rabbi Akiva's law of the seized ox. The Rosh likewise writes that each partner guards only his own share, as with an object worth two hundred where each guards it at different times and responsibility always rests upon both. The Rama's inference from the glosses to the Rif is not compelling, and even if that source holds otherwise, it is a lone view against all the authorities cited. I later found that Maharam Alashkar, responsum 4, quotes Maharam that ordinary partnership constitutes guarding while the owner is employed with the custodian, so the partner is exempt even for negligence. The partners become paid custodians only where one expressly says, 'Guard for me today and I will guard for you tomorrow.' This is the correct reading of the Gemara, which asks why the arrangement is not guarding while the owner is employed and answers that they expressly arranged alternating days. The Ittur states the same. Maharam Alashkar's contention that other commentators disagree is unfounded; it appears that he overlooked the authorities cited above. General statements that partners are paid custodians must be understood as referring to an express arrangement of alternating days. The statement of Semag is no proof to the contrary, because in the laws of rental he expressly explains that 'Guard for me and I will guard for you' is guarding while the owner is employed, while alternating days create paid custodianship. The same reading applies to the responsum of the Rosh and to statements of the Geonim concerning a partner who sold on credit. Such cases may involve an assigned period of management, or sale on credit may itself be treated as direct damage, with 'negligence' meaning an act of damage. Nor is there support in the Rambam. On the contrary, the Rambam omitted Samuel's statement that partners become paid custodians because he regarded partners like all other persons and relied on his general ruling that simultaneous reciprocal guarding is guarding while the owner is employed, whereas alternating days create paid custodianship. A responsum of the Rambam must likewise be corrected by supplying a missing word, so that it states that partners are not judged by the rule of reciprocal guarding, but rather their ordinary arrangement is guarding while the owner is employed until they expressly stipulate alternating days. Rabbi Shimon Duran's responsum does not prove otherwise. Even were a single authority found to disagree, that view would be nullified against the many authorities and the plain sense of the Gemara. Tosafot's question, 'What does Samuel teach us?' itself proves this, for otherwise Samuel would teach that partners are paid custodians even without an express stipulation. Maharit, siman 123, follows Maharam Alashkar without addressing these points and attempts strained reconciliations among the writings of Maharam. In truth, the Mordechai's statement, 'This does not appear correct, since he did not expressly say,' is Maharam's own language. The earlier wording in the Mordechai contains a textual error and should be corrected accordingly, as reflected in other manuscripts and in Maharik, shoresh 155. The reference to study before Rabbi Yitzchak of Vienna also proves that the speaker is Maharam, who was his student, not the Mordechai. We therefore follow Maharam as quoted by Maharam Alashkar. See Responsa Maharam Mintz, siman 59; Responsa Rabbi Shlomo Kohen, book 2, siman 108; and Responsa Maharashdam, siman 127.
