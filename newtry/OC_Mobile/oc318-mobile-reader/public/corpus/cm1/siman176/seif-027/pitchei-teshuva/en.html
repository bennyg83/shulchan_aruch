The recipient may withdraw. Shevut Yaakov, II, 184, rules that under the customary iska documents of his time the borrower cannot simply withdraw during the fixed term. He distinguishes the classic iska, where the recipient is personally obligated to work with the capital and may therefore withdraw like a worker, from modern documents that fix a stated return unless the recipient chooses to swear periodically that no profit was earned. In the latter arrangement there is no comparable personal servitude, and the recipient must generally honor the stated term. He further relies on the Ritva's view that even an ordinary worker's right to withdraw may be limited where a kinyan was made, and notes that many such iska documents also recite an actual oath. Tumim, however, rejects the underlying ruling of Shiltei Gibborim on which Shevut Yaakov relied, arguing that storekeepers and similar borrowers may repay early. Shaarei Deah likewise writes that where an iska document fixes a return for a full year, the borrower may repay principal and accrued return before the year's end. He interprets the contrary source as speaking of a borrower who wishes to withdraw only because the agreed rate has become expensive and seeks to substitute a lower rate. Netivot HaMishpat offers a different practical reconciliation. In a modern iska where the parties compromise on a fixed annual return, the borrower may cease active business, but if he wishes to avoid the full annual compromise he must open the actual accounting and swear how much was earned, then pay the lender's share. He cannot simply prorate the fixed compromise, because the fixed term was itself part of the lender's consideration. Beit Ephraim similarly distinguishes a document that states returns in periodic units, such as a set amount per week, quarter, or month, from one that states a single annual percentage. In the former, early repayment can terminate the arrangement at the relevant period. In the latter, the borrower cannot avoid the annual amount without invoking the actual profit mechanism and the oath structure of the heter iska. Pitchei Teshuva also notes an internal qualification in Shevut Yaakov's reliance on an oath clause. Shevut Yaakov elsewhere writes that if it is proven that a worker actually swore, the oath can prevent withdrawal during the term, but if a document merely recites that an oath was taken and the actual oath is not established, the recital may not suffice.
