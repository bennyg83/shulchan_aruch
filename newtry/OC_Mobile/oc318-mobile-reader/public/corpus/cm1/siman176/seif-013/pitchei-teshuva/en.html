Even where both partners began working together and the ordinary rules of guarding while the owner is employed would exempt them even from negligence, a partner who actively departs from the agreed method and causes loss is not merely negligent in guarding, but is considered to have caused damage with his own act, and is liable. The Shach above was uncertain whether a partner who sold on credit while both partners were jointly active should be liable, suggesting either that the earlier geonic rulings concern partners who worked in separate turns, or that an unauthorized credit sale itself is direct damage. Shev Yaakov and Mishneh LaMelech support the latter distinction. They compare it to a custodian who transfers property to a lesser level of custody: even if the first custodian enjoyed the exemption of guarding while the owner was employed, reducing the level of custody is treated as active damage. Mishneh LaMelech distinguishes ordinary negligence, which is exempt when the owner is employed with the custodian, from an affirmative act that causes the loss. He considers the Raavad's view concerning a wife who breaks utensils while doing household work, and suggests that even the Raavad may exempt only unintentional damage arising in the course of work, not a deliberate change in the entrusted method, even where the actor intended benefit. Beit Meir agrees that direct damage is generally distinct from negligence, though he leaves room for a possessor to rely on the Raavad and Maggid Mishneh regarding wholly inadvertent damage. Shaar Mishpat cites a responsum of the Rif holding that where both partners are active and one sells on credit, the loss is shared because negligence while the owner is employed is exempt. Shev Yaakov, however, applies the law of a custodian who transfers property to another custodian to a practical case. Reuven and Shimon were partners and both worked in the business. Reuven went to a fair, purchased merchandise, and sent it home with Levi, who later claimed that it was lost from his horse without negligence. Levi was an unpaid custodian. Shimon argued that Reuven had no authority to send partnership goods through another, especially someone Shimon would not trust on oath. Reuven answered that he acted for the partnership's benefit and believed Levi trustworthy. Shev Yaakov ruled for Shimon. A partner who transfers partnership property to another custodian is governed by the rule that the owner may say, "You are trustworthy to me on oath; the second custodian is not." This applies with particular force where the partner reduces paid custody to unpaid custody. The fact that partnership custodial duties arise automatically rather than from an express deposit does not save Reuven. Where the second custodian is not one whom the other partner normally trusts, the transfer itself is treated as active impairment of the security of the property, and liability remains even if the partners otherwise stood in the relationship of guarding while the owner was employed. Therefore Shimon need not bear a share of merchandise lost while in Levi's custody.
