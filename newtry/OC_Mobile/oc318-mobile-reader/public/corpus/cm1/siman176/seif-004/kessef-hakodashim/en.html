He deviated in a matter of the partnership. One received merchandise on credit, and another person came and proposed to become a partner in the goods. They agreed that if, by a specified date, the second person delivered his signed obligation for half the purchase price to the original seller, who would thereafter look equally to both of them, the merchandise would belong to them in partnership. It appears that the stated date may be material. One might argue that punctuality is important only when actual money is due, because possession of money itself provides benefit, whereas here the earlier delivery of a signature gives only peace of mind and no tangible benefit. If the second partner eventually delivers the required signature, perhaps it is then revealed retroactively that the partnership existed from the outset. This may resemble the Taz in Yoreh Deah 236 concerning time clauses that are not always intended as absolute conditions but only as means of implementing the parties' principal purpose. However, if the agreement also provided that the goods would be transported and the proceeds deposited with a third party, and that the third party would know from compliance with the time condition how to divide the proceeds, then the time clause clearly governs a practical monetary consequence and is more likely intended as exact. Even if the goods were eventually not sold for cash and no direct time-value benefit arose, once the parties framed the time stipulation as essential it may still control the partnership. If the proposed partner fails to give his signature by the stipulated date, perhaps he acquires no partnership share. On the other hand, if the parties contemplated from the beginning that the time clause might never affect actual cash, perhaps it was not intended absolutely. The result may therefore depend upon an assessment of the parties' intent and commercial practice. This requires careful study.
