Reuven is believed through his migo. Baer Heitev cites the Shach's objection to the Rama, that Reuven should not be believed to say, "I released the debtor with your authorization," by a migo that he could have denied releasing him, because such a migo stands against the presumption that a person does not gratuitously relinquish another's claim. The Taz raises the same difficulty and interprets the Rama as speaking only where there is no evidence that the debt remained unpaid. Shaar Mishpat argues that the Rama is correct even where witnesses establish that the debt was unpaid, so long as there is no testimony that the agent released it without authority. A debtor himself, when facing an oral loan, is believed to say, "You waived the debt," by a migo that he could have claimed payment. We do not say that such a migo is defeated by a presumption that people do not waive debts, because people do in fact waive claims and make gifts. The same should apply to an agent who says that the creditor instructed him to waive. The Sema in siman 123 similarly cites Rashba that if Reuven says Shimon authorized him to waive a gentile's debt, he swears a rabbinic oath and is exempt. Netivot HaMishpat resolves the Shach differently: the doubtful case is where the agent claims that he was told to issue the release in his own name, so that the debtor would not recognize the creditor as the benefactor. If the agent claims that he was instructed to waive explicitly in the creditor's name, and he possesses a migo, he is believed. Pitchei Teshuva questions why the Shach's objection should then have been stated broadly. Beit Meir offers another distinction. A creditor who personally waives receives whatever goodwill flows from the gift; an agent who is free either to communicate the waiver or conceal it may himself receive the debtor's gratitude, so there is a stronger presumption against believing that the creditor ordered the waiver. Even if the agent announced it in the creditor's name, much of the goodwill may attach to the agent. Pitchei Teshuva notes, however, that this is difficult in light of the Sema in siman 123, unless that case is limited to one where it was not independently established that the gentile still owed the debt. The matter therefore requires study.
