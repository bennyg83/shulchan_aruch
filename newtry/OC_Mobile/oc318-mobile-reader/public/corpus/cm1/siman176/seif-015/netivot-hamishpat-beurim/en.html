And he cannot divide. According to this, he also cannot invoke gud o agud, because each can say, "The combined fortune of two is better." Gud o agud is a substitute for division, and wherever a partner cannot demand division he likewise cannot demand gud o agud. The inability to divide means that one partner wishes to stop transacting with the partnership funds after the merchandise has been sold. But when the proper time to sell existing merchandise arrives, either can compel sale even before the formal end of the partnership. Where there is no fixed customary time for sale, if ordinary commercial practice indicates that waiting is likely to increase profit, one cannot force an immediate sale. But where there is no gain from delay, and certainly where delay risks loss, either can compel the other to sell. Rif writes this in Bava Metzia, chapter HaMekabel. If one partner refuses to perform his work, the other can compel him to hire another worker in his place. A partner has the status of a contractor-worker, and siman 333, seif 6, provides that where such a worker retracts one may hire replacements against him up to forty or fifty zuz where funds are held. A partner cannot freely retract like an ordinary day worker, for the reason Sema gives in seif katan 44. Only where the partner is ill or under ones, as in seif 9, can he avoid the work without being charged for a replacement, though he then need not receive profit for work he did not perform. If he simply refuses, a replacement can be hired against him. Alternatively, the willing partner may choose to terminate the partnership because he did not join on the understanding that the other would refuse to perform. If one partner refuses to contribute the promised capital, the law depends on the stage of the partnership. If they initially agreed that each would contribute one hundred but began transacting only with fifty each, and no kinyan was made on the full one hundred, one cannot compel the other to supply the remainder. The untransferred money never became partnership property. The party is bound only as to the portion with which they actually began transacting. As I wrote above, to prevent future retraction a full kinyan in the money is needed, such as lifting the joint purse, whereby each acquires a share in the other's money, as Maharit, part 1, siman 140, writes. The other partner may, however, terminate the partnership, saying, "I did not join on these terms." But if they already transacted with all the capital, then during the fixed term sold some merchandise and divided the cash temporarily, each taking a portion home, and later another purchase opportunity arises, each can compel the other to contribute his proportionate funds. Even if one partner has already spent or lost the money in his possession, he must supply replacement funds. By becoming hired workers and partners to one another, each is like one who was hired together with his capital. The first meshichah of the partnership money obligates him to maintain equivalent capital throughout the agreed term. This resembles one who rents an unspecified donkey under siman 310: meshichah of the first donkey obligates the lessor to provide another if the first dies. So too, meshichah of the first partnership funds creates an obligation to maintain replacement capital through the agreed period. Likewise, if they originally agreed only to join one hundred each, but because of an unforeseen risk or business need additional capital is required to preserve the joint enterprise, they can compel one another to contribute where the added expenditure benefits both, as explained in siman 178.
If he changed or was negligent. This appears to depend on the dispute in siman 306, seif 8, regarding an individual planter, whether he is treated as permanently warned and may be removed for a mistake without prior warning. A partner is comparable to a worker, as Beit Yosef writes. Accordingly, Rambam and Mordechai need not disagree. Both can hold that an ordinary worker can be removed without prior warning, but Rambam can be speaking of a deviation whose loss is recoverable, such as changing from wheat to barley, where the expected wheat profit is known and the loss can be measured and reimbursed. Mordechai can be speaking of a deviation whose loss cannot be recovered, such as refusing to contribute funds. In that case it is impossible to calculate how much profit would have been made had the funds been supplied, because the partners might have turned over the capital repeatedly and produced a much larger amount. Sema in seif katan 20 similarly says that an unskilled planter creates an irrecoverable loss because one cannot know how much improvement proper planting would have produced. Therefore the partner can be removed for such a deviation. According to the second opinion in siman 306, prior established misconduct or warning is required before removal. On that view a partner too would need either a pattern of misconduct or prior warning. A partner who steals from the partnership likewise causes an irrecoverable type of loss and can therefore be removed.
