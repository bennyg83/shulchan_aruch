And his partner cannot prevent him. Shaar Mishpat writes that if the other partner prevented a sale because he genuinely expected the merchandise to rise, and it later fell, he is not liable for the resulting loss. The same is true even if the merchandise was later sold at the lower price without his knowledge. Radbaz, I, 399, held a person liable where he knowingly prevented a sale at the established proper time, when it was certain that delay would reduce the price, treating the obstruction as dina de-garmi. Shaar Mishpat explains that there is no contradiction. Radbaz's case involved obstruction not directed toward the partnership's benefit and resembles intentional damage. Where the partner intended benefit because he reasonably believed the goods would appreciate, the case resembles inadvertent damage, for which the Sages did not impose the same penalty. He later found the same distinction in Raavan and in Maharit, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 110. See also above regarding Noda BiYehuda, Tinyana, responsum 38, where a partner asserted that his conduct had been intended for the partnership's benefit.
