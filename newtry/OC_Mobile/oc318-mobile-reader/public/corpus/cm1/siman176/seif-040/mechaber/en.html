Residents of a town purchased a Torah scroll for sixty dinars and stipulated that if one of them left to settle elsewhere, those remaining would pay him his share. If one now wishes to leave and demands his share according to the scroll's increased present value, while the others offer only his proportion of the original purchase price, the remaining residents prevail. Rama: If Reuven and Shimon were partners and held promissory notes, and the community assessed a tax against them and seized the notes for Reuven's tax, Reuven must pay Shimon his share. He cannot say that Shimon's share of the tax should also be deducted, because Reuven benefited from the notes by using them to discharge his own obligation, and he cannot pay Shimon's tax without Shimon's consent. If Reuven bought merchandise and gave Shimon, Levi, and Yehudah each a one-quarter interest in profit and loss, the merchandise then lost four hundred, and Shimon and Levi withdrew and paid Reuven three hundred, Yehudah must pay one hundred if he too wishes to withdraw. Their partnership concerned the profit and loss in that merchandise, not rights in whatever one partner might later acquire from another. Where many people are partners, one cannot withdraw without the consent of all. If a person receives merchandise to transport elsewhere and divide the profit, and before he transports it its value falls locally, the merchandise is reckoned at its value when he actually transports it, not its value when he received it.
