Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, responsum 47, was asked about Reuven and Shimon, partners, where Reuven borrowed money from a gentile for the partnership and the gentile mistakenly gave too much. Shimon demanded a share of the mistaken excess. Tumim reconciles the Rama here with siman 62 by saying that there the brother expressly stated that he intended the gain for himself, whereas here the gain was received without such declaration. Ketzot HaChoshen rejects that distinction, but Chatam Sofer defends it. Chatam Sofer then suggests another resolution. The Yerushalmi's case may concern brothers who remained in an inherited undivided estate, not voluntary business partners. Brothers in a family estate did not choose to bind themselves to one another; the estate simply remained jointly held as in their father's lifetime. Their incidental finds are therefore not necessarily common property. Voluntary partners, by contrast, bind themselves to one another by agreement, and it is reasonable that even a windfall or mistaken payment connected with partnership activity should be shared. On that basis even the Baal HaItur, who disputes the interpretation of the Yerushalmi regarding brothers, may agree in a voluntary partnership. Thus, in the case before him the mistaken excess ought to be divided. Nevertheless, because this is an inference rather than an explicit ruling, Chatam Sofer would not extract money against the Shach. If one partner already seized the disputed share, however, it would not be taken from him.
