And Levi did not give him the money. If a partner was required to contribute money and did not do so by the known time for buying the merchandise, it may be that no formal demand is necessary. If he did not contribute, the partnership may lapse as to him. Taz writes regarding an ordinary debt that a demand is required, though that itself is disputed. The responsum of Maharam cited in siman 176, seif 41, appears to indicate that even after a demand the partner did not lose his share. The questioner there, cited in Mordechai in the chapter Mi SheHayah Nasui, assumed that failure after demand caused loss. Yet that case is not decisive, because the partnership was already complete before Reuven advanced his money, and Maharam therefore treated Reuven's later payment as made on behalf of the partners. Here, by contrast, if one advanced his own money and only afterward the partnership agreement was made without discussing reimbursement, the other partner is naturally obligated to contribute his proportionate share, as the questioner there argued. If he does not, he may have no claim to the profit. This accords with Sema in seif 7 that the partners' monetary contributions are ordinarily to be equal, and one who fails to fulfill that condition may lose the corresponding profit. Still, perhaps a demand is needed, as in siman 73 regarding certain oath obligations. Knesset HaGedolah disputes Taz in Yoreh Deah 161 and holds that no demand is needed there, but that may be because of the positive mitzvah of repayment. Maharam adduces proof from the beginning of HaMafkid that only where a person causes beit din to trouble itself is he considered not to have paid. The Gemara there suggests that if judicial compulsion becomes necessary he is certainly treated as one who did not pay, whereas where he merely says he will not pay after first saying he will, the question remains unresolved. Perhaps the same distinction applies to partners. Maharam's point may be that a short delay, even after demand, does not constitute refusal because most people are not particular about a brief postponement. By contrast, a delay of the kind merchants commonly consider material may forfeit the partner's profit. It is inequitable for one person to provide all the capital and labor while the other later claims an equal profit, especially where the money itself is the primary vehicle of the business. Accordingly, where the partner knew that his contribution was then due and failed to provide it for a commercially significant period, there is strong reason to say that he lost his claim to the profit. The exact requirement of demand requires further study.
