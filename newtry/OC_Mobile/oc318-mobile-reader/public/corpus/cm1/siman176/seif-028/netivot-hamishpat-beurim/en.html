That a non Jew owes them. See Sema, seif katan 65, citing Piskei Mahari. Mahari abbreviates the incident, but its meaning appears to be as follows. Reuven and Shimon formed a partnership to lend to a non Jew. Each contributed ten gold pieces. Ten more gold pieces of interest accrued, and they capitalized the interest into principal, creating a debt of thirty gold pieces on which the non Jew would then pay further profit. Reuven then purchased part of Shimon's share of the receivable and gave Shimon ten gold pieces, so that Reuven would thereafter receive profit on twenty-five gold pieces and Shimon on five. Later the local ruler invalidated the capitalization of the interest. Mahari rules that if, when Reuven gave Shimon the ten gold pieces, he explicitly said he was paying for part of the original principal, then the loss caused by canceling the capitalized interest is borne equally by Reuven and Shimon, and Shimon loses his remaining five gold pieces. But if Reuven simply gave him ten gold pieces and said that he was buying five gold pieces of Shimon's share in the overall debt, then Reuven purchased two parts of the true principal and two parts of the capitalized interest. The loss attributable to Shimon from cancellation of the capitalized interest is therefore only one third.
Wherever there is a loss. This applies specifically where the debt is impaired because the debtor is violent or powerful, in a situation where ordinary assessment indicates that through effort the whole debt can be collected, or that nothing can be collected at all. In such a case a partner who acts may say, "I am saving for myself," like the caravan case in siman 181. But where ordinary assessment indicates that only half can be recovered, such as a debt of one hundred where the debtor has only fifty, one partner cannot simply announce that he is withdrawing from the joint debt and then race to collect for himself as though they were independent creditors, where with movable property the first to collect may sometimes prevail. Since it is known that the debtor has only fifty, the partnership effectively owns a receivable worth only fifty. The partners must divide that value equally, twenty-five each. If one takes more, he has not divided evenly but has taken the entire realizable asset and left his fellow nothing. A violent debtor is different. Although the receivable may also sell for only half because collection requires great effort, the partner who exerts himself can say: "The reason it is discounted is the labor required. To a person willing to labor, the entire debt remains collectible. You too may exert yourself and collect your whole share. I need not lose merely because you are unable or unwilling to make the effort." This also resolves an apparent difficulty in Mechaber's wording. He says that one partner did not wish to give a bribe, implying that if both were willing to give a bribe, one could not say, "I will act for myself." Why should this differ from any impaired debt, where one may say, "I will save for myself"? The answer is that where ordinary assessment indicates the whole amount can be recovered through effort, one can say, "I will not work on your behalf. Work yourself." If the other cannot make the effort because of his own ones, the first need not lose because of that. Likewise, if no recovery is ordinarily possible at all, the property is effectively despaired of, and one may say, "I will save for myself." But where even with bribes only half is expected to be recovered, if both partners are willing to pay the bribe, one cannot appropriate the recoverable half for himself. If the other refuses to contribute to the bribe, however, it resembles siman 181 where recovery is possible only with difficulty and one partner announces that he will save for himself; he may then acquire what he saves. See Sema, seif katan 68, concerning one who erred and received less. The responsum does not discuss a person who knowingly took less but falsely told the non Jew that he took more, for that would certainly create liability because he effectively released the non Jew. Rather, it concerns one who forgot and collected only his own share. If he first said, "I am collecting for myself," the collection is his. Otherwise he must share what he collected with his partner.
