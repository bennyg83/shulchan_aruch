The second partner cannot require him to swear. VeShav HaKohen, responsum 58, discusses a case where the second partner, before leaving town, expressly protested that his fellow should not litigate with the third party until he returned. The first partner ignored the protest, litigated, and the court required the third party to take the partnership oath, which he did. When the second partner returned he sought to require a new oath. VeShav HaKohen concludes that the matter is disputed. From the Rambam and Tur it appears that the rule "an oath to one is an oath to a hundred" ordinarily rests on the idea that the first claimant acts as agent for the others. Their discussion of certain geonic oaths therefore had to add that the defendant need not swear separately to every creditor even where agency cannot realistically be implied, because those oaths are only a later enactment and were not imposed with such precision. In the present case, agency cannot be presumed because the second partner expressly protested. Since the oath is a mishnaic partnership oath, one could therefore require a second oath. Rashba, however, as cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 87 regarding one who swore outside the claimant's presence, indicates that the rule "an oath to one is an oath to a hundred" applies in all circumstances, not only through agency. VeShav HaKohen therefore leaves the practical ruling unresolved.
