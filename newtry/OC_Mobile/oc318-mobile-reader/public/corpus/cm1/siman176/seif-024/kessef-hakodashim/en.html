I wrote elsewhere that just as a partner who becomes ill may receive food and medical expenses from the partnership, a partner who is detained may likewise have his maintenance paid by the partnership, even if the redemption payment itself is not a partnership obligation. The distinction in siman 177 between losses caused by a partner's negligence may apply. If the detention arose only because of conduct treated as negligent in the non-Jewish courts, but not by Torah law, perhaps it should not be treated like theft or wrongdoing that shifts the entire loss, especially where the misconduct had no connection to partnership business and the other partner derived no benefit. On the other hand, if conduct is not negligent at all under Torah law, perhaps it should not create partnership negligence merely because secular authorities punish it. This requires careful study. Partners owned merchandise. One told the other: 'If you do not contribute the money, you have no right in the goods.' The other replied, 'Keep it; I waive my share to you.' The first then moved the merchandise, but the act of meshichah was not into his own domain in the other's presence, nor did the other tell him 'pull and acquire.' Even though the goods were transported through the public domain in the other's sight, this was not an effective acquiring meshichah. We therefore ruled that the partnership remained. Had there been a valid meshichah in his presence, the result would require study. At the beginning of HaKotev, a kinyan can sometimes validate even a deficient formula such as 'I have no claim or dealings in it.' Perhaps the same is true of a waiver concerning a specific object when accompanied by a kinyan. The Shulchan Aruch discusses the issue principally in the context of a wife in Even HaEzer, and Maharik contains language suggesting that even a kinyan may not always cure an inadequate formula. It may also be that only a kinyan sudar has this effect, whereas meshichah does not transform deficient language into effective transfer. Certainly a mere movement performed in the other's presence, where it might have been intended only to carry the goods to a safer place, is weaker. This requires extensive study. Partners held a business right involving kosher meat under a yearly arrangement, where toward the end of the year it was customary to waive half or more of a payment in order to encourage slaughter and preparation for the next period. One partner, because he had rented the right for the following year, did not wish to grant the customary concession. If there is a clear commercial assessment that the current partnership would lose more by refusing the concession than the amount saved, the other partners may grant it, because the waiver serves the present partnership. A division by alternating days is not practical, even with three appraisers, because the rights are not equal in kind and cannot sensibly be assigned by lot. There was also an agreement between two people that if they obtained money from a certain person for a commercial venture, one would hold two-thirds and the other one-third. One of them later obtained money from a different person because he never reached agreement with the first anticipated financier, and he transferred some or all of those funds to his colleague. Whether the original partnership terms automatically govern this new financing depends on the wording and commercial intent of the first arrangement, and requires study.
