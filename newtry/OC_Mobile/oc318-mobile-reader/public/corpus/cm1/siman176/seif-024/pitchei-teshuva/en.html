If he divided before three, he accomplished nothing. This is the language of the Rambam and Tur. It can be understood to mean that if the merchandise itself is still present, the invalid division must be undone, so that any rise or fall in its value is still shared. But if the merchandise has already been sold and the partner subsequently made profit or suffered loss with the cash proceeds allocated to his supposed share, perhaps the later result belongs to him alone. The Sema, however, cites the Tur's later statement that if one partner divided without the other's knowledge and then profited or lost, everything remains shared as though there had been no division. That wording seems broader and would include subsequent dealings with the sale proceeds. Shevut Yaakov, I, 162, questions this reading at length. He distinguishes two situations in the Tur. Where the partnership was for no fixed term, or the fixed term has expired, each partner is entitled to demand division. If the property requires appraisal, the division must be done with the other's consent or before three competent appraisers. If he divided before fewer than three, "he accomplished nothing" means that the division of the existing merchandise is void, with consequences for any later appreciation or depreciation of that merchandise. But once the merchandise has been sold, the partnership itself has already ended, and later profit or loss in the money should not necessarily be shared. By contrast, the Tur's later statement concerns a division attempted during the agreed partnership term, when one partner has no right to dissolve the partnership unilaterally even before three. Such a division is entirely ineffective, and subsequent profit or loss remains partnership property. Shevut Yaakov suggests that even the Rama, who reads the Tur's clauses together, may mean only profit or loss in the merchandise itself, not independent profit later earned with money obtained from its sale. Once the partnership has ended and the merchandise has become cash, money is regarded as effectively divided. Tamim Deim, citing Ri, expressly distinguishes a partner who divided wine without authority and then sold it and profited with the money. Ri holds that the other partner cannot claim the later gain, no worse than in the law of ordinary robbers. Tamim Deim himself inclines to share the profit because the first partner did not intend theft but thought he was making a valid division, yet Shevut Yaakov says this contrary view is not decisive. At minimum the issue is a doubt of law, and the possessor may plead kim li like Ri. Although partners cannot always plead kim li where the disputed property itself is jointly owned, that limitation does not necessarily apply to a new profit arising only afterward. Shevut Yaakov adds that where the parties expressly formed a partnership for a term and the term has expired, the case is still clearer. From the moment the term ends, the other's property in a partner's hands is merely a deposit. Profit later earned with the money allocated to his own share belongs to him. Maharam Alshich, Maharit, and Maharam di Boton support this understanding. The Taz's ruling regarding an iska recipient who continues holding the lender's money after maturity is different, because the money originally came into his hands as a loan and he was obligated to return it at the fixed date; his failure to return it may imply continuation on the old terms. Ordinary partners, by contrast, are both obligated at expiration either to divide or expressly renew the partnership. Shevut Yaakov applied these principles to Yaakov and his sons Reuven and Shimon. After Shimon died leaving minors, beit din appointed four guardians, including Yaakov and Reuven, and they created a new partnership for a fixed term in which Reuven would manage the property. When the term expired, Reuven informed his father and the local rabbi that he would no longer continue the partnership, though he did not separately notify the minors or the two absent guardians. He made a full accounting, treated incoming funds as available for payment of debts and division, and thereafter managed the minors' portion only in his capacity as guardian. The orphans later demanded a share of profits Reuven earned with his own portion, arguing that no formal division had been made before beit din and all guardians. Shevut Yaakov held that Reuven had a strong defense even if the merchandise had been divided without full notice, because the partnership term had ended and leading authorities hold that later profits from the cash proceeds belong to the individual partner. In any event, the actual case was stronger: Reuven had informed the court, and he and his father were themselves the principal guardians. He did not secretly seize specific merchandise, but accounted for partnership receipts, took his own share, and continued managing the minors' share for their benefit as guardian. There was therefore no basis to disturb the division, apart from whatever partnership oath might still be required.
