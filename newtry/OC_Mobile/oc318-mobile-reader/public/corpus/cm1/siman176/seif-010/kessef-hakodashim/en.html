He sold on credit. Where a partner sold partnership goods on credit, the profit is shared while the loss from the unauthorized credit may fall upon him. If one partner contributed substantially more capital and the partner with the smaller contribution made the credit sale, then if the debt is ultimately collected at a profit there is strong reason to say that it is revealed retroactively that the credit sale was for the partnership, as though both partners had consented. If no fixed date had been set for liquidation, the larger contribution was necessarily exposed both to immediate cash sales and to later sales whose profit arose from waiting, and credit sales fall naturally within that commercial purpose. Even where a fixed time for sale had been contemplated, if the credit sale generated profit unavailable for cash at that time, the selling partner acted as the other's agent with respect to that profit, and therefore the profit should be shared equally as though the credit had been authorized from the outset. If the debt was known to be secure and an unforeseen event later prevented collection, the selling partner may have no liability, particularly where the goods had been sent to that location with both partners' knowledge and the inability to collect arose from the location itself. If the partner sold on credit in a distant place because immediate cash sale would have caused a serious loss, his liability should in any event be measured against the cash value of the goods there, not against their value at home, since shipment to that place was authorized. If keeping the goods there as a deposit also carried risk, commercial experts may need to assess comparatively the risk of deposit against the greater risk and profit potential of credit. This resembles valuation of uncertain benefits by expert appraisal. The primary liability for an unauthorized credit sale arises where the merchandise could readily have been sold for cash. If the selling partner is required to pay immediately because he extended credit, but lacks funds and is compelled to swear that he cannot pay, and he later succeeds in collecting the debt at a large profit, perhaps that later profit belongs to him alone. One could analogize this to the beginning of Bava Metzia, where an oath can shift beneficial rights. Yet the comparison is uncertain. He may argue that because the loss was entirely his while any profit would otherwise be shared, his own fortune caused the inability to pay immediately and his partner never acquired the later appreciation. If he actually paid his partner cash immediately, it appears stronger that he acquired the receivable for himself, even though a debt is not ordinarily purchased with money, because the original arrangement may be viewed retroactively as one in which he accepted the receivable personally if his partner refused to bear its risk. Perhaps even before payment he can demand, 'If you insist that I pay the cash value immediately, transfer to me your share of any later profit.' If the ordinary time for sale had not yet arrived, however, the other partner cannot compel immediate payment before that commercial date. If the non-selling partner expressly instructed him not to sell on credit, and further stated that any credit sale would belong solely to the seller, then the receivable clearly belongs to the seller alone. There may also be a distinction depending on whether the buyer knew the goods were partnership property and believed both partners had authorized the credit. The goods themselves did not cease being partnership property merely because one partner deviated; the real question is to whom the buyer's obligation was created. This requires extensive study. As a general matter, an unauthorized credit sale is a deviation and the selling partner is not the other's agent for an imprudent act. Even in a distant place, the principle 'better one measure from the ground than a large measure from the roof' teaches preference for secure present value over speculative gain. Mere commercial estimates that the debt is safe may not create guarantor liability in reverse, just as one who relies on another without saying 'see that I rely upon you' does not thereby become a guarantor. Prior authorization of beit din may alter the matter. If animals were taken to market and could not be sold except at a substantial discount, slaughtering them for sale as meat may count as a reasonable corrective measure if the expected proceeds are at least comparable to the available animal price. In a genuine doubt whether an act was a commercially proper repair or an unauthorized deviation, the partner holding the partnership money may say, 'Perhaps you acted improperly and therefore owe the loss,' unless expert commercial practice establishes otherwise. Similarly, choosing litigation under oath rather than a compromise is not necessarily an improper deviation when the partner had no authority to compromise and no opportunity to consult his fellow; a compromise approved by a competent beit din may likewise be considered commercially reasonable. Where goods were sold on agreed credit and the partners later divided their remaining property, failure to collect the debt exactly at maturity does not automatically obligate the collecting partner to pay the capital owner. Credit by nature includes uncertainty and the parties consented to it. Only when reasonable commercial assessment shows that the debt has become effectively uncollectible does liability arise according to the partnership terms. If they borrowed money together to fund the credit transaction, both must pay the lender at maturity. If one borrowed with authorization for the partnership, the loan likewise binds them.
