And he shall not engage in other merchandise. See Shach, seif katan 22, who writes that if he transgressed and engaged in it, the profit is his own. Only where the deviation involved partnership funds is the profit shared, but where he dealt with his own money the profit belongs to him. At first glance this is difficult. Beit Yosef writes that the rule that he may not engage in other merchandise is derived from the Tosefta concerning one who places his fellow in a shop, and the Rambam compares the two cases. Yet Yoreh Deah 177:29 states explicitly that where one places his fellow in a shop and the latter sells and conducts business with his own money, the profit is divided equally. It appears that the cases differ. Here, regarding partners, the case must be one where they partnered only with respect to a defined sum, as Beit Yosef writes on the Tur, for if they are partners in all their affairs the profit is certainly shared. Indeed, even regarding theft and robbery the Rama rules in seif 12 that the proceeds are shared. This differs from one who places his fellow in a shop. There, the latter obligated himself to conduct whatever business the owner would give him, as the Taz writes in Yoreh Deah 177, seif katan 44. He therefore stands toward the owner as a worker, whose hand is like the owner's hand. This is comparable to a worker hired to gather finds of fish who finds some other lost object, which belongs to the employer. So too, if he was hired for all types of work, profit from another type of work belongs to the employer; see siman 270. Since he is like a worker for all commercial matters, his status is comparable to partners in all their business, where even a transaction conducted with his own money is shared. In truth, even where one places his fellow in a shop, if the latter undertook only a specific type of business and then bought a different type with his own money, although he was prohibited from doing so initially, his hand is not obligated to the owner for that different business, and the profit he earns from it does not belong to the owner. It resembles the worker told, "Weed with me today," whose find belongs to the worker, as explained in siman 270:3. So too with partners, outside the sphere in which they partnered, one is not the other's worker. Therefore, although he was prohibited from engaging in the other business, if he did so the profit belongs to him.
Merchandise that is always sold on credit. See Sema, seif katan 33, through his words excluding a case where it is sometimes not sold on credit. His meaning is that some people sometimes do not sell that type of merchandise on credit at all. But if the ordinary practice is always to sell some of that merchandise on credit and some for cash, he is exempt. With merchandise ordinarily sold entirely on credit, he is exempt even for debts owed by gentiles. Although siman 253 explains that one cannot rely at all on their payment, the responsum of the Rosh cited by the Tur in this siman nevertheless makes clear that he is exempt.
And if he warned him. It appears that although one partner may object, the other may say: since it is ordinary for this merchandise to be sold on credit, and the purchaser is considered reliable by beit din, this is equivalent to the time for sale of this merchandise having arrived. He can therefore divide and tell his partner, "I will sell my share on credit, and you take your share." In a matter whose normal practice is such, each can compel the other to divide. The objecting partner can only prevent the other from selling his own share on credit.
And he shall not deposit it. In my humble opinion, if he gave the property to a person with whom the other partner regularly deposits his property, and it was stolen while in that person's custody, the depositing partner is exempt, just as any guardian who transfers property to someone with whom the owner regularly deposits is exempt; see siman 291:26. However, if damage results to the commercial venture, for example because the custodian was not at home at the time the merchandise needed to be sold, the partner who deposited it must compensate the other for that loss. Even so, this does not make his initial act negligence with respect to the theft, because the rule of an act that began in negligence applies specifically within the law of guardianship.
Or he sold on credit. If he sold on credit, he is liable only for the amount the item would have been worth in a cash sale. Even an actual tortfeasor does not pay more than the item's cash sale value. This is clear.
The profit is divided equally. See Sema, seif katan 37, through his words that this partner did not intend by his deviation to acquire for himself. His language implies that if the partner stated before witnesses that he was acquiring for himself, the profit would be his. Although such a declaration would not help where he did not deviate from the partnership arrangement, since partners cannot withdraw even with respect to future profit, as explained at the beginning of this siman, it would help where he changed the arrangement. At first glance this contradicts the Sema's own statement in seif katan 32 that if a partner conducts a different business for himself, the profit is still shared, and only if he joins another person using his own money is the profit his. The Shach apparently disagrees with the Sema only where the partner conducts the other business with his own money for himself, but where he conducts another business for himself using partnership money, even the Shach appears to agree that the profit is shared. A partner is not comparable to an agent, because an agent can withdraw from the agency, whereas partners cannot withdraw from the partnership obligations already undertaken. See what Beit Yosef writes in seif 36 in the name of the Rashba. With Hashem's help, this will be explained in siman 183.
