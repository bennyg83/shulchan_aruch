In siman 131, seif katan 14, the Sema asks that with respect to a guarantor the law is that he must pay even if the gentile falsely accused the guarantor, whereas here liability arises only if Shimon proves what occurred. His question is evidently that the partner entered as guarantor for his fellow partner, and should therefore be liable even for a false accusation. The Sema answers there that the cases differ, because the gentile's original dealing was only with Levi, and it is unusual for the gentile later to demand payment from Reuven. Therefore Reuven did not accept liability for a false accusation. This answer is very strained. The gentile did not wish to deal with Levi unless Reuven entered as guarantor for him, and under their law a gentile creditor may proceed against the guarantor. Moreover, seif 12 in the gloss states that if the partners divided other profits and a false accusation was later brought against one of them, the other is not obligated to assist him. This implies that he is exempt even where the gentile dealt only with that partner. It therefore appears to me that the case of a guarantor is different because there is a compelling presumption regarding the intent of both parties. Had they explicitly discussed the matter, they certainly would have agreed that the borrower bears even the risk of a false accusation. The guarantor surely would not have agreed to guarantee the debt unless the borrower undertook to bear that risk, for why should the guarantor voluntarily expose himself to such trouble? The guarantee benefits the borrower, not the guarantor. The borrower too surely would have agreed to assume the risk, because had the gentile trusted him directly he would have undertaken the same exposure to false accusation. Now that the gentile refuses to rely on him without another person's guarantee, and under gentile law the creditor proceeds against the guarantor, the guarantor takes the borrower's place with respect to that risk solely for the borrower's benefit. There is no reason to assume that the borrower would refuse to bear the risk into which the guarantor entered for him. Since it is a compelling presumption that had they expressly stipulated they would have accepted this arrangement, we attribute their failure to spell it out to an oversight, much as we say concerning ordinary liability that an omitted stipulation was left unstated only through oversight. Here, by contrast, Shimon conducted the transaction without Reuven's knowledge, as explained by the Tur. Reuven participated only because he later agreed to Shimon's acts. There is no compelling presumption that Reuven intended to assume liability for a false accusation. He can say that had Shimon expressly stated that Reuven would bear the risk of such an accusation, Reuven would never have joined the partnership. Even if Reuven had said expressly that he did not accept that risk, Shimon might still have accepted him as a partner because of the genuine risk relating to Yehudah, namely that Yehudah might in fact fail to pay. Shimon may have wanted the partnership solely so that Reuven would share that ordinary credit risk. Since there is no compelling presumption that Reuven accepted the risk of a false accusation, he cannot be charged with it. For this reason it appears to me that Reuven can say at the outset, "Either accept upon yourself the risk of a false accusation as well, or the entire venture belongs to me alone." Since, given the known likelihood that a gentile may make such an accusation, there is no definite profit, Reuven need not accept the transaction on the terms Shimon chose. However, if one partner conducts business with partnership money without the other's knowledge, and a false accusation is made that causes a loss to the partnership funds, the other partner cannot demand reimbursement from him. Seif 12 indicates that only where a partner enters a venture involving uncertainty of nonkosher meat and the like does he bear the loss personally. This implies that if he conducts ordinary merchandise without his partner's knowledge, the loss is shared. At first glance this is difficult in light of siman 178, where one who spends his own money without his partner's knowledge can recover only to the extent of the resulting improvement. The distinction appears to be that when a person spends his own money without the partner's knowledge, even if he intended to improve the partnership property, he recovers his expenditure only up to the amount of the improvement. But if he conducts business using partnership money, any resulting loss is shared, because the parties surely entered the partnership with that understanding. Here, however, the case is one where Shimon sold to Yehudah for more than the amount at which Shimon had bought from the gentile. He intended to increase the partnership profit without Reuven's knowledge, and for that purpose issued a promissory note and subjected himself personally for the benefit of the partnership. This is equivalent to contributing his own property for the partnership's benefit. There is no difference whether he gave his own cash or placed his own property under personal obligation. Therefore he cannot charge Reuven unless Reuven agreed to his acts. One who contributes his own property, intending without his partner's knowledge to improve the partnership, recovers only his expenditure up to the amount of the improvement.
