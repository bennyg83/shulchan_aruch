The partnership. Baer Heitev states that the heirs may likewise withdraw. Maayan Ganim writes that if they do not withdraw and the iska continues in their hands after their father's death, they must give the investor his share of profits earned afterward, provided they knew of the iska and chose not to return it. If they had no knowledge of it, they plainly did not conduct the business for the investor. Avkat Rochel, responsum 136, records a question sent to the Beit Yosef by Rabbi Yitzchak Ashkenazi. Shimon received one hundred from Reuven as an iska, purchased merchandise, and shipped it by sea with Reuven's authorization. Shimon died on the journey and was cast into the sea, while Reuven was not present to receive the property. The merchandise later sank or was lost. The question was whether Reuven could collect the loan half of the iska from Shimon's heirs, or whether the business reverted automatically to its owner at Shimon's death. A second question concerned a case where the merchandise reached land, was sold, and produced a profit. The Beit Yosef answered that the Rambam's rule that death of a partner or manager terminates the partnership means only that the surviving party may now demand division and cannot be compelled to continue. It does not mean that the assets automatically return to the investor at the instant of death. The heirs do not discharge the loan half until it actually reaches Reuven. Therefore, if the merchandise was lost, Reuven may collect from Shimon's estate the amount corresponding to the loan half. If it was sold at a profit and Reuven had not terminated the arrangement when Shimon died, the heirs stand in Shimon's place with respect to profit and loss and divide the profit with Reuven. Pitchei Teshuva notes that this may be compared to heirs who continue using a borrowed cow for the remainder of the original loan period, though that analogy requires care because heirs are not liable for accidental death of the borrowed cow. The distinction is that a borrower's liability for unavoidable accident does not attach until the accident occurs, whereas liability for the loan half of an iska attaches from the moment the money is received. Even so, Pitchei Teshuva says the Beit Yosef's ruling deserves further analysis. Shevut Yaakov, I, 161, writes that even if a partnership document says the partnership shall continue "from now and forever," death of a partner still creates at least a doubt whether the heirs can be compelled to continue. To bind them certainly, the instrument must expressly state that the obligation continues to the partner and his descendants after him forever.
