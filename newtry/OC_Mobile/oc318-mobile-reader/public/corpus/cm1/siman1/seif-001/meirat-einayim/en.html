Nowadays the judges adjudicate. On the verse, 'Judges and officers you shall appoint for yourself in all your gates,' the Tur writes that the Gemara concludes that in Eretz Yisrael judges must be appointed in every district and every city, whereas outside Eretz Yisrael in every district but not every city. This applied when semichah existed. Nowadays, when semichah no longer exists, all judges lack Torah authority, for the verse 'these are the laws that you shall place before them' refers to ordained judges and not ordinary judges, and we today are ordinary judges. We therefore adjudicate only as agents of the earlier ordained courts, and that agency applies only to common matters involving monetary loss. This explains the Mechaber's words. The Tur also cites Rabbi Yirmiyah's exposition that 'which you shall place before them' teaches that beit din must have available its instruments of enforcement, such as a staff, strap, and shofar for excommunication. It is surprising that the Mechaber and Rama omit this, and I have not seen elder rabbis careful to keep these implements before them. Perhaps they understood that this applied only to ordained judges, although Rav Hai's plain wording and the Tur's citation do not imply that distinction.
Laws of admissions and loans. The Tur explains: where one sues on the basis of witnesses who heard the debtor admit, or witnesses who saw the loan. The same applies to a claim of a loan without witnesses for purposes of a hesset oath; see the Perishah.
And they involve monetary loss. In inheritance and gifts as well, according to the claimant's allegation there is monetary loss. The reason in all these matters is to prevent the door from being closed before borrowers and, conversely, to close the door before wrongdoers. The Sages therefore authorized today's courts to act as agents of the earlier ordained courts. It appears to me that the same may apply to other weighty matters, for we find that converts are accepted nowadays even though conversion is uncommon, and we also find coercion regarding gittin in Bavel even though that does not involve monetary loss. See the Ran, Beit Yosef, Darkei Moshe, and Rama's gloss.
For example, an animal that injured, etc. The Mechaber adopts Rambam's wording at the beginning of chapter 5 of Hilchot Sanhedrin. Rambam calls a derivative of horn, where the conduct is unusual and not for the animal's benefit, 'injury.' Therefore the Mechaber writes without qualification that such cases are not adjudicated. This is not limited to an animal injuring another animal, but applies whenever it causes an unusual type of damage. Conduct that is normal or for the animal's benefit is not termed 'injury' here but ordinary damage. Thus later he writes that damage by tooth and foot is collected by judges outside Eretz Yisrael, even when one animal damages another, because a normal occurrence between animals is adjudicated.
For example, double payment. The Mechaber follows Rambam. This is difficult because double payment is independently a fine, and fines are not collected in Bavel without ordained judges. The Tur instead mentions humiliation. Likewise, in the earlier category of uncommon matters involving monetary loss, the Tur writes injury by one person to another rather than an animal's unusual horn damage, which itself has the law of a fine. One can answer that since even humiliation and personal injury are not collected because Chazal gave uncommon or non-monetary-loss claims the legal status of fines for this purpose, Rambam gave as examples matters that are literally fines. The same principle applies to anything Chazal treated as a fine by withholding collection nowadays.
Such as one who struck his fellow. Rambam and the Mechaber use the Mishnah's wording in chapter HaChovel. Below, siman 420, seif 41, the Mechaber explains it according to Rambam as one who strikes another with his palm and pays a sela, or slaps his face, etc. Rashi and Ran explain differently. Rashi there implies that a fixed amount is what gives an award the character of a fine. Nevertheless, even an unfixed claim that is uncommon and lacks ordinary monetary loss is not collected nowadays and is called a fine in this context because Chazal gave it the law of a fine for collection purposes. Tosafot likewise indicates this.
More than the amount of the damage. For example, a thief pays double, one who slaughtered or sold stolen livestock pays four or five times, one who robbed and falsely swore pays principal plus a fifth, and an innocuous ox pays half-damages. The exception of half-damages from pebbles means where an animal walks in a public domain and kicks pebbles that break vessels. This is ordinary conduct and a derivative of foot, and by strict law the owner should pay full damages, but a halacha transmitted to Moshe at Sinai limits payment to half-damages, as explained below in siman 390.
Experts. Meaning people tested and experienced in law, as explained in siman 3, seif 2.
The ordained judges. Rambam explains at the beginning of chapter 4 of Hilchot Sanhedrin that the term semichah derives from Moshe placing his hands upon Yehoshua and appointing him. Semichah in later generations was not performed by laying hands, but by calling the candidate 'Rabbi' and saying, 'You are ordained and have authority to adjudicate even fines.' Since each ordained judge had to derive ordination from another in an unbroken chain back to Yehoshua, except for the special possibility involving all sages of Eretz Yisrael, the institution retained the name semichah. See Rivash concerning the Ashkenazic ordination custom, cited by Rama in Yoreh Deah. The Mechaber's careful phrase 'experts ordained in Eretz Yisrael' follows Rambam, who writes that only a beit din ordained in Eretz Yisrael bears the title 'elohim' in the judicial sense, that ordination is granted by a court in Eretz Yisrael, and that one cannot be ordained outside Eretz Yisrael. An ordained court that later travels outside Eretz Yisrael may nevertheless adjudicate fines there.
