They adjudicate, etc. By Torah law there is a commandment to appoint judges and officers in Eretz Yisrael in every city, and outside Eretz Yisrael in every district. The judges themselves, however, must be ordained, for the verse uses the term "Elohim" in this section, and we rule that the passages are combined. Nowadays, when there are no ordained judges, the Torah commandment of appointing judges in this sense is no longer operative. Nevertheless, for the proper ordering of society, the Sages ordained that even unordained judges may adjudicate common monetary matters such as admissions and loans, so that violent men should not prevail. They act as agents of the earlier ordained courts. Even so, the rabbinic institution follows the pattern of Torah law, and therefore there remains an obligation to appoint judges in Eretz Yisrael in every city and outside Eretz Yisrael in every district. Bach writes likewise. The Tur cites the Gemara in Sanhedrin: the verse says, "which you shall place before them." It should have said, "which you shall teach them." The Gemara derives from this the implements of the judges. Rav Hai Gaon writes that a staff and strap for punishment, and a shofar for excommunication of transgressors and recalcitrants, should be available at the sitting of beit din. The Mechaber and Rama omitted this, and elder rabbis have not practiced it. Sema suggests that perhaps they understood the teaching to apply only to ordained judges. Yet Rav Huna, who was not ordained, had such implements brought when he went to adjudicate. One could answer that when ordained judges still existed in Eretz Yisrael, even judges outside Eretz Yisrael needed these implements in order to compel obedience to rulings connected to those courts, whereas nowadays, when no ordained judges exist at all, they are unnecessary. This is still forced, especially because Rambam records laws that are not currently practiced and yet omitted this rule. It therefore appears that Rambam regarded the derivation as an asmachta rather than an actual obligation. The Gemara's report that Rav Huna personally did this then shows it was an extra measure of diligence rather than a universal requirement. Rabbi Akiva also derives the wording "which you shall place" in Eruvin 54b for another teaching, that one must present the law clearly. Rambam therefore omitted the implements entirely.
Admissions and loans. Rashi in the first chapter of Sanhedrin and the Tur explain this as a claim supported by witnesses to an admission or witnesses who saw the loan. Perishah and Sema write that the same applies even without witnesses, for purposes of a heset oath, except that at the time of the original enactment heset had not yet been instituted. This does not fully answer the wording, because even before heset one could have a Torah oath where the defendant admits part of the claim. The better explanation is that the Sages enacted these courts because of the need to prevent violent people from retaining money in circumstances where there were witnesses to the loan or admission. Had the lender chosen to lend entirely in private and rely only on personal trust, there would have been less reason for a special public enactment, since he caused his own difficulty. Once the Sages instituted adjudication for witnessed loans and admissions, however, they applied it broadly to all common monetary claims, whether the resulting oath is Torah or rabbinic.
And they involve monetary loss, etc. The same applies to inheritances and gifts where, according to the claimant's account, there is actual monetary loss. The requirement of monetary loss is specific to monetary matters. In other areas everything depends upon frequency. Thus conversions are accepted and a husband may be compelled regarding a get nowadays because such matters are common. Sema.
For example, an animal that injured. This means damage by the horn, where the act is unusual and not performed for the animal's benefit. Damage by tooth and foot is not called "injury" in this sense but ordinary damage, and it is adjudicated nowadays. Sema.
And similarly all fines, etc. This is a borrowed expression. Matters that are not adjudicated nowadays because they are uncommon and do not involve ordinary monetary loss are called "fines" in this context. Sema.
Ordained in Eretz Yisrael. Ordination means that one is called "Rabbi" and given authority to adjudicate. The chain must continue person from person back to Yehoshua, who was ordained by Moshe through laying on of hands. For that reason the institution is called semichah even though later ordination was not performed physically by hands. See Rambam, who discusses this at length. Sema.
