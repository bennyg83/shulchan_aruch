Laws of admissions and loans. The Tur explains that this means a claimant who sues on the basis of witnesses before whom the defendant admitted the debt, or witnesses before whom the loan itself was made. See Taz, who writes that the Tur wished to exclude another explanation of Rashi, namely that "admissions and loans" refers to a partial admission and a complete denial, which would imply that ordained experts are required even for a matter of rabbinic origin. It appears to me, however, that since we rule regarding a hesset oath that if the defendant refuses to swear, beit din does not descend to his property but places him under ban until he swears, there is no practical distinction regarding hesset. Even in matters that our courts cannot adjudicate, they may place him under ban under the enactment of the two academies. The Tur therefore specifically explains "admissions" as a claim established by witnesses to an admission. Accordingly, the Sema's statement that the same applies where he sues without witnesses, with the practical difference being a hesset oath, is difficult, because, as stated, hesset creates no practical difference here.
An animal that caused injury. The Shach writes that the same applies to damage caused by fire and a pit, citing Maharshal, Perek HaChovel, siman 5. In my view, since Hagahot Ashri there rules that we do adjudicate fire and pit, and cites only Maharit as uncertain, while Tosafot also appears to hold that fire and pit are adjudicated, the matter is clear. Tosafot on Bava Kamma 14b discusses the Mishnah's phrase "before beit din," which excludes an ordinary lay court, and notes that even according to the opinion that "ox" refers to foot and "maveh" to tooth, which are not fines, the Mishnah can still use that phrase just as it says "before witnesses" even though the exclusion of one who admits a fine and is then contradicted by witnesses applies only to fines. If fire and pit required ordained experts, however, the phrase could simply apply directly to those torts, which are included among the four primary categories. It follows that Tosafot plainly assumes that fire and pit do not require ordained experts. Since Tosafot is certain on this point, a later doubt cannot displace that certainty. Therefore fire and pit do not require ordained experts.
