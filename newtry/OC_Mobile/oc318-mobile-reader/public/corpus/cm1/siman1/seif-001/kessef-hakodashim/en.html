Seif 1. In the present time. The Urim VeTumim writes at the beginning of siman 1 that in the present era, when there are no ordained judges, whatever the Sages instituted they instituted in a manner analogous to Torah law. Thus, with respect to common matters in which we act as agents of the ordained judges, there remains a mitzvah to appoint judges. In Eretz Yisrael the appointment is for every city, and outside Eretz Yisrael for every district. This includes appointing an officer to enforce their rulings, corresponding to the Torah command. It appears, however, that with respect to pure halachic instruction, without coercion, there may be a Torah obligation even now, since the requirement of ordination is needed only for coercive judicial power. Where the matter is only instruction, without need of compulsion, it is incumbent to designate competent teachers among the people. Yet outside monetary and capital law we do not find an explicit mitzvah to appoint decisors. There is a mitzvah upon one who knows how to rule to rule correctly, and this is counted among the mitzvot, but I have not presently found an explicit Torah command to appoint teachers. There is also the command of "you shall not deviate," and the command to follow the majority, requiring obedience to proper halachic authorities. If one doubts whether a teacher is ruling correctly, he must inquire among the great sages of Israel until the proper ruling becomes known and then follow it. The Torah explicitly commands appointment only regarding the Great Beit Din in Jerusalem when the Beit HaMikdash stands, and regarding monetary law, where we were commanded to appoint judges and officers. The adjacent prohibitions against bribery and favoritism concern disputes between people, and all the more capital law. Rambam's language in Sefer HaMitzvot indicates that this command also includes general protection against wrongdoing among people. This requires further study, because if so it would seem applicable even without ordained judges, since protection from wrongdoing does not itself appear to require ordination. The authority of townspeople and the city scholar to enforce communal enactments also appears to have a Torah basis. Perhaps, however, because the verse says "judges," meaning ordained judges, this particular mitzvah applies only when ordination exists. Rambam's omission of appointing judges from his list of positive commandments applicable at all times proves nothing, since there he lists only commandments incumbent upon every individual without a special circumstance. In any event, outside Eretz Yisrael this command falls upon the people of the district, and in Eretz Yisrael upon the people of each city, not upon each individual. Sefer Chareidim does count appointing judges among Torah commandments applicable at all times. Perhaps he means that ordination could in principle have existed at all times were it not for external circumstances, though he did not count collection of fines as applying at all times. This requires further study. Perhaps there is likewise a constant duty to appoint halachic instructors, even for all mitzvot presently observed, and this may be included in appointing judges. The term mishpat embraces the investigation needed to know the ruling, the ruling itself, and the authority to enforce it, as Rashi explains in many places. Indeed, there may be an even greater need to appoint instructors in matters of prohibition and permission so that the public not stumble. In monetary disputes, the litigants ordinarily will not rest until they learn the law from competent sages, for a person does not abandon his money for nothing. In communal halachic matters, by contrast, responsibility can be neglected like a pot belonging to partners, so there is greater need to appoint proper instructors. Perhaps the same geographical measure applies, one in each city in Eretz Yisrael and one in each district outside it. Or perhaps that measure outside Eretz Yisrael is rabbinic and no fixed measure was given for instruction, the duty being according to need to remove stumbling blocks. On this approach, even monetary instruction without an officer may require appointment according to local need, comparable to the laws concerning one who says, "I will go to the Great Beit Din," and parties who seek adjudication outside their city in siman 14. Nevertheless, until the matter is resolved by careful study, we cannot depart from the Urim VeTumim, who does not mention an appointment obligation concerning instruction, which at first glance implies that no such command was imposed. Even so, all agree that there is a prohibition against appointing one who is not sufficiently wise for the halachic responsibilities required of the position. Even if a candidate possesses many other virtues, he must be evaluated according to the type of halachic wisdom the office demands. Perhaps, although the Sema and Urim VeTumim write that without ordained judges there is no present Torah mitzvah of appointment, nevertheless judgments rendered today by virtue of the agency of the ordained judges fulfill a positive commandment, or at least one of the positive commandments connected with adjudication counted by Sefer Chareidim. Although the Urim VeTumim writes that the agency given by the ordained judges to the unordained exists as a measure for the welfare of society, many details of monetary law are themselves not explicit in the Torah and are embraced by the command to judge properly in accordance with the reasoned enactments and agreements of each generation. The Sages only ruled leniently in cases of doubt concerning rabbinic enactments; observance of the enactments themselves is mandated by Torah authority. Thus the Sages said that the words of the Sages are weighty and beloved. Perhaps the same is true here. Still, it is possible that when the ordained judges empowered others to adjudicate, they granted only permission to judge and to use enforcement in common matters, not an obligation to appoint them. If so, the matter may remain a doubt concerning the scope of the enactment. On the other hand, perhaps the Urim VeTumim's own rule that everything the Sages enacted they enacted in the pattern of Torah law itself decides the point, namely that they instituted a duty to appoint proper judges and officers in every required jurisdiction. The matter requires further study.
