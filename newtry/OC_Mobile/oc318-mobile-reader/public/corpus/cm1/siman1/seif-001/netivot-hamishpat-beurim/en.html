In the present time, etc. Regarding the rule that we act as the agents of the earlier ordained judges, the Gemara appears to indicate that this is only rabbinic, for it gives the reason of preventing the doors from being locked before borrowers. In my view, however, its essential force is from Torah law; the Torah entrusted its implementation to the Sages, and they made us agents only where there is concern for closing the doors. Proof is that we accept converts in the present time on the basis that we act as their agents, and the conversion is fully effective, so that subsequent kiddushin by another person cannot take effect. The Sages have no power to uproot Torah law through an active act. Likewise we compel the giving of gittin, even though a get compelled by unqualified laymen is invalid by Torah law. It is strained to say that the Sages simply annulled the original kiddushin, for if so this should have been stated explicitly because it creates practical legal consequences. Ramban and Rashba, however, write that the agency is rabbinic and that in gittin the effectiveness rests on annulment of the kiddushin; see what I write below in seif katan 5. Ketzot HaChoshen explains that Tur interpreted admissions as witnesses to admissions rather than a case of complete denial because a hesset oath is enforced only by excommunication and not by seizing assets. In his view there is therefore no practical distinction, since even in penalty cases one may excommunicate. This is not convincing. If the hesset oath itself were not adjudicated, the excommunication in seif 5 would not apply. There the ban is to bring about appeasement; here the ban enforces an actual judicial ruling requiring the oath. If there is no adjudication, there is no such ban. Indeed, one who denies a penalty is not placed under ban merely for denying it. Moreover, in hesset the ban is not lifted until he swears or pays the entire claim, unlike penalty cases, as Sema explains.
