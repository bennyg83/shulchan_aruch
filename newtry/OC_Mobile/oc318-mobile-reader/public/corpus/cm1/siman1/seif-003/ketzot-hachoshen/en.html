Some say this applies specifically to robberies and injuries that are common. The Sema writes that Tosafot distinguishes differently: only robberies that arise through bodily injury are not adjudicated, and this is the Mechaber's view when he writes generally that robberies are adjudicated. Maharsha, however, explains that the Mishnah's "robberies and injuries" means robberies independently and injuries independently, as the Gemara itself derives "robberies" from the verse, "the owner of the house shall approach the judges." Yet today independent robbery claims are adjudicated. This requires explanation: why in the Mishnah's period did independent robbery require ordained experts, while today it does not? It appears that the purpose of removing the requirement of ordained experts in robbery cases is to prevent the door from being opened to wrongdoers. In the Mishnah's period ordained experts were available. Wrongdoers were already deterred by the possibility that the victim would trouble himself to seek out an expert court. Therefore the Sages did not remove the requirement of ordained experts for robbers. They did remove it for admissions and loans, because lenders would otherwise hesitate to lend if they might later be forced to seek out experts. Today, however, when ordained experts no longer exist, the Sages had to remove the requirement entirely from robberies and from every frequent matter involving monetary loss, so that wrongdoers should not be left without judicial restraint. This is indeed the approach of the Rosh, and I later found it stated by Ramban and Ran. According to this, the Gemara in the first chapter of Sanhedrin gives the reason of not locking the door before borrowers because it addresses the Mishnah's period, when experts existed and only admissions and loans were exempted. Perek HaChovel and Perek HaMegarash, by contrast, discuss our era, when there are no ordained experts and the basis is that we act as agents of the earlier courts. Nevertheless, this cannot be the approach of Tosafot, because Tosafot at the end of the first chapter of Bava Kamma asks why the Mishnah needs to exclude lay courts even according to the view that the categories there are tooth and foot, which are not fines. If Tosafot held that in the Mishnah's period even ordinary robbery, fire, pit, tooth, and foot required experts, there would be no question. Therefore Tosafot must hold that there is no distinction between the Mishnah's period and ours, and that only robbery arising through bodily injury requires experts. The Shach likewise explains the Mishnah that way, unlike Maharsha. The Kesef Mishneh, however, writes that Tosafot's distinction is Rambam's view, as the Sema says. This is difficult, because Rambam rules that pain, medical expenses, and loss of work are adjudicated even when they arise from uncommon injuries, such as severing a hand. Why should robbery arising from injury be weaker? It therefore appears that the Shach is correct: Rambam also holds that robberies arising through injury are adjudicated, and excludes only the penal aspect of robbery. The Tumim's question from the Mishnah's wording, "robberies and double payment," is no difficulty, because Rambam himself uses the same formulation and the Shach explains that there are two forms of double payment.
