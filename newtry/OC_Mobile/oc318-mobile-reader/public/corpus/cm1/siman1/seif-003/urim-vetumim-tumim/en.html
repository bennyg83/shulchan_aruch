Although Sema's words are stated in several places, Shach correctly observes that according to that approach Rambam and Mechaber should have stated the central law that robberies committed through personal injury are not adjudicated nowadays, as the Gemara says. Shach's own answer had already been brought by Kesef Mishneh and rejected for several reasons. Shach also writes that Maharsha's explanation is strained. His intent is clear: if the Mishnah's term "robberies" means ordinary robbery without personal injury, then the difficulty remains, because ordinary robbery is something for which later courts do act as agents. Indeed, the problem becomes sharper, because the Mishnah must be speaking of payment of principal, since double payment is listed separately. Why then should expert judges be required for principal, when principal robbery claims are adjudicated through agency? Rosh's language at the beginning of Sanhedrin also requires explanation. He first writes that admissions and loans do not require expert judges or examination and inquiry, whereas robberies and personal injuries do require experts and examination and inquiry. Yet in HaChovel he concludes that anything common is adjudicated through agency, and says that personal injury is uncommon. This implies that every common matter, including robbery, is adjudicated nowadays, so that only personal injury remains excluded. His two discussions appear contradictory. Bach already noted the problem and proposed that there were two separate enactments, one because of "closing the door before borrowers" and another through judicial agency. But Giddulei Terumah has correctly written that the Gemara does not sound this way. The two are one and the same: because of concern that lenders would stop lending, Chazal authorized later judges to act as agents of the ordained courts. The language in Gittin indicates this, Rashi says so explicitly in Bava Kamma 84b, and Tosafot write similarly in several places. The answer can be developed from Tosafot in Sanhedrin 3a. Tosafot ask that we find several places where robbery and personal injury were adjudicated in Bavel. One of their answers is that the phrase sometimes means robbery that arose through personal injury, which is uncommon, whereas robbery and personal injury taken separately can be common. Maharsha explains that wherever the Gemara elsewhere says "robberies and personal injuries," it can sometimes mean robberies arising through personal injury. But in our Mishnah, the words must mean ordinary robbery and ordinary personal injury, because the Gemara itself derives "robbery" from the verse "the master of the house shall approach the judges," and treats "personal injuries" separately. At first glance one might object: if the Gemara only initially thought of the verse for robbery, how can that prove the Mishnah means ordinary robbery rather than robbery arising through assault? The answer is that if the Mishnah intended robbery through assault, it should not have listed "robbery" generically. It would have been more natural to place such cases with damages and half-damages. The Mishnah's structure therefore proves that its "robbery" means robbery itself. In my novellae I suggested that this distinction may underlie the disagreement between R. Abahu and Rava in the opening sugya of Sanhedrin. R. Abahu does not establish the Mishnah as Rava does, with monetary cases before three lay judges but robbery and personal injury before three experts, because he does not read "robbery and personal injury" as robbery through injury. He is therefore troubled that ordinary robbery is adjudicated without experts. He instead understands the Mishnah as teaching that monetary cases, meaning robbery and personal injury, require three, not necessarily that all three must be ordained, while admissions and loans can sometimes be adjudicated by a single expert. Rava, by contrast, can follow Tosafot's reading that the special robbery category is robbery arising through injury, and therefore he can preserve the distinction between lay and expert judges. Even if one does not accept that particular reconstruction, the fundamental distinction remains. Tosafot explain at the beginning of that passage that concern for the orderly functioning of society does not require the same relaxation in robbery as in loans. A lender may easily refrain from lending if convenient judges are unavailable. A robber, however, knows that the victim will trouble himself to locate expert judges in order to recover his stolen property. This gives us a clear distinction between Eretz Yisrael at a time when ordained judges were readily available and Bavel, or later generations, when they were not. In Eretz Yisrael while experts were common, there was no need for a special social enactment to allow lay judges to hear ordinary robbery claims. The robber knew that the victim could go to an ordained beit din and recover the stolen property. The Mishnah therefore properly says that robbery requires experts. But outside Eretz Yisrael, far from ordained judges, and all the more so in generations when even Eretz Yisrael lacked practical semikhah, there is an obvious need for judicial agency. Otherwise anyone could steal with impunity. Would a victim spend one hundred in expenses to recover one hundred by traveling to a distant ordained court? If no ordained judges existed anywhere, violent people would simply seize and steal without judicial restraint. There is no greater public need than to empower courts to adjudicate such cases. This also resolves Tosafot's question from Gittin 88. The Gemara says that R. Yosef can act in a get case because "we perform their agency," and asks: if so, why not robbery and personal injury? It answers that these are uncommon. Tosafot understand the question as arising from R. Yosef's own conduct, because in his era ordained judges were absent in Bavel and exceedingly difficult to find even in Eretz Yisrael. Why then did he refrain from judging robbery and personal injury? Tosafot answer that the "robbery and personal injury" excluded there means robbery arising through personal injury, which is extremely uncommon. Ordinary robbery itself remained subject to later judicial agency because public order required it. This is Maharsha's intent when he says that the phrase in other Talmudic passages can bear that meaning. He is referring principally to the Gittin passage from which Tosafot's entire question arises. And now, the Mishnah is not difficult. It speaks in Eretz Yisrael, in an era in which ordained judges were common, and therefore ordinary robbery remained for experts. Later, when semikhah diminished or disappeared, ordinary robbery became subject to lay adjudication through agency because otherwise civil order would collapse. In truth, this also explains Rosh. At the beginning of Sanhedrin he is explaining the Mishnah itself, which reflects the period when ordained judges existed. He therefore says robbery and personal injury require experts and examination and inquiry. Later, in HaChovel, he discusses the practical law of later generations. There he says that robbery is sufficiently common to be adjudicated through agency, unlike personal injury, which remains too uncommon for that enactment. "Common" there does not mean as common as admissions and loans. It means sufficiently common that, once ordained judges are unavailable, the needs of society require ordinary courts to hear it. Thus there is no contradiction in Rosh. This appears to be the view of Rambam as well. In Hilchot Sanhedrin, chapter 5, halachah 8, Rambam writes that fines such as certain robberies and personal injuries are adjudicated only by three expert judges ordained in Eretz Yisrael. That is the Mishnah's basic law for a functioning semikhah system. In halachah 9, however, he writes that courts outside Eretz Yisrael do not adjudicate double payment. The implication is that they do adjudicate the principal of robbery. Had he meant to exclude robbery altogether, he should have said so explicitly. Then, in halachah 13, Rambam states: one who stole or robbed is made to pay the principal by courts outside Eretz Yisrael, but not the double payment. He deliberately says "courts outside Eretz Yisrael" rather than "lay judges." This is because the operative distinction is not simply expert versus lay. It is that outside Eretz Yisrael, where ordained judges are unavailable, public necessity requires later courts to act as their agents. Rambam's repeated use of "courts outside Eretz Yisrael" rather than "lay judges" is therefore exact and supports this entire explanation. Rambam also speaks in that formulation rather than simply "nowadays" because he maintains that even in later generations semikhah could theoretically be renewed if all the sages of Eretz Yisrael agreed to ordain one qualified sage, who could then ordain others. Rashba objected that if this were so, the statement praising those who did not abolish the laws of fines would lose its force, since semikhah could be restored by agreement. Rambam himself anticipated the difficulty. He writes that Israel is dispersed and therefore universal agreement is practically impossible. In his commentary to the Mishnah he says that before the coming of Mashiach, when Hashem turns the hearts of the people and their merits, repentance, and unity increase, such agreement may become possible. This language suggests that Rambam requires a true consensus, not merely ordinary majority rule. Otherwise why would such extraordinary spiritual unity be necessary simply to ordain one sage? History itself shows how difficult this is. When the sages of Tzfat later attempted to renew semikhah through R. Yaakov Beirav, R. Levi ibn Habib and his supporters strongly opposed the move. Rambam's broader view also fits his position that it is not certain Eliyahu must personally come before Mashiach. In Hilchot Melakhim he writes that the prophetic verse need not be understood as a guaranteed personal appearance of Eliyahu before Mashiach. This helps explain why Rambam needed another route by which an ordained supreme court could exist before the final redemption. If, however, one accepts the position of most authorities that Eliyahu will indeed come first, there is no proof for Rambam's renewal theory. Eliyahu himself received semikhah in the continuous chain through earlier prophets, and he could ordain others and restore the great beit din. Some argued that Eliyahu, now an angelic being, cannot function within human semikhah. I do not understand that claim. If so, why would questions of Shabbat boundaries apply to Eliyahu's arrival, as the Gemara discusses? If he is entirely outside human mitzvah categories, there should be no issue of techum at all. Yet the Gemara treats his arrival on Shabbat as halachically meaningful. Thus he remains relevant to the Torah's legal framework, and all the more so to semikhah. R. Yaakov Beirav's attempt to revive semikhah by collective agreement therefore does not have a decisive proof once one adopts the accepted expectation of Eliyahu's coming.
