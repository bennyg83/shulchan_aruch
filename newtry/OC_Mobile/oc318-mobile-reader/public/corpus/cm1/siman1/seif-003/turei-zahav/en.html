Whoever judges a true judgment with complete truth becomes a partner with the Holy One, blessed is He, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that the word “with complete truth” warns the judge not to combine his love for the party he finds innocent, or his hatred for the party he finds liable, with the judgment. That is, the judge should derive no inner pleasure from vindicating one litigant or from ruling against the other. This appears forbidden because, once his heart inclines in that direction, there is reason to fear that on another occasion he may distort the judgment because of it, for he is then like one who has a personal interest. This resolves the difficulty from the statement that concerning any judge who does not judge a true judgment with complete truth, the verse says: “Because of the oppression of the poor, because of the groaning of the needy, now I will arise, says Hashem.” According to the Beit Yosef, why should a judge's affection for the successful litigant produce oppression of the poor? According to our explanation, it is clear. The verse carefully says “now I will arise.” Even though no oppression of the poor has yet occurred, Hashem arises now because He sees that this inclination will ultimately lead to oppression. I heard this alluded to in the verse, “These too are for the wise: showing partiality in judgment is not good.” The expression “is not good” is difficult. According to this explanation, however, the verse means that one who shows favor to the successful litigant and rejoices in his vindication, although the ruling itself is just and he has committed no present injustice, nevertheless acts in a manner that “is not good,” because of what it will ultimately cause. All this follows the approach of the Beit Yosef. Tosafot, however, in the first chapter of Bava Batra 8b, explain that “with complete truth” excludes a fraudulent case. Even if witnesses testify, the judge does not impose liability, as is derived in Sanhedrin from “with complete truth,” so that he should not distort the judgment. Thus Tosafot explain the phrase differently from the Beit Yosef. Their words “so that he should not distort the judgment” require explanation, since the Torah already states, “You shall not distort judgment.” It appears that they refer to what the Tur writes in siman 15 concerning a fraudulent case. An ordinary person may not say, “My heart believes that the matter is false,” and the Tur concludes that the judge should withdraw and not adjudicate it. Tosafot therefore first state that a fraudulent case is excluded, so that the judge may not rule on the basis of those witnesses. Lest he then say, “In such a case I shall rule the opposite way, extract money, or take some other action contrary to the testimony,” Tosafot add that he may not do so, because he might thereby distort the true judgment, since he may be mistaken in thinking the case fraudulent. Rather, he must remain passive and take no action. Tosafot's words are therefore exact: he must not distort the judgment, meaning the very case already described as appearing fraudulent to him. This is a correct explanation.
