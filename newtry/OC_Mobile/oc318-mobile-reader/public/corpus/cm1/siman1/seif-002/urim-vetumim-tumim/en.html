But loss of work and medical expenses, etc. This is Rambam's view, and the commentators have already questioned it from the Gemara, which states explicitly that in cases of personal injury, even though there is monetary loss, we do not act as agents of the ordained judges. Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh write that Rambam inferred his distinction from the Gemara's language, "anything assessed as a slave," meaning that specifically damages requiring appraisal as though the injured person were a slave are outside our jurisdiction, whereas medical expenses and loss of work do not require such an appraisal. Even so, the Gemara's explicit statement that in personal injury we do not exercise agency, despite monetary loss, still appears difficult. It is possible to explain, along the lines of Ir Shushan, that although assaults themselves are common, it is uncommon that a person is struck so severely that his market value would actually be reduced were he sold. By contrast, it is common for a person to be beaten badly enough to require bed rest, medical treatment, and loss of work. Yet this explanation is strained, and it still does not resolve the ruling that damages for impairment are not adjudicated nowadays. The Gemara itself asks from humiliation and impairment, since they are common, and answers only regarding humiliation that it contains no direct monetary loss. It gives no such answer regarding impairment. Taz therefore wished to delete the word "impairment" from the Gemara, but Heaven forbid that we amend the text of the Talmud in this manner. Sema's distinction between a direct act and an appraisal likewise does not adequately resolve the Gemara. It therefore appears that Rambam understood the Gemara's phrase "uncommon" in a different sense. The act itself need not be uncommon. Assaults and quarrels are certainly frequent. Indeed, the sages of Bavel were described as contentious, and Scripture says, "Hashem will give you a trembling heart." Rather, the monetary deficiency relevant to the legal payment must itself be a type that is common and readily recognizable. If the loss is not ordinarily apparent, or people are not generally competent to identify and quantify it, Chazal did not appoint later courts as agents of the ordained judges for that category. This is especially true of bodily damages assessed by asking what the person would be worth if sold as an eved Ivri. There is hardly anything more uncommon than such a valuation, particularly when the laws of an eved Ivri are not in practical operation. What ordinary, direct monetary loss can one point to apart from this hypothetical sale value? Thus when Rashi explains that bodily damages are appraised according to what the injured person would have been worth had he needed to sell himself as an eved Ivri, that very form of valuation is what the Gemara calls uncommon. The same is true of impairment. Its entire monetary measure is the difference between what one would pay for a virgin maidservant and for one who had relations, for purposes of marrying her to one's servant. This too is an uncommon form of valuation and an unusual kind of monetary deficiency. This is the intent of the Gemara's expression "anything assessed as a slave." The point is not merely that an appraisal is required, but that the payment depends on an unusual hypothetical slave-market appraisal. In the initial stage of the sugya, before the Gemara introduced the requirement of monetary loss, the distinction of common and uncommon had to refer to the occurrence itself, and in that sense impairment can be called common. In the conclusion, however, once the Gemara makes direct monetary loss part of the test, impairment is considered uncommon because its monetary deficiency exists only through this unusual appraisal. Medical expenses and loss of work are different. The underlying injuries are common and the monetary loss is also common and readily measurable without valuing the victim as a slave. These therefore can be adjudicated nowadays. This reconciles Rambam's position. Know also that Bach cites Tosafot as holding that ordained judges are required only in order to compel, whereas for ordinary adjudication without coercion semikhah is unnecessary in other civil matters. This is not correct. Bach was misled by Tosafot's abbreviated wording at the end of Gittin. Tosafot at the beginning of Sanhedrin, s.v. "let us require," imply the opposite. They ask why Scripture needs the word "elohim" when the requirement could be derived from "before them," and answer that "before them" is needed for coercion. This refers to a case in which the substantive law is already established by ordained judges and all that remains is to compel compliance, such as compelling a recalcitrant husband to give a get where the law obligates him. The word "elohim" teaches that actual adjudication itself also requires qualified judges. Thus, d'oraisa, both adjudication and coercion require ordained judges. Mordechai at the end of HaManiach requires study. From his discussion of R. Yehudah's ruling against a man who struck another, he seems to suggest that because the payment there was fixed and known, namely that one who strikes another pays a sela, it can be collected in Bavel. Yet the Gemara explicitly says that where there is no direct monetary loss, we do not act as agents. Similarly, the Gemara's case of one who kicks another and the response, "are you collecting fines in Bavel?", cannot be resolved merely by saying the amount is fixed. The matter requires further analysis.
