Likewise, one who stole or robbed. See Sema and Shach at length. The principal explanation is that of Ran and Ramban in their discussions of the opening Mishnah of Sanhedrin: the requirement of ordained experts in robbery applies where such experts are available. Where no experts are available, and all the more in our time when there are none, ordinary judges may adjudicate. Urim VeTumim reached this by reasoning, but these are already the words of Ran and Ramban. The reason is as Tosafot explain in Sanhedrin: for robbery we were not concerned that requiring experts would close the door before those who suffer wrongdoing, because even if experts are required a robber will still rob, assuming the victim will trouble himself to seek experts. That logic applies only where experts exist. For admissions and loans, however, the enactment preventing the doors from being locked before borrowers applies even where experts are available, because a lender will refuse to lend if he expects to need experts in order to collect. When Shach says that the penalty component of robbery, such as double or fourfold and fivefold payments, is uncommon, he means that the circumstances creating those penalties are uncommon. The principal itself is collected because the underlying theft is common, as Sema explains.
