Likewise, if he seized property. Darkei Moshe cites Mordechai that Riva ruled like Rabbeinu Tam regarding principal damages, but regarding other fines he followed the dissenting authorities and allowed seizure of any property. Sema, seif katan 18, cites this as well. I am surprised by this presentation. The entire dispute concerns principal damages. Otherwise, where would one find a fine connected to "the damaging object" itself? Tooth and foot are not fines and can be collected in Bavel. A mu'ad ox cannot practically be established nowadays when there are no ordained judges in Eretz Yisrael and therefore no valid warning process there. Thus the only relevant case in which one could require seizure specifically of the damaging object is keren. That is precisely where Rabbeinu Tam says one must seize the damaging object itself, while the other authorities disagree. How, then, can Riva's ruling be described as a compromise between two views when the dispute itself exists only in the very case called principal? Anyone who examines Mordechai will see that he is not discussing this issue at all. He writes that "in other matters" the rule follows Rif. But where do we find that Rif explicitly disputes Rabbeinu Tam regarding the form of seizure for uncollectible fines? Rif merely quotes the Gemara's statement "if he seized," without explaining how the seizure must be made. It is strained to say Mordechai inferred from Rif's position in the case "he claimed wheat and admitted barley" that seizure can help even when the seized thing is not the damaging object. If that were Mordechai's intent, why cite Rif specifically rather than simply the authorities who openly disagree with Rabbeinu Tam? It is therefore clear that Mordechai refers to a different dispute. Rabbeinu Tam holds that where one claimed wheat and the defendant admitted barley, seizure does not help unless there is a claim that extends over two possible liable objects, such as two animals pursuing two others, as Rosh explains. Rif, by contrast, holds that even where the plaintiff claimed wheat and the defendant admitted barley, seizure can help. Riva compromised between them in the case of two tam oxen that caused damage, where the plaintiff says the larger one damaged and the defendant says the smaller one did. That is a classic case of "claimed wheat and admitted barley." If the plaintiff seizes one ox, he cannot say: either this is the ox I claim caused the damage and therefore it is mine, or, if the other ox caused the damage, I hold this one as security for the other. That does not work, because a tam ox pays only from its own body. One tam ox cannot be held as security for the liability of a different tam ox. This form of seizure, which would operate only because of a possible waiver or indirect security interest, is not comparable to seizure that is permitted because no ordained beit din is available to collect a fine. The two mechanisms are entirely different. But in other cases of "claimed wheat and admitted barley," where payment is not restricted to the body of a particular damaging animal, seizure can help according to Rif through the plaintiff's either-way argument. This is the plain and correct reading of Mordechai. Rosh also implies that even where a person seized the actual damaging animal for keren, the seizure can fail if the defendant simultaneously holds other property of the plaintiff equal to the amount at issue. Rosh writes that Rif omitted the Gemara's unresolved question whether there is a special change-rule for pebbles, because in any event such payment cannot be collected nowadays without seizure, and Rif holds that once seized we do not remove it. But one could ask: the question would still matter where the plaintiff seized the damaging animal while the defendant held other property of the plaintiff. For purposes of collecting a fine, seizure of the damaging animal might help, whereas for an ordinary unresolved monetary doubt seizure should not help when the opposing party is itself holding offsetting property. Rosh's omission therefore proves that even for collection of a fine, such a seizure does not necessarily help when the opposing party holds a counter-possession. The issue cannot be reduced simply to "the plaintiff seized the damaging object."
You shall return it. Mordechai at the end of HaManiach records an incident that came before Riva. One man injured another, and the injured party seized a silver cup belonging to the assailant. The rabbi was uncertain how to rule. R. Shemaryah was sitting before him and said, "Would you like me to exempt him?" The rabbi answered yes. R. Shemaryah replied: say that the injury must be appraised. We say in HaChovel that we are not competent in such appraisals, and every matter requiring this kind of appraisal is not adjudicated in Bavel. Mahari Weil, responsum 28, inferred from this that since we are not competent to appraise the injury, the injured party may retain everything he seized. Darkei Moshe did not reject that inference in principle, but only wrote that we are in fact competent to make such appraisals, as Rosh states. It would therefore seem that where the appraisal truly cannot be established, Darkei Moshe would agree with Mahari Weil that the seizing party may retain what he took, as I discussed in Urim. All of these authorities appear to have understood R. Shemaryah's phrase "Would you like me to exempt him?" as meaning to exempt the person holding the silver cup from returning the excess beyond the actual injury value. According to that reading, because the amount cannot be appraised, he keeps what he seized. But this is astonishing. There is no more obvious example of seizure based on doubt. Even according to Rif, who allows seizure in certain cases of unresolved law, that is where the legal doubt has arguments on both sides. Here the uncertainty is merely factual valuation. How can one seize another's property simply because one does not know whether the other person owes that amount? All the more so according to Rosh, who holds that seizure does not help in an unresolved legal doubt, it certainly should not help in this valuation doubt. The correct explanation is therefore the opposite. "Would you like me to exempt him?" means: would you like me to exempt the assailant from further payment, so that the injured party must return the silver cup without retaining it as compensation. R. Shemaryah exempted the assailant for the stated reason: the injury requires a form of appraisal in which we are not competent, and courts in Bavel do not adjudicate such appraisal. Since the amount is unknown, the injured party's possession of the cup is mere seizure in doubt and does not help. He must return it. Maharshal in HaChovel, ruling 2, explains Mordechai this way, and Maharam Tiktin writes similarly. One should see there as well how they answer the apparent difficulty from the Gemara's statement to the injured person, "Go, let him be assessed like a slave," followed by the qualification that the case involved seizure. That passage does not support Mahari Weil's conclusion. Accordingly, the rulings of Mahari Weil and Darkei Moshe, insofar as they allow a person to retain property merely because the amount of injury cannot be appraised, cannot be sustained. One may not retain another's property merely on account of uncertainty in valuation.
We collect everywhere. This is the view of Mordechai and Rosh. They discuss Rif's ruling concerning one who sells his slave to non Jews, where Chazal imposed a fine upon the seller. Rif cites a Gaon who says that only ordained judges may impose that fine. Mordechai and Rosh disagree. They hold that when Chazal themselves enacted a monetary penalty as a protective fence for one of their institutions, that penalty may be collected everywhere. This creates a difficulty in Rama. Here Rama rules without qualification like Rosh and Mordechai. Yet in Yoreh Deah, siman 267, Mechaber rules like Rif and Rambam that the fine for selling a slave to non Jews is collected only by ordained judges, and Rama adds no gloss there. It appears that Rama distinguishes between two categories. When the matter itself is common, but there is no direct monetary loss and therefore ordinary courts would not normally exercise the agency of ordained judges, the reason for non-collection is simply that the usual social need for agency is absent. In such a case, if Chazal themselves created a penalty as a fence and protection for one of their enactments, that very protective purpose becomes the social necessity. The penalty can therefore be collected everywhere, because enforcing the words and enactments of Chazal is itself a central public need. But where the reason ordinary courts do not adjudicate is that the underlying event is uncommon, Chazal never delegated that category to unordained courts in the first place. Even if a rabbinic enactment later imposes a fine, when the case itself remains unusual it was not entrusted to unordained judges. Chazal did not see fit to create standing judicial authority for rare events. This distinction explains Rambam. In Hilchot Shabbat, chapter 20, Rambam rules that one who sells a large domesticated animal to a non Jew can be fined up to ten times its value. He does not there distinguish between ordained and unordained judges. But in Hilchot Avadim, chapter 8, concerning one who sells a slave to non Jews, he says that only expert ordained judges have authority to impose the fine. If the rule were simply that every rabbinic fine requires ordained judges, Rambam should have stated that restriction first in Hilchot Shabbat as well. The distinction must be as explained. Transactions involving large animals are common, while sales of Jewish slaves to non Jews are uncommon. Gittin explicitly indicates that the slave case is unusual. Thus in a common rabbinic penalty intended to preserve a rabbinic safeguard, later courts may act everywhere. In the uncommon slave case, even a rabbinic fine remains outside the delegated jurisdiction of unordained judges. Hagahot on Rif cites Riaz and brings the Gaon's ruling that the slave fine is not collected outside Eretz Yisrael. It adds that in Kuntres HaRa'ayah he distinguished this case from other rabbinic fines that are collected outside Eretz Yisrael. This supports exactly the distinction stated here. That, in my view, is Rama's position. His ruling here concerns ordinary rabbinic enactments and penalties that arise in common situations. In the uncommon slave case he agrees with Rif, Rambam, and Mechaber that only ordained judges may impose the fine.
