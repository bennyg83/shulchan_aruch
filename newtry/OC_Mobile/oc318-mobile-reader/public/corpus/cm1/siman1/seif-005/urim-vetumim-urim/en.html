And once he gives, etc. He need not pay the exact full amount of the injury. Rather, he pays according to beit din's judgment, an amount reasonably close to the damage. This is why Rambam's wording, and the corrected wording of the Shulchan Aruch according to Sema, does not make release from the ban depend upon whether the injured party personally accepts the payment. If strict Torah law required the full amount, his willingness to be appeased would not control it. The Tur and Rosh indicate that Rav Sherira Gaon's rule is only a Geonic enactment and not the law of the Gemara itself. Therefore, if the tortfeasor dies, his son should not be placed under a ban. The enactment was made to restrain violent offenders and prevent damage in the world. It is enough to penalize the wrongdoer himself; the son who did nothing should not be added to the enactment.
And likewise if he seized, etc. He may seize even an object other than the thing that caused the injury. However, with a tam ox, whose liability is paid from the body of the animal itself, Mordechai cites Ri Baal HaTosafos as inclined to Rabbeinu Tam's view that only the damaging animal itself is subject to seizure. See Tumim. Seizure can also secure double payment and fourfold or fivefold payment. If he did not seize, however, the offender is placed under a ban. Once he pays an amount close to the principal loss, the ban is lifted. Sema, against Ir Shushan. If the offender admitted liability, seizure is not automatically defeated merely because the admission was not before an ordained beit din. Sema and Shach write that nowadays every admission is effectively outside beit din because we lack ordained judges. But in Yoreh Deah, siman 267, Shach himself challenges this and agrees with Beit Yosef that an admission defeats collection of a fine only where witnesses later arrive. If no witnesses later arrive, one of two things is true: either the admission has no judicial force and the claimant has no basis for seizure, or it is a valid admission and then he has admitted a fine. Nimukei Yosef in Perek Merubah says this explicitly, and this is the correct view. Shach cites Ramban in Milchamot that if a depositary falsely claims theft and is himself the thief, thereby becoming liable for double payment, seizure by the owner does not help, because liability for the double payment arises only after an oath before beit din. In this respect we have no competent beit din nowadays. Maharshal writes that one should not seize through a non Jewish authority. He does not clarify whether, after the fact, such seizure is effective. I will discuss that elsewhere.
We do not listen to him. Maharshal distinguishes between a true fine, which we do not adjudicate, and an ordinary monetary claim that we cannot adjudicate merely because it is uncommon. For a fine, we do not make an appraisal before seizure, following the Rosh. For an ordinary monetary claim that is simply outside our current jurisdiction because it is uncommon, we appraise immediately so the claimant knows how much he may seize. Shach.
Return such and such. Darchei Moshe and Sema reject Mahari Weil's ruling that because we are not expert in valuation, the claimant may retain everything seized. The Rosh's procedure is to appraise and require return of the excess. Mahari Weil's language would imply that when beit din cannot make a reliable valuation, the claimant may retain property because of the doubt. In my opinion, anything beyond the amount of damage clearly established cannot be retained. See Tumim. Sema writes that if the seizure occurred before witnesses, the claimant must prove the amount of damage. If the seizure occurred without witnesses, so that he has a migo of complete denial or return, he is believed regarding the amount owed him because of the damage, whether principal or fine, through that migo. Even concerning a fine, a valid migo can establish his claim, as explained below in siman 359.
With fines written in the Torah, etc. Although Torah fines are not collected nowadays, local communal authorities may adjudicate them through the seven good men of the city. Sema in the name of Mordechai. It appears that this requires the agreement of the communal Torah scholar as well.
But fines instituted by the Sages. See Tumim, where I distinguished between common and uncommon matters. An uncommon matter is not collected outside Eretz Yisrael even in such circumstances. A judge may, however, impose a penalty payable to the government upon one who refuses to obey Torah law. Sema in the name of Terumat HaDeshen. This point requires careful qualification.
