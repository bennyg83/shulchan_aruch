Yam Shel Shlomo writes that the tortfeasor cannot retake it, because once it entered the victim's hands the victim acquired it by law, unlike ordinary seizure in a case of unresolved law. Yet Rosh in Bava Kamma implies that the two are equivalent. He explains Rif's omission of the unresolved question whether pebbles damage is reduced to one quarter on the ground that in Babylonia, if the victim does not seize we do not collect, while if he does seize he receives the full amount because of legal doubt. This shows that the seizure is treated like ordinary seizure in a doubtful case. Still, there is one respect in which this seizure may be weaker: seizure from orphans does not help here, because by Talmudic law the movables of orphans are not encumbered even for damages, and the Geonic enactment collecting from heirs' movables was not made for these penalty-like cases. Ordinary seizure in an unresolved monetary issue can, however, be effective even against heirs. The Shach writes that seizure can help even where the defendant admitted before the seizure and even before witnesses were actually produced. Yet in Yoreh Deah he himself proves that seizure is ineffective where there are no witnesses at all. His meaning here must be that the witnesses have not yet been brought before beit din, not that none exist. Since in our time we are not an ordained beit din for penalties, some authorities hold that once the injured party seized he need not formally bring the witnesses before us; his own knowledge of their testimony suffices for self-help. But where no witnesses exist at all, seizure certainly does not help. Likewise, where experts exist, he must bring the witnesses before them. Ketzot HaChoshen's attempt to explain the case of Rabban Gamliel and the slave by saying the master's admission prejudiced others because it would forbid the slave to a maidservant is incorrect. The emancipation is a benefit to the slave, and in any event the master's admission is effective regarding the monetary ownership even if it is not effective for matters of personal status. As to Sema's distinction between horn damage and other penalties, there are other examples of seizing the damaging object itself, such as certain derivatives of pit damage. Finally, Sema is correct that a migo can help after seizure. Once the court is empowered to adjudicate because of the seizure, the resulting oath is no different from any other oath ruled by beit din.
