The Darkei Moshe cites the Mordechai that Riva ruled regarding damage by the horn in accordance with Rabbeinu Tam, while other penalties remained according to those who disagree, and the injured party may seize with respect to all such matters. See the Sema. By 'other penalties' this includes an animal that injured a person, and even unusual cases of tooth and foot, as explained in seif 3. Tumim expressed astonishment at this and argued that one finds no category of damages that is a penalty except horn. This is no difficulty, as we have written.
We do not remove it from his possession. The Shach writes that even if the defendant admitted before the seizure, the seizure is effective, because nowadays his admission does not exempt him, and this applies even if witnesses never came. So write the Tur and Ramban, and so too the Sema. See Darkei Moshe, who writes in the name of Binyamin Ze'ev, siman 132, that although an admission today is not considered an admission before beit din so as to exempt him if witnesses later come, nevertheless without witnesses he is not liable for a penalty through his own admission. The language of Raavad and Ramban also somewhat implies that they disagree only where witnesses subsequently came, but without witnesses all agree that he is exempt. Nevertheless, Darkei Moshe concludes that perhaps no distinction should be made and Ramban disagrees in every case, so that if the claimant seized we do not take it from him. Thus Darkei Moshe did not regard liability without witnesses as obvious, but stated it only as a possibility, unlike the Sema and Shach, who regard it as certain that even if no witnesses came he is liable. Their position is very difficult from the Gemara in chapter Merubah. According to the view that one who admits a penalty and afterward witnesses come is exempt, the Gemara challenges from the incident of Rabban Gamliel, who was told that he no longer had witnesses, implying that had witnesses later come he would have been liable. The Gemara answers that Rabban Gamliel's admission was outside beit din. If an admission that does not exempt him would itself obligate him even without witnesses, because the admission of a litigant is like one hundred witnesses, why was he told that he no longer had witnesses? Rabban Gamliel's own admission should stand in place of witnesses, and it was not an admission that exempts from a penalty because it was outside beit din. I later found that the Shach in Yoreh Deah, Hilchot Avadim, brings this proof to reject the Levush and She'erit Yosef, who hold that nowadays one becomes liable for a penalty through his own admission without witnesses. It is astonishing that he did not note that this is also the view of the Sema and, as he understood it here, his own position. Perhaps Rabban Gamliel was not believed through his own admission because his admission harmed another, since it prohibited the slave to a maidservant, similar to the law of a divorced woman, where the husband is not believed through the principle that a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses because he thereby prohibits her to a Kohen, as Tosafot write in the first chapter of Gittin. See also the first chapter of Bava Metzia, where liability to an oath through witnesses is derived a fortiori from one's own admission: if his own mouth, which does not obligate him in a penalty, obligates him in an oath, then witnesses, who obligate him in a penalty, certainly obligate him in an oath. If one's own admission also obligated him in a penalty whenever the rule of admission in a penalty did not exempt him, what superiority would witnesses have? His own admission would also obligate a penalty in such a case, while where the admission exempts him from the penalty, witnesses who come later likewise do not help because he has already been exempted. Thus the statement that one's mouth does not obligate a penalty cannot mean merely that his admission is too weak to be relied upon for a penalty, for according to that view it does obligate wherever it does not exempt. Rather, his own admission has a separate quality of exempting him from a penalty when made before beit din, even if witnesses later come. Witnesses are therefore not stronger than his admission in the relevant respect. It consequently appears that one's own admission does not obligate a penalty at all, even where he would not become exempt through the rule of admission in a penalty.
And if the injured party says. Know that Shulchan Aruch here does not distinguish between actual penal liabilities and other cases of theft, personal injury, and unusual monetary claims lacking ordinary out-of-pocket loss. But there is a clear distinction. With true monetary liability, although we do not adjudicate it nowadays when it is unusual and lacks ordinary monetary loss, the defendant himself must nevertheless pay in order to fulfill his obligation toward Heaven, and if he does not pay he is a robber. Actual penalties, however, do not carry such a heavenly obligation, because all penal law depends upon 'whom the judges condemn.' If beit din does not adjudicate it, there is no separate heavenly monetary liability. So too in the Yerushalmi at the end of the third chapter of Ketubot: may we tell him, 'Fulfill your obligation toward Heaven'? It is learned from the incident in which Rabban Gamliel knocked out the tooth of his slave Tavi and came before Rabbi Yehoshua, saying, 'Tavi my slave, I have found a basis to free him.' Rabbi Yehoshua said to him, 'You have nothing in your hand, for penalties apply only before beit din and with witnesses.' The Yerushalmi asks whether he should nevertheless be told to fulfill his obligation toward Heaven. It is clear from there that even one who admitted a penalty and afterward witnesses came is exempt, even from a heavenly obligation, and even if the claimant seized, for there the slave is himself in possession of his freedom. Tosafot likewise write in Ketubot 33b that in that case he is not liable even to satisfy Heaven. Only regarding monetary liability did Rava say in chapter HaSocher et HaPoalim that the Torah prohibited the hire of a prostitute even where he had relations with his mother, because he remains obligated to pay in order to satisfy Heaven. A penalty, however, is imposed only through beit din, as it says, 'whom the judges condemn,' excluding one who condemns himself. Monetary liability is different: although in an unusual case without ordinary monetary loss beit din does not adjudicate it nowadays, the wrongdoer remains a robber if he does not pay. Witnesses were created only for liars, and beit din is needed only to rescue the oppressed from his oppressor.

The Sema writes that a migo is effective even regarding a claim to a penalty, as the Mordechai writes at the end of chapter HaManiach and the Tur at the end of siman 399. The Shach likewise rules at the end of siman 399 that where the claimant seized, a migo is effective with respect to a penalty. I found, however, a dispute among the great authorities on this point in Sefer HaZechut of Ramban, siman 26. Raavad writes that where the claimant seized before the defendant's admission, there were no witnesses to the seizure, and he could have denied ever seizing it, but when they came to beit din the defendant denied the penalty and afterward admitted it, Raavad was uncertain whether the claimant's migo is equivalent to witnesses, as we ordinarily say that the argument 'why would he lie' is like witnesses. If so, since this seizure carries a migo it should be like witnesses, and had the defendant denied and witnesses then come he would have been liable, so the property was properly seized before the denial and admission. Or perhaps, since had the defendant admitted he would have been exempt, the claimant's migo cannot be stronger than the defendant's own admission, and a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. Wherever an admission is ineffective to create liability, perhaps a migo is likewise ineffective. Raavad concludes that this question in penal law was not properly clarified to him. Ramban responds that this uncertainty has already been resolved by his position that nowadays one can become liable through seizure because the defendant's admission has no effect. Even regarding an admission before expert judges, Ramban says the Torah did not exempt one who had already become liable; rather, it provided that he is not initially obligated through his own mouth, as it says, 'whom the judges condemn,' excluding one who condemns himself. Here the defendant does not become liable through his own words. We believe the claimant who says, 'He became liable to me for a penalty and I seized from his property as payment.' Thus this is still a case of condemnation by judges, not self-condemnation. The defendant's later admission neither adds nor subtracts. All the more so where the claimant seized movable property and the defendant sues him to return it while the claimant seeks payment of the penalty in money: this is not the defendant confessing to a penalty but rather a claimant who acted promptly and gained. In chapter Shor Shenagach Arba'ah VaChamishah the Gemara says that the borrower could claim, 'Had it been an innocuous ox I would have driven it into the marsh,' and answers, according to Rabbeinu Chananel's version, that the injured party had already seized it. Some versions read that beit din had already seized it. In any event, Ramban regards the law as clear. His citation of Rabbeinu Chananel's version supports his view that a migo is effective for a penalty. The Gemara there discusses an animal borrowed as an innocuous ox that was found to have been forewarned, where the owner pays half the damages and the borrower the other half. The Gemara asks that the borrower should say, 'Had it remained innocuous I would have admitted and become exempt,' and even according to the view that half-damages are monetary liability, he should say, 'Had it remained innocuous I would have driven it into the marsh.' It answers that the injured party had already seized it. Ramban understood the case as seizure without witnesses, giving the injured party a migo, so that the claim 'I would have admitted and become exempt' no longer helps, because the other party has a migo. This demonstrates that a migo is effective in penal law. My learned brother, Rabbi Yehudah HaKohen, said that Raavad follows his own view that the half-damages of an innocuous ox differ from other penalties. Even if the defendant admitted on his own, beit din tells him to pay because the principal damage is a debt against him, although beit din does not compel payment. If the injured party seized, we do not remove it even if he seized before witnesses, unlike other penalties. Rashba cites this position of Raavad in his novellae to the third chapter of Bava Kamma, and the Maggid Mishneh cites it in the name of Raavad in chapter 9 of Hilchot Nizkei Mamon and indicates that Rambam agrees. According to this, Rabbeinu Chananel's version is more easily explained according to Raavad: even if the injured party seized before witnesses, the defendant cannot say, 'I would have admitted and become exempt,' because with the half-damages of horn, once the injured party has seized, the defendant's admission does not remove the property from him. According to Ramban, by contrast, one must establish the Gemara as seizure without witnesses, a detail not stated there. Rashba writes that he did not understand Raavad's reasoning, but in light of this there is support for it from Rabbeinu Chananel's version. As a matter of halacha, Raavad's uncertainty cannot overcome Ramban's certainty that a migo is effective regarding a penalty. Nevertheless, it appears that even according to Ramban this applies only before expert judges. Nowadays, when there are no expert judges, the claimant who comes before beit din holding an object and says that the defendant owes him a penalty is like one who comes before witnesses with a pledge and says that the owner owes him money. Even in ordinary monetary law a migo does not help before witnesses in the same manner that it helps before beit din, as the Rosh writes concerning a pledge. See Shulchan Aruch siman 72, seif 18, and siman 133. Since nowadays there are no expert judges and beit din does not issue a penal judgment, except that where the claimant has witnesses we do not remove what he has seized and he may act for himself, a claimant without proof cannot rely on seizure. When he appears with the object before today's beit din, they function for this purpose only as witnesses who have now seen the object. His migo is thereby lost, and he must return it. Ramban's proof from Rabbeinu Chananel's version concerns expert beit din, where the migo operates. See Rama in siman 399, where we discuss this further. There is also a difficulty from the incident of Rabban Gamliel and Tavi. Rabbi Yehoshua told him, 'There is nothing in your words, for you no longer have witnesses.' According to Ramban, who says a claimant to a penalty is believed through migo, Rabban Gamliel seemingly had a migo, since if he wished he could have freed Tavi directly. We find likewise that a husband who says, 'I divorced my wife,' is believed through a migo that he could divorce her now, and one is believed regarding a slave for the same reason when he says, 'I made so-and-so my slave a free man.' The fact that one who frees a slave violates a positive commandment should not destroy the migo. It appears, however, that because this migo comes through Rabban Gamliel himself, who is the party becoming obligated, it remains self-condemnation. Ramban spoke only where the migo belongs to the claimant, so the liability comes through the claimant's assertion and is not self-condemnation. Here, by contrast, the migo itself comes through Rabban Gamliel, the person who would become obligated. However, according to what I wrote above in seif katan 5, that an admission outside beit din does not exempt as self-condemnation, this remains difficult. Examine the matter carefully.
