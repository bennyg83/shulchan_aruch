Likewise, if. This is stated in the Gemara there, in Bava Kamma 15b, and in Ketubot 48b.
And if. This follows the reading of the Rosh there, which reads 'if.'
(Anthology) And if, etc. The Rif does not write this, nor does Rashi there, s.v. de'i tafas.
But penalties, etc. This is stated there on 96b and by the Rif there.
(Anthology) All this, etc. The Rosh writes this in the chapter HaSholeach and disputes the Rif there. The Mordechai writes likewise. Their proof is from the rule that a beit din may impose corporal punishment and other sanctions beyond the letter of the law, from the case in the ninth chapter of Bava Kamma concerning a habitual robber, and from the statement in the third chapter of Sanhedrin, 'Darken his eyes.' This is the meaning of the reference to siman 2. The proof is not conclusive, however, because those cases concern sanctions that judges in each generation impose at their own discretion. Otherwise, the Rif's rulings would contradict one another. See Yoreh Deah siman 267, at the end, where the Shulchan Aruch rules without qualification like the Rif.
(Anthology) All this, etc. The words of the Rosh and Mordechai require study. How is this different from the case of the handle of a hoe at the beginning of HaManiach? The Taz raises the same question, as stated in seif 1 concerning all penalties.
(Anthology) All this applies specifically, etc. The Darkei Moshe writes this in the name of the Mordechai at the end of HaSholeach. The Mordechai states in siman 533 that one who sells his slave to gentiles is penalized. The Rif records a Gaon's view that this penalty is not collected outside Eretz Yisrael. Rabbeinu Baruch distinguishes between penalties written in the Torah, such as those for rape, seduction, defamation, the thirty shekalim for a slave, double payment, fourfold and fivefold payment, and half damages, which are not adjudicated in Babylonia, and penalties instituted independently by the Sages. He cites the habitual robber in HaGozel, the order to darken a litigant's eyes in Zeh Borer, and the rule that a beit din may punish beyond the letter of the law. The Rosh likewise expresses surprise, and the Tur cites him in Yoreh Deah siman 267. Yet the Shulchan Aruch there, at the end, and the Rambam there rule like the Rif. The objection of the Mordechai and Rosh may be answered by saying that each judge may impose a sanction according to the needs of his time and place, as stated in siman 2, but not a fixed penalty. This is unlike the Mordechai and Rama's understanding, for the Rif and Rambam agree with the discretionary authority described there. The Rambam and Shulchan Aruch here in seif 1 likewise write, 'and all penalties that the Sages imposed.' The Mordechai, however, holds that the payment there is not a penalty, but an assessment by the Sages of the value of the humiliation. The Rambam inferred from the statements at the beginning of HaManiach and HaChovel, 'Are you collecting a penalty in Babylonia?' that it is indeed a penalty. The Mordechai follows Tosafot, who explain that the term 'penalty' there refers to an uncommon liability with no direct monetary loss, unlike the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in seif 1. At the end of siman 382 the Shulchan Aruch writes, 'and at the present time,' following Tosafot in Bava Kamma. According to the Rambam's first view there, which leaves the amount to the judges' discretion, all authorities agree. According to the second view, which treats it as a fixed penalty, it must follow the Rif and Rambam. See the Rif there. In siman 363:3 the Shulchan Aruch writes, 'if he has become established,' which requires study, since according to his view this is a fixed penalty. The Rif's language can be understood to mean that in such a case each court imposes a sanction as it sees fit, as explained above. See there.
