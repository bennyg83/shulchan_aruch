Revealing a secret. Baer Heitev includes within this rule that a judge should not tell a litigant which judge ruled against him. Knesset HaGedolah writes that this applies when one tells the litigant on his own initiative. If the litigant asks, however, and the respondent sees that the litigant is correct and that the judges made a reversible error, such as an error in an explicit rule, there is no prohibition. Ketzot HaChoshen cites this. Shaar Mishpat objects from a responsum of the Rosh, cited by Tur 154:11, concerning judges who mistakenly required Shimon to move his building away from Reuven's window. Shimon had already moved it, yet the Rosh ruled that one is commanded to tell him he was not required to do so, because this is restoration of lost property and the judgment can be reversed. The Rosh's responsum implies that this obligation exists even if Shimon did not ask. Shaar Mishpat therefore limits the prohibition to an irreversible error, such as an error of judicial discretion, while where the decision can be reversed it is a mitzvah to inform the litigant. Urim VeTumim questions whether one may tell the litigant directly even in a reversible error. Perhaps one should first approach the judge and tell him he erred and should reverse his ruling, because telling the litigant directly may still border on harmful speech. If the judge stubbornly refuses to acknowledge the truth, one may then inform the litigant so he can have other sages review and overturn the mistaken judgment. In my view, this can also answer Shaar Mishpat's objection to Knesset HaGedolah: the Rosh's statement that one must tell Shimon may refer to a case where the judges were first approached and refused to correct themselves. If the matter can be fixed by speaking to the judges, why tell the litigant? The questioner was apparently asking because the judges would not concede, and the Rosh ruled that the error was one in an explicit rule and therefore Shimon must be informed if no other correction is possible. Knesset HaGedolah's ruling can thus also stand: if the litigant himself asks, one may answer immediately, though he wrote only that there is no prohibition, not that there is a mitzvah, because the matter might also be corrected by first speaking with the judge. The Rosh's responsum warrants further study.
