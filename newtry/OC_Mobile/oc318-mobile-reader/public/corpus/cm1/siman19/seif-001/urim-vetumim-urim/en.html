The greatest of the judges. The reason is so that the litigants will not discover which judge favored and which opposed them. Sema says that if a lesser judge announced the ruling, a losing party might suspect that he personally was the one who ruled against him. That explanation is incomplete. If court procedure always required the junior judge to announce the decision, no inference could be drawn. Rather, the senior judge naturally should announce the ruling because he is head of the panel. Yet one might think that if the two junior judges overruled the senior, the senior should not have to announce a decision contrary to his own opinion, and that one of the judges who actually favored the result should speak. Tur therefore explains that the senior always announces the decision without variation, so the litigants cannot infer whether he agreed or was outvoted. Bach's reading appears correct.
He is included in "one who goes about as a talebearer and reveals secrets." Likewise, after the litigants leave beit din, an outsider should not tell one party that the judge ruled incorrectly. Mahara Stein derives this from Bava Kamma 99b. If the mistake is one that would not alter the judgment even if identified, revealing it serves only as harmful speech and is certainly prohibited. If it is a reversible error in monetary law, however, there is room to ask whether disclosure is permitted in order to save the person's money. Perhaps the proper course is not to tell the litigant directly, but to approach the judge and explain the error so that the judge himself can correct the ruling. Telling the party first can still involve at least the dust of harmful speech. If the judge stubbornly refuses to acknowledge a clear error, then it is permitted to inform the affected party so that he can bring the matter before other Torah scholars and overturn the mistaken ruling.
