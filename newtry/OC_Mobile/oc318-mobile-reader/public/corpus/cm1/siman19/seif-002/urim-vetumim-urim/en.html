In the manner explained above in siman 14. There it was explained that if a party says, "I will go to the Great Beit Din," a written decision is supplied. Sema asks why that special case is needed; the Gemara's language "How do we write the decision?" appears to require careful formulation in every written ruling. One answer is that below, in simanim 39 and 79, we find that ordinarily one should not issue a written judgment at all because the defendant may later have paid. Rama therefore points specifically to the case in siman 14, where everyone agrees that a written record is needed for review by the Great Beit Din. More likely, Rama is teaching an additional novelty. The Shulchan Aruch says, "If he asked them to write for him." Why mention a request? Because even where the document is being written so that a higher court can examine whether the first judges erred, the written record still should not identify which judge favored which side. One might think that a dissenting judge could insist, "Write that I disagreed, so that if the higher court finds an error they will not think I was ignorant or perhaps personally liable under the laws of judicial error." Rama teaches that even then the court should not identify the individual positions, because doing so would violate the rule against revealing deliberative secrets. We are not concerned enough about a possible later finding of error to override that rule. Sema further says that once a case has already been decided by one beit din, another beit din may not reopen the same dispute merely because a litigant tries again, since one beit din does not ordinarily scrutinize another. Mabit, citing Radbaz, indicates that nowadays courts may scrutinize prior courts because of concern for judicial error, based also on Rabbeinu Yerucham and Rashba. Shach below siman 39 limits this: where there is some concrete indication that the first court erred, later review is appropriate. Absent such indication, even today one beit din does not routinely reexamine another. Rabbeinu Yerucham adds that if students believe their teacher erred, they should not act to overturn the ruling until they hear the teacher's explanation, for he may have a valid reason they have not considered. This is based on Bava Batra.
"From their words so and so prevailed." Sema says that if all judges agreed, the decision should not use the phrase "from their words," because that might falsely imply internal disagreement. I disagree. Mahari Weil, Maharsha, and Maadanei Yom Tov explain that one reason a three-judge court avoids the stigma of a presumptuous two-judge court is that any individual judge can say, "What could I do? My colleagues outvoted me." If the wording of the written decision disclosed whether there had been disagreement, this protection would disappear. Therefore the same neutral formula should be used in every ruling, whether unanimous or divided, so that the parties cannot infer the internal votes. Chavot Yair rules that if one judge refuses to sign because he believes the majority erred, he can nevertheless be compelled to sign. This dispute was already addressed by Mabit, Beit Yosef, and Radbaz. Mabit said he should not sign a ruling he believes mistaken. Beit Yosef required him to sign. Radbaz distinguished: if withholding the signature would not invalidate the practical result, he need not be forced to sign; if it would prevent implementation, he must sign. In my view Beit Yosef is correct. Otherwise the dissenting judge effectively reveals the court's internal deliberations. Mabit suggested that court scribes could sign instead, stating simply that "from the court's words so and so prevailed." That is not ordinary practice. The Jerusalem Talmud says that if three judged and one died, the remaining two sign and write, "Although we signed as two, we judged as three." This clearly contemplates judges, not scribes, signing the judgment. Even more explicitly, the Jerusalem Talmud in Sanhedrin says that once the matter is concluded, Rabbi Yochanan requires the dissenting judge to write "exempt" with the majority. We rule like Rabbi Yochanan against Reish Lakish. Beit Yosef's ruling is therefore correct.
