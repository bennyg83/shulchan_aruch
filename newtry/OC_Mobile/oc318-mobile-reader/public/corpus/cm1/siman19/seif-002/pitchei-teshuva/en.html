That they write the judgment for him. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 89, records a question about a written judgment obligating a defendant to take either a hesset oath or a biblical oath. Should the document state that if he does not swear he must pay a specified amount? There is reason to do so, since the oath may cover numerous details, and if the judgment is vague and the defendant later refuses to swear, the parties will have to return to beit din to determine the amount, causing disrespect and suspicion that the judges seek additional fees. Yet there are difficulties. Courts customarily fix a date for the oath, so writing that failure to swear by that date automatically creates monetary liability seems strange. Moreover, with a hesset oath the defendant may reverse the oath onto the plaintiff. Asking the defendant in advance whether he intends to swear or reverse it would improperly suggest a strategy to a litigant. In my view, if the defendant simply refuses to swear and does not invoke reversal, the plaintiff is not told to swear; the defendant must pay. All the more so, the judgment should not mention reversal unless the defendant raises it, especially since even after reversing the oath he can retract. If reversal were written, one might also need to address whether the plaintiff seeks only acceptance of a ban, and so forth. The responsum records no answer from the queried gaon, and in the addenda Chavot Yair suggests perhaps the matter was so obvious that no answer was needed: the court may set a time for the oath but write only generally, 'If he does not swear, he will be liable,' without tying payment to the stated date. The possibility of reversal is no objection because the phrase means only while the oath rests on him, and he may reverse it if he wishes. This discussion plainly includes a hesset oath, since only hesset is reversible. I find this difficult, because siman 87, seif 9, rules that if a person refuses a hesset oath, beit din does not descend to his property but instead places him under ban. If the judgment simply says that failure to swear means he must pay, it sounds like an ordinary monetary judgment and could produce an incorrect result. Amudei Or, siman 108, likewise writes that he does not state in a hesset judgment, 'If he does not swear he shall pay,' because refusal does not authorize collection from his assets; where governmental permission exists, the remedy is ban. Writing 'he shall pay' would imply seizure of assets, and he therefore rejects Chavot Yair's view.
