Seif 1. Note: I was asked about the current commercial custom whereby a formal handshake transaction known as a handslag, together with a deposit, effects a complete acquisition. Even apart from witnesses to the handshake, a customary merchant's memorandum certainly has the force of situmta and, together with the deposit, effects acquisition. Although movables are not ordinarily acquired by deed, commercial custom can give the memorandum binding force just as it gives force to the handshake. The claim that one local rabbi once ruled otherwise is meaningless. A single rabbi cannot create a commercial custom, especially in monetary law where such a practice would advantage one party and disadvantage another. Even small communities that conduct little trade follow the established practices of the larger commercial centers around them. A custom requires repeated practice, not a single incident, and in the major communities the custom is that such a transaction is fully binding. Even if one treated the matter as doubtful, Torah law originally makes money acquire movables; the Sages later enacted that money alone should not acquire. Where merchants have adopted a binding custom, one can say that the transaction reverts to the Torah baseline. At most there is uncertainty whether the rabbinic enactment applies despite the custom, and in a doubt concerning an enactment we retain the underlying law. This can matter even in extracting money. Nor does it matter that only part of the price was paid. Shulchan Aruch states that an earnest payment coupled with acquisition can bind the whole transaction. Commercial practice clearly treats the deposit as directed to the entire sale, as shown by the fact that retraction exposes the party to the sanction of “He Who exacted punishment.” Therefore the parties' intent embraces the whole transaction, and the same custom can make the acquisition fully effective. This appears clear and correct.
