Rabbi Akiva Eiger, responsum 134, cites Yam Shel Shlomo, Bava Kamma 8:60, in the name of the Mordechai, that situmta is found to acquire only something already in existence, since it is like a full kinyan; with respect to something not yet in existence it cannot be stronger than an ordinary kinyan. Radbaz rules likewise, as do Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat here. Netivot also cites Maharshal, responsum 34, who held that a lease-right may be acquired by a document even though it concerns something not yet in existence, because this is the prevailing custom and resembles situmta; Netivot suggests that this may be because it is also recognized by dina de-malkhuta. Chatam Sofer, Choshen Mishpat, siman 66, however, rules simply that situmta can also acquire something not yet in existence. The Rosh, responsa 13:20, states that where common practice is to convey future lease rights by written contracts, the custom is effective through situmta. Chatam Sofer explains the Mordechai's statement in the name of Rabbeinu Yechiel concerning one who promised another the privilege of circumcising his son: the promise is binding by situmta because that was the custom, but if the child had not yet been born it was ineffective because there was no custom to promise a mohel before the child existed. That person departed from the customary practice, so situmta did not apply. The Rosh, by contrast, dealt with a custom that specifically included future interests, and therefore it was effective. Chatam Sofer further writes that situmta certainly can overcome an asmachta, because the defect of asmachta is lack of settled intent, while an established custom itself creates settled intent. Yet in his case, a Jew who bought timber from a nobleman under a contract deposited the contract with a second Jew who was to pay the nobleman five hundred gold coins, stipulating that if the first did not repay by a certain date the contract would become absolutely the second Jew's. That remained an asmachta and was not acquired, because Chatam Sofer had never heard of any custom validating such forfeiture; to the contrary, such cases were adjudicated under ordinary Jewish and civil law. Thus the second Jew did not acquire. This conclusion requires study, because the plain meaning of the Mordechai does not appear to agree, and Radbaz I, siman 278, also indicates otherwise.
