I further found. Situmta apparently effects acquisition from a non-Jew as well. It seems that the practice need not have been tested for legal enforceability in non-Jewish courts. Whenever merchants rely upon it to finalize a sale, it constitutes situmta. Thus the customary written contract used when purchasing from non-Jews, what is called a contract, constitutes situmta even for movables. A handshake is likewise commonly treated as situmta. Even with noblemen, many of whom refuse to shake hands, one who does so has placed himself within the general commercial practice. If a second buyer later takes the merchandise because the seller refuses to deliver it to the first buyer, and the second says, “I do not know whether you had a handshake agreement,” this may be treated as “I do not know whether I ever became obligated,” since the merchandise is effectively unavailable to the first buyer in any event and the second is not required, merely because of doubt, to take it and deliver it to him. Perhaps even if it is known that there was a handshake or a writing that is effective as situmta, or even a writing operating in a manner comparable to movables acquired together with land, if there is absolutely no possibility of litigating with the powerful party who seized it, the case may resemble property taken by non-Jews or an item swept away by the sea. If the second buyer initially did not know of the first sale and paid money, and he will incur a substantial loss while the first buyer will in no event receive the merchandise, there may be room to apply the principle of one benefits while the other suffers no loss. The distinctions in siman 363 must be considered, but where the adverse party is too powerful to sue, the second buyer’s taking does not necessarily create a compensable benefit at the first buyer’s expense. Perhaps even where there is only doubt whether there was an oral agreement contrary to the plain wording of the first buyer’s document, the second may say, “I do not know whether I became obligated.” One claimed that he bought wax from a non-Jew by situmta, to be brought for weighing, and that the non-Jew also owed him an amount equal to the wax’s value. When the non-Jew brought the wax for weighing, the buyer was not home. The buyer’s friend seized the wax from the non-Jew and gave the non-Jew his own signed undertaking to return either the money or the wax. Before the non-Jew left, another Jew attached the wax, asserting that the non-Jew owed him money, which the non-Jew admitted. It appears that the first claimant has the stronger right to the wax. Even if it was not yet certain that the non-Jew had finally left it there, the first claimant and his friend acted to seize it, and without the prior sale there would have been no basis to seize it. Where there is no stronger presumption of prior ownership in favor of either claimant because the wax is in a third party’s hands, and the first claimant says with certainty that he acquired it while the second says only that he does not know whether there had been a sale, certainty prevails over doubt. Even if the second were to assert positively that there was no sale, the matter could resemble one who says “half is mine” against one who says “all is mine,” assuming it is established that the non-Jew owes each of them. Still, because the first claimant asserts a certain acquisition, it appears that the property cannot be taken from his presumptive right. Perhaps the second may require him to accept a ban against falsehood. If the non-Jew, speaking innocently, says that he did sell it, no such ban should be required, for just as Shach in siman 149 writes that a non-Jew’s innocent statement can create liability for a rabbinic oath, so it may remove the need for a ban that exists only because of doubt. If the second claimant later physically seized the wax and asserts that the non-Jew owed him, and the non-Jew confirms that debt while denying the first sale, perhaps the second can retain it because he now has certain possession and with movables the first possessor prevails. Yet because his seizure occurred after the dispute arose and without permission, perhaps it is ineffective even where the non-Jew supports him. This requires further study. Situmta may perhaps require an act rather than mere speech. Reason suggests, however, that if commercial practice treats a verbal commitment itself as final, it too can constitute situmta. Perhaps an auction, called a listatsye, can likewise be situmta, as one dayan ruled, although perhaps that practice is not sufficiently fixed. Even where situmta effects acquisition, it does not necessarily create a new personal obligation where none otherwise exists. Thus, if after it became clear that there was no claim of overreaching in the sale a party nevertheless consented by handshake to add an additional amount, that is an undertaking to pay what he does not owe, and situmta does not create such a liability. A kinyan sudar can create a personal obligation through the exchange inherent in the kinyan, but ordinary forms of situmta are practiced for acquisitions, not for gratuitous obligations. The same distinction appears to apply to custom. See also the glosses of Imrei Baruch.
