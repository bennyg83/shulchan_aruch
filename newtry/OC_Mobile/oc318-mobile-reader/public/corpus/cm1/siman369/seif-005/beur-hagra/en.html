Because, etc. This is Rambam's language and follows the latter version. Although despair alone does not acquire, here there are despair and change of possession, because the exchange resembles a sale. The first version treats the exchange as a gift, for which the market enactment was not made, and thus calls it despair alone. The ruling also specifies that the recipient does not know it was stolen; otherwise it resembles purchase from a notorious thief, where the market enactment does not apply. Even the Rosh agrees in such a case. All this follows Rambam's approach that the market enactment can remain relevant even after despair, whereas according to Ri it is unnecessary once acquisition has already occurred. And its presumption, etc. Although with a customs officer, even a Jewish one, the owner is not presumed to have despaired, here the very fact that the officer exchanged the item indicates definite despair. The Gemara's formulation supports this distinction. Rashba in his novellae analyzes the two baraisos similarly, though in a responsum he explains that one source speaks where despair was known and the other where despair occurred only after the object reached the purchaser. Rambam follows his own position that even despair after change of possession can acquire. Rashba further writes that one who rescues property from the robber may take it even initially and that the rescue itself constitutes change of possession, but Rambam does not accept every part of that approach. Baal HaMaor interprets the dispute as concerning excess value and the duty to act beyond the letter of the law; Ramban and Rashba disagree. Where it is known, etc. This follows the rule that change of possession without despair does not acquire. However, where circumstances clearly show that the owners despaired, this counts as known despair, as indicated in Bava Kamma 116b. Thus Shulchan Aruch states “and its presumption,” etc., while here it adds “where it is known,” etc.
