And he does not know with certainty that this item was stolen. Rambam writes this in chapter 5 of Hilchot Gezeilah, following his view that if the purchaser knew the object was stolen, the purchaser must pay the victim its value. Rif and Rambam hold this generally regarding despair together with a change of domain: although the purchaser acquires the object, he must still pay its value to the victim, as the Mechaber records in their names in simanim 356 and 361 and elsewhere. It is therefore surprising that Rama did not note here that according to the dissenting authorities cited there, Rabbeinu Yitzchak and the Rosh, even if the purchaser knew with certainty that the item was stolen, it nevertheless belongs to him after the acquisition. See the Derishah, where I discussed this at length, and above in the Sema to siman 361, where I brought proof. Apparently Rama followed the Beit Yosef and Kesef Mishneh, who explain Rambam's phrase for another reason based on the sugya in the final chapter of Bava Kamma. I recorded that explanation in the Derishah. Therefore Rama left the ruling here unqualified. In the Derishah, however, I explained why in my opinion their interpretation should be rejected. See also above, siman 368, where I explained the words of Rambam and the Mechaber.
