[Seif 3] Even though most of his property. Maharshal writes that the principle that anything fixed in its place is treated as half and half does not apply here, because that principle applies only when the prohibited item is recognizable in its place, as Tosafot and the Rosh write in the chapter Gid HaNasheh. Nor is this comparable to the beginning of siman 358, where it is forbidden to buy an article that is presumed mostly stolen. The Maggid Mishneh explains that there the purchased article itself is presumed stolen, or most articles of that type are stolen, such as objects that people do not ordinarily sell from their own property, and therefore we follow the majority. Here, however, the purchased article itself is not presumed stolen at all; only the seller is a robber. Therefore, even if only a minority of his property is his own, purchase is permitted. Maharshal objects that the same logic should require us to follow the majority status of the seller, just as we follow the majority status of the object, even though the seller is not presumed a thief or robber. He therefore explains that the minority that belongs to the seller is known with certainty, whereas the majority is only presumed, and that certain minority outweighs the presumption. Yet the Rambam's wording, that most of it is stolen from him, implies that the majority may be known with certainty. It appears to me that leniency is proper here because the permitted minority is clearly before us, since we know he has some property of his own. It is therefore as though the permitted articles were fixed in their place, and the rule of fixed status applies. In siman 358, by contrast, no definite permitted item is before us, while the majority is stolen, and we therefore follow the majority.
