The rule that a customs collector is treated like a robber applies when the collector is a non-Jew, or when he appointed himself, or when he was appointed by the king but no fixed amount was set and he takes whatever he wishes. But if the king fixed a definite tax even if he ordered Jews to pay more than non-Jews, this is still considered a fixed amount for each person. Maharik, shoresh 194. and appointed a Jewish collector to collect it for the king, and it is known that this collector is trustworthy and adds nothing to the royal assessment, he is not presumed a robber because dina d'malchusa dina applies. Moreover, one who evades this customs charge violates the prohibition against theft because he steals the king's allotted due, whether the king is Jewish or non-Jewish. Likewise, if a Jew purchased the right to collect the customs from the king, one who evades it robs the Jew who purchased that right. Some say that even when it is known that the Jewish collector takes more than the fixed assessment, one may not evade the amount legitimately fixed by the king, because it is forbidden to steal even from a robber. Beit Yosef. If, however, a non-Jew purchased the right to collect the customs, evasion is permitted where it is treated like avoiding repayment of his loan and no desecration of the Divine Name will result. Rama: Some say that even when the collector is Jewish, if he did not purchase the right for himself but merely collects for the king, then although evasion is prohibited under dina d'malchusa, if a person does evade payment the collector cannot compel him to pay, because it resembles avoiding repayment of a loan. If, however, fear of the king is involved, the collector may certainly compel payment. Ran, chapter 4 of Nedarim.
