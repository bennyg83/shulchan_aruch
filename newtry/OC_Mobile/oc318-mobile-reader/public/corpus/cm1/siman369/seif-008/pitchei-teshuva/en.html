And whatever he takes from this person alone. See Responsa Shevut Yaakov, part 2, siman 181. He writes that at first glance the same should apply to a tax exemption: if a ruler improperly waives one person's tax, this too should be included in theft, for by doing so he increases the burden on others, and his giving is equivalent to taking. In truth, however, this is not so. Even without this point, the Rambam and Shulchan Aruch here appear to contradict what was stated earlier, that if a ruler became angry with one of his servants and took his field, this is not theft. One must therefore say that it is not theft specifically where he became angry with him, presumably because of some rebellion, since it is a law of the kingdom to confiscate the property of a rebel. Conversely, if the ruler favors a particular person and exempts him from the tax, he certainly would not have exempted him had that person not rendered him some service, and this too falls within the law of the kingdom. According to this, the Rama's words in siman 163, seif 6, concerning one whose share of the taxes was waived by the ruler, are readily understood. See there.
