Likewise, if a king becomes angry with one of his servants or officials from among the people of the land and confiscates his field or courtyard, this is not considered theft, and one may benefit from it. One who purchases it from the king acquires it, and the former owner cannot remove it from him. As also written above at the end of siman 237. But if a king takes a person's field or courtyard contrary to the laws he has established, he is a robber, and the original owner may recover the property from one who purchased it from the king. The general principle is that a law enacted by the king for everyone, and not directed at one individual alone, is not theft; but if he takes from a particular person contrary to the publicly known law, he has committed robbery. Rama: If the king enacts a rule specifically for a particular trade, such as a rule concerning moneylenders at interest, some say dina d'malchusa does not apply because the law is not enacted for everyone. Maharik, shoresh 66. Some say dina d'malchusa applies only to taxes and customs connected to land, because the king may condition residence in his country on compliance with these rules, but not to other matters. Rosh, chapter 4 of Nedarim, in the name of Maharam, and Mordechai, chapter HaGozel Batra. Others disagree and hold that dina d'malchusa applies to every matter. Mordechai there in the name of Tosafot and Terumat HaDeshen, siman 309. Therefore, a lender who holds a pledge may sell it after one year where that is the law of the realm. Ibid., in the name of Ri bar Peretz. This is the primary ruling, as explained above, siman 356, seif 7.
