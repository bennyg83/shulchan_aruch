Seif 8. "Similarly, a king who became angry with his servants, etc." Note: See my Torah work to Parashat Re'eh, written in 5616, where with the help of Heaven I derived a novel rule. Although the authorities write that the law of the kingdom is not law when the king does not impose the decree upon everyone, that limitation applies when all are members of his own country. If he decrees against resident foreigners who are not citizens of his realm, then even if he does not impose the same decree upon his own citizens, the law of the kingdom can still apply. I also wrote there that if he rules over the entire inhabited world, the rationale of the law of the kingdom does not apply, because one cannot say that he may require compliance on the ground that otherwise he does not wish them to dwell in his land, since there is nowhere outside his dominion for them to go unless they fly in the air. See there.
Ibid., in the gloss. "Some say that we apply the law of the kingdom only to taxes, etc." Note: This is extremely difficult to me. Bava Batra 54b explicitly asks, "Did Shmuel really say this? Shmuel said that the law of the kingdom is law, and the king says that land is not acquired except by an official document." What is the Gemara's question if the law of the kingdom applies only to taxes? What connection would that principle have to methods of acquiring land or movable property? It necessarily appears that the principle can apply in all legal matters. True, one might answer that this itself explains why we rule there that the person who took possession acquires, even though we also rule like Shmuel that the law of the kingdom is law; the Rashbam labors to reconcile the two rulings, whereas perhaps they can coexist because we hold that the law of the kingdom applies only to taxes. Nevertheless, the Rosh is especially difficult. In his rulings to Bava Batra he writes that if the royal law is to transfer the property to the first purchaser, the first purchaser acquires, and we rule accordingly above in siman 194. That implies that he holds the law of the kingdom to apply beyond taxes. Yet here the Rosh is cited among those who limit it to taxes. His rulings appear contradictory. The Beit Yosef in Yoreh Deah, Hilchot Nedarim, indicates that the Rosh's commentary and his rulings are by the same author, as is also clear from the Rosh's rulings to Nedarim. His words therefore require study. The Rama too is difficult, for in siman 194 he rules without qualification and does not mention the dispute that he records here. This requires very great study.
