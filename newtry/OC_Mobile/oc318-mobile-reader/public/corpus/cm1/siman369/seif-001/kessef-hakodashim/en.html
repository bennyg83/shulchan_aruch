The law of the king is law. A townsman's garden had fixed boundaries under the city's official plan. Later a stable for imperial cavalry horses was established on part of that garden. The non-Jewish owner subsequently sold his house to a Jew and wrote that he was selling the entire garden as well, although the stable had already been established. Another Jew later obtained a document from the city official granting him the stable site for a commercial purpose, on condition that he establish the stable elsewhere as directed by the official. It appears that because the sovereign's authority is recognized throughout that country, the principle that the law of the kingdom is law applies. If the imperial administration authorizes the city official to choose sites for military stables even within private town gardens, and to move the stable elsewhere later as he sees fit, then once the official exercises that authority the former private claim may no longer control. Perhaps the official has broader authority to take town gardens and substitute other land where a public purpose requires it, though the exact limits of the local law require clarification. At minimum, with respect to military stables this appears to be an established governmental power. If the existence of the earlier private garden is known only through one witness, the person claiming through the official may also have the stronger position because he had no reason to know of the contrary private claim. The rule that one who must swear but cannot swear pays may not apply in such a circumstance. This requires further study.
