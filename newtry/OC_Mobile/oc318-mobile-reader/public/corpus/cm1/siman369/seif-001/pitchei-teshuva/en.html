And whatever he takes from this person alone. See Shevut Yaakov, volume 2, siman 181, who initially writes that the same should apply to a royal waiver of tax. If a ruler improperly exempts one person from tax, that too would appear to be robbery because the burden is thereby shifted to others, and giving a waiver can have the same effect as taking money. He then rejects this. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch themselves distinguish a ruler who singles out one person and takes from him from a ruler who, in anger at a servant, confiscates that servant's field, which is not treated as robbery. One must therefore say that the latter case involves punishment for some rebellion, and the law of the kingdom authorizes confiscation from a rebel. The converse follows: if the ruler favors someone and exempts him from tax, presumably the ruler considers that person to have rendered some service or benefit for which the exemption is granted. That too is within royal law. This explains Rama in siman 163, seif 6, concerning one whose share of taxes was waived by the ruler. See there.
