He may work him, etc., like a servant. The Rambam and Tur write this. The Beit Yosef explains that this does not mean he may impose the degrading service of a Canaanite slave, such as carrying his utensils after him to the bathhouse or removing his shoes, as Scripture says, "You shall not work him with the labor of a slave." Although regarding an ordinary Jew who was never sold as a Hebrew servant such forms of service are permitted, as the Rambam writes in chapter 5 of Hilchot Avadim, because the Torah prohibited them specifically for a Hebrew slave whose spirit is humbled, here the law of the kingdom grants authority to exact labor from these people and their status therefore resembles that of a Hebrew servant. Accordingly, he may not impose upon them the degrading labor of a Canaanite slave. This appears correct.

One who marries a woman in a place where they adjudicate according to gentile law, etc. If his wife dies, her father cannot thereby deprive the husband of his inheritance. One may ask from Rama at the beginning of siman 248, where he writes that if a will was made in a place where the custom is to adjudicate according to gentile law, and the parties later come before a Jewish court, we do not say that inheritance cannot be interrupted. That ruling is from Rivash, siman 52, and there it is explicit that the heir later went to another place where Jewish law was followed, yet the ruling still follows the law of the place where the will was made. The cases differ. There, the testator died in the place where gentile law governed and made an unspecified testament saying, "and after him, to so and so." We say that he certainly intended the expression as people there ordinarily understand it, namely literally, even if the first recipient is someone legally fit to inherit. Here, however, when the man married the woman, inheritance was not yet at issue and there was no revelation of intent that he married on condition to follow the local custom. The husband is presently before us and says he never intended to marry on condition that if she died he would not inherit her. We do not uproot Torah law on that basis. Rama therefore concludes, based on Rashba, that this is not a matter of dina demalkhuta dina. That is, one might argue that since they lived in a place where the courts apply gentile law, and under that law the husband does not inherit his wife, their present dispute should be adjudicated that way and Jewish law should enforce the royal law. Rama answers that this inheritance rule is fixed by Torah law. The husband need not submit to gentile judges, because dina demalkhuta applies only within its proper sphere, and the Jewish beit din must prevent the wife's father from taking the matter before gentile courts.
We say dina demalkhuta dina only regarding a matter that benefits the king, etc. See Darkei Moshe, which cites Rashba in somewhat different language: dina demalkhuta dina was said concerning matters that are truly laws of kingship. Rules applied by secular courts merely because they are found in their legal books are not necessarily laws of the monarchy itself. If one were to say otherwise, Heaven forbid, Jewish civil law would be nullified. Darkei Moshe also cites Maharik and Mordechai and writes that they agree in principle. It appears that this means even if the king expressly decrees that people must follow the secular courts' legal rules, Jewish judges are not thereby bound to apply those rules merely because dina demalkhuta is law. That principle was stated regarding royal legislation and governance. Accordingly, Rama's earlier statement in seif 8 that some say dina demalkhuta applies "in every matter," and that this is primary, must also be understood within this framework. "Every matter" means even matters other than taxes and royal dues, but still matters of royal governance. Proof is that in Darkei Moshe, immediately after citing the Mordechai in Tosafot's name that dina demalkhuta applies broadly, he cites Maharik's limitation that even according to those views it applies specifically to governmental rules and practices of kings. See there carefully.
