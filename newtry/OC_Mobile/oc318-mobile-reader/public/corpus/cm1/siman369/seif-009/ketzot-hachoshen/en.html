But a tax. Nowadays one townsman can be seized for another's communal tax because residents are mutually responsible. Maharshal limits this where the authorities themselves seize a person. If one resident voluntarily pays another's tax, he cannot necessarily impose reimbursement merely by his own choice. In my view, where one already holds his fellow's property, ordinary rules of setoff apply even outside tax law. The doctrine of one who pays another's debt gratuitously applies chiefly where he had no property of the debtor in hand. Rosh's responsum concerning joint debt instruments does not contradict this. There the community collected Reuven's tax from jointly owned instruments. Reuven then wished to keep Shimon's share on account of tax Shimon would owe later. Rosh ruled that he could not. Once Reuven's present tax was paid using Shimon's property, Reuven immediately owed Shimon that share. He could not retain it merely because Shimon might later owe the community, especially where the future debt had not yet been paid and Rabbi Natan's lien was unavailable. But where one townsman actually pays tax already chargeable to another, the communal structure is different. Townspeople are involuntary guarantors for one another and, in effect, partners in the communal tax burden. A guarantor who pays can ordinarily recover from the principal, and partners who discharge a joint debt can recover the other's share. Therefore a resident who actually pays another's existing communal tax can have a reimbursement claim even if the payment was voluntary.
