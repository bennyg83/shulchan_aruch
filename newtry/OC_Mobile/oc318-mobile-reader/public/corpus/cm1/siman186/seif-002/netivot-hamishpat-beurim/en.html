Even on the return. See Sema, seif katan 4, who gives the reason that the return of this journey is itself part of the outward journey. This comes from the Gemara. At first glance it is difficult according to Rashbam and Rosh, who hold that one who takes an item for inspection remains liable for ones even after revealing that he does not want it, until he actually returns it to the seller. Beit Yosef asks from the case of one who takes goods to send to his father-in-law, where he is exempt from ones on the return, and answers that there he receives compensation if the father-in-law does not accept the goods, and this weakens his borrower status. If so, why is the special rationale that "the return of this journey is part of the outward journey" needed here? The principal here does not pay the sender compensation, so even without that rationale the sender should remain liable for ones until actual return, like one who takes an item for inspection. There are further difficulties. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 81, in the name of Tosafot of Sens, asks how the Gemara proves that after the days of borrowing expire the custodian becomes a paid custodian because "since he benefited, he provides benefit," from the law of one who takes goods to send. Tosafot of Sens asks that there the holder is a true renter because he pays the seller compensation if the gift is rejected. Shitah answers that during the return the rental period has already ended. But immediately before that Shitah cites Ritva that a paid custodian remains liable until he informs the owner, proving this from an artisan. If so, the question returns: why is the holder no longer a renter during the return merely because the gift was rejected, unless he informed the seller? Beit Yosef in siman 200 also asks against the poskim who hold that once the buyer reveals dissatisfaction he is exempt from ones, from the case of the flask sent with a child, where the sender remains liable even after returning it to the child and thereby revealing that he does not want it. A fourth difficulty arises from the Yerushalmi cited by Beit Yosef in Tur, siman 343, concerning one who borrowed today and rented tomorrow, with a question about the intervening night. The version cited there appears contrary to Rashba's version of the Yerushalmi at the beginning of HaShoel, and Rashba's explanation is itself difficult. The case certainly concerns borrowing during the day and not at night, yet the distinction the Yerushalmi draws concerning the night is hard to understand. The matter can be reconciled by distinguishing a loan for a fixed period from a loan for completion of a task. Where an object is borrowed for a fixed time, borrower liability ends when the fixed period ends. The end date itself is like advance notice from the lender that at that time he will take back his property. It is as though the lender had said, "At the end of the term, return my property." The same applies to a renter when the rental period ends. But where one borrows or rents an object to perform a particular task, he remains borrower or renter until he returns it, or until he says, "Take your property," or at least informs the owner that the task is complete. This resembles an artisan, who remains a paid custodian until he says, "I have finished." Once the fixed borrowing term ends, the borrower does not become completely exempt. He becomes a paid custodian because "since he benefited, he provides benefit," as the Gemara explains. This is different from an ordinary paid custodian whose fixed custody term ends. A paid custodian may remain liable so long as the deposit remains with him and he continues to receive his pay, unless he gives notice. Borrower and renter, however, use the item for their own benefit. When their known use period ends, they are no longer permitted to use it without the owner's consent. That itself marks the end of borrower or renter status. Now suppose one borrows for one task and afterward rents for a second task. Once the first task ends, he need not notify the owner in order for the rental phase to begin, because the owner already instructed that after completion of the borrowing task he should continue holding the item under the rental arrangement. This is no different from the owner saying, "When the borrowing period ends, give the item to that renter," and telling the borrower to hand it over. If the designated renter is the same person, no separate notice is necessary. This explains the Yerushalmi concerning borrowing today and renting tomorrow. If animals ordinarily spend the night in the owner's stable, then when the borrowing day ends the night is naturally a time when the item should have returned to the owner. The end of the borrowing day is therefore like the lender saying, "Take your property," and borrower liability has ended. But in a place where borrowed animals are not ordinarily returned at night, the borrowing responsibility continues through the night, because the general rule is that borrower liability does not end until the time at which the owner would normally retake possession. This also reconciles Rashbam and Rosh with the other authorities. When they write that even after the holder reveals dissatisfaction he remains liable for ones, they must mean that he revealed it before witnesses or to third parties but did not inform the seller himself. Such a private declaration does not end the seller's reliance on him, any more than an artisan's private statement before witnesses that his work is complete. By contrast, the authorities who say that revealing dissatisfaction ends liability mean that he communicated this to the seller. That is equivalent to saying, "Take your property," and ends borrower liability. The flask sent with the child is no difficulty. Returning it to the child is not notifying the seller. Delivering it to a minor is no better than declaring before witnesses that one no longer wants it. So long as the seller himself has not been informed and the item has not been returned to him, the holder remains liable. This also resolves the proof in Bava Metzia concerning a borrower becoming a paid custodian after the borrowing period. The Gemara proves from the fact that the holder remains a paid custodian even after he informed the seller and the borrower liability had ended. Thus it must be that after borrowing ends, the former borrower still has paid-custodian liability because he previously benefited. The fact that he had earlier been a renter or received some compensating benefit does not itself explain the continuing liability. Finally, the Gemara's statement here that "the return of this journey is part of the outward journey" is necessary because, unlike a trip to a particular identified recipient such as one's father-in-law, the purpose here is to sell the goods generally. Even after the holder fails to sell them in one location and informs the owner of that failure, the commercial mission has not necessarily ended. He may still sell them on the return journey, even at the door of his own house. The return is therefore still part of the selling mission itself. For that reason he remains liable as on the outward journey, notwithstanding notice that he could not sell in the place to which he first traveled.
And he is liable only for theft and loss. This is difficult to me. This "some say" is plainly the view of Tosafot and Rosh, and according to them the same law should apply even to the first clause concerning one who takes goods to send to his father-in-law. If so, Mechaber should have explained that under this view, when the merchandise is not readily marketable, the holder is only an unpaid custodian on the return journey. Bava Metzia 81 proves that after the borrowing period ends the holder becomes a paid custodian because "since he benefited, he provides benefit." Shitah Mekubetzet asks that in the goods-sent-to-the-father-in-law case the holder pays compensation and is therefore already a paid custodian. It answers that once the father-in-law rejects the goods, the period of that rental has ended. The remaining paid-custodian liability exists only because he had previously been a borrower. Accordingly, where the merchandise is difficult to sell and the holder was never a borrower in the first place, he should be entirely exempt from paid-custodian liability on the return, and have only the law of an unpaid custodian. Even according to what I wrote above, that the Gemara's proof concerns the period after notice to the seller, while before such notice a different rationale could sustain paid-custodian status, the same difficulty remains. Once he has notified the seller, he should at least become only an unpaid custodian under this view. Mechaber should have stated this explicitly.
