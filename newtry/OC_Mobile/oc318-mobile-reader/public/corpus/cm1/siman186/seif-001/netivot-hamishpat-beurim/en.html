And they were lost through ones while going, he is liable. This is Rambam's language. Mishneh LaMelech asks that in chapter 4 of Hilchot Mechirah, concerning one who takes utensils in order to inspect them, Rambam requires both a fixed price and a readily marketable item, as is evident from Nedarim 31. Here he records no such conditions. The omission of a fixed price is not difficult. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 81 explains that one who takes items in order to send them to his father-in-law is ordinarily understood to have fixed the price beforehand. How could he take an item to present to his father-in-law without knowing its price? Perhaps the father-in-law will like it and the seller will then demand an unaffordable price. By contrast, one who merely takes an item to inspect it may reasonably do so before price is fixed, and therefore Rambam had to state expressly that the price was fixed in that case. The real difficulty is why Rambam does not require that the item be readily marketable. Lechem Mishneh further asks that Rambam's implications contradict one another. At first he says that with a readily marketable item the buyer is liable for ones, implying that with an ordinary item he is exempt. This is in fact evident from Nedarim 31. But at the end Rambam says that with merchandise that is difficult to sell he is exempt, which implies that an ordinary item carries liability. I am also troubled by Mechaber, who here explains "readily marketable" as an item with many potential buyers, while in siman 200 he explains it as an item especially desirable to this buyer. The matter can be resolved as follows. Tosafot and Rosh struggle with the fact that Nedarim shows liability for ones to be based on the law of a borrower, because it requires the benefit to belong only to the buyer and not to the seller, whereas Bava Batra 88 implies that liability is based on his status as buyer. Shitah Mekubetzet in Bava Metzia 81 distinguishes: where the price is fixed but the item is not particularly marketable, liability is based on buyer status; where it is particularly marketable but the price is not fixed, liability is based on borrower status. The first part is compelling. Once the price is fixed, he can indeed have the law of a buyer even though the seller too benefits. Buyer liability does not require exclusive benefit to the buyer. But the second part is difficult. If the price is not fixed, how can the item be considered especially attractive to the buyer? Nedarim itself shows that if the seller demands more than market value, the item ceases to be "readily marketable" even to that buyer. Without a fixed price, perhaps the seller will demand an excessive amount. Rambam therefore appears to hold that when the price is fixed, even an ordinary item gives the holder the status of a buyer. He becomes a borrower only when the item is also especially marketable, so that the entire benefit lies with him and not with the seller. Thus two bases of liability can coexist: buyer liability and borrower liability. A practical difference appears after he reveals that he does not want the item. Rashba and Rosh write that one who takes an item to inspect it remains liable for ones even after revealing that he does not want it. Beit Yosef explains that every borrower remains responsible until the object returns to the lender. This rationale applies to borrower liability. But where liability rests only on buyer status, the basis is that the seller cannot retract during the relevant period. Once the holder announces that he does not want the item, the original meshichah as a prospective buyer is canceled. The seller can now take the item back, and the holder no longer has buyer status. This resembles a conditional purchase in which the condition fails. Therefore the liability based solely on buyer status ends when he gives notice. This resolves the sugya in Nedarim. Shmuel's ruling implies that the holder remains liable even after revealing that he does not want the item. That can only be because he has borrower liability, meaning the entire benefit is his. Therefore the Gemara establishes the case specifically with an especially marketable item. Rambam accordingly records the condition of a readily marketable item in the law of one who takes an item to inspect, because he is addressing Shmuel's rule of continuing liability after notice. For that, borrower status is necessary. But in chapter 2 of Hilchot Sheluchin, concerning one who takes items to send to his father-in-law, Rambam says he is liable only on the outward journey, not on the return. During the outward journey, before he has revealed dissatisfaction, buyer liability alone suffices. Since the price is assumed fixed, he is liable even if the item is ordinary. No special marketability is needed. This also resolves Lechem Mishneh's conflicting implications. Rambam's first statement about an especially marketable item concerns Shmuel's rule, liability even after notice, and therefore requires borrower status. Rambam's later statement that an item difficult to sell is exempt even earlier means that such an item does not even create buyer status, because the holder never reasonably expects to keep it. An ordinary item occupies the middle category: during the outward journey he is liable as a buyer, but after revealing dissatisfaction he is no longer liable for ones because he was never a borrower. There is also a difference between one who takes an item to inspect and one who takes it to send as a gift. If he takes an ordinary item at its market price merely to inspect it, and the item is generally difficult to sell, it is close to certain he will not buy it. He therefore does not acquire buyer status. But one who takes the item as a gift for his father-in-law has already taken it substantially as a completed purchase, subject only to the condition whether his father-in-law will accept the gift. Even if the merchandise itself is difficult to sell, the holder has strong reason to expect that his father-in-law will accept it as a gift. He therefore has buyer status on the outward journey. This also explains the different definitions of "readily marketable." In siman 200, where Mechaber cites Rambam's rule regarding one who takes an item to inspect it, the relevant question is whether the object is especially desirable to this buyer, because borrower liability depends on whether the entire benefit is his. Here, however, the cited opinion is Rosh's view concerning taking goods to send or resell. In that context the relevant idea is commercial marketability, whether there are many ready purchasers, because the holder's intention depends on the likelihood that the third party will accept or that he can resell the goods. Mechaber therefore had to adopt Rosh's own interpretation for Rosh's rule.
