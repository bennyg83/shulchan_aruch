His fellow is not obligated to pay. The Shach writes that this is so even if he truly owes the debt. The Sema and the Perishah object that according to the Tur and Rabbenu Tam, and likewise according to the Rama, who rules that with respect to a gentile all agree that one who pays another's debt without his knowledge may recover, why should he be exempt here? They answer that since he was compelled into doing so and did not pay voluntarily, he is exempt. See Sema, seif katan 6, and Mahara"sh, siman 76, see there. These words are difficult. What difference does it make to the debtor whether the payer was compelled or not? He can always say: I wished to do so; although it began under compulsion, in the end I willingly paid on your behalf. Who would be foolish enough, upon seeing himself seized, not to wish to pay if otherwise he would lose reimbursement? Therefore, it appears as I explained above: since evasion of a loan owed to a gentile is permitted, this is treated as an unlawful exaction, and the debtor may say, this was merely an arbitrary claim against you; what have I to do with you? Your misfortune caused it. As for the Perishah's objection that, on the contrary, regarding an arbitrary exaction the authorities cited above are more stringent and require reimbursement, one may distinguish. That rule applies where his fellow is in the same country and can himself be seized. According to those who hold that one who pays another's debt without his knowledge may recover, he may recover there as well, because he has driven a lion away from the other's property, as explained in the Gemara. But if his fellow fled and is not in that country, and the gentile authorities have no power over him there, while this person who remains under their power was seized, the concept of driving away a lion does not apply, for the debtor is not under their power at all. Therefore his fellow is exempt, except in the case of taxes, where, because the residents are mutually responsible, he must reimburse one who was seized for him even if he himself was not in a place where he could be seized. This is simple and correct. For this reason the Tur presented this as an independent ruling, because it has no connection at all to the rule of one who pays another's debt, and this is clear. It further appears that even according to Rabbenu Tam, if the payer has a right to recover from the creditor, the debtor may say: you are not my litigant; go back to the creditor. Certainly, even according to Rabbenu Tam, if the debtor expressly protests that another should not pay his debt and nevertheless that person pays it, the debtor is plainly exempt. This case is the same, because he continues to protest: go to the creditor. Otherwise, Tosafot in Gittin 13b, s.v. gufa, in the name of Rabbenu Tam, would be difficult. They write that ma'amad sheloshtan operates even against the debtor's will, for otherwise, according to the opinion that ma'amad sheloshtan applies only to a deposit, why is the special rule needed? The transferor could simply say, acquire it for him. This implies that according to the opinion that ma'amad sheloshtan applies even to a loan, there is no difficulty, because acquisition on another's behalf does not apply to a loan, as the Rashba indeed writes there, explaining that this is the point of dispute. Yet why would the special rule be necessary even for a loan? Once the debtor is released from the first creditor by the latter's assignment to the recipient, he should owe the second creditor under the ordinary rule of one who pays another's debt, especially since this was with his knowledge, according to the present assumption that the transfer is not against his will. It is difficult to say that where the recipient receives it as a gift, the rule of paying another's debt does not apply, since the entire enactment was made for purchasers and borrowers, as explained by the poskim and as I wrote explicitly above in siman 126 and siman 91. We must therefore say that Rabbenu Tam follows his own view, like the Rosh, that the recipient may retract. Thus, without ma'amad sheloshtan, the debtor too could retract, because this is not considered payment on his behalf, for he could say to the recipient: go to the first creditor. However, one may say that if the debtor owes by a written instrument and his property is encumbered, then when the creditor transfers that one hundred to the recipient, the recipient acquires all the rights of the first creditor and may collect from encumbered property by virtue of the first creditor's instrument. But if the recipient's claim rests only on the rule of one who paid the debtor's debt, the lien of the instrument has terminated, and the new debt arising because he paid for him is merely a later oral loan. Therefore, even according to the Shach, who holds that ma'amad sheloshtan does not apply to a written loan, there is still a practical difference regarding an earlier oral loan as against a later oral loan. As for the incident involving the money of Rabbi Abba, where it was necessary that he write, "I have received," in order to transfer it by means of land because perhaps Rabbi Abba would die before writing it, why could he not simply write that the agent had paid on his behalf, so that it would constitute payment of another's debt and the debtor would have to reimburse him? One may answer that Rabbi Abba did not trust the agent, lest he keep the money for himself, whereas in an acquisition the document included full authorization for whatever litigation might arise. This is strained, since it would mean that he did not trust Rav Safra. Similarly, Tosafot in Bava Kamma, in the chapter Merubah, write that strictly speaking a loan cannot be transferred by any kinyan, and only with an authorization did the Sages act leniently as an enactment. Why was that necessary? Let the creditor write to the authorized agent that the agent paid him, and the agent can then sue the debtor for reimbursement. One cannot answer that he fears the agent may retain the money, because according to Ameimar, who holds that even under an authorization, if the agent seizes the money we do not remove it from him, the question remains: why is authorization needed at all, when it is not an ordinary Torah mode of transfer? We must again say that the purpose is to preserve priority, so that the creditor does not lose his earlier priority against the debtor through this authorization. If the authorized agent were merely deemed to have paid him, his claim against the debtor would arise only from the time of that payment, since the original debt had already been waived and satisfied. According to the Bach, who holds that Rabbenu Tam agrees regarding an oral debt and disputes only a debt in writing, the matter is easier. But his words do not appear correct, as the Shach writes. What difference is there between shekalim and an oral loan? After all, he holds that a debt written in the Torah is not treated as a debt in a written instrument.
Because he was compelled to compromise, etc. He cannot retain the teacher's money. It appears that the wording "was compelled" is not precise; rather, the case is that he will be compelled. The intent of the Hagahot Mordechai is as follows. The householder wishes to retain money belonging to the teacher because he expects that he will have to pay on the teacher's behalf, and these funds would then be needed for that payment. The Mordechai rules that he cannot retain them, because a person's property is like his guarantor, and one does not proceed against a guarantor before first demanding payment from the borrower. This is exactly as I wrote: so long as the householder has not yet paid, and the claim is that the teacher should pay, he cannot retain the teacher's assets. It is therefore explicit that the Mordechai holds that if the householder had already paid and then demanded reimbursement from the teacher, the teacher would be obligated to pay, and if he did not, the householder could retain the teacher's property just as one may proceed against a guarantor. If the Mordechai held that the teacher is exempt even after the householder paid, the principle that a person's assets are guarantors and that one does not proceed against the guarantor first would be irrelevant, for the debtor himself would be exempt. Thus the matter is clearly as I wrote, and the Rama's phrase "was compelled" is imprecise, as stated. Accordingly, all the Shach's discussion in seif katan 11, rejecting the Rama's ruling, emending the Mordechai, and asserting that the teacher's deposit was in the hands of a third party, is unnecessary and unconvincing. The Mordechai does not imply that; rather, the correct explanation is as I have written, and there is no difficulty. We must say that the case is one in which there is an available forum in which to sue the teacher, and he is not in the category of one who dissipates his assets, in which case his property may be attached. The teacher is not within that category. In particular, here it is not yet certain that the gentiles will come and that the householder will be required to pay. For such a mere possibility, one may not attach the teacher's assets. In any event, the entire law of attachment is an enactment, and we apply the principle that one should not extend an enactment beyond its stated scope.
But if he has already appeased, etc. The Shach in seif katan 18 expresses surprise at the wording "some say," since this is an explicit Gemara: Rava said that one townsman may be seized for another townsman, etc., but once the year has passed, since the king has been appeased, he has been appeased. It appears, however, that the Gemara states only that the king's tax collector has no authority to seize one person for another after the collector has already satisfied the king from his own funds. In such a case, the law of the kingdom does not authorize him to collect from one resident on account of another once payment has already been received, as Rashi expressly explains there. But if the king's collector nevertheless violated the rule and seized one person for another, forcing him to pay, we have not learned from that passage that the other person is exempt from reimbursing what was paid for his tax. With respect to taxes, one person may ordinarily be seized for another, and this is not included in the ordinary rule of one who pays another's debt without his knowledge, because during the tax year the person seized was himself within the collective obligation. Even if afterward the tax collector no longer has authority to seize him, why should his fellow be exempt? His fellow still owes the tax, and the wrongful seizure of this person arose because of that fellow and on his account. The Rama therefore teaches that once the king has been appeased, the character of a tax obligation has terminated. It is then like other debts, regarding which one person is not subject to seizure for another. This also appears to be how the Rama understood the Hagahot Mordechai, which discusses whether the debtor must reimburse the person who paid on his behalf. See there carefully.
