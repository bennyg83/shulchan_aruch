His fellow, etc. The Shach understands this to apply even where the other person actually owes the gentiles, for that is how the Mordechai reads and it is also implied by the Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 163. The Sema and Perishah ask according to the Tur, Rosh, Rabbeinu Tam, and the Rama, who hold that where one pays another's gentile creditor without authorization the debtor is liable, why should he be exempt here? The Sema, seif katan 6, answers that because the payer was coerced into paying and did not pay voluntarily, he is exempt. This is strained, because he can always say that his initial involvement was coerced. In Tumim I concluded that since withholding repayment of a gentile's loan can be permitted, the gentile's seizure here is legally an unjust exaction rather than payment of the other person's debt. See there. As for the Derishah's statement that with an unjust exaction the authorities are stringent, I distinguished in Tumim. If the debtor is in the same country and could himself be seized, then according to those who obligate one who pays another's debt without authorization, he too is liable. But if the debtor is in another country and cannot be seized, he is exempt, except in taxes, where all members of the community are mutually responsible and he must reimburse. In my opinion this distinction is clear, and that is why the Tur placed this as an independent law, because it is not simply a case of paying another person's debt. I also discussed according to Rabbeinu Tam whether the payer has recourse against the creditor, since the debtor can say, 'You are not my litigant.' I wrote that even if the payer paid a written debt, once we analyze his claim as one who paid another's debt, his right against the debtor is only oral. See also what I wrote to reject somewhat the Bach's view that Rabbeinu Tam agrees in an oral debt, in addition to what the Shach writes in this siman. See there at length.
Because he had to settle. See Tumim. The wording 'had to' is not precise. The Rama should have written 'will have to,' as I proved from the Mordechai, the source of the Rama's law, and as explained in the following seif katan. I also concluded that the case assumes there is some possibility of claiming against the teacher. Otherwise, the matter might fall under the rule of one who is dissipating his assets, where beit din can restrain the assets, as explained above. According to our reading, however, that point is unnecessary, because it is only doubtful whether the gentiles will come, and property is not restrained merely because of a doubt. The entire remedy of restraint is itself an enactment, and we should not extend it beyond its stated scope.
He cannot withhold them. The Sema writes that although the Rama above says that some hold that where one paid a gentile the debtor is liable according to all opinions, because there the payer had to pay the gentile, the cases differ. There the payment was made for the friend's benefit, since the gentile would otherwise have pursued the friend. Here the teacher already left and the gentiles could no longer pursue him, but instead brought an unjust claim against the homeowner. See Sema. The Shach disagrees, consistently with his own approach. The matter nevertheless requires study. See Tumim, seif katan 2, and what I wrote in seif katan 5, where I agreed as practical law with the Sema. The Sema further writes that this differs from the Tur and Mechaber at the end of siman 126, where if coercers come against Reuven because of Shimon's deposit, Reuven may satisfy them from the deposit itself. There they know the deposit is in his possession and came because of it. Moreover, the authorities there expressly limit the rule to a deposit. Here, money left by the teacher with the homeowner is not treated as the identical property at risk. The Shach, seif katan 11, writes that since the case is where the teacher lost the gentiles' pledges and is legally obligated to pay, as appears from the Mordechai and Rama, why should the homeowner not withhold from the teacher's property because he had to pay for him? Even according to those who hold that one who pays another's debt, even to a gentile, is exempt, and even according to those who hold that a person seized for another is exempt although the other truly owes the debt, this case is different. The homeowner became liable because the teacher was in his house, the teacher truly owed the money, and the homeowner had to pay in his place. The Shach further argues at length that the Rama did not accurately copy Hagahot Mordechai and proposes emendations in both texts. See Tumim, seif katan 6, where I showed that no emendation is necessary and that the Mordechai and Rama are both correct and clear.
Because of the fixed tax. Shiltei Giborim in Bava Batra writes that this applies only to a tax imposed by the ruler of this country. If Reuven belongs to a different kingdom and is seized because of Shimon's tax in this kingdom, since that king wrongfully seized him, Shimon owes him nothing. See below siman 369, Shach, and what I wrote there.
A year. Maharshal in Bava Kamma writes that nowadays the law of the kingdom permits collection indefinitely, especially since gentile rulers often extend payment terms for Jews for several years. Therefore property may be seized for the tax even after a long period. See Shach.
And some say, etc., but if the king has already been appeased, etc. The Shach, seif katan 18, asks why the Rama presents this as 'some say.' The Beit Yosef cites the same rule from Hagahot Mordechai, and the Sema, seif katan 6, cites Rabbeinu Yerucham, yet it is an explicit Gemara in Bava Kamma. The Shach likewise questions Yam Shel Shlomo for asking why the Tur omitted that Gemara, when the Tur actually cites it in siman 369. See Tumim, where I defended the Rama and explained that his use of 'some say' is appropriate, because the precise rule he states is not actually proven by that Gemara. See there.
Who borrowed. Meaning, even if the community leaders already borrowed money and appeased the king, the community must reimburse them, because the leaders are like guardians of the community and acted as its agents and for its benefit. See Sema. The Shach likewise writes that the leaders are appointed officials, comparable to guardians and partners, and that they borrowed on behalf of everyone, who are therefore obligated to repay them. This is correct. See Knesset HaGedolah, who cites many responsa disputing whether one who smuggles another's property past customs is entitled to reimbursement of the customs charges from the owner of the merchandise, and additional laws concerning smuggling.
