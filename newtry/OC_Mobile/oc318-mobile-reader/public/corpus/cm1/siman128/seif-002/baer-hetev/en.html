His fellow. This applies even though his fellow actually owes the money to them. So states the Mordechai, and the same appears from Responsa Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in siman 163. Shach.
To pay. It appears that even the authorities who hold that one who voluntarily pays another person's ordinary debt, other than a wife's maintenance, may recover from the debtor agree here that no reimbursement is due. In the ordinary case he paid voluntarily and bestowed a benefit, so the Sages required reimbursement to prevent people from refusing to perform acts of kindness. Here he was forced to pay against his will. Thus, even where he paid a non-Jew, a case in which all agree that voluntary payment ordinarily requires reimbursement, if he was seized by the non-Jew because of the other person, the other person need not reimburse him. Sema.
To withhold them. The Sema writes that although the Rama above cites an opinion that payment to a non-Jew must be reimbursed according to all authorities, here the teacher's employer was forced to pay a non-Jew. The cases differ. Above, the payer acted for his fellow's benefit, because the non-Jewish creditor would otherwise have pursued the debtor. Here the teacher had already left, so the non-Jews could not pursue him and instead fabricated a claim against the employer. This is also unlike the ruling at the end of siman 126, where violent persons come against Reuven because of Shimon's deposit held by Reuven and Reuven may satisfy them from that deposit. There they knew the deposit was in his possession and came because of it. Furthermore, that ruling is expressly limited to a deposit, not a loan, which is given for expenditure. Likewise, the money the teacher left with his employer is not treated as a specific deposit still intact. The Shach rejects this ruling. Since the teacher caused the loss of the non-Jews' collateral and was legally liable for it, why should the employer not retain the teacher's property after being forced to pay on his behalf? Even according to authorities who exempt one who pays another's debt to a non-Jew, and even according to those who exempt one seized for another despite the other's actual liability, this case is different. The employer became liable because the teacher had been in his house, the teacher truly owed the money, and the employer was compelled to pay it for him. The teacher therefore must reimburse him, and the employer may retain property in his hands. See the Shach. See also the Taz on this law. He further writes that if the non-Jews detained the employer's money and he consequently had to compromise with them, even the Rama agrees that the teacher must reimburse him. This follows from the end of siman 126 even in a case of coercion, all the more so here where the teacher is legally liable. It is also clear from Responsa Rama 86.
Who was seized. See Responsa Mabit II, 123 and 124; Responsa Maharashdam 55, 176, 325, 329, and 346.
The tax. Shiltei HaGibborim writes that this applies specifically to the tax of the same kingdom. If Reuven, who belongs to another kingdom, was seized for Shimon's tax in this kingdom, the official acted as a robber and coercer, and Shimon owes nothing. See below, siman 369. Shach.
Year. Maharshal writes that nowadays the king's law permits collection without time limit, particularly because the rulers of the nations often extend kindness to Jews by deferring the tax for several years. Therefore distraint may continue indefinitely. Shach.
They borrowed. Meaning even if the communal officials already borrowed money and appeased the king. The reason is that they function as trustees of the community and acted as the community's agents and for its benefit. Sema.
A loan. See Responsa Chacham Tzvi 68. One who saves another's property from an unlawful customs levy, where he has no connection at all to the merchandise and acted on his own, cannot demand what the owner otherwise would have paid in customs. If, however, he was the owner's agent to sell the merchandise, it is doubtful whether the saving belongs to the agent. The general rule is that the burden of proof is upon the claimant. Mahara Sasson 201. Responsa Divrei Rivot 111 and Maharashach II, 3 and 91, rule that the agent acquires the amount saved from customs. Maharashach further writes that the same applies to a partner who evades customs: the saving belongs to him alone rather than to the partnership. If the evasion causes a loss to the owner of the merchandise, the agent or partner must pay from his own funds under the law of garmi. If one pays another's debt in his presence, it is possible that the debtor must reimburse him. Maharashdam, Even HaEzer 36. One who saves another's money from a robber or coercer is entitled to reimbursement. Divrei Rivot 92; Maharib LeV I, 104, and II, 27. Maharashdam, Choshen Mishpat 330, writes that this is disputed by Rashba and Rosh. See Responsa Mabit II, 44. If one became another's agent and, because of that agency and on account of the principal, a false claim was brought against him, the principal must compensate him. Mabit I, 123.
