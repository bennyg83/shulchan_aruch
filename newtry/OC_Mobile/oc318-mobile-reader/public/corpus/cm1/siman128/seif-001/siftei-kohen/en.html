The debtor is not required to pay, etc. The Sema distinguishes this from the sale of a promissory note, where the purchaser may collect because writing and delivery place him in the seller’s position. Here, one merely paid another’s debt without first receiving the creditor’s power. This is correct, but the Sema improperly criticizes the She’erit Yosef, whose case involved a gentile’s sale of a Jewish note without writing and delivery. The She’erit Yosef’s question was whether gentile law makes such a transfer effective, not whether an ordinary written assignment resembles voluntary payment of another’s debt. He therefore agrees with the Sema’s basic distinction. Nevertheless, the implication that a gentile’s transfer is ineffective unless the Jew paid with authorization is not compelling. The Nimukei Yosef, Rama, and Maharam of Lublin indicate that under gentile law such a transfer may be treated like a Jewish transfer made with writing and delivery.
Even if the creditor pressed him to pay, etc. Many authorities disagree with this unqualified ruling, and in my view it remains an unresolved question of law. The Rif, Rambam, Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Ri’az omit the Yerushalmi’s ruling concerning a pressing creditor. They apparently maintain that where the creditor was pressing, the payer may recover. The Yerushalmi itself can be read as concluding that such payment is reimbursable, because its attempted proof from one who pays another’s shekel or sacrifices is answered by construing those cases as ones where the funds had already entered the proper hands and were later lost. The Babylonian Talmud likewise limits those cases to money already collected and lost. Consequently, the Yerushalmi does not establish exemption for a pressing creditor. Although more than thirty authorities exempt one who voluntarily pays an ordinary debt, that does not resolve the case of a pressing creditor. Rabbeinu Chananel, Rabbeinu Tam, the Rosh, Bach, and Sema support liability, and the omissions of the Rif, Rambam, Semag, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Ri’az point in the same direction. Therefore, where the payer seized property in a case involving a pressing creditor, it is not removed from him.
We say that the debtor could have appeased him, etc. This reason applies to ordinary payment of another’s debt without authorization. With a pressing creditor, the relevant alternative reason is that the debtor might have found friends willing to pay on his behalf. The Mechaber abbreviated the matter.
This also appears to me to be the primary ruling, etc. The Sema and Bach argue that the Rosh followed Rabbeinu Tam and that the matter is therefore doubtful. In my humble opinion, one who pays another’s debt without authorization loses his money, and even if he seized property it is removed from him. This is the primary reading of the Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds and is the view of Rashi, Rif, Rambam, Maggid Mishneh, Migdal Oz, Hagahot Maimoniyot, Ri, Riva, Baal HaIttur, Mordechai, Agudah, Ran, Nimukei Yosef, Ritva, Rabbeinu Yerucham, Raavad, Baal HaTerumot, Ramban, Raavan, Semag, Maharam, Rashba, Or Zarua, Ri’az, the Mechaber, Rama, and many later authorities. The Rosh also appears to agree, as is evident from his responsa and his discussion of a guarantor who paid. Mahari Weil did not clearly rule like Rabbeinu Tam; he merely joined that opinion to a case where the payer already held the debtor’s property. Thus, the ordinary rule is that the payer loses his money, even after seizure. Where he held the debtor’s money at the time of payment, he may reimburse himself. Where the creditor held a pledge and the payer took that pledge, however, the matter remains doubtful. Since Rabbeinu Chananel and Rabbeinu Tam exempt the debtor even without a pledge, and several authorities treat their view as viable, the payer who holds the pledge may say that he relies on them and the pledge is not removed from him.
Some say this applies only when he pays a Jew, etc., and this is the proper ruling. The Rama’s ruling is astonishing. He misunderstood the responsum of Maharil, and it conflicts with many authorities. There is no distinction between a Jewish and a gentile creditor. Maharam, Maharil, She’erit Yosef, Maharik, and other sources show that even where the creditor is a gentile, one who pays a lawful debt without authorization ordinarily loses his money. The decisive distinction is whether the underlying demand was lawful. If the gentile acted through false accusation, confiscation, or robbery, the payment saved the victim from an unlawful loss and must be reimbursed. If the debt was genuinely owed, the ordinary rule of paying another’s debt applies. The argument that the debtor could have found friends to pay for him can apply even to a gentile creditor. The Rashba’s ruling concerning communal officers who borrowed from a gentile likewise implies that, absent partnership or direct compulsion arising from the community, payment would be treated as unauthorized payment of another’s debt. Accordingly, just as I ruled above that payment to a pressing creditor is a doubtful matter, payment to a gentile creditor is at least doubtful, and perhaps even those who impose liability for a pressing Jewish creditor agree that payment of a lawful gentile debt is not recoverable.

But if he held property belonging to the other person, etc. Maharam of Lublin questioned Mahari Weil’s ruling, because the Yerushalmi’s argument that the debtor could have appeased the creditor might seem to apply even where the payer held the debtor’s property. In my view, Mahari Weil is correct. The principal reason for denying reimbursement is that the payer voluntarily drove away a lion from another’s property, and the Sages presume that he acted for the sake of the mitzvah and intended to release the debtor. Where he already holds the debtor’s property, that presumption does not apply, for he plainly intends to reimburse himself. The Hagahot Mordechai and the Rama in Even HaEzer likewise permit one who supports another’s wife while holding the husband’s property to deduct his expenditures. The text there should be read accordingly, despite later emendations. In any event, under Rabbi Natan’s lien, which in my view applies by Torah law even where the intermediate debtor has other assets, the result is even clearer. If the holder wishes to pay the creditor, he may do so from the debtor’s property in his hands, and Mahari Weil’s ruling follows directly from that lien.
Some say that even if he gave his own pledge for another, since he could have given the other’s money or pledge. The Rama derived this from the Hagahot Mordechai, but that source concerns a case where oppressors would otherwise have seized the other person’s property by force. That differs from the present case. One might therefore distinguish between actual payment, which is final, and merely giving one’s own pledge, before payment has occurred. The matter requires study. According to the rule stated above, however, Rabbi Natan’s lien applies even where the debtor has other assets, so the holder may pay the creditor directly even before giving a pledge. This is the primary ruling.
