Collateral. The Shach concludes that where the payer held collateral, the matter is a doubt in law. The burden of proof is upon the claimant, and if the payer has already seized property we do not remove it from him. See there.
To pay. This is not comparable to one who purchases another's promissory note and can collect from the borrower. There the debt is sold by writing and delivery, placing the purchaser in the creditor's position to collect from the debtor. It resembles the Tur's ruling in siman 116 concerning a purchaser without a warranty. Here, however, the case is one who paid another person's debt without first receiving the creditor's rights and authorization. This is unlike Responsa She'erit Yosef 58, which equates the cases. Sema. The Shach writes that the Sema's criticism is unnecessary, because She'erit Yosef agrees with the Sema's rule; in that responsum there was no writing and delivery, as is explicit in the question. See there.
Pressing him. The Shach discusses this law at length and concludes that it remains a doubt in law. If one who paid a pressing creditor has seized property, we do not remove it from him. See there.
I would have appeased him. This reason applies to the earlier rule of one who pays another's debt without his knowledge. With a pressing creditor this reason does not apply. Rather, another reason appears in Tosafot: the debtor might say that he would have found friends to pay on his behalf. This is similar in substance to the former reason, and the Mechaber abbreviated the matter. Shach.
Primary. The Sema writes: The Rama's statement that the first opinion appears primary follows what he wrote in Darkei Moshe, based on the Beit Yosef's argument against the Tur's attribution to the Rosh of Rabbeinu Tam's view. In the Derishah, however, I proved that the Tur is correct and that the Rosh indeed follows Rabbeinu Tam. It therefore appears that the halachah follows them and the other dissenting authorities, especially since Mahari Weil, who was later, ruled likewise. The Bach similarly writes that the Rosh follows Rabbeinu Tam, making this a doubt in law. The burden of proof is upon the claimant, and if the payer seized property we do not remove it from him. The Shach disagrees and concludes that the payer loses his money, as the Mechaber rules, and even if he seized property it is removed from him. This appears to be the primary conclusion of the Bavli, and more than thirty authorities agree. The Yerushalmi is also explicit. Accordingly, the opposing position is nullified. See the Shach at length.
To a Jew. The Shach writes that the Rama's distinction is difficult. He understood Responsa Mahari Weil 82 in a manner the responsum does not support, and the distinction also contradicts many authorities. The law is clear that there is no difference between a Jewish creditor and a non-Jewish creditor. See his proof. He concludes that according to his ruling that a pressing creditor creates a doubt in law, the same applies to a pressing non-Jewish creditor. It is even possible that one who disputes the rule regarding a pressing Jewish creditor agrees that payment to a non-Jewish creditor is lost.
Redeemed. According to what I proved above, that there is no distinction between a Jew and a non-Jew, the same applies where one redeemed another's collateral from a non-Jew. The matter remains a doubt in law. See Responsa Maharashdam 99. Shach.
In his possession. The Sema, citing the Beit Yosef, explains that the payer can say: Had I not paid, beit din would have compelled me to satisfy the debt from the debtor's property in my possession under Rabbi Natan's lien. Certainly, if the debtor's money itself was in his hands, he may say, "What loss did I cause you by voluntarily giving it to the creditor?" The Shach notes that Responsa Maharam Lublin 22 questioned Mahari Weil's ruling, since according to the Yerushalmi's rationale that the debtor could have appeased the creditor, even one who holds the debtor's property should lose what he paid. The Shach answers that Mahari Weil's ruling is correct. Tosafot and the authorities indicate that the principal rationale is that payment is like driving away a lion: even though the payer incurs loss, the Sages presume he acted for the mitzvah and intended to exempt the debtor. Where the debtor's property is already in his hands, however, he presumably does not intend to release him. Moreover, as the Shach proved in siman 86:2, Rabbi Natan's lien is d'oraisa even where the debtor has other assets, although beit din does not compel collection through the third party when other assets are available. If the third party wishes to pay, however, he may do so under Rabbi Natan's lien. Thus this rule is correct independently of the preceding reasoning. Shach.
Collateral. The Rama derived this from Hagahot Mordechai. The Shach writes that it requires study, because there the case concerns one whose own collateral prevented violent persons from taking his fellow's property, unlike the present case. According to what the Shach wrote above, that Rabbi Natan's lien applies even where the debtor has other assets, the distinction is immaterial. Even if the payer did not provide collateral, he has authority to pay the creditor, as stated at the end of siman 105 in the name of Chiddushei Rashba. This is the primary ruling. Shach.
