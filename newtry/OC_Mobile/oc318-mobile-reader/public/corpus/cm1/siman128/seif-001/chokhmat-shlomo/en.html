Seif 1: One who pays his fellow's debt, etc. Note: In Gittin 58b we learn of Giddel bar Ila'i, who accepted land for payment of the royal tax from the people of Bagei, until Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua said that he had placed his money on the horn of a deer. It is surprising that the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh omit this ruling, although it has practical consequences. The Gemara itself also requires explanation. Why should he lose his money? According to those who hold that one who pays a pressing creditor for another must be reimbursed, the difficulty is obvious. Even according to those who exempt in that case, royal taxes are different, for one who pays another's royal tax is certainly entitled to reimbursement. Rashi appears to address this. He explains that once others take the land, the king will collect his share from them and will not credit the first payer. Thus, unlike an ordinary case of paying another's debt, the landholder is not enriched by the payment, because the new holders will again have to pay. Nevertheless, why should those who take the land not be liable under dina de-garmi? Their act is comparable to burning another's promissory note, and indeed to destroying his collateral, because the land served as security for the tax payer's expenditure. It therefore appears that the early authorities omitted the ruling because Rav Huna son of Rav Yehoshua followed the opinion that does not impose liability for dina de-garmi. According to our ruling that dina de-garmi is actionable, this is comparable to one who burns another's note, or a stakeholder who returns the note to the borrower and thereby causes loss. The takers must reimburse his expenditure, after which he returns the land to them. This appears correct to me.
Ibid., seif 1. While traveling I was asked about a nobleman who bought merchandise from a Jew and paid for it, but the seller deceived him by supplying inferior goods. The nobleman did not notice and gave the material to a tailor. He later accused the tailor of having exchanged the merchandise, and the tailor was forced to pay. The tailor then sued the merchant, knowing that the merchant had caused the loss. Some judges wished to exempt the merchant because one person is not seized for another's obligation, while others sought to obligate him by Rabbi Natan's rule. I answered that the exempting view is correct. Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply here. If it did, why do we need the special basis of dina de-malkhuta for one who pays another's tax? The taxpayer owes the king, while the king, having taken improperly, would owe the payer. Likewise, why is one exempt where a settlement was reached with the king? We must say that Rabbi Natan's rule applies only within obligations of Israelites, not where an Israelite is indebted to a non-Jew and the non-Jew is indebted to another Israelite. Although the poskim write that Rabbi Natan's rule can involve a non-Jew, that is where the third party is a non-Jew who owes an Israelite, so that his debt is subject to the first Israelite's lien. Here the nobleman voluntarily paid the money, and the defect lay in the merchandise. In a mistaken sale his claim is for return of the money, which is treated as a loan claim; collection from the Jew through the nobleman's claim would therefore amount to extracting a loan debt from a non-Jewish creditor, and Rabbi Natan's rule does not apply. The merchant is therefore exempt. This is my inclination, stated briefly because I was traveling and without books before me.
