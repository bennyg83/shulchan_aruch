Even if he held a pledge, the one who paid may take it. One should not object from the law of a promissory note, where if the creditor transferred the note by writing and delivery the purchaser could collect. A pledge requires no writing; once the creditor transfers the pledge, the purchaser acquires it, as explained in siman 66, seif 8, and even a later waiver by the creditor is ineffective because transfer of a pledge is stronger than transfer of the note. Why, then, does one who pays the creditor and takes the pledge not automatically step into the creditor's position? The answer is that in siman 66 the purchaser enters with intent to acquire the debt, and the borrower's debt is never extinguished. Here, however, the payer's act discharges and removes the creditor from the debt. He enters only with the expectation of later collecting reimbursement from the borrower. Once he discharges the creditor, the creditor's lien on the pledge is extinguished and the pledge naturally reverts to the borrower. If, before payment, the creditor expressly says, 'Acquire the pledge,' then the payer certainly acquires it. This is no worse than writing and delivery of a note.
Even if the creditor pressed him. See the Shach's lengthy discussion. He concludes that even with a pressing creditor one cannot say that the payer would otherwise have appeased him. He explains the Yerushalmi's discussion of one forbidden by vow from benefiting another who nevertheless brings the other's obligatory bird offerings. The Yerushalmi says this applies where nothing came into the other's hand. The Shach understands that the case of bird offerings resembles a pressing creditor because beit din takes collateral for them. The Yerushalmi's answer is therefore that the offerings had already been brought and were afterward lost from the Kohen's hand, so the owner was no longer personally obligated; this parallels the Gemara's principle that communal funds are separated even for lost offerings. Tumim criticizes the Shach very sharply, arguing that one whose bird offerings are lost or invalid must surely bring others, and even if an offering was brought and then became invalid the owner must replace it. He says the Shach improperly equated obligatory offerings with the half-shekel. Tumim overlooked an explicit Mishnah in Shekalim, chapter 4. Rabbi Shimon says that beit din made seven enactments, including one concerning invalid bird offerings, that replacements come from communal funds. Rabbi Yehudah says the supplier of birds must supply replacements. Rambam and Bartenura explain that if the birds fled or were found invalid, beit din stipulated that replacements be purchased from the chamber and assigned to the owners; according to Rabbi Yehudah, the supplier replaces them. Thus, according to both opinions, once the birds were delivered to the Kohen and then lost or disqualified, beit din no longer takes collateral from the owner. According to the Rabbis communal funds supply new birds, and according to Rabbi Yehudah the supplier does. This is indeed the meaning of 'they separate for the lost,' and the Shach's substantive ruling is correct even though he did not cite this Mishnah.
If he paid a non-Jew. See the Shach, who reasons that since even a pressing Jewish creditor does not create reimbursement liability, there is no reason to distinguish a non-Jew. Tumim writes that because one may evade a non-Jewish loan, the payment is merely removing an external threat. This is not convincing. The debt itself is a true legal obligation just like a debt to a Jew. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 359 expressly prohibit robbing or withholding even the smallest amount from either a Jew or a non-Jew, and Shulchan Aruch defines withholding to include both loans and wages. In Arakhin, chapter Shum HaYetomim, the Gemara discusses a non-Jewish debt where the parties accepted adjudication under Jewish law, again showing that the debt is legally payable. Rashi's statement that evasion of a non-Jewish loan is permitted concerns a case where the debtor says to an heir, 'I already paid your father,' and the heir does not know the facts. Where the debtor knows the debt remains unpaid, it is forbidden to withhold it, even the smallest amount.
