Had the creditor pressed him for payment, the borrower might have appeased him, etc. It appears that the text should read, 'we say that the borrower might have appeased him,' because it is not certain that he could in fact have done so. We merely say that perhaps he could have appeased the creditor, and the burden of proof lies upon one who seeks to extract money. In the Perishah I explained that this differs from one who purchases another person's promissory note and may collect it from the borrower. There the seller transfers the note by writing and delivery and places the purchaser in his own legal position to collect the debt. It is comparable to the rule in siman 116 concerning one who purchased land without warranty and whose field was seized by the seller's creditor: if the creditor had first given the purchaser the promissory note and authorization, the purchaser would stand in the creditor's place to recover from the seller. Here, by contrast, the person merely paid another's debt and did not first receive the creditor's power and authority. This differs from the view that equates the two cases.
And not like those who disagree. The Tur cites those who hold that the Yerushalmi's exemption applies specifically to one who pays the maintenance obligation owed to another man's wife. There is an additional reason for exemption there, because the husband can say: Had you not provided her maintenance, she would have economized and supported herself from her earnings, or gone from door to door. The same applies to one who reimburses a person who fed another man's wife, because the law of the payer follows that of the person who supplied the food. But where one pays an ordinary debt that the borrower was obligated to pay, these authorities require reimbursement. Rama writes that the first view is primary, following what he wrote in Darkei Moshe on the basis of the Beit Yosef's criticism of the Tur's attribution of the contrary view to the Rosh. In the Derishah I demonstrated that the Tur's reading is correct and that the Rosh agrees with Rabbeinu Tam, namely the dissenting view. In my opinion the law follows them, especially since Mahari Weil, a later authority, also ruled this way.
But if he paid a gentile, etc. A gentile is ordinarily appeased only by actual money. The Ir Shushan correctly adds that where gentile merchants customarily compromise with debtors by accepting adequate guarantees and extending payment over long periods, if a Jew voluntarily pays such a gentile merchant on behalf of a Jewish debtor, takes the promissory note, and then seeks reimbursement, the debtor may say: I could have appeased the gentile with guarantees and obtained a long extension. This is a sound distinction.
But if he had property belonging to his fellow in his possession, etc. This may also be learned from siman 105, where the Tur and Mechaber rule that if a custodian delivered the deposit directly to the depositor's creditor, the custodian is exempt. The Beit Yosef explains the reason there: only when the payer now seeks to extract money from the debtor can the debtor say, I could have appeased the creditor. Even if the payer redeemed the debtor's pledge from the creditor, the payer is considered one seeking to extract. But if the payer already holds property belonging to the debtor, he may say: Had I not paid the creditor, beit din would have ordered me to satisfy him from the property of yours that I hold, under Rabbi Natan's rule. This is all the more so if the payer already held the debtor's money. What loss did he cause by voluntarily transferring it to the creditor?
