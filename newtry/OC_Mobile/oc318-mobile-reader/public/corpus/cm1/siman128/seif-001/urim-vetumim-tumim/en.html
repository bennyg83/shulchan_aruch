He appeases the creditor and the creditor forgives him. The Shach in seif katan 3 argues at length that because the Rif, Rambam, and Semag omit the Yerushalmi's distinction concerning a pressing creditor, it is strained to assume they accepted that distinction. In his view, the Yerushalmi's proof comes from the Mishnah that one may pay another person's shekel. According to our Gemara, however, that case provides no proof, because it can concern a person who already sent his shekel through a paid guardian and it was lost. In that circumstance he is exempt, as the Rambam rules in Hilchot Shekalim, and no further collection is made because terumah is taken even for lost shekalim. After all his lengthy discussion, however, his words do not stand. If he were correct, we would have to say that the Mishnah's statement "he may pay his shekel" applies only where the obligated person had already sent his shekel through a paid guardian and it was lost. How then could the Rambam write without qualification, "One who has vowed not to benefit his fellow may pay his shekel"? He should have explained that this is permitted only where the other had already sent the shekel and it was lost. Otherwise the payer would be causing the other a direct Torah-law benefit by preventing collection. The same issue applies to the Semag, who likewise states the rule without qualification. Even regarding the separate rule that one may pay another person's debt, their approach would require a distinction that the ordinary case does not involve a pressing creditor. That omission is less difficult, because an ordinary creditor may not always be pressing at that moment. But the omission concerning shekalim is decisive. The unqualified wording implies that one may pay immediately, whereas on the Shach's reading it would be forbidden except in the uncommon case where the obligated person already paid through a paid guardian, the shekel was lost, terumah was already taken on his behalf, and only then did the other person contribute a replacement. Without all those facts he would still be subject to collection. The Gidulei Terumah misunderstood Rashi in Ketubot as saying that since terumah is taken for lost shekalim, even if the person never sent his shekel there is only a mitzvah obligation remaining and no enforceable payment. At first glance Rashi's language may sound that way, but that cannot be his intent. If the treasurer would still seize property from him, then when the other person pays he clearly saves him from actual collection, which is no less than paying another's debt. Rashi himself in Nedarim explains that the case is where the person sent his shekel and it was stolen or lost after the terumah was taken. The Gidulei Terumah overlooked that Rashi. There is an additional point. Even if the initial stage of the Gemara interprets the case as after terumah was taken, once the conclusion establishes the general rule that paying another's debt is permitted according to Chanan, there is no reason to retain the special lost-shekel scenario. Otherwise the separate clause "he may pay his shekel" would be unnecessary. If one may pay an actual enforceable debt, certainly one may replace a shekel where the original payer is no longer collectible because terumah was already taken for the lost coin. We must therefore say that in the conclusion the Mishnah's shekel case concerns an ordinary current obligation. It teaches that even a pressing obligation can be paid.
The Rashba in his chiddushim to Nedarim writes expressly that in the conclusion the shekel clause is no longer limited to a lost shekel, although he does not state this proof. In my view the redundancy makes the point compelling. In truth the reason the Rif, Rambam, and Semag omit the Yerushalmi's distinction becomes clear from Tosafot in Ketubot in this sugya, beginning "Whose view is this? Chanan's." Rabbenu Tam understands our Gemara to disagree with the Yerushalmi. Tosafot write that they must disagree in any event. The Yerushalmi establishes the shekel clause even according to the sons of the Kohanim Gedolim, reasoning that a wife is different, despite the fact that shekalim are subject to forced collection. Rav Oshaya, by contrast, establishes the Mishnah according to Chanan. Moreover, according to Rava, who establishes payment of another's debt as a loan made on condition that it not be collected, the shekel case must be a lost and already accounted-for shekel, while the Yerushalmi implies that it concerns an ordinary obligation. We should examine why Tosafot prove disagreement specifically from the shekel clause. They could seemingly have said more directly that according to Rav Oshaya the rule "he may pay his debt" follows Chanan, whereas the Yerushalmi allows it even according to the Sages; and according to Rava, the debt clause itself applies only where the loan was made on condition that the lender cannot force payment, whereas the Yerushalmi speaks of an ordinary debt. Why did Tosafot instead focus on the shekel? It appears clear that Tosafot do not understand the Yerushalmi's distinction between a "pressing" and "non-pressing" creditor as the ordinary practical distinction that Ri suggested, namely whether this particular creditor is presently demanding payment. An ordinary creditor is presumed eventually to press for payment. If he does not demand today, he may demand tomorrow. All creditors are pressing in the sense that they retain the legal power to compel payment, some quickly and some more patiently, but the debt ultimately remains enforceable. Rather, Tosafot understand the Yerushalmi and our Gemara as addressing the same distinction. Rava's case of "a loan made on condition not to collect," as Rashi explains, means that the creditor agreed not to compel payment until the debtor voluntarily wishes to pay. The Ritva likewise explains that the essential point is that the creditor lacks power to force him. This is what the Yerushalmi calls "a creditor who does not press," meaning one who has no legal ability to press because the loan was made on such a condition. An ordinary creditor, who has the legal right to demand payment whenever due, is called "pressing," because that legal power exists and can eventually be used. The Yerushalmi therefore first says that the rule "he may pay his debt" can be understood as a creditor who is not pressing, exactly Rava's case. It then asks: we have found the rule for a non-pressing creditor, but from where do we know it for a pressing creditor, meaning an ordinary enforceable debt? It proves this from the shekel clause, where there is no contractual condition and forced collection applies. Tosafot, wishing to show that the Yerushalmi disagrees with both Rav Oshaya and Rava, could not prove it from the debt clause itself, because the Yerushalmi can establish that clause as a non-pressing creditor in precisely Rava's sense. Rav Oshaya too would agree that even the Sages permit payment of such a debt under that condition. Tosafot therefore bring proof from the shekel clause. The Yerushalmi expressly treats shekalim as the paradigm of a pressing obligation and still permits payment even according to the Sages. This disagrees with both Rav Oshaya and Rava, for according to either of them the Sages could permit the shekel only in the special lost-shekel situation described above. Tosafot's language is exact and leaves no doubt. Once this is understood, the poskim acted properly in omitting the Yerushalmi's separate formulation. The Rif explicitly rules according to Chanan from our Gemara: even without a condition barring collection, one may pay another person's debt. That is precisely what the Yerushalmi calls a pressing creditor. The Rambam and Semag rule the same. The Yerushalmi's distinction therefore adds no separate practical category after we follow Chanan. In my view this is unquestionably the intent of Tosafot, and it also explains the Rif and Rambam. There is no difficulty. The Shach attempts to support his position from the Yerushalmi in Nedarim, which omits the shekel proof and instead brings proof from the rule that one may bring another person's bird offerings for a zav or similar obligation. The Yerushalmi says, "This is where nothing enters his hand," which the Shach interprets to mean that the offerings had already entered the Kohen's hand, were then lost, and the owner no longer remained liable, analogous to a lost shekel. With due respect to the Shach's great Torah stature, this interpretation is not correct. Apart from the fact that the wording "where nothing entered his hand" cannot bear the meaning "where something had entered his hand," requiring the text effectively to be reversed, the underlying law is wrong. We have never heard that if a zav's bird offerings were designated and delivered to a Kohen and were then lost or invalidated, the owner is exempt from bringing replacements. Even if a korban was actually offered and became invalid, or a chatat was offered with an invalid intent, the owner must bring another. Until a valid korban is offered, his obligation remains. The proper explanation of the Yerushalmi is simple. It proves that even a pressing debt can be paid from the rule that one may bring another person's obligatory bird offerings. That obligation cannot simply be forgiven, so it is plainly "pressing." Why is payment nevertheless permitted? Because nothing tangible enters the prohibited beneficiary's hand. The payer does not give him an item. He merely spends his own money in a way that saves the other person an expense. The beneficiary receives no tangible property from his fellow. It is therefore only a case of driving away a lion, a relinquishment of an indirect benefit, and is permitted. The same applies to paying an enforceable debt. This is the meaning of the phrase "where nothing entered his hand." It refers to the absence of tangible receipt by the beneficiary, not to the prior delivery and loss of offerings. See the Rashba to Nedarim, whose explanation plainly follows this understanding. The Yerushalmi sometimes states the matter briefly and elsewhere at length, but both the Ketubot and Nedarim passages lead to the same conclusion: even a pressing creditor may be paid. This is the position of the Rif, Rambam, and the great majority of authorities. This is precise.
But if he paid a non-Jew, he is liable. This is according to all opinions. The Shach in seif katan 6 challenges this at length, arguing that no such distinction appears among the poskim. Since we rule that even payment of a pressing creditor does not create reimbursement liability, why should paying a non-Jewish creditor be different? His objection is strong, and I have little basis to defend the Rama except as follows. Ri, Maharam, and those who follow them agree that the rule exempting one who pays another person's debt without authorization applies where the debtor was legally obligated to pay. But where the debtor was legally exempt and the third party nevertheless pays, the payer has suffered a loss caused by the debtor and may recover. Tosafot say similarly regarding a shepherd who hires other shepherds up to the value of the protected property. Thus, if under law the debtor owed nothing, payment on his behalf can create liability even where the amount paid equals the alleged debt. Accordingly, because we rule that a Jew may retain a loan owed to a non-Jew where such retention is legally permitted, the non-Jew's demand for payment can be viewed, in that limited halachic respect, as an external claim rather than an enforceable Jewish-law debt. The Jew is permitted not to pay. If another Jew then pays the non-Jew and thereby removes that threatened claim, he has not merely paid an enforceable debt but has protected the debtor from a loss he was not legally required to bear. In such a case all can agree that the payer may recover, even if the amount equals the original loan, as Tosafot in chapter HaKones explain. This is especially plausible because a Jew naturally prefers to owe another Jew rather than remain exposed to a non-Jew's demand. This approach also explains why the Shach's numerous proofs do not necessarily contradict the Rama. Most of the responsa he cites concern cases where the non-Jew already held Jewish property or possessed Jewish collateral. In those circumstances one cannot simply invoke the permissibility of withholding an unsecured loan. Likewise, where a Jew holds a deposit belonging to a non-Jew, perhaps the concept applies only to an unpaid loan and not to a deposit at all, as I have discussed elsewhere at length. But with an unsecured loan where Jewish law permits retaining payment, the non-Jew's demand may resemble an external claim, and one who pays to remove it may therefore recover. This is the best defense of the Rama. The practical law nevertheless requires study. This is precise.
But if he had property of the other in his hand, etc. And he used it to pay the other's debt, what he did is effective. The Shach in seif katan 8 writes that some challenge this rule. He concludes that where the original debtor has no other assets, everyone agrees the holder is exempt because Rabbi Natan's lien directly subjects the property in his hands. The dispute concerns a debtor who has other assets. If that is the Shach's distinction, I do not understand the proof he brings from Hagahot Mordechai at the end of Ketubot, concerning Reuven who seized property belonging to his son-in-law Shimon because Reuven was supporting his daughter, Shimon's wife. The Mordechai rules that because Reuven actually seized property, this is not included in the ordinary rule that one who pays another's obligation without authorization has placed his money on the deer's horn. The reason stated there is Rabbi Natan's lien. That case necessarily concerns a debtor without other collectible assets, or else follows those poskim who apply Rabbi Natan's lien even when the first debtor has other property, as discussed above in siman 86. But our present dispute, according to the Shach, specifically concerns a debtor who does have other assets and where we therefore ordinarily do not apply Rabbi Natan's lien. The Mordechai would then prove nothing. At first glance Tosafot in Ketubot and Bava Kamma, chapter HaKones, appear to prove the opposite. They ask why a finder of lost property can receive payment like a worker, why a guardian who advances money for additional shepherds or staffs can recover, and why one who abandons his own donkey to save another's donkey receives compensation. They struggle to distinguish these cases from ordinary payment of another's debt. If mere possession of the other's property eliminated the "driving away a lion" rule, there would be no question in cases where the payer held the other's property. It is strained to distinguish between holding the other's money and holding his movable objects on the ground that movable objects remain in the owner's domain. Our present ruling and the Hagahot Mordechai appear to include both loans and deposits. The words of Mahari Weil can be reconciled with Tosafot by retaining Rabbi Natan's lien as the basis, even where the debtor has other assets. The reason Rabbi Natan's lien is ordinarily not enforced when the first debtor has other assets is analogous to the rule that one does not collect from encumbered property where free property is available. That protection is for the benefit of the intermediate debtor. He can waive it and say, "I do not wish to avail myself of the Sages' protection," choosing instead to pay the ultimate creditor directly. Once he does so, Rabbi Natan's lien can attach to the property he holds. One might ask from Bava Kamma, where a depositary who hands over a tam ox to the injured party can invoke Rabbi Natan's lien only when the tortfeasor has no other assets. Why can the depositary not say there too, "I decline the Sages' protection"? The answer follows Rivah in Mordechai, chapter Shnei Dayanei, who writes that a person cannot reject a rabbinic protection where doing so would cause loss to another. In the ox case the tortfeasor can say, "Had you returned the ox to me, I could have driven it into the marsh," thereby preventing collection. Handing it to the injured party creates a concrete loss to the owner. The depositary cannot waive the owner's protection in a way that harms him. Here, by contrast, paying an actual debt does not constitute such loss, as Tosafot in Bava Kamma and Ketubot explain. The holder can therefore say, "I do not wish to use the Sages' rule directing collection first from the debtor's other assets." He pays the ultimate creditor under Rabbi Natan's lien. Consequently, Hagahot Mordechai states the rule simply under Rabbi Natan without distinguishing whether the debtor has other assets. In either case the holder can choose to apply Rabbi Natan's lien where no one else is harmed. Tosafot's questions from lost property, guardianship expenditures, and saving another's donkey likewise remain, because those cases do not involve Rabbi Natan's lien at all. See siman 105, where I discussed this and where the Ramban's position makes the law explicit. Also see the Rosh's responsum siman 76. The Rosh initially considered saying that even where a person holds his fellow's property he remains liable if he pays the other's debt, but ultimately withdrew that position because of the law stated above in siman 105. He too did not focus on whether the debtor had other property, because Rabbi Natan's lien can operate through the holder's choice as explained.
Since, had he wished, he could have given his fellow's property, etc. The Shach in seif katan 9 asks that the Rama derives this rule from Hagahot Mordechai cited above at the end of siman 126, but there the coercers themselves had power to seize the fellow's collateral. The cases therefore appear different. In my view the Rama did not intend to equate the factual forms of coercion. Above, the person was actually forced to surrender property; here there is no coercion. Rather, the Rama derives only the underlying logic from the Hagahot. Hagahot Mordechai rules that where coercers come specifically to take another person's property but instead take the guardian's own property, the owner must reimburse the guardian. The reason is that the guardian was legally permitted to give the other person's property when the coercers came to seize that very property. Since he had the right at that moment to substitute the owner's property for his own, the owner must repay what the guardian lost. The Rama applies the same principle here. Had the person paid the creditor using the debtor's collateral already in his possession, he would have been exempt. Instead he paid using his own money. As long as he still holds the debtor's property, he retains the practical choice to substitute that property and recover his own payment. Therefore the debtor must either reimburse him or allow the substitution. Although the factual circumstances differ, the legal logic is the same. There is no difficulty.
