Seif 1. (He had a pledge. See Shach, seif katan 1.) Although one who pays another person's debt without authorization is ordinarily not reimbursed, the rule is different when the payer protects or releases property of the debtor that is being held as security. This has a practical application to brokerage fees. Sometimes the broker waits to receive his fee until the buyer comes to collect the merchandise. If the buyer sells the merchandise onward and the broker lawfully detains it until his brokerage fee is paid, the obligation to satisfy that fee remains upon the original buyer whose transaction generated it. The brokerage debt does not ordinarily shift to the subbuyer merely because he purchased the merchandise with the expectation of collecting it from the broker's location. Even if he could have anticipated that the broker might still be waiting for payment, the seller must disclose such an obligation if he wishes to impose it on the buyer. The same applies to what merchants call a contractual commission or similar commercial charge. Even if it is sometimes deferred until delivery of the merchandise, unless the seller stipulated otherwise it does not automatically become the obligation of the subbuyer. This is especially so where the usual practice is to pay such a fee immediately upon completion of the transaction and documentation rather than at final delivery. A sufficiently established majority practice can be decisive in defining the ordinary terms of a commercial transaction.
