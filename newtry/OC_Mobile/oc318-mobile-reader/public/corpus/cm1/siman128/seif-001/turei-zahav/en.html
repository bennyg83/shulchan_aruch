(Seif 2, gloss: Because he was compelled to compromise). Sema, seif katan 7, explains that the demand was merely a fabricated gentile claim, which would imply the same law even after a compromise was already concluded. It appears otherwise from Mordechai's gloss, which says that the teacher left and had not compromised with the gentile. This implies that only before a compromise was reached may the employer not withhold the teacher's money. Once a definite compromise creates an actual debt, ordinary seizure is effective. Mordechai's reason is that if the employer has a claim against the teacher, he should sue him and recover through beit din, since a person's assets stand as guarantors and one does not pursue the guarantor before the debtor. This shows that the principal concern is that the teacher may never become liable at all. A lien on assets does not arise until the debtor's liability has been established, and many events may prevent any compromise. Once a definite compromise was made, however, the assets immediately became subject to the obligation. Nor is this merely indirect damage, for the employer effectively became the teacher's guarantor under the known gentile practice that the householder must produce the teacher whom he maintained in his home. He may therefore seize property once the debt created by the compromise is certain. Rama's phrase “he was compelled to compromise” means that he faced the need to compromise, but had not yet done so. According to Sema's explanation that it was only a fabricated claim, why did Mordechai need the rule that assets are guarantors? It would suffice that the claim was baseless. And what would it mean to say, “if he has a claim, let him sue,” when the entire matter was only a gentile fabrication? The explanation I have given is therefore correct.
