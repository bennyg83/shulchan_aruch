A pledge. In truth, if a pledge was in his possession, the matter is a doubt of law. The burden of proof is on the claimant, and if the payer seized property, beit din does not remove it from him. See Shach.
To pay. This is unlike one who sells a promissory note to another, where the purchaser can collect from the borrower. There, the debt was sold by writing and delivery, and the purchaser of the note stands in the original creditor's place to collect from that creditor's debtor. Here, however, the case is one who paid another person's debt without first receiving authority from the creditor. See Sema. The Shach agrees with the Sema and understands She'erit Yosef similarly, contrary to the Sema's assumption that She'erit Yosef held otherwise.
Even if the creditor was pressing him, etc. The Shach, seif katan 3 and 5, discusses this at length and holds that most authorities disagree, maintaining that where the creditor is pressing the debtor, the person who paid is entitled to reimbursement. The Shach argues that the Rif, Rambam, and Semag cite the Yerushalmi in Ketubot as law but omit its rule regarding a pressing creditor, showing that they do not accept the exemption in that case. He therefore concludes that at minimum the case of a pressing creditor is a doubt of law and the burden of proof is on the claimant. See Tumim, where I wrote that although Gidulei Terumah agrees with this, my heart does not incline to it. According to their approach, the Rambam's ruling in Hilchot Shekalim, that one who vowed not to benefit from another may pay that person's shekel, should have distinguished between a shekel already sent and lost and one not yet sent. I also raised a difficulty on Gidulei Terumah's proof from Rashi in Ketubot, overlooking Rashi in Nedarim. I further discussed Tosafot at length, offered a satisfactory explanation of the Yerushalmi, and gave a sound reason why the Rif and Rambam omitted that Yerushalmi passage. See there. The Shach also maintains that the Yerushalmi itself agrees that with a pressing creditor the payer must be reimbursed, bringing proof from the Yerushalmi in Nedarim. See Tumim, where I showed that his words are difficult and that his proposed emendation of the text was unnecessary and still does not solve the issue. In my opinion the Yerushalmi is clear as I explained it, and the Rashba in Nedarim also somewhat supports my interpretation. It is therefore clear that the Rif, Rambam, Semag, and the majority of authorities agree with the Mechaber that even where the creditor is pressing, the payer loses his money. One should not depart from the Mechaber's ruling. See Tumim at length for the reasoning.
Would appease. The Shach writes that this reason applies to the earlier case of one who pays another's debt without his knowledge. With a pressing creditor, this particular reason does not fit; rather, the reason is like Tosafot's statement that the debtor might have found friends who would have paid on his behalf. This is similar in substance, and the Mechaber abbreviated. In truth, according to my interpretation in Tumim, seif katan 1, of a nonpressing and pressing creditor, and according to the Rif, Rambam, Semag, and Yerushalmi, the Mechaber makes no distinction at all between them. On that reading, the Mechaber's reason can still be that the debtor might have appeased the creditor. Nevertheless, the Shach's formulation is preferable.
The primary view. The Sema writes that the Rama's statement that the first view is primary follows the Beit Yosef's argument against the Tur's attribution of Rabbeinu Tam's position to the Rosh. The Sema writes that in the Derishah he proved the Tur correct, that the Rosh does agree with Rabbeinu Tam, and therefore he considers their view the practical halachah, especially since Mahari Weil, a later authority, also ruled that way. The Bach likewise holds that the Rosh agrees with Rabbeinu Tam, and therefore treats the matter as a doubt of law, with the burden of proof on the claimant; if the payer seized property, it is not removed from him. The Shach, seif katan 5, argues at length that the Mechaber is correct, the payer loses his money, and even seized property is removed from him, because the Yerushalmi explicitly rules so and most authorities agree. He rejects the Sema's contention that the Rosh and Mahari Weil agree with Rabbeinu Tam, and adds that even if they did, they would be isolated against the majority. The Shach's conclusion is correct. The majority of authorities agree with the Mechaber, and as I wrote above, in my view this applies even to a pressing creditor, all the more so to a nonpressing creditor.
But if he paid a gentile, etc. The Shach, seif katan 6, argues at great length against the Rama and concludes that the Rama misunderstood Mahari Weil. In addition, nearly all responsa authorities make no distinction between a gentile and a Jew in this law. The Shach also asks why this should be better than a pressing creditor, and concludes that even if a pressing creditor is a doubt of law, here all agree that the payer loses his money, contrary to the Rama. In truth, according to what I concluded above, that all authorities clearly hold that even with a pressing creditor the payer loses his money, the same would appear to follow here. Nevertheless, see Tumim, seif katan 2, where I wrote that we are obligated to seek a sound basis for the Rama. Since withholding repayment of a gentile's loan can under certain circumstances be permitted, this can resemble saving another from an unjust exaction. In that setting reimbursement can be due, like a shepherd who hires additional shepherds up to the value of the flock. By contrast, with a pledge or deposit this is not within the category of withholding a gentile's loan, and it is obvious that the payer loses his money. The various contradictions cited by the Shach all concern a deposit or pledge. Even so, the distinction is strained and the law requires study.
All the more so if he redeemed his pledge. The Shach writes that according to what he said above, that there is no distinction between a Jew and a gentile regarding one who pays another's debt, the same is true concerning a pledge. Just as the law is doubtful regarding a pledge held by a Jew, so too with a gentile. See Shach. This is clearly a doubt of law.
And paid his debt, what he did, etc. The Beit Yosef writes that the payer can say: had I not paid the creditor, beit din would have ordered me to pay him from what I hold under the rule of Rabbi Natan. All the more so if I actually held your money. What loss did I cause you by paying him on my own? This ruling originates in Mahari Weil. See Responsa Maharam Lublin, siman 82, who disputes Mahari Weil and writes that his ruling requires study, because the Yerushalmi's reason that the debtor could have appeased the creditor implies exemption even where the payer holds the debtor's money. See Shach, seif katan 8, who rules that Mahari Weil's law is correct and consistent with Tosafot and the authorities, because the principal reason is that the payer merely removed a threat. Although the debtor thereby benefits, the Sages assessed that the payer acted for a mitzvah and intended to exempt him from reimbursement. But when he already holds the debtor's property, presumably he does not intend to waive reimbursement. The Shach further writes that according to what was explained above in siman 86, seif 2, the lien of Rabbi Natan applies by Torah law even where the debtor has other property, although beit din does not compel payment from the third party if the debtor has assets. If the third party wishes to pay, however, he may do so under Rabbi Natan's lien, so Mahari Weil's rule is correct without further reasoning. See Tumim, where I discussed this at length. At first glance Tosafot in Ketubot and Bava Kamma appear contrary to Mahari Weil, because they ask from several cases where one saves another's property and receives reimbursement, even though according to Mahari Weil there would be no difficulty, and they give strained answers. I concluded that Mahari Weil does not disagree with Tosafot. His rule rests on Rabbi Natan's lien even where the debtor has other assets. I also explained that the cases raised by Tosafot differ because Rabbi Natan's lien does not apply there. See there for the reasoning. See also siman 105, where I discussed this at length, and Responsa Rosh, klal 76.
A pledge. The Shach writes that the Darchei Moshe derived this from Hagahot Mordechai. In my opinion it requires study, because there the case is that if the payer had not given his own pledge, the coercers would have seized his friend's property by force, as explained above at the end of siman 126. That is unlike the case here. See the cited siman, where I discussed this at length. The Shach further writes that according to what he explained immediately above, that Rabbi Natan's lien applies even where the debtor has other property, there is no practical distinction, and even without giving his own pledge he has the right to pay the creditor. See Shach and what I wrote in the preceding seif katan.
