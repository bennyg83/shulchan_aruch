In the case of a non Jew, a transfer without writing and delivery can have the same effect that a transfer to a Jew has with writing and delivery. The reason is that one who obligates himself to a non Jew accepts the legal rules governing transactions with him, as Tumim writes in siman 104 in the name of the Rashba. If their law makes the borrower liable to whomever the creditor hands the note, even without formal writing and delivery, it is comparable to an obligation expressly payable to the bearer.

If he paid the non Jew. See Shach, seif katan 4. One who pays another person's valid debt is considered merely to have driven away a lion and is not reimbursed, because the debtor was already legally obligated to pay that creditor, and the payer therefore did not create or avert a loss that the debtor was not already bound to bear, as Rashi explains. Here, however, where one pays an unlawful demand made by a non Jewish official, he has saved the other person from a loss that the latter was not legally obligated to bear, and reimbursement is therefore due. Although this initially appears reversed, the explanation is that Bava Kamma 81 records among Yehoshua's conditions the rule concerning one who pours out his wine to save another's honey. Saving another from loss is part of hashavat aveidah, and everyone is obligated to do so. Consequently, when a person spends his own money to prevent another's loss, it is as though he did so with the owner's consent, meaning with the consent imposed by the Torah, as can be understood from Rashi in Bava Kamma 21. Paying another person's ordinary debt is different, because there is no general obligation to discharge another's debts without authorization. Thus, even where one saves another from a false claim or other loss, reimbursement is due only when the owner was absent. If the owner was present, the rescuer should have stipulated for reimbursement, as stated in siman 264, seif 3. Likewise, an unpaid custodian is expected to obtain shepherds and staffs when necessary to save entrusted property, but his failure to spend money does not thereby become negligence. The true rule is that a duty to save at another's expense can exist even though failure to advance one's own money does not make the custodian negligent. There is also a textual error in the Shach's continuation. The repeated expression implying that the non Jew cannot be appeased by a particular kind of "silver appeasement" should simply read "money."
He had property of his fellow in his possession. Tumim asks from Tosafot in Bava Kamma 58 concerning a custodian who pays for shepherds and staffs, although he already possesses the other person's property. There is no difficulty. Hagahot Maimoniyot writes that if a payer discharged another person's debt using that person's collateral, he is exempt, whereas if he used his own money he may still exchange what was paid by giving the creditor the debtor's property and taking back his own. Thus, either the debtor reimburses him or he can effect that exchange. This reasoning applies only to payment of a debt. In the case of driving away a lion, he could not have saved the property by simply transferring the owner's property to the threatening party; he had to spend his own funds. The Sema's explanation based on Rabbi Natan's lien likewise does not apply to the case of driving away a lion.
