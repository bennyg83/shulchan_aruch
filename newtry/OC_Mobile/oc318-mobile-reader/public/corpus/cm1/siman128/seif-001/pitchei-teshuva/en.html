To pay him. See Baer Heitev. Responsa Beit Ephraim, Choshen Mishpat, siman 16, discusses one who owed a nobleman a fixed sum. The nobleman attached grain belonging jointly to the debtor and a partner until the partner was forced to give the nobleman a debt document for the amount. The nobleman then released the original debtor in writing and declared that he would have no further claim against him, but only against the partner who had taken his place. When payment became due, the partner warned the original debtor not to pay, because the debtor had already been released and the debt now rested on the partner, who in turn held a claim against the nobleman and intended to offset the two debts. The original debtor ignored him and paid the nobleman. The partner then argued: you paid my debt without my consent; I had a valid claim against the nobleman and would have offset it, and now perhaps I will never collect that claim. The partner was in possession of other partnership money, and the judges disagreed whether he could retain it. Beit Ephraim rules that if the nobleman's written release fully discharged the original debtor so that the nobleman could no longer return to him, then the original obligation had effectively been paid and extinguished as to him. The partner's subsequent obligation to the nobleman is like a new loan. If the original debtor voluntarily paid it, he has the law of one who pays another person's debt without authorization, and the partner need not reimburse him. Even according to those who hold that this rule does not ordinarily apply where the creditor is a non Jew, that is where the debtor merely claims that he could have obtained a release through persuasion, while experience shows that the non Jew would not release him without payment. Here, by contrast, the partner asserts an actual monetary claim against the nobleman with which he could offset the debt. The suggestion to compare this to siman 176, seif 47, is incorrect. There the reason is that one partner need not antagonize the government official from whom Reuven's debt could be collected. Here, even if the original debtor could prove that the nobleman would have harmed him had he refused payment, that would amount to an extortionary threat, and one person may not save himself from financial harm by using another's money. Certainly, if it is clear that the partner held a liquid claim equal to the debt, there was no outstanding debt to pay; the original debtor simply gave away his own money. Even if the matter is uncertain, the partner's assertion that he could have offset the claim is a valid defense. Beit Ephraim adds that even if the original debtor received the partner's debt document from the nobleman upon payment, that does not necessarily help him. According to She'erit Yosef, one who pays another's debt without authority remains an unreimbursed volunteer even if he receives the debt document, unless the document was first transferred to him as a purchase, with the creditor conveying his rights. If the document was handed over only as a receipt for payment, once the nobleman received the money the original debt was extinguished. All the more so here, where the partner claims that the document was already satisfied through setoff. Even in a Jewish debt, a purchaser of a note cannot collect when the debtor claims payment unless the seller swears; a non Jewish creditor cannot provide that oath. The volunteer therefore placed his money at risk and has no claim against the partner. In siman 19 Beit Ephraim discusses a second case. A buyer purchased a house subject to a nobleman's lien, and the sale agreement allowed the buyer to retain part of the price until the lien was removed. The seller died, and the buyer later purchased the lien from the nobleman for a certain sum and retained that amount from the heirs. The heirs argued that they could have settled the nobleman's claim for less. Beit Ephraim rules for the buyer. The sale agreement expressly allowed him to retain the money until the nobleman was removed, and the nobleman was in fact removed only through the sum the buyer paid. The heirs cannot recover merely by asserting that the nobleman might have accepted less when they cannot prove that he would have done so. Nor is this like one who pays another's debt, because the buyer acquired the lien as part of the transaction. See there.
