Seif 1. That he wrote to him in a document. Siman 40 teaches that where a person creates a new obligation by writing it in a document, the obligation does not take effect merely through writing. The document must be delivered to the person in whose favor the obligation is created. This is also the language of the Shach and Urim VeTumim. Delivery to a non-Jew does not itself make the obligation effective. Even delivery to a Jew is insufficient unless the obligor states that he is delivering it to that person in order to transfer and acquire it on behalf of the beneficiary so that the obligation will take effect. Some expressions used in non-Jewish commercial language signify a binding obligation even though the corresponding wording among Jews does not. I have heard, for example, that a particular term meaning "engagement" or "commitment" functions as an obligation in their legal usage. Still, even if the instrument is written in their language and script, where a Jew signs it one must determine whether Jewish legal usage regards that expression as creating a personal obligation. I have written elsewhere that the customary handshake or handclasp treated as situmta may not necessarily have the power to create a new personal debt. A kinyan sudar is ordinarily connected with preparation of a written instrument, and can therefore function like the formal statement "I owe you" in a document, which creates a new obligation through admission. Situmta, by contrast, is a commercial act whose ordinary function is to acquire property, not to create a written personal debt. Unless the local custom specifically uses it to assume obligations, it may lack that power. Where the custom is only to use the act for acquisition and no practice of creating personal liabilities through it is known, it appears clear that situmta does not create such a debt. The matter nevertheless requires further study.
