Be witnesses for me. At the beginning of Perek HaNosei the Gemara records a dispute concerning one who says to another, "I owe you a maneh." Rabbi Yochanan obligates and Reish Lakish exempts. The Gemara ultimately establishes the case as one who says, "I owe you a maneh in a document." Rashi explains this as an existing admission written and delivered before witnesses. Rabbeinu Tam explains it as a new undertaking created through a full written instrument. Rif and Rambam interpret it differently: he says orally, "I owe you a maneh in a document," and the very formulation creates liability even though both parties and the witnesses know that no prior debt existed. The later authorities ask how mere words can create such an obligation without kinyan, document, or consideration. The correct explanation is that Rambam relies on acquisition through formal admission. A person's legal admission can itself transfer property or create rights even where both sides know that the factual content of the admission was previously false. Tosafot explicitly recognizes acquisition by admission in the case of Issur the convert, and such admission operates for a healthy person as well as one dying. Thus Rambam does not hold that arbitrary words alone create obligations. The operative mechanism is a formal admission, which is itself a valid mode of acquisition. This also explains why the Gemara compares the phrase "in a document" to saying, "You are my witnesses." A formal admission requires the legal setting that gives the statement testimonial force. One must know, however, that this mode of acquisition by admission is not completed in private. Ordinary modes of acquisition do not require witnesses except to prevent later denial, because the acquisition itself occurs independently. Admission is different. Its very legal force depends upon a formal witnessed admission. If he says it privately, or even before witnesses without establishing them as witnesses where the circumstances require that, there is no acquisition by admission. This resolves Tosafot's question in Bava Metzia concerning one standing at his granary who transfers produce and then redeems second tithe on money in his house. Tosafot asks why he does not transfer the money through admission, as Issur did. Since the Gemara's case can be one without witnesses, there is no difficulty. An ordinary sudar kinyan could still work privately because witnesses are needed only to prove it. Admission acquisition cannot. This distinction also answers later writers who assumed that admission is merely another private mode of acquisition. It is not. Its formal testimonial character is part of the acquisition itself.
