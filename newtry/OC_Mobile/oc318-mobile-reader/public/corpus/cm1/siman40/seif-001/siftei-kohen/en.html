One who obligates himself, etc. This means that he knows he does not presently owe the other person anything and voluntarily creates a new obligation, like a gift. But if one admits to his fellow under the assumption that he already owes him, and afterward it becomes known that he was mistaken, whether the mistake is established by witnesses or the supposed creditor admits that nothing was owed, this is an admission made in error and is entirely ineffective. This is so even if he delivered a document before witnesses and witnesses signed it, as the Tur writes. The rule is straightforward from below, siman 126, seif 3, and many other places: every kinyan made in error is reversed.
How? One who says to witnesses, etc. The Sema writes that many authorities disagree with this and hold that a person who seeks to create a new obligation can do so only if he writes it in a document, or if he says, "I owe you one hundred by document," etc. The Sema was not precise. He should have written that those authorities hold that even saying, "I owe you one hundred by document," is ineffective, and that only an actual written document works. Maggid Mishneh, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah, explicitly says so in the name of Ramban, Rashba, and other commentators. He concludes that according to this approach there is no way for the person to become obligated except by actually writing a document. They therefore disagree with the Mechaber in two of his three categories. Ran at the beginning of chapter HaNose likewise writes according to this approach that words alone cannot create a new monetary obligation; there must be receipt of money, a kinyan, or a document. According to Rashi, no form of present self obligation without a kinyan is effective, as Beit Yosef explains Rashi. Shiltei Giborim at the beginning of chapter HaNose similarly rules in the name of Riaz that one who seeks to obligate himself in something he does not already owe, saying, "I hereby obligate myself to give you one hundred," does not become liable merely by saying "You are my witnesses," and even a signed document delivered to the recipient does not create the obligation unless a kinyan is made, according to Beit Hillel. See Ritva at the beginning of chapter HaNose and see below, seif katan 5.
One who says to witnesses, "Be witnesses for me that I owe so and so one hundred," etc. In this first category, and in the third category where he says before witnesses, "I hereby owe you one hundred by document," the creditor may collect only from unencumbered property. In the middle category, where the obligor writes in a document, "I owe you one hundred," and delivers that document in the presence of witnesses, the creditor may collect from encumbered property according to the view that witnesses to delivery give the document its legal force, even if the writing is not in the obligor's own hand. If he did not deliver it before witnesses, the creditor may collect from unencumbered property if it is in the obligor's handwriting, as I explain below, seif katan 5. Ramban and Rashba agree to this middle category. So too do Ran and Maggid Mishneh, as well as Tosafot in the name of Rabbeinu Tam, the Rosh, the Tur, and the other authorities. Ritva at the beginning of chapter HaNose writes that Rabbeinu Tam's approach is correct. This is the primary law, contrary to Shiltei Giborim cited in the preceding seif katan. The same is true if witnesses themselves wrote a document stating that the obligor created a new obligation and instructed them to write it. This is effective even according to Ramban, Rashba, and the authorities who follow them. Ran at the beginning of chapter HaNose clearly implies this. See there carefully.
"Be witnesses for me," etc. Maggid Mishneh writes that Ramban, Rashba, and other commentators disagree and bring proofs that a person cannot create a new obligation through words alone, even by saying "You are my witnesses," without a kinyan or written document. Perhaps their proofs are those brought by Ran at the beginning of chapter HaNose. A guarantor, for example, generally requires either a kinyan or the benefit he receives when money is advanced in reliance upon him. Likewise, the Gemara in chapter HaZahav, concerning custodians of land, establishes the case as one where a kinyan was made from them. In chapter HaShoel, when a gratuitous custodian stipulates to have the liability of a borrower, the Gemara asks, "By what means? By mere words?" and answers that the custodian accepts liability in exchange for the benefit of being trusted. Ran ultimately explains that according to Rif and Rambam, when the Gemara establishes the land custodian's undertaking through a kinyan, it need not mean that a kinyan is the only possible mechanism. Saying "You are my witnesses" could likewise work, for on the same logic the Gemara could have established it where he obligated himself by document, since we rule like Rabbi Yochanan. The Gemara chose a kinyan because that is the most common form of acquisition. A guarantor differs because his obligation resembles an asmachta, and therefore "You are my witnesses" alone is insufficient. In my view, Rif and Rambam can be explained more sharply. Only here, where a person creates an immediate and unconditional obligation, can a written self obligation or "You are my witnesses" be effective. A land custodian or borrower does not become liable immediately, but only if a future loss occurs, which resembles the conditional undertaking of a guarantor and therefore has an asmachta character. A kinyan is required there. Rambam's own wording is precise. He begins: "One who obligates himself in money to another without any condition at all is liable, for this is a gift and not an asmachta. How? One who says to witnesses, etc." Why stress "without any condition" and "not an asmachta" unless he intends this very distinction? Rabbeinu Yerucham, netiv 15, part 3, also cites this language. This appears clear.
Or he wrote it for him in a document, etc. The Bach writes that this works only where the document is in the obligor's own handwriting. If it is not in his handwriting and he did not personally sign beneath it, the Tur implies that according to Rabbeinu Tam, even if he delivered it before witnesses he cannot collect even from unencumbered property, because the obligor can say, "I was merely jesting with you." The Bach says Mordechai states this explicitly and Hagahot Ashri likewise. I am astonished by this. According to the view that witnesses to delivery create the legal force of a document, why should the absence of the obligor's handwriting matter? Once he delivered it before witnesses, the delivery witnesses create public notice and the creditor can collect even from encumbered property. A plea of jesting has no place in such a case. Even according to the Sema's version of the Mechaber below, seif 2, requiring the writing to be such that it cannot be forged, that condition addresses the possibility of alteration, as the Sema explains there, seif katan 6. Apart from forgery, collection can be made even from encumbered property although it is not the borrower's handwriting. The Tur explicitly states at the end of siman 69 that even when a document is not in the borrower's handwriting, witnesses to delivery give it force. The same must be true here. There is no reasonable basis to distinguish for this purpose between one who already owes and one who is creating an obligation now. Beit Yosef likewise writes at the end of this siman that Rambam would agree with Rabbeinu Tam's rule that if one writes in his own hand, "I, so and so, owe you one hundred," and delivers the document before witnesses, it is treated as a documented loan. The fact that it is in his handwriting certainly does not diminish it. Although its language is not ordinary document language, its written admission combined with delivery before witnesses suffices. Beit Yosef clearly understands that the essential force for encumbered property comes from the delivery witnesses. The Bach may have inferred his position from Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri asking, according to Rashi, that if the person did not sign beneath the writing he should be able to say, "I was jesting with you." One might ask why that defense exists if the writing was delivered before witnesses. But the inference is not compelling. They may understand the sugya according to the authority who does not hold that delivery witnesses create a document. If delivery witnesses did create the document, Reish Lakish's exemption would be difficult on any explanation. Rabbeinu Tam's explanation avoids that difficulty because the case concerns a newly created obligation. Moreover, below in siman 81, seif 19, seif katan 49, I have already refuted the words of Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri from Rashi, Tosafot, and other authorities. The Bach's statement that Mordechai and Hagahot Ashri explicitly support him is also inaccurate. They do not mention delivery witnesses at all. They cite Rabbeinu Tam that one creates the obligation by writing in his own hand, "I, so and so, owe you one hundred," and signing beneath it, which concerns collection from unencumbered property, as the Tur writes in Rabbeinu Tam's name. Tosafot and the Rosh at the beginning of chapter HaNose can likewise be explained this way. This also resolves Tosafot's difficulty there, 102a. Tosafot asks that if Rabbeinu Tam treats the written guaranty as a complete document creating the obligation when delivered before witnesses, then the same logic should create a lien upon encumbered property, because delivery witnesses generate public notice just as in an ordinary loan. According to what I have explained there is no difficulty. The guaranty case need not involve delivery before witnesses; it may simply be a handwritten statement, "I am guarantor." I later saw that Chochmat Shlomo and the novellae of Maharsha noticed this point. The Tur writes: "Rabbeinu Tam explains that the passage does not discuss an admission of an existing debt, but a person who now obligates himself for one hundred and writes in his own hand, 'I, so and so, owe you,' and delivers the document. By delivery of this document he now becomes liable for one hundred. If he delivers it before witnesses, collection may be made from encumbered property. If he delivers it privately, collection is from unencumbered property." The Bach understood the requirement of the obligor's handwriting as applying even where there are delivery witnesses. That is not the Tur's intent. The Tur had just cited Rashi's explanation, which requires a case not in the obligor's own handwriting, because if it were in his handwriting he would already be liable by his admission even without delivery witnesses, and Reish Lakish and Rabbi Yochanan could not meaningfully dispute it, since the Mishnah states that one who produces a person's handwriting collects from unencumbered property. The Tur therefore says that Rabbeinu Tam avoids that forced limitation. Even if it is the obligor's handwriting, the authorities can still dispute because he is not admitting an old debt, but creating a new one through this writing. When the document is delivered before witnesses, the dispute can concern collection from encumbered property; when delivered privately, it concerns unencumbered property. The Tur had to mention handwriting for the private delivery case. But if it is delivered before witnesses, there is no need that the writing be his own. Any text incapable of alteration can create a lien according to the view that delivery witnesses give documents force. A clear proof is Maggid Mishneh, chapter 11 of Hilchot Mechirah. He writes that if the obligor writes, "I hereby owe you one hundred," even if there are no witnesses signed in the document, the recipient acquires the right. This is what Rambam means by "or he wrote him a document." Maggid Mishneh adds that this works only if he delivers the document before witnesses, according to the view that delivery witnesses effect acquisition, or alternatively if the document is in the obligor's own handwriting and he gives it privately, in which case it allows collection from unencumbered property. If another person wrote it and it was delivered privately, it is certainly ineffective. Beit Yosef and Darkei Moshe cite this. Although Maggid Mishneh writes it according to Rambam and elsewhere says Ramban and Rashba dispute Rambam, they plainly dispute only Rambam's first and third categories, namely words alone with "You are my witnesses" or an oral statement "I owe you one hundred by document." Maggid Mishneh explicitly says they agree to the middle category of an actual document. Rabbeinu Tam therefore certainly agrees as well. It also follows that one who writes the obligation in his own hand and delivers it privately creates an obligation collectible from unencumbered property even according to Ramban and Rashba. Maggid Mishneh's wording proves this, and the Tur's presentation of Rabbeinu Tam proves the same. Rabbeinu Tam himself agrees with Ramban and Rashba in Tosafot, chapter HaNose, that a new obligation cannot be created merely by saying "You are my witnesses." The difference between creating a debt through a formal document before witnesses and creating it through one's own handwriting for unencumbered property is therefore not substantive. This also resolves the Tur's language below, siman 60, seif 9, concerning one who obligates himself, where the Tur cites Ramban and then says that if no kinyan was made and he did not obligate himself "by document," collection is from unencumbered property, whereas if a kinyan was made or he obligated himself by document, collection is from encumbered property. Beit Yosef, the Bach, Gidulei Terumah, gate 64, part 1, page 346, and other later authorities were forced to say that this latter sentence is the Tur's own language and not Ramban's, because Maggid Mishneh says that Ramban does not allow a new obligation without a document. In my humble opinion their difficulty is unnecessary. Maggid Mishneh excludes only oral "You are my witnesses" and an oral statement, "I owe you one hundred by document." A handwritten self obligation, however, allows collection from unencumbered property even according to Ramban, as Maggid Mishneh himself implies. The Tur in siman 60 therefore speaks of such handwriting. This is the primary rule.
For he obligated himself just as a guarantor becomes obligated. Rambam's intent appears to be to prove that the sugya at the beginning of chapter HaNose discusses a person creating an obligation he did not previously owe, because the Gemara there challenges the rule from the law of a guarantor. Rif uses this proof as well. See there.
Just as a guarantor becomes obligated, etc. The Sema explains this as referring to a case where, before the witnesses signed, the document stated, "So and so borrowed from so and so, and so and so is guarantor," after which the witnesses signed. He says the guarantor becomes bound even without a kinyan because his obligation appears in the document. This is inaccurate. Rambam, the Tur, and the Mechaber below, siman 129, seif 4, hold that a guarantor whose undertaking appears before the witnesses' signatures still requires a kinyan in the relevant case. Rambam and the Tur and Mechaber here mean only a conceptual comparison: a person can obligate himself even though he was not previously liable, just as a guarantor assumes liability even though the principal debt was never his. This is straightforward.
