One who obligates himself, etc. This applies only where he was not mistaken. If he mistakenly thought he was already obligated when in truth he was not, all agree that his admission has no effect. This may be established either by witnesses or by the creditor's own admission that the statement was mistaken, and there is no dispute about this. If the creditor does not admit the mistake and only the debtor says, "I was mistaken," while having a migo that he could say, "I paid," see below in siman 81 and Tumim concerning the views of Rashi and Tosafos.
Let witnesses be upon me, etc. One may collect only from unencumbered property, because so long as he did not write and deliver a document, even one signed by witnesses of delivery, all agree there is no collection from encumbered property. See Tumim, where I reconcile Rambam's words.
That I owe, etc. Many authorities, including Tosafos, Rosh, Ramban, and Rashba, disagree with this ruling. They hold that where the person was not previously indebted and the creditor admits that no debt existed, he cannot create the obligation merely by saying, "You are witnesses," or even, "I owe you one hundred in a document." Rather, he becomes obligated only by actually delivering a document, whether a document signed by witnesses, a document established through witnesses of delivery, or a signed document in his own handwriting. This is unlike Sema, who holds that if he says, "I owe you one hundred in a document," all agree that he becomes obligated. That is incorrect, for according to the authorities listed above even this language is ineffective until a document is delivered, as the Shach writes. Sema writes that the Tur omitted the law of one who says, "You are witnesses that I owe," where no debt existed because the Tur does not accept Rambam's position. This is inaccurate, because the Tur cites the law in siman 60. The correct analysis is therefore that according to Rambam he is liable in every such formulation, whereas according to the dissenting authorities he becomes obligated only through a document and not otherwise. Sema himself wonders why the Mechaber and Rama did not mention these major dissenting authorities. Certainly the party in possession may say that he relies upon them. Nevertheless, Maggid Mishneh and Ran write that Rambam's reading fits the language of the Gemara better, and for that reason the ruling was decided in his favor.
Or if he said to him, "I am obligated," etc. Shach concludes that Rambam's rule, that oral commitment suffices without a document, applies only where a person undertakes an absolute obligation without attaching it to a condition, for then he definitively resolves to bind himself. But where an unpaid bailee agrees to become liable like a paid bailee, so that his obligation depends upon a future event, such as theft, it resembles an asmachta because he assumes that no mishap will occur. In such a case even Rambam requires a kinyan or a document. This resolves the difficulty raised against Rambam from the case of an unpaid bailee who undertakes the liability of a paid bailee.
Since he said, "in a document." It follows that if a shechiv mera said, "I owe you one hundred," without saying "you are witnesses" and without mentioning a document, Mabit, part 1, siman 322, holds that since the words of a shechiv mera are treated as written, he becomes obligated. See there. His ruling requires study, because according to what I wrote below in siman 255, even a shechiv mera who did not say, "Give," and did not deliver a handwritten acknowledgment may still say that his statement was made only to avoid appearing wealthy, and we make that claim for his heirs. His words therefore require further examination.
Like the guarantor becomes obligated. Meaning that just as a guarantor receives no money yet nevertheless becomes obligated, so too this person, although no prior debt existed, can voluntarily create an obligation upon himself. As for why a guarantor himself sometimes requires a kinyan, and the difficulty from the Gemara, see what I wrote below in siman 129 at length in explanation of Rambam.
