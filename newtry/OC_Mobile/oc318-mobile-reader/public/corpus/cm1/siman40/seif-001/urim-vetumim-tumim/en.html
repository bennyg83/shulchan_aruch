Even though he had not owed him anything, etc. The Shach writes in seif katan 1 that if a person admitted in error, believing that he owed money when in fact he did not, and this was established by witnesses, or the creditor admitted that nothing was owed, the person is exempt. This is correct, but the Shach should have explained that when the creditor admits that nothing was owed, he must also admit that the original admission was made in error. If the creditor admits only that there had previously been no debt, that proves nothing, because a person can obligate himself even where he previously owed nothing. Thus, when the Tur says that the matter was established by witnesses or the creditor admitted that he did not owe him, the meaning is that it was also established or admitted that the original admission was mistaken. Otherwise the debtor cannot simply claim, "I was mistaken," unless he has a migo, and even there the authorities dispute the matter below in siman 81. The Sema in the Derishah wished to explain the Tur's statement, "or the creditor admitted," according to Rashi, while according to Rabbeinu Tam the creditor's admission would not suffice and witnesses would be required. He therefore explains why Baal HaTerumot, who follows Rabbeinu Tam, mentions only proof by witnesses. This seems to assume that the creditor admitted merely that no prior debt existed, but did not admit that the debtor had been mistaken, and that according to Rashi a person cannot newly obligate himself without a kinyan when nothing was previously owed. This interpretation does not work. If the witnesses testify that they know he was mistaken and that the admission was made in error, this is equivalent to the creditor himself admitting that the admission was mistaken. If, however, the witnesses merely know that no prior debt existed, that does not help according to Rabbeinu Tam, as explained. Moreover, it is clear that even according to Rashi, if a person formally admits that he owes one hundred to another, although in truth he previously owed nothing, he nevertheless becomes obligated so long as the admission was not mistaken. Otherwise Rabbeinu Tam could not have brought a proof for his own explanation from the Yerushalmi, which states that Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish agree that one who admitted owing one hundred because he assumed he owed it, and it was then found that he did not, is exempt. Were Rashi's view that a new obligation cannot arise through admission, that Yerushalmi would instead be decisive support for Rashi, because the Bavli could then be reconciled simply: in the Bavli Rabbi Yochanan obligates only where the creditor asserts that there really is a debt, while the Yerushalmi exempts when it is established that no debt ever existed. Rabbeinu Tam could not cite it as a proof against Rashi. It is therefore clear that Rashi agrees that a proper admission can create liability even if no prior debt existed. The Yerushalmi's exemption concerns a demonstrated mistaken admission. This must also be so from the Gemara's challenge from the case of one who writes to a kohen that he owes five selaim. Why should that difficulty be resolved according to Rabbi Yochanan if the kohen admits that no debt exists apart from pidyon haben? If the son is not thereby redeemed, why should the father be obligated? The question applies to all views unless a formal admission can itself create liability. The same is implied by the admission used by Issur Ger in Bava Batra 149. That device appears to work without a kinyan. If there had been a kinyan, its effectiveness would be obvious, especially since a partial deathbed gift made with a kinyan is irrevocable even if the giver recovers. The entire proof from Rav Mari would be unnecessary. It follows that the formula of admission itself is not a kinyan, yet it creates liability. All the poskim cite the incident of Issur as proof of this. The question is different where one does not speak in the form of an admission but says expressly, "I hereby obligate myself to give you such and such," creating a new obligation. Riaz, cited by Shiltei Gibborim and by the Shach in seif katan 2, writes that if one says, "I am obligated to give you one hundred," when he actually owes nothing, even if he delivers a signed document, nothing takes effect without a kinyan. Tosafos also appears to imply this according to the Derishah's reading of Rabbeinu Tam. One might therefore think that this is Rashi's conclusion as well. That conclusion, however, is difficult. Rashi explains in Ketubot that a shtar haknaah may be written whether the borrower ultimately borrows or not, which indicates that the obligation can arise through the document itself. Even if one forces that case into a kinyan, there remains Abaye's principle that the witnesses' signatures create rights from the time of signing. If according to Rashi delivery of a document saying, "I owe you one hundred," cannot obligate a person where no money was lent, how could any lien arise before funds were advanced? Similarly, in the case of a document written in trust, Rashi explains that the lien can arise immediately even though the loan will take place later. This points strongly to the ability of a document to create a new obligation. One could answer that Rashi speaks only of an instrument accompanied by a kinyan, and that Abaye's rule is a special enactment whose underlying reason is not extended elsewhere. But this is strained. The language of Tosafos also indicates that the Yerushalmi is a support for Rabbeinu Tam only if Rashi agrees that a formal written obligation can create liability. Otherwise the Yerushalmi would support Rashi rather than Rabbeinu Tam. It therefore appears clear that where the obligation is created in a full document executed by witnesses, the person may obligate himself even though no previous debt existed. The question concerns a mere handwritten instrument. Rashi need not hold that a simple handwritten note, without witnesses, is sufficiently powerful to create a new obligation. Tosafos at the end of Perek Get Pashut states that a loan without witnesses does not create a Torah level lien, indicating that a handwritten note lacks the full force of a witnessed document. Accordingly, there is a genuine distinction between Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam. According to Rabbeinu Tam, their dispute concerns one who writes in his own hand a new obligation even though he previously owed nothing, because the Gemara compares the case to a guarantor after the signatures, which is essentially a written self obligation. According to Rashi, by contrast, their dispute concerns an admission, and from Rashi's explanation there is no basis to say that a mere handwritten document can itself create a new obligation. A full document executed by witnesses can do so. This also fits the language of the Rosh, who, when explaining Rabbeinu Tam, says expressly that the person wrote in his own hand. His question, "How can he obligate movable property through this document?" refers specifically to this inferior handwritten instrument, not to a full witnessed document. A full document creates a lien on land, and land can be acquired through a document. This appears to me the correct distinction.
Thus, where the language is one of admission, even a handwritten instrument can obligate according to Rashi, because admission itself has the force of one hundred witnesses. The case of the five selaim owed to the kohen is according to Rashi a handwritten instrument, yet Rabbi Yochanan obligates even though there was no prior debt. By contrast, a newly created obligation in the form, "I obligate myself," requires a full document with witnesses according to Rashi, while Rabbeinu Tam allows even the handwritten instrument. Be witnesses for me, etc. If one merely says, "Be witnesses for me," or "I hereby owe you one hundred by document," this does not by itself create a lien on real property, as the Shach writes. At first sight this is difficult from the Gemara's proof from one who marries a woman and undertakes to support her daughter. The Gemara initially assumes a case of this kind, according to Rambam, meaning that he says, "I owe you by document." Why then can the daughter collect from encumbered property? If the commitment was written explicitly in a proper document there would be no proof. One answer is that the initial assumption was that, like a sale made before witnesses, such a public undertaking has a voice and therefore can be collected from encumbered property. Tosafos in Gittin 51 uses this reasoning. A more satisfactory explanation is that the baraita's statement that after the parties die she is supported from encumbered property means only that there exists a legal possibility of such collection, namely where a kinyan was made. With a stepdaughter, a kinyan can create collection from encumbered property; with one's own daughters, there is no equivalent arrangement. Thus the statement does not mean that an ordinary oral undertaking always collects from encumbered property. Tosafos in Ketubot 102 explicitly explains that without a written instrument one collects only from unencumbered property, and that the case there happened to involve a kinyan. Rambam's commentary on the Mishnah likewise supports this understanding.
Or he wrote to him, etc. Mordechai, cited in Hagahot Ashri, asks according to Rashi, who explains the case as one where the person did not sign: through what act does he become obligated? Why can he not say, "I was merely joking with you"? Mordechai therefore adopts Rabbeinu Tam's explanation that the entire discussion concerns a person who admits that he previously owed nothing and now wishes to create a new obligation, and "one hundred by document" means that he executed a full document of obligation despite the absence of a prior debt. The Bach inferred from Mordechai that according to Rabbeinu Tam, if the person did not sign the instrument, even delivery before witnesses is ineffective. The Shach objects that since we rule that witnesses to delivery effect the document, a writing delivered before witnesses is a full document even if it is not signed and even if it is not in the obligor's handwriting, provided it cannot be forged. The Shach therefore says that Mordechai's challenge to Rashi must have been according to Rabbi Meir, who holds that witnesses to signature effect the instrument. That answer is difficult. It would make the entire dispute of Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish depend upon Rabbi Meir, while according to Rabbi Elazar Reish Lakish would concede. The Gemara could then answer every challenge simply by saying that the source follows Rabbi Elazar. Moreover, the entire dispute would be contrary to our ruling like Rabbi Elazar. Tosafos already addressed this in Ketubot. They explain that the phrase used in the document is an inferior or incomplete expression, one that does not constitute a proper dispositive document merely because it was delivered before witnesses. That is why Reish Lakish can say that it creates no obligation, while Rabbi Yochanan nevertheless gives it force. This also explains why the Gemara compares the matter to a guarantor who writes his guarantee after the signatures. It is not the ordinary dispute between signature witnesses and delivery witnesses. There are two ways to understand Tosafos's further question against Rabbeinu Tam from the guarantor case. First, as explained above in simanim 43 and 45, even a handwritten document can create a danger of an earlier date being used for a later obligation. A loan document without a kinyan may not be delivered to the borrower for later use, because he might write in Nisan and borrow only in Tishrei, thereby improperly collecting from purchasers. Where a guarantor appears in the document without a kinyan, the same concern applies to the guarantor's property. The witnesses must therefore deliver the document to the lender on the day of the loan. That delivery itself is the clearest form of witnesses to delivery. If so, even wording such as "So and so is guarantor" should create a lien through delivery witnesses. This yields Tosafos's difficulty. This remains true even if the guarantor was not physically present at the final delivery. Delivery witnesses may act through agency; a get is delivered to a wife before witnesses even when the husband is overseas. If the guarantor wrote his undertaking and gave it to the witnesses, and they then delivered the loan document to the lender, they act as his agents and as witnesses to delivery. Nor can the case be explained as a shtar haknaah or through Abaye's rule that signatures create rights from the time of signing. According to Ben Nanas, who follows Reish Lakish's premise that a person cannot create this obligation through the inferior writing itself, those devices cannot help. The obligation can arise only through delivery connected to the actual advance of money. This also explains Tosafos's statement in Ketubot that the Gemara's question, "we require a dispositive document," is directed specifically against Ben Nanas. Second, the question can be framed according to Rabbi Elazar's general rule that delivery witnesses are essential. Where the writing is not itself a full dispositive document, signature witnesses alone cannot create a new obligation, because this is not merely an evidentiary document but an instrument whose purpose is to create the obligation. If there are no delivery witnesses, it is like a shard and cannot obligate even the debtor's unencumbered property. If there are delivery witnesses, however, then according to Rabbi Elazar it should be a full document and should collect from encumbered property. This creates a dilemma for Rabbeinu Tam: without delivery witnesses, there is no effective instrument at all; with delivery witnesses, why should it collect only from unencumbered property? Mordechai may understand, however, that delivery witnesses must be specifically designated as such. Rashba, at the end of Gittin, explains that where a person writes, "I owe you one hundred that I borrowed from you in this document," he becomes obligated when he appoints witnesses and delivers it before them for the purpose that they serve as witnesses to the loan. By contrast, if witnesses merely happen to be present when the paper is handed over and were not designated for that purpose, they are not witnesses to delivery in the relevant sense. Many authorities who require a formula such as "sign and testify" likewise indicate that designation is needed. According to this approach, the guarantor case may involve witnesses who happened to be present but were not appointed as delivery witnesses, and Mordechai can therefore ask why the guarantor cannot claim that he was joking. Tosafos, however, do not appear to accept such a requirement of designation, as is evident from their question that the guarantor's document should have full force through delivery witnesses. Maharshal and Maharsha struggled with this question, while the Shach rejected Tosafos's concern by saying that the case did not involve delivery witnesses. In my view the Tosafos question can be explained in the two ways just stated and remains powerful. This also explains Tosafos's earlier question on Rashi. They ask that if the problem is merely inferior wording, the same problem should exist even when the guarantor's language appears before the signatures. One cannot answer simply that before the signatures the guarantee was made at the time the money was advanced and therefore the guarantor became obligated orally. That may create personal liability, but according to Rabbi Elazar signature witnesses alone do not create the document's lien. If the language is too defective to function through delivery witnesses, there is no proper document for collection from encumbered property. Tosafos therefore answer that before the signatures people do not ordinarily use the inferior wording, while after the signatures they may do so because the loan has already been completed. The conclusion is that the Bach's inference from Mordechai has some basis if one requires designated delivery witnesses, but as a matter of practical law the simple implication of the poskim does not support such a limitation. I have written above the Rashba's formulation, and perhaps distinctions remain. See also what I write in simanim 51 and 129 concerning whether Rabbi Elazar always requires delivery witnesses, and in siman 81 in Tumim, seif katan 25, where I explain that where there is an overt act we do not ordinarily suspect joking. Accordingly, there is no firm basis for the Bach's ruling.
