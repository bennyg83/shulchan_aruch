That I owe. Shach writes that any conditional undertaking resembles guaranty and therefore requires a kinyan. That is Rambam's view, who requires a kinyan even for an ordinary guarantor in certain post-loan settings. Other authorities who treat guaranty like ordinary self-obligation disagree: just as a person can create an unconditional written obligation, he can create a conditional obligation or guaranty through the same form. The commentators struggled with Rambam's reading of the Mishnah concerning a guarantor whose undertaking appears after the signatures. The correct explanation is that the Mishnah still involves a kinyan, but the document itself records that kinyan after the signatures. Rabbi Yishmael and Ben Nanas therefore dispute two matters: whether the kinyan can bind a guarantor after the money was given, and whether the guarantor's own written admission after the signatures is sufficient evidence that such a kinyan occurred. This also explains why the Talmud connects the dispute of Rabbi Yochanan and Reish Lakish over written self-obligation to the dispute of Rabbi Yishmael and Ben Nanas.
