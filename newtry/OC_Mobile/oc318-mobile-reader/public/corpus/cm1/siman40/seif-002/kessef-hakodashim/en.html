See Shach, seif katan 4. The Shach writes in siman 40 that according to Rambam, who holds that saying, "I owe you one hundred" creates an obligation even by mere words, the reason a guarantor is not bound after the money has already been given, and at the time of the loan is bound only because of the benefit that the lender trusted him, must be that the undertaking is an asmachta, as the Ran writes at the beginning of HaNoseh according to Rambam. See also the Sema in siman 129 and the Shach there. The Shach further writes that when an unpaid custodian undertakes to have the liability of a borrower, the Gemara's question, "By what act? By words?" and its answer that there was a kinyan or the benefit of being trusted, likewise rest on asmachta, because he obligates himself in the event of an unavoidable accident. The Ran himself, however, concludes there that in Bava Metzia 58 the Gemara could also have established the case by his saying, "I am obligated," but instead chose the ordinary case of a kinyan. At first glance this contradicts the Shach, for according to him the formula "I owe you one hundred" should not help there because the undertaking is an asmachta. Nevertheless, the Shach's reasoning is very clear. Just as the Ran himself says regarding a guarantor that it is an asmachta because he does not undertake an absolute liability, the same applies to custodians. At first I thought that the Ran was forced to explain the Gemara this way because we rule that asmachta does not apply to consecrated property, as stated by the Rama at the end of siman 207 and in Yoreh Deah 258:10, without recorded dispute. In Bava Metzia 58, according to Rashi, the question concerns consecrated property, so the Ran had to explain that the reference to a kinyan was not exclusive. On that reading, the Shach's formulation might sound as though this qualification were unnecessary. Yet this did not satisfy me, because the Sema there quotes the Mordechai, and the Mordechai himself says that the reason asmachta is ineffective there is the law of a vow. That has nothing to do with Rambam's rule, since even those who disagree with Rambam regarding a bare admission agree that a person can obligate himself through a vow, as in "that which leaves your lips shall be charity." It might therefore appear that the Ran understood the Gemara there as referring to attached produce, as the Or Zarua explains; see Urim VeTumim, siman 66. His words would then be consistent, but this would remove what I wrote above in support of the Shach and would produce a practical difference: according to the Ran, regarding land and similar property the undertaking would be effective according to Rambam by words alone. Still, according to Rashi the question returns: why did the Gemara establish the case by a kinyan when the custodian could become liable by a vow? We must say that the Gemara did not wish to establish a mere vow obligation. It speaks of an intrinsic liability of the custodian, perhaps so that there should be no annulment of the vow, so that his heirs would also be liable, or so that he would not need formally to transfer the consecrated property to the public. Rather, the liability arises primarily from the custodianship through a kinyan or another valid mechanism. Accordingly the Ran is well explained. The Mordechai there cites the Ran that asmachta works for consecrated property because on Torah law every asmachta is effective, and the Sages did not enact their invalidation in the case of consecrated property. Thus, according to Rambam, saying "I am obligated" regarding consecrated property creates a complete Torah obligation like a kinyan, and the rabbinic invalidation of asmachta was not imposed there. We therefore must explain that the Gemara's reference to a kinyan was not exclusive. What the Urim VeTumim writes there, that one cannot formulate a case of "they made a kinyan from him" regarding consecrated property, is not correct. It can be done with the witnesses' kerchief through the principle analogous to a Canaanite slave, as the Rama explains in siman 190. The Ketzot HaChoshen, seif katan 5, discusses this well in resolving the Taz in siman 123. See there.
