The tenant is believed through a migo. See Shach, seif katan 5, who makes two points. First, if the parties dispute the agreed rent only after the rental period, why should the tenant not be believed through a migo that he already paid? Second, even without a migo, the principle that land stands in the owner's chazakah should not decide a dispute concerning money after the use is over. Surely a landlord cannot claim any amount he wishes merely by saying, "That was the rent you agreed to." Shach also distinguishes a mortgage case from the bathhouse case. Tumim questions him. The distinction is nevertheless straightforward. In a mortgage where the money was prepaid, had the parties come to beit din at the start of the disputed year, the landowner would have been believed both to remove the possessor from the land and to retain the prepaid money because he himself possessed it. Once the possessor stayed in the land, his later possession resembles seizure in the presence of witnesses and does not overcome that original status when the doubt arises from their competing claims. In the bathhouse case, by contrast, if they came at the beginning of the disputed month and the tenant made a definite claim that the agreed twelve dinars covered thirteen months, then although the owner could remove him from the land based on land-possession, the tenant still held the money and could deduct the intercalated month. Thus his monetary possession is stronger, and after he has used the premises he need not pay more when he maintains the same definite claim. Shach's citation from Mordechai that the rule applies even at the end of the month is meant to address another difficulty. In a sale, where it is known that the object was transferred by kinyan sudar and the parties dispute only the price, the buyer can be believed. Why should rental differ, since rental for a day is called a sale? The answer is that a sale removes the object fully from the seller's domain. Rental does not. The landlord retains the underlying land, and in the conceptual model I have explained elsewhere, the tenant often has primarily a personal claim that the landlord provide a house. Rental is therefore not fully equivalent to sale for these evidentiary rules. Now Rama's disputed gloss can be understood. The case of seif 2 must concern rent not prepaid, because ordinary rent is payable at the end and Rama's following "some say" states that after the due time the tenant can say "I paid." Suppose the tenant says he rented for ten years and is entitled to continued occupancy at the agreed price, while the landlord says the term already ended. Or suppose the landlord admits the remaining term but says the annual rent is ten, as paid in the first five years, while the tenant says it was five and refuses to pay more for the remaining years. The landlord can ordinarily rely on the land's chazakah to remove the tenant unless the tenant has a stronger monetary defense. Rama's "some say" teaches that after each year's payment date, if the tenant can say "I paid" regarding past years, he may retain the past rent still in his hands and use it to secure the landlord's ongoing obligation to provide the remaining house. He can either withhold that money until the landlord provides the premises or rent another house and apply the retained money to the increased cost. Mordechai can disagree because he does not accept a migo from one fund to a different monetary claim. In a mortgage, the migo operates on the very property already treated as acquired to the creditor. In an ordinary rental, by contrast, the tenant's claim for future housing is a different monetary obligation. Nimukei Yosef can be cited as disagreeing and allowing the migo even in rental. This explains Rama's use of that source.
