Nevertheless, some say, etc. The Rama used the formulation ‘some say’ because he followed what he wrote in Darkei Moshe: the landlord takes a hesset oath, and the Mordechai at the end of HaShoel likewise rules that if the landlord says, ‘I rented it to you for ten,’ and the tenant says, ‘for five,’ or the landlord says, ‘I never rented it,’ and the tenant says, ‘you did rent it,’ the land remains in its owner’s possession. The Rama understood the Mordechai and Tur to mean that in these claims the landlord is believed with an oath even after the lease term. Nimukei Yosef, however, writes that where the tenant has a migo that he could have said, ‘I never rented it,’ he is believed, and the same follows where he could have said, ‘I paid.’ In my view no authority disputes this. It is elementary that a tenant who has a valid migo is believed, as is explicit in the Gemara and in the Tur and Mechaber at the end of this siman. The Tur’s unqualified statement that the landlord is believed concerns a case in which the tenant has no migo, and he relied on what he wrote later. The Bach likewise explained the Tur in this way. The Rama’s understanding of the Mordechai also appears incorrect. The Mordechai states that where the landlord says ten dinars and the tenant says five, the landlord has the upper hand because land remains in its owner’s possession; even if the dispute comes at the end of the month, the whole month belongs to the landlord. The same applies if the tenant says that he rented and the landlord denies having rented. But where they agree on the rental rate and the payment date has passed, and the tenant says that he paid, the tenant is believed with an oath; during the term he is not believed. The phrase ‘even if he comes at the end of the month, the whole month belongs to the landlord’ is not an independent ruling about a dispute over the rental rate. It quotes Rav Nachman’s language concerning ‘one dinar per month, twelve dinars per year,’ where the wording itself created uncertainty from the outset. There the land’s prior possession resolves the doubt. In an ordinary dispute at the end of the term, however, the landlord seeks to extract money from the tenant, and the fact that the land belongs to him does not establish how much rent is owed. The rule that land remains in its owner’s possession helps the landlord remove the tenant, or require him to pay the amount the landlord asserts while he remains, but it does not defeat a tenant’s migo when the landlord later seeks money. Indeed, if this principle alone established the landlord’s monetary claim, why would the tenant be believed after the term to say that he paid? Even within the term the landlord is believed only because a person does not ordinarily pay before the due date. Rashi expressly explains Rav Nachman’s ruling by noting that the uncertainty arose at the beginning of the month, so that the land is placed on its prior status and the tenant is found to have occupied another’s property without payment. That reasoning does not apply where the parties later dispute the rental rate with definite claims. The Rashba, Rivash, Baal HaIttur, and the ruling above in siman 312, seif 16 all indicate that wherever the tenant has a migo he is believed. This is the correct ruling.
After the term, etc. This includes a claim such as, ‘I never rented it and never became liable for rent.’ The same applies even within the term when the tenant has another migo. For example, if the tenant says that only three years of the period stated in the document have elapsed, he is believed by the migo that he could have claimed ownership. Nimukei Yosef states this expressly in the name of the Ran. Baal HaIttur similarly explains the present law as referring to one who occupied for three years in a case where the owner protested, so that the tenant lacks a migo of ownership; alternatively, it concerns only two years of occupation. Although Baal HaIttur also writes that after three years the production of a lease document defeats the migo, that follows his initial view regarding a Sura mortgage. According to his final conclusion in the name of Rav Meir bar Chanoch, a migo remains available in a Sura mortgage, and the same applies to a lease, as explained above at the end of siman 150. The Rama speaks of ‘after the term’ because he addresses a landlord who seeks to collect money after the tenant has returned the land; that circumstance ordinarily arises only after the lease has ended.
