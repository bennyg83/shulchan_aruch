The fruits remain in the possession of those who consumed them. Sema, seif katan 11, explains that even where a written deed explicitly says the house was rented for three years, one party says the three years ended and the other says they did not, and the tenant already occupied the third year, we leave what was consumed in his hands. The case must concern a claim made during the third year, before its rent became due. After the due date, the tenant could simply say "I paid," and the existence of the document would not weaken him. During the term, however, he is ordinarily not believed to say he paid. His strength comes from the written three-year deed, which functions somewhat like a receipt against the landlord's claim. Still, because if the dispute had arisen at the start of the third year the landlord could have removed him based on the land's chazakah, the document alone is not a full receipt for that year. The reason we nevertheless do not remove what he already consumed is that the matter is one likely to become clarified. The tenant was allowed to remain and use the property while clarification was possible. If it ultimately cannot be clarified, the normal land-possession rule can again require removal.
