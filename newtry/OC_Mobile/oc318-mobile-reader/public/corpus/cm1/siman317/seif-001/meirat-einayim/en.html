The correct reading is: whether the rental was by document or witnesses, or without witnesses, etc. Meaning witnesses to the acquisition by which he rented it, preventing the tenant from later making a claim of ownership. Such witnessed acquisition is comparable to a document, as explained at the beginning of siman 70. The phrase "without witnesses" then means, all the more so without a written instrument. The Tur and Rambam in chapter 7 of Hilchot Sechirut use the language, "whether by document or by witnesses," which can also mean that there were witnesses but no kinyan, or that the witnesses are no longer present, making it effectively an oral rental. All formulations teach the same novelty: even where a rental document exists, the tenant can be believed after the term to say he paid the rent. This differs from a written loan, where the borrower is not believed to say "I paid" so long as the promissory note remains in the lender's possession, because the note was written principally for collection and the lender can say, "If you paid me, why is my note still in my hand?" A rental document is different. It may have been written mainly to prevent the tenant from later claiming ownership of the property. Even if the document says that a kinyan was made regarding everything written in it and also records payment of the rent, we do not assume the kinyan strengthens the payment clause, unless a separate document was written specifically concerning the rent and includes a clause of credibility. In that case he cannot say he paid. Likewise, if he accepted a kinyan before witnesses obligating himself to pay the rent, he cannot later claim payment without proof. Rivash and Darkei Moshe state this.
If you have been sued within the rental time, the renter does not pay off but at last:
Or he may claim against him concerning the money he previously gave, etc. The Tur says that after he pays again, he may bring an independent claim against the landlord for the money he says he previously paid and require a hesset oath. That is also the Mechaber's intent. In the Perishah I wrote that the word "or" is not literal, because he can do both. Before paying he may impose a general ban upon anyone who wrongfully took his money, and after paying he may sue the landlord for the amount he claims he previously paid. Ordinarily, however, if he intends to sue the landlord directly, he does not first impose a general ban because his claim is against a specific person. I also explained why here he must first pay and only afterward make the hesset claim, unlike one who says he repaid a loan before its due date, where the Tur rules in siman 78 that before paying again he may make the lender swear that he has not already been paid. Rent is different because people do not ordinarily pay before the term. The tenant may be removed before the term ends and then not owe the entire rent. Therefore advance payment is unusually unlikely. A loan obligation, by contrast, unquestionably exists. A borrower may fear that if he keeps the money he will spend it and have nothing left when due, and may therefore pay early. See the Derishah.
Even on the thirtieth day. Meaning even at the beginning of that day. A person commonly pays at the beginning of the day on which the term ends, even though legally he cannot be compelled to vacate or complete payment until the end of that day, whether in a loan or rental. When the Tur and Mechaber write at the end of siman 78 that he is believed only if he makes such a claim at the end of the day, that concerns a different migo. There he says on the due date, "I paid you before the term," and he is believed through the migo that he could have said, "I paid you now." Such a migo works only at the end of the day. At the beginning it is an audacious migo, because the creditor stands before him and directly says, "You did not pay me." By the end of the day the audacity is less, because he could say, "I paid you last night at the beginning of the night and you forgot because of your preoccupations through the night and day." Tosafot writes this. But where he directly claims, "I paid you today," ordinary practice is to pay even at the beginning of the due date, and he is believed without relying upon a migo.
Or the tenant shall swear, etc. This is not comparable to the rule in siman 312 where the landlord uses ambiguous language such as a month and a year, and even after the tenant has already lived in the disputed thirteenth month he must pay rent again. There the uncertainty existed from the beginning of the disputed month because it arose from ambiguous contract language. Here no uncertainty existed previously. The tenant unquestionably owed the rent. The only new issue is his present claim that he has already paid it. In that situation we leave the money where it presently stands.
