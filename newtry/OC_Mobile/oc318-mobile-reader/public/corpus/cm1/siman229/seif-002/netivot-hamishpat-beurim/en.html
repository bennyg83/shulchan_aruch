One who removes pebbles. This law is cited in Beitzah 38. The sugya is difficult, as Pnei Yehoshua notes. Because my explanation has practical consequences, I will summarize the sugya and explain it. The Mishnah says that a woman who borrows water and salt for her dough has the dough subject to the Shabbat boundary of both owners. The Gemara asks why the water and salt are not nullified into the dough. Tosafot explain that the question is not ordinary nullification of a permitted item. Rather, the prohibition of techumin depends on the name of the owner attached to the object. Since the dough is primarily the borrower's, perhaps the ownership-name of the water and salt should disappear through majority nullification. R. Abba answers by analogy: if one se'ah of one person's wheat became mixed with ten se'ah of another's, could the latter simply eat everything and rejoice? His point is not that he would receive the extra wheat for free. One who benefits while another suffers loss must pay. Rather, perhaps the first owner's name should disappear from the actual kernels, making the mixture fully the second person's property regarding future appreciation and permitting him to consume it, while leaving only a monetary obligation to pay value. The Gemara asks why the example was not wheat mixed with barley. The reason must be that different species do not nullify one another in the same manner, while according to the Sages wheat in wheat can be nullified in prohibition law. If so, perhaps ownership-name should also disappear. R. Safra supports R. Abba from the rule that one who removes pebbles from another's threshing floor must pay him the value of wheat. The pebbles themselves may be ownerless. If ownerless wheat fell among another's wheat and remained identifiable, a finder could acquire it. Why should mere mixing make the owner of the main wheat acquire the pebbles? It is also strained to say that the case necessarily concerns threshing in the grain-owner's courtyard, where courtyard acquisition applies, because the baraita is stated generally. Moreover, perhaps courtyard acquisition does not apply to something that is not itself property but only causes monetary value. R. Safra's proof must therefore be that the pebbles became nullified within the wheat and lost their original ownerless designation. The same should then be true of ownership-name in wheat. Abaye answers that one must distinguish between property with a claimant and property without a claimant. An ownerless designation can disappear through nullification, but ownership of an actual claimant does not. The Gemara challenges this from R. Chiyya's rule that neveilah is nullified in slaughtered meat for tumah purposes. Tumah law can depend on the owner's designation and intention. If actual ownership-name never disappears through nullification, perhaps neveilah belonging to someone should likewise retain that status for all ownership-dependent prohibitions. One might answer that the rule applies only where the neveilah has no owner. But ownerless objects acquire a Shabbat boundary, showing that ownerless status is itself treated as a legally significant ownership-type status. If ownerless status is treated like ownership for prohibition law, why should it disappear here? The Gemara ultimately answers that prohibition can be nullified, but monetary ownership is not nullified. Even ownerless monetary status is not turned into another person's property merely by mixture. The pebbles are different because they are not independently property at all. Concerning the prohibition aspect, however, ownership designation can be treated as nullified where the halachah depends on it. The remaining question from techumin is answered by a special decree. The practical conclusion is that the one who removes pebbles from another's threshing floor is liable even if the grain was threshed in ownerless premises and the grain owner never acquired the pebbles. Likewise, if Shimon threshed his grain in Reuven's house and Reuven removed the pebbles from Shimon's threshing floor, Shimon must pay Reuven for them to the extent the halachah recognizes their effect on the measured grain. The obligation does not depend on prior courtyard acquisition of the pebbles.
