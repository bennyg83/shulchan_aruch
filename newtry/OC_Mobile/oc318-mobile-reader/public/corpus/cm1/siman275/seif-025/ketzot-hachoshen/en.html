He thought they were his own and did not acquire. This rule is specific to ownerless property and the estate of a convert. In a gift, where another person's transferring intent is present, the recipient can acquire even if he did not know that he was performing an acquisition. Nimukei Yosef cites Raavad in the case of Rabbi Anan: had the transferor known and intended to convey, Rabbi Anan would have acquired even though he himself did not know he was acquiring. Raavad distinguishes the case of one hoeing the estate of a convert while thinking it his own. There no other person's intent exists. Rashba states the same principle in Gittin concerning an elder who lent money to villagers and then transferred his documents to them. Although the borrowers did not know of the transfer, another person's intent to convey allowed them to acquire. Several later responsa therefore erred when they wrote concerning sale of a cow to a gentile to avoid firstborn sanctity that even if the gentile pulled the animal, he did not acquire unless he consciously intended acquisition, based on the convert's estate case. Where a Jew intentionally transfers to the gentile, another person's transferring intent is present. One should not object from Rashba's rule that delivery acquires in the public domain but meshichah in a side street, where the recipient's later meshichah shows he did not wish to acquire by the earlier delivery. Another person's intent eliminates the need for positive acquisition intention only where the recipient is neutral. If he affirmatively intends not to acquire through a particular act, he cannot be forced to acquire against his will. Nor does the Tur's citation of Rabbi Meir HaLevi Abulafia contradict this. A valid hazakah requires an act objectively demonstrating improvement or possession of the land. Another person's intent can supply missing subjective acquisition intent, but cannot turn an act that is not legally a hazakah into one. Rav Hai says that in a purchase the surrounding circumstances ordinarily demonstrate that the buyer acts to acquire, but where the act itself is a recognized hazakah, such as locking or fencing, the transferor's intent can supply the missing subjective intent.
A difficulty remains from Yoreh Deah. The Tur says that when a gentile measures wine, even if he lifts it while measuring, he does not acquire unless the lifting was first done for acquisition. Beit Yosef explains that lifting done only for measurement is not an acquisition. Yet according to the rule just stated, where another person is conveying, the recipient's lack of acquisition intent should not matter. This can be answered according to what I wrote in siman 255 from Rambam. Another person's transferring intent helps through the rule that one may acquire for a person in his absence. Where the recipient himself performs the physical act but lacks intent, the transferor's intent can supply the missing awareness as a form of acquisition on his behalf. This mechanism may not apply to a gentile because ordinary acquisition on behalf of a gentile does not operate in the same way. That resolves Mishneh LaMelech's question. In siman 200 the Tur rejects Raavad's view that measurement on the seller's carriers fails for lack of acquisition intent, while in Yoreh Deah he accepts that very reasoning regarding a gentile. The difference can be that another person's intent supplies acquisition intention for a Jew through the doctrine of acquisition on his behalf, but not for a gentile. The remaining difficulty is Raavad himself, who is cited by Nimukei Yosef as recognizing that another person's intent eliminates the recipient's need for intent. Yet elsewhere Raavad says measurement does not acquire because it was intended only for measurement. Perhaps these are two different authorities bearing the title Raavad, though the matter requires study. Chacham Tzvi discusses whether the intention required for chalitzah is mitzvah intention or acquisition intention. He suggests it is the latter and therefore a female minor could in principle perform chalitzah where another person's intent supplies the acquisition component. But if another person's transferring intent means the recipient does not need acquisition intent at all, why does the Mishnah require both parties to intend the chalitzah? This supports distinguishing chalitzah. The required intention there is not merely the recipient's acquisition awareness but a special statutory intention that both parties understand the act as terminating the levirate bond.
Maharit indeed writes that the Gemara's requirement, "until both intend," excludes a misleading chalitzah where one party thinks the act serves another purpose. But if both understand that this chalitzah releases her, they need not maintain a special internal concentration at the moment of the act. Chacham Tzvi challenges Maharit, but this formulation already brings the requirement closer to statutory awareness rather than ordinary acquisition intention.
Ramban says essentially the same. Regarding a deaf mute, the Gemara says chalitzah fails because he cannot perform the required recitation. Ramban asks why the case does not fail earlier for lack of intention. He answers that although a deaf mute is like a person lacking full legal capacity for mitzvot, he has some intention where others stand over him and guide him. The baraita requiring both parties to intend excludes cases such as, "Perform chalitzah and thereby you will marry her." If he understands through signs that this act releases her, that is sufficient intention. His weak intelligence can support that basic awareness. Thus Ramban explicitly supports Maharit's approach.
