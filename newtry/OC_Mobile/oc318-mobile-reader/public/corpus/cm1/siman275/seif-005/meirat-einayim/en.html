Anything that separates for purposes of peah, etc. These two seifim, 5 and 6, are from Rambam, chapter 1 of Hilchot Zechiyah, and their source is Bava Batra 55a. Rashbam explains the separation for peah: if a ravine or water channel lies between two tracts of land, they are treated as two fields, so the owner cannot satisfy both with a single peah but must leave peah separately from each. The "ravine" means hard ground full of pebbles and stones unfit for planting, from the expression "an enduring ravine that is neither worked nor sown."
Anything that separates with respect to the public domain. This too is stated there. The baraita says: if one carried half a dried fig into the public domain, set it down, then in the same lapse of awareness carried another half dried fig into the public domain, he is liable, since the minimum food measure for the melacha of carrying is a dried fig. Rabbi Yose says this applies only if he carried both pieces into one domain; if into two domains, he is exempt. Rava concludes that not only a private domain or karmelit separates, but even a pisla, meaning a piece of wood or column ten handbreadths high and less than four wide, or the reverse, which is a makom patur, constitutes a separation. The reason is that we find that such a place has independent significance regarding a get. If a husband lent his courtyard to his wife so that it would function as her courtyard for receiving a get, and he threw the get there but it landed on such a pisla, she is not divorced. The pisla is considered a separate domain and is not subsumed in the courtyard he lent her, because he lent one place, not two.
