One who takes possession, etc. This seif is the language of the Rambam, chapter 1 of the Laws of Acquisition and Ownerless Property, law 9, and its source is the Gemara in Perek Shor Shenagach et HaParah, 49b. Maharshal there, siman 18, overlooked these words of the Rambam. At first glance, however, the Rambam's ruling requires study. The Gemara there records that Rav Yeiva Saba asked Rav Nachman: What is the law regarding one who takes possession of a convert's documents after the convert dies without heirs? Perhaps one who takes possession of the document intends to acquire the land, but he has not taken possession of the land, and he does not acquire the document either because he had no intent to acquire it. Or perhaps his intent also extends to the document. Rav Nachman said to him: I teach that he acquires it. Does he need it merely to cover the mouth of a flask? Rav Yeiva replied: Perhaps indeed he needs it to cover the mouth of a flask. This indicates that the question remained unresolved. Why, then, did the Rambam rule unequivocally that he acquires the paper? Lechem Mishna raises this question and leaves it unresolved. Gidulei Terumah, at the end of Shaar 49, writes that Rav Nachman's formulation implies that if he needs it to cover a flask, his intent certainly extends to the paper. Although I found a similar explanation in Shiltei HaGibborim, Perek Chezkas HaBatim, folio 189a, it does not appear correct to me. Moreover, the Gemara implies that only where he takes possession without specification is there a question, but if he expressly takes possession in order to acquire the land, it is obvious that he does not acquire the paper. How, then, could the Rambam write, 'One who takes possession of the document in order to acquire the land'? Lechem Mishna answers that the Rambam means that his principal intent is to acquire the land, while he nevertheless also intends to acquire the document. This is strained, and it does not explain why the Rambam departed from the Gemara's wording. One might answer that the Rambam had a different version of the Gemara. This is expressly indicated by the Maggid Mishna and Migdal Oz, who write that Rav Yeiva Saba asked Rav Nachman this question and Rav Nachman resolved it in this manner. Perhaps their text read: Rav Yeiva said, 'Perhaps he needs it to cover a flask,' and Rav Nachman answered, 'If so, even when his intent is directed toward the land he acquires it.' There is no reason to wonder that the textual versions differed so greatly. In Tekfo Kohen, siman 9, I cited a similar variation at the end of Perek HaPo'alim, 93b. The Nimukei Yosef in the first chapter of Bava Metzia and the Maggid Mishna, chapter 3 of the Laws of Hiring, write that the question there remained unresolved. I likewise found in an ancient handwritten parchment Gemara that the passage concludes with teiku, while in our texts the question is expressly resolved. The Beit Yosef and Bach below, at the beginning of siman 303, also describe it as a question that was resolved in Perek HaPo'alim. The Rif and Rambam likewise appear to regard it as resolved, as I wrote there in siman 420. Nevertheless, it appears more likely to me that the Rambam was troubled by the very premise of Rav Yeiva's question to Rav Nachman. Even if the possessor had no intent to acquire the document, why should he not acquire it? It is no worse than a person's courtyard, which acquires for him even without his awareness, in accordance with Rabbi Yose bar Chanina. The Rambam was also troubled by the Rif's omission of this question. He therefore understood that Rav Yeiva and Rav Nachman did not accept Rabbi Yose bar Chanina's rule. We, however, rule in accordance with Rabbi Yose bar Chanina. Consequently, this question has no practical application, because even if the possessor had no intent directed toward the document, a person's hand acquires for him without his awareness. When the Gemara later challenges Rava, asking why his courtyard should not acquire for him in light of Rabbi Yose bar Chanina's rule, it does not challenge Rav Yeiva and Rav Nachman because they may disagree with Rabbi Yose bar Chanina. Rava, however, appears in Perek HaShoel, 102a, to accept Rabbi Yose bar Chanina's rule. The Rif therefore omitted this question, since once we rule in accordance with Rabbi Yose bar Chanina, it has no application. For this reason the Rambam intentionally changed the Gemara's wording and wrote, 'in order to acquire the land,' teaching that even though his intent was not directed toward the document, he acquires it. It is possible that the Rosh held the same view, for he too omitted this question. This is unlike Maharshal, who writes that the Rif and Rosh did not cite the law, and that the Rambam did not mention it, because it is uncommon. Nevertheless, Hagahot Ashri in the name of Or Zarua, Perek HaParah, and Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 49, write that the question remained unresolved, and this also appears from Rabbeinu Yerucham at the end of Netiv 21. The matter therefore requires study. See also Maharshal there, siman 18, regarding the laws of seizure in this case.
The land described in that document, etc. This implies that the case concerns a deed of sale or gift. The Maggid Mishna and Rabbeinu Yerucham explain it this way, and Baal HaTerumot writes similarly at the end of Shaar 49: 'It is reasonable that here too the lien lapses, for the question concerning one who takes possession of a convert's documents and which remained unresolved does not concern his debt instruments. In that case it is obvious that they have lapsed,' and it appears that the text should read 'lapsed' rather than 'were paid,' unlike the version in Gidulei Terumah. 'Rather, the question concerns deeds of gift or acquisition of lands that the convert acquired from others.' It appears that they were compelled to explain the case this way because the Gemara says, 'but he has not taken possession of the land.' If the case concerned a debt instrument, even if he took possession of the land he would not acquire it, for it is no better than movable collateral, as stated below in seif 27. Lechem Mishna and Gidulei Terumah likewise write this. It also appears to me obvious that the Gemara's question is not whether the possessor is an ignoramus who errs in the law, for we do not suspect that. Rather, the question concerns a deed of sale, where his intent is directed toward the land and he plans to take possession of the land afterward. If the case concerned a debt instrument, how could his intent be directed toward the land? Immediately upon the convert's death, the lien lapses and the landowner retains his own land, as Baal HaTerumot writes: this one withdrew toward death and that one remained in life. This, I believe, is Baal HaTerumot's intent in writing that the question does not concern debt instruments, because in that case it is obvious that they lapse. Meaning, it is then obvious that the possessor's intent is not directed toward the land but only toward the document, and he acquires the document. This is unlike Gidulei Terumah, who strained Baal HaTerumot's words. According to this, if one takes possession of a deed of sale and afterward takes possession of the land before anyone else does, he acquires the document as well. The Gemara's question was only whether, since he had not taken possession of the land, perhaps he also failed to acquire the document. This clearly implies that if he did take possession of the land, he acquires the document. All this appears entirely clear to me. Although Rashi and Hagahot Ashri explain the passage as involving a debt instrument, the Rambam and those who follow him appear primary. In practical law, however, there is certainly no dispute regarding the land itself. Whether the document is a deed of sale or a lien document, the possessor does not acquire the land, according to both Rashi and the Rambam. Shiltei HaGibborim and Maharshal likewise write that Rashi also explains the passage as referring to a deed of sale, and mentioned a debt instrument only to teach a greater novelty. See what I wrote above, siman 66, seif 23.
