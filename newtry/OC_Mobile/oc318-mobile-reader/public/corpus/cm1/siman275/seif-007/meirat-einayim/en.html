Everything called by the convert's name is acquired. Earlier, in seif 3, the ruling was that if the field has no marked boundaries, one act with a hoe acquires only the area a team can plow out and back. The Maggid Mishneh reconciles the Rambam by saying that there the possessor did not make a complete act of possession but merely struck once with a hoe, while here he made a full act of possession. The Beit Yosef answers that here he intended to acquire the entire area. In the Derishah I proved that the Tur did not understand the Rambam according to either explanation, and Rama follows the Tur. Rather, the Tur understood the Rambam to distinguish whether the fields are called by the convert's name. If they are, one acquires them all; if not, he acquires only the area a team can plow out and back.
Specifically irrigated fields. Fields that must be watered from a well because rain alone is insufficient are called irrigated fields. The ruling says that specifically with such fields, if they are all called by the convert's name and the possessor made one act with a hoe while intending to acquire all of them, he acquires the whole even without expressly stating his intent. The Tur indicates that merely being called by the convert's name is not enough. They must all be called by the name of the well from which they are irrigated. Since the fields share one well and one name, they combine as one field. A rain fed field is not joined to others in that way, so ordinary unspecified possession does not acquire them all, as the ruling concludes.
If he expressly said that he intended to acquire all of them. This shows that Rama did not accept the Maggid Mishneh's or Beit Yosef's reconciliations of the Rambam that I mentioned above. According to the Beit Yosef, the Rambam's present case already concerns one who explicitly intended to acquire the entire plain. If so, Rama should have written merely that some say specifically with an irrigated field an express statement is unnecessary, rather than saying that with a rain fed field one acquires all only if he expressly states his intent. Nor does Rama follow the Maggid Mishneh's distinction between a full act of possession and a single strike with a hoe, because this alternative opinion is the Tur's, and the Tur's distinction between irrigated and rain fed land concerns acquisition by a hoe. The correct explanation is therefore the one I wrote above according to the Tur.
One furrow along its length and one across its width. A furrow is the measure of the distance the oxen travel in one row before turning to make the next row beside it. The Tur explains that this measure applies specifically when the act with the hoe was at the edge of the convert's field. If he struck in the middle of the boundary, he acquires one furrow's width to his right, one to his left, and one forward. Thus, if a furrow is one hundred cubits, he acquires one hundred by two hundred. If he struck in the middle of the field, he acquires two hundred by two hundred. See the diagram and explanation in the Tur.
If it is a field enclosed by a boundary, etc. The boundary surrounding it makes it one field. This is not as the Ir Shushan writes, implying that the boundary reduces his acquisition and that he acquires only what lies inside it. The point is the opposite: the boundary unifies the whole field as one parcel. The Ir Shushan also abbreviates incorrectly when discussing the two cases of striking in the middle or at the boundary. He omits the Tur's distinction that if the strike is at the extreme edge of the field he acquires only one hundred by one hundred, while if it is in the middle he acquires more. See the Rosh, Tur, and Derishah.
