One stroke of a spade. Ketzot HaChoshen writes that ownerless property can also be acquired through agav land and that agav does not require another person's conscious transfer. In my view this is incorrect. Agav is derived from the verse describing the transfer of fortified cities, and there another person consciously transferred the property. Ketzot himself cites the incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders, where they did not use agav because tovat hana'ah is not treated as money. If agav worked for ownerless property without any transferor, why would that matter? Even if tovat hana'ah were no better than ownerless property, agav should work. This is a strong proof that it does not. His attempted resolution is strained. Indeed, even if tovat hana'ah is not money, they could theoretically abandon the tovat hana'ah, because hefker can operate on such benefit, as I wrote in Mekor Chaim, Hilchot Pesach. The fact that agav was still unavailable confirms the requirement of a transferring mind. Ketzot also cites Rashba in Bava Kamma 52, who writes that one who takes possession of a convert's land acquires the documents connected to it because a deed is the "halter of the land." From this he infers agav works with hefker. That is no proof. A deed that is legally the control instrument of the land is stronger than ordinary agav; it is treated as connected to the land itself and therefore passes with it. Ketzot further writes that if one appoints an agent to take possession and the agent does not intend to acquire, the principal nevertheless acquires because the principal intended acquisition. At first glance Rama in seif 21 says the opposite: where one did not intend to acquire, or built through workers, no acquisition occurs. Sema, seif katan 26, explains the latter as workers who themselves did not intend acquisition. This supports the contrary rule. See what I wrote in siman 188. Machaneh Efrayim raises several related questions concerning a person who forbids his own property to himself by vow: does the property become ownerless so others may take it against his will? Ran in Nedarim 85 cites Rashba concerning one who says, "Kohanim and Leviim may not benefit from me," that they may take the priestly gifts against his will. Rashba learns from this that if a person forbids his own property to himself, others may take it against his will. Machaneh Efrayim questions this. Priestly gifts involve only tovat hana'ah, and once the owner is forbidden all benefit nothing remains for him. But one who forbids his ordinary property to himself still has legal rights: he can allow his children to take it, or borrow money and allow creditors to collect, as Nedarim 47 says. Ran there explains that "if he dies he shall not inherit" does not mean he never acquires the inheritance. The property is legally his, but he may not benefit from it. If so, his ownership did not disappear and why may others take against his will? In my view Rashba's words are correct. According to the opinion that tovat hana'ah is fully monetary, the owner has a genuine proprietary interest in priestly gifts. He can sell the tovat hana'ah, for example giving a buyer the right to choose a particular kohen, and in some cases its value approaches the value of the whole item. Kiddushin can even be effected with it. Likewise, if he owes money and possesses priestly gifts, creditors can collect the monetary value of his tovat hana'ah. Therefore, where one forbids property to himself, the same principle should govern both ordinary property and priestly gifts. The reason others may take is not that his formal ownership vanished immediately. Rather, once he is forbidden any benefit, he may not stop anyone from taking. Ran in Nedarim 47 explains that when he "gives" the property to his children, he merely shows them where it is and they take it for themselves in a manner resembling ownerless property. If he could exclude strangers until a favored person appeared, there would be no greater benefit than this. He would be using the property to benefit someone who pleases him, which is itself forbidden. Therefore he may not protest against any person who comes to take, whether a relative, stranger, non Jew, or someone who may carry the property far away. Nor may he guard it for himself. Once property is left in a public, unguarded place where anyone may take it and the owner may not prevent them, it resembles intentional loss and functions through despair. This form differs from ordinary hefker because he did not verbally declare it ownerless. Ordinary hefker requires an affirmative declaration. Here the legal result arises because the prohibition prevents him from asserting any control, which produces ye'ush rather than full hefker. Tosafot in Bava Kamma 66 write that ye'ush is not complete ownerlessness. As I explained in siman 262, true hefker leaves the owner's domain even before another acquires, while ye'ush requires another person's acquisition. Therefore property forbidden by vow remains legally the owner's until another person acquires it. This explains Sema in siman 245, seif katan 18, who writes that if a creditor comes and takes such property for repayment, the debt is discharged. The debtor cannot say the payment was mistaken because the property was ownerless and not his. Before another person acquired, it remained legally his, and when the creditor took it as repayment he received property still belonging to the debtor. It also explains Ran in Nedarim 85, who says that if another person acquired the property before the owner sought release from his vow, later release cannot remove that person's rights because there is no release from hefker. At first glance this is difficult: if the sage renders the vow mistaken from the beginning, why should the prior abandonment not also be mistaken like any acquisition made in error? According to what I have written, it is clear. While the vow stood, the owner had no right to prevent anyone from taking and therefore actually despaired from the property. He did not know he would later seek release. If another person acquired during that valid despair, the acquisition is complete. Later removal of the despair cannot undo a completed acquisition any more than later withdrawal of despair after a finder has already acquired. If the owner seeks release before anyone acquires, however, he need not perform a new act of acquisition. The despair ends before lawful acquisition by another and his ownership remains, like property saved from a lion before a finder acquires. See siman 262. Machaneh Efrayim also writes that one who forbids his property to himself may give it to charity because mitzvot were not given for benefit, analogizing to Eruvin 30 where one may use bread forbidden by vow for an eruv. In my view this should not be stated. Nedarim says that one who vows not to benefit from a spring may immerse there for a mitzvah in winter but not in summer. Even though mitzvot were not given for benefit, where the mitzvah also produces bodily benefit the act remains forbidden. Giving a gift to a poor person includes more than the mitzvah of charity. The poor person becomes grateful to him, which is a benefit, just as giving a gift to a non Jew can be prohibited for that reason. In addition, he saves his own money by satisfying a charity obligation. This is stronger than the uncommon perutah of Rav Yosef; here the poor person is already present and the saving is immediate. The eruv case likewise applies specifically where he walks for a mitzvah from which he receives no personal benefit and is not financially obligated. If he goes to a mitzvah involving benefit, such as a wedding, or to an obligation that would otherwise require him to spend a perutah on another eruv food, using the forbidden loaf saves him money and should be prohibited. Machaneh Efrayim also questions Rashba, responsum 602, who rules that if a man forbade his property to a woman and then betrothed her with it, she is not betrothed because prohibited benefit cannot be acquired. He argues she could still acquire it to give to her children or creditors. There is no difficulty. Acquiring it precisely for that purpose is itself a major benefit to her and therefore forbidden. He further asks from one who vows not to benefit from his son-in-law, where giving a gift to the daughter can be prohibited because what she acquires her husband acquires. He argues the husband cannot acquire prohibited benefit. But once the father transfers to the daughter, the property has passed to another person and the husband acquires from her, not directly from the person who prohibited it. He also asks from Nedarim concerning one who says, "My loaf is forbidden to you," and later gives it to the other as a gift, where the Gemara says the recipient does not wish to acquire a prohibited object. He infers that if he did wish to acquire, he would acquire, proving one can own prohibited benefit in order to give it away. This is no proof. Even items of bodily sanctity, from which there is no ordinary beneficial ownership at all, can create me'ilah when transferred. The liability arises because the recipient seeks to acquire and treats the object as his. Rashba similarly explains idolatry becoming property of a Jew because he wishes to acquire it. The same principle applies here.
