Once he struck it with one blow of a hoe, he acquires the whole. I was uncertain whether one who takes possession of the property of a deceased convert, with movable property located within it, acquires those movables through kinyan agav. Perhaps kinyan agav works only where there is another person transferring ownership, whereas one who acquires on his own cannot acquire through agav. The practical difference is where the law of courtyard acquisition does not apply, such as where the courtyard is not guarded and he neither stands beside it nor says, "My field shall acquire for me." At first glance there is proof from the fact that houses are acquired by chazakah in the property of a deceased convert, as indicated in Bava Basra, daf 53, concerning two houses, one inside the other. According to the poskim who hold that a house has the status of something detached and subsequently attached, so that its law is like movable property, as explained in siman 95, and as I wrote there in seif katan 3 in the name of Magen Avraham, siman 637, the acquisition of houses there would necessarily be through agav. This would prove that ownerless property can also be acquired through agav. One can nevertheless answer that the house is acquired through the law of a courtyard, because by acquiring the land beneath it through chazakah he simultaneously acquires the house through his courtyard, in accordance with what I wrote in siman 202 that acquisition of the courtyard and the object given can take effect simultaneously.
There is, however, a clear proof that one can acquire ownerless property on his own through agav from the discussion in Bava Kamma, daf 117, regarding the dispute between R' Eliezer and the Sages concerning one who stole land and a river swept it away, whether land is subject to theft. The Baraisa states: one who steals a cow and it is swept away by a river must provide another cow, according to R' Eliezer, whereas the Sages say he may say, "Your property is before you." Rav Pappa explains that the case is where one stole another person's field while a cow was lying in it, and the river swept it away. R' Eliezer follows his view and the Sages follow theirs. Rashi explains that the cow was lying there and the robber did not perform meshichah upon it. According to R' Eliezer, who holds that the field is acquired by the robber, the cow is acquired together with it and he is liable to pay, because movable property is acquired agav land. According to the Sages, he acquires neither the field nor the cow. In the case of a robber there is no other person consciously transferring ownership. Yet the robber requires a kinyan in order to become liable for unavoidable loss just as one requires a kinyan to acquire ownership, and Rashi says that he acquires through agav. This proves that kinyan agav operates even where one acquires without another transferor, as with ownerless property. Rashi did not explain that he acquires the cow through courtyard acquisition, for a courtyard acquires because it is a person's legal domain, whereas a robber acquires land only with respect to liability for unavoidable loss and has no proprietary right in the courtyard itself. Although with all objects subject to theft he pays according to the value at the time of theft and need not pay for his use of them, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, siman 363, and Rama, siman 371, that does not make the unlawfully occupied courtyard his domain. He entered it without permission. Therefore Rashi explained the acquisition as agav rather than courtyard acquisition. It is thus clearly proven that agav works for the property of a deceased convert.
There is nevertheless a distinction between ownerless property and property received as a gift. Regarding a gift we rule that agav does not require the movable property to be physically gathered upon the land, as explained in Shulchan Aruch, siman 202. Regarding ownerless property, however, it certainly must be physically gathered there. If one takes possession of the property of a deceased convert, he obviously cannot thereby acquire through agav every ownerless object in the world. The reason appears to be that ordinary agav requires the giver to say, "Acquire it together with the land." With ownerless property there is no giver who can say this. Even if the acquirer himself says, "I wish to acquire it together with the land," that statement is ineffective, because the formula "go and acquire together with the land" requires another person transferring ownership. Where the movables are physically gathered upon the land, however, the formula is unnecessary, as Rambam writes in chapter 3 of Hilchos Mechirah and as Shulchan Aruch rules in siman 202, seif 2. Maggid Mishneh explains that the Gemara's statement that agav does not require the movables to be gathered refers to where the giver says, "Go and acquire together with the land"; without such a statement, they must be physically gathered there. Since items physically within the land are regarded like the land itself, as Sema writes in siman 202, this also works for ownerless property: by acquiring the land, he acquires the movables that are gathered within it. This also explains why the Gemara in Bava Kamma specifically says that the cow was lying in the field. If agav never required physical presence, that detail would be unnecessary. Because there is no other transferor to say, "Go and acquire together with the land," the cow must be physically present. This supports Rambam's rule, even though Maggid Mishneh, Ran, and the Rosh question his distinction.
According to this, however, there is a difficulty from Bava Metzia, daf 11. The Gemara states that courtyard acquisition requires the owner to stand beside his field. R' Abba challenged Ulla from the incident involving Rabban Gamliel and the elders, where Rabban Gamliel said: "The tithe that I will measure is given to Yehoshua, and its place is rented to him," etc. R' Yehoshua and R' Akiva were not standing beside Rabban Gamliel's field. Ulla answered that Rabban Gamliel transferred the movable property to them through agav. R' Zeira accepted this, but R' Abba did not. Rava said that R' Abba was correct not to accept it: did they not have a sudar through which to acquire by chalipin? Rather, the benefit of choosing to whom to give the tithes is not money for purposes of acquisition by chalipin; likewise, it is not money for purposes of acquisition through agav. Rashi explains that this right is not property belonging to Rabban Gamliel. He merely made the produce ownerless toward them, and when he rented its location to them, their courtyards acquired it as with other ownerless property.
According to what I wrote, that ownerless property can be acquired through agav when it is physically gathered upon the land, what is Rava's question from chalipin? Chalipin certainly does not work because the right to choose the recipient is not money and therefore is not the owner's property to transfer. But agav should work perfectly well, because after it becomes ownerless the recipients could acquire it themselves through agav, just as they could acquire ownerless property through a courtyard.
It appears that this can be answered according to the Shitah Mekubetzes there, which writes concerning the statement that the benefit of choosing the recipient is not money: even if one holds that it is money, it is not sufficiently significant money to be acquired through chalipin. Thus the sugya can accord both with the view that this right is not money and with the view that it is money. Although it cannot be acquired through chalipin or agav because those are weak forms of kinyan, if one person gave another a maneh and transferred to him the right to choose the recipient of these fruits, he would acquire it. Otherwise the Gemara should simply have said that the right is not money and cannot be acquired. By saying that it is "not money to acquire through chalipin," it implies that in some forms it can indeed be acquired. These words are compelled, because Rava explicitly holds in Nedarim, daf 85, that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money. Rather, Rava means that even though it is money, it is too slight a monetary right to be acquired through the weak kinyanim of chalipin and agav. See Responsa Maharit, Yoreh De'ah, siman 5, and what I wrote in siman 350, seif katan 1. According to this, R' Yehoshua and R' Akiva could not acquire the produce on their own as ownerless property according to Rava, because Rava holds that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money and therefore the produce still has an owner. Yet the owner cannot transfer this slight monetary right through the weak kinyanim of chalipin or agav. Thus it remains the owner's property and is not hefker.
With this, the words of the Rif can also be reconciled. In the first chapter of Bava Metzia he rules that one must stand beside one's courtyard even where another person is transferring ownership, unlike Rav Pappa there, who says that a conscious transferor is different. Rambam and Shulchan Aruch in siman 200 likewise rule this way. The commentators asked: if so, the incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders must have operated through agav. But since we rule that the benefit of choosing the recipient is not money, the owner cannot transfer it, and it also cannot have been acquired through the courtyard because they did not stand beside the field. Shach in siman 203 similarly challenges the Rama, who writes that this right cannot be acquired through chalipin, based on Hagahos Maimoniyos in the name of Baal HaItur, while Baal HaItur elsewhere writes that merely renting the location would not suffice without agav. If so, he apparently understands the Rabban Gamliel episode as agav, which would imply that this right is acquired through agav. According to what I wrote, the Rif and Rambam hold that the benefit of choosing the recipient is not money at all. It is therefore ownerless with respect to the recipients, who can acquire it themselves through agav when the produce is physically gathered upon the rented ground. Rava, however, holds in Nedarim that the benefit is money, so R' Yehoshua and R' Akiva cannot acquire it on their own as hefker. Since the owner likewise cannot transfer this slight right through the weak kinyanim of chalipin or agav, Rava rejects the agav explanation. The words of Baal HaItur are also reconciled: he can hold that the benefit is not money, so the owner cannot transfer it at all, even by inheritance, as Rama writes in siman 276, but when the produce is physically within their domain the recipients can acquire it themselves through agav as hefker.
According to this, Rav Pappa's answer is also well understood. The Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Metzia asks against Rav Pappa, who says that a conscious transferor is different: if the benefit of choosing the recipient is not money, there is no conscious transferor at all. Mordechai answers that because Rabban Gamliel also transferred the courtyard to them, with respect to the courtyard there was a conscious transferor. According to what I wrote, Rav Pappa's answer is entirely proper because he can hold like Rava that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money. Agav and chalipin are weak kinyanim and cannot transfer that slight right, but a courtyard is a strong acquisition, and therefore it can acquire through a conscious transferor. The Rif and Rambam, however, hold that the benefit is not money and there is no conscious transferor at all. Therefore they cannot explain the incident as Rav Pappa does and require the acquirer to stand beside the courtyard even where another person seeks to transfer ownership. The Rosh, who rules like Rav Pappa in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, follows his own view that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money, as he writes in HaIsh Mekadesh in accordance with Rabbeinu Chananel. Thus each authority follows his own broader position.
There is still room for scrutiny. The incident of Rabban Gamliel and the elders took place in the year of biur, when Rabban Gamliel was required to remove the priestly, Levite, and poor gifts from his possession, as Tosafos write there. Even according to the view that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money, the owner has only the value of that benefit, not ownership of the body of the gifts. Tosafos in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, daf 7, regarding the rule that if a Kohen seizes a doubtful firstborn animal it is not removed from him, ask why the original owner cannot say that he wishes to give it to another Kohen. They answer that according to the view that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money, the owner can claim only the value of that benefit. If so, even if the benefit is money, how could R' Yehoshua and R' Akiva acquire the body of the produce through courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor? Rabban Gamliel possessed a transferable monetary interest only in the value of the benefit, not in the body of the gifts.
According to Ritva at the end of HaIsh Mekadesh, who writes that one who steals terumah, according to the view that the benefit of choosing the recipient is money, must pay double according to the full value of the terumah because the entire terumah is regarded as the owner's property through that monetary right, Rav Pappa's position is understood. If the benefit is money, all of Rabban Gamliel's produce is regarded as his property and can be acquired through a courtyard with a conscious transferor. According to Tosafos in the first chapter of Bava Metzia, however, even when the benefit is money the owner has only the value of that benefit, and the difficulty remains. Ritva's view itself also revives Tosafos's question in the case where a Kohen seized the doubtful firstborn. This requires further study. It therefore appears that according to Rav Pappa, R' Yehoshua and R' Akiva acquired through both courtyard and agav. The body of the produce, which had no owner, was acquired through agav like other hefker because it was physically gathered upon the land. The monetary value of the benefit of choosing the recipient, which belonged to Rabban Gamliel and could not be acquired through the weak kinyan of agav, was acquired through courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor. This also resolves Shach's question in siman 200 against the Rosh. The Rosh rules like Rav Pappa that the Rabban Gamliel episode involved courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor, yet in the first chapter of Kiddushin, daf 26, the Gemara seeks to prove that land sold to one person can carry movable property given as a gift to another through agav from that very incident. If according to Rav Pappa the incident involved only courtyard acquisition, how can it prove agav? According to what I wrote, even Rav Pappa requires agav for the body of the produce. The courtyard with a conscious transferor acquired only the monetary value of Rabban Gamliel's benefit of choosing the recipient. Therefore the incident remains a valid proof for kinyan agav.
After I wrote these words, a great scholar replied to me as follows: It appears to me that there is a clear proof from the Gemara in the first chapter of Kiddushin, daf 27, which asks whether land can be given to one person and movables to another through agav. Why does the Gemara not ask the reverse case, where one acquires land from one owner and movables from another owner, and the acquirer says that he wishes to acquire these movables through agav of that person's land? It must be that we follow entirely the intent of the transferor rather than the intent of the acquirer, and therefore ownerless movables cannot be acquired through agav. He offered another proof from Tosafos in Elu Metziyos, Bava Metzia, daf 26, s.v. "where he remained silent," who ask why the courtyard of the owner of the heap does not acquire for him. Why did Tosafos not ask that he should acquire through agav? It must be that agav does not apply to ownerless property, and Tosafos's answer there would likewise not answer a question based on agav. End of his words. This is not a refutation. Regarding land and movable property belonging to two different people, I certainly agree that the distinct owners divide the acquisitions. If one buys adjoining land from one owner with no boundary separating them, one act of chazakah acquires all of it. If he buys from two different owners, even without a boundary separating the fields, he requires a separate chazakah for each, because the different ownerships divide them. My argument that ownerless property can be acquired through agav when physically gathered upon the land is because the movables are then like part of the land itself. Where there are two different owners, even contiguous property is divided by their separate ownership. Therefore the Gemara's failure to ask the reverse case proves nothing.
His proof from Tosafos in Elu Metziyos is likewise no proof. Every ordinary kinyan, including meshichah, mesirah, chazakah, and agav, requires intent to acquire. A person's courtyard is different because it can acquire for him without his knowledge. Therefore Tosafos ask why the courtyard should not acquire for the owner of the heap without his awareness. They could not ask from agav, because agav requires intent to acquire. See Responsa Maharit, part 1, siman 150, who writes that a person's hand is superior to hoeing because we find that a person's courtyard acquires without his knowledge, and if his courtyard acquires without his knowledge, all the more so his hand; hoeing, however, works through chazakah rather than through automatic acquisition. See there. Agav is like other kinyanim and requires intent. See what I wrote in siman 261, seif katan 2.
I later found explicit support for our position in the novellae of the Rashba, that ownerless property can indeed be acquired through agav. See Bava Kamma, daf 52, where Rashba writes: "He likewise did not acquire the document because he did not take possession with intent for the document." This means that once he did take possession of the land, he could compel delivery of the document wherever it was, because the document is the tether of the land. End of his words. One who acquires the land and thereby acquires the document wherever it is does so only through agav, as explained in HaSefinah, Bava Basra, daf 86, in the Baraisa concerning three types of documents. The discussion in Bava Kamma concerns the property of a deceased convert. Thus it is explicit that agav works in ownerless property. Although I wrote that ownerless property ordinarily requires the movable property to be physically gathered upon the land, a document that serves as the tether of the land is regarded like the land itself and does not require the formula "acquire the document together with the land." This is clear.
