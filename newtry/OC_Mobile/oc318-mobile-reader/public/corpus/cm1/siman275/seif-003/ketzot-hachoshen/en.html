Once he struck it with one blow of a hoe, he acquires the whole. I was uncertain whether one who takes possession of ownerless property of a deceased convert and movable property is located within it acquires the movables through agav. Perhaps acquisition by agav works only where another person consciously transfers ownership, while one who acquires on his own cannot use agav. The practical difference arises where courtyard acquisition is unavailable, such as an unguarded area where the acquirer does not stand nearby and did not say, 'My field shall acquire for me.'
At first glance there is proof from houses acquired by chazakah in the estate of a convert, as indicated in Bava Batra 33 concerning two houses, one inside the other. According to authorities who treat a house as an item detached and then affixed, with the law of movable property as explained in siman 95, and according to what I wrote there in seif katan 3 citing Magen Avraham, siman 637, such a house must be acquired through agav. This would prove that ownerless property can be acquired through agav. Yet one can answer that the house is acquired through courtyard acquisition once the person acquires the ground beneath it, in accordance with what I wrote in siman 202 that acquisition of the courtyard and of the gift can arrive simultaneously.
There is, however, a clear proof that ownerless property can be acquired by agav from Bava Kamma 117. Rabbi Eliezer and the Sages dispute whether one who stole land that was then swept away by a river must replace it, depending on whether land can be stolen. Rav Pappa explains the baraita about one who stole a cow that was swept away by a river as a case where he stole a field containing a cow lying in it. Rabbi Eliezer follows his view that land can be stolen, while the Sages follow theirs. Rashi explains that the robber did not physically pull the cow. According to Rabbi Eliezer, because the field is acquired to the robber for purposes of liability, the cow lying in it is acquired together with the field through agav, and the robber must pay. According to the Sages, he acquires neither the field nor the cow. In a robber's case there is no conscious transferor. The robber nevertheless needs an acquisition to become liable for accidents just as a purchaser needs an acquisition to acquire. Rashi says he acquires the cow through agav. This proves that agav works even in self-acquisition from ownerless-like property.
Rashi did not explain that the cow was acquired through the courtyard, even though the robber acquires the field for purposes of liability, because courtyard acquisition depends on a person's legal domain. A robber acquires the land only for liability for unavoidable loss and has no legitimate proprietary domain in it. Although in cases of property susceptible to theft the robber pays according to the time of theft and need not pay for use, as explained in simanim 363 and 371, that does not make the unlawfully occupied courtyard his domain. He entered without permission. Hence Rashi correctly uses agav rather than courtyard acquisition.
There is nevertheless an important distinction between ownerless property and a gift. For a gift, we rule that movables need not be physically gathered upon the land for agav acquisition, as stated in siman 202. For ownerless property they certainly must be gathered there. If one acquired the estate of a convert, he clearly cannot say that by agav he intends to acquire every ownerless object in the world. The reason is that ordinary agav without physical presence requires the transferor to say, 'Acquire it together with the land.' With ownerless property there is no transferor who can say this. Even if the acquirer himself says, 'I wish to acquire it through agav,' that cannot substitute for another person's instruction. But where the movables are physically gathered upon the land, Rambam, chapter 22 of Hilchot Mechirah, holds that no statement of 'acquire through agav' is necessary. Maggid Mishneh explains that the Gemara's rule that agav does not require the movables to be gathered applies where the transferor expressly says, 'Acquire them together with the land,' whereas without that formula the movables must be physically there. When the movables are gathered upon the land they are treated as part of the land itself. Therefore self-acquisition from ownerless property works in that form as well. This also explains why Bava Kamma specifically says the cow was lying within the field. If agav never required physical presence, that detail would be unnecessary. But because no transferor exists to say, 'Acquire through agav,' the cow must be gathered there. This is strong proof for Rambam's distinction between agav with and without physical presence, despite Maggid Mishneh, Ran, and Rosh questioning Rambam's rule that gathered movables do not require an express agav formula.
A difficulty arises from Bava Metzia 11. The Gemara requires a person to stand next to his field for courtyard acquisition. Rabbi Abba challenges Ulla from Rabban Gamliel's tithe distribution, where Rabban Gamliel said, 'The tithe that I will measure is given to Yehoshua, and its place is rented to him,' and similarly concerning Rabbi Akiva. They were not standing beside Rabban Gamliel's field. Ulla answered sharply that the transaction there was through agav. Rabbi Zeira accepted this, but Rabbi Abba did not. Rava said Rabbi Abba was correct not to accept it: did they have no sudar with which to acquire by chalipin? Rather, the benefit of choosing the recipient of tithes is not monetary property that can be acquired by chalipin, and similarly is not monetary property that can be acquired through agav. Rashi explains that the entitlement was not Rabban Gamliel's property. He merely rendered it ownerless toward the recipients, and by renting them its location the courtyard acquired it for them like other hefker.
According to what I have written, if hefker can be acquired through agav when physically gathered upon the land, why does Rava compare agav to chalipin? Chalipin certainly fails because the right of choosing the recipient is not money and therefore is not the owner's transferable property. But agav could still work through the recipients' self-acquisition of the hefker, just as a courtyard can acquire hefker.
This can be answered from Shitah Mekubetzet there. It writes that even if one holds that the benefit of choosing the recipient is monetary, it is not sufficiently substantial money to be acquired by chalipin. The sugya can therefore fit both the view that it is not money and the view that it is money. Although it cannot be acquired by chalipin or agav because those are relatively weak modes of acquisition, if one gave a person a maneh and transferred his entitlement in the produce, the recipient could acquire it. Otherwise the Gemara would have said simply that the entitlement is not money and cannot be acquired at all. The formulation, 'not money to acquire by chalipin,' implies that there are modes through which it can be acquired. This is necessary because Rava explicitly holds in Nedarim 85 that the right of choosing the recipient is money. Rava therefore means: even if it is money, it is too slight a proprietary interest for the weaker kinyanim of chalipin and agav.
Accordingly, Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Akiva could not acquire the property on their own as hefker according to Rava, because Rava holds that Rabban Gamliel retained a monetary proprietary interest in the selection right. The property therefore still had an owner. Rabban Gamliel, however, could not transfer that slight proprietary interest through the weaker acquisitions of chalipin or agav. This also reconciles Rif's position. Rif requires standing beside one's field even when another person consciously transfers ownership, rejecting Rav Pappa's distinction that a conscious transferor is different. Commentators asked: if so, how can the Rabban Gamliel episode be explained through agav, when we rule that the choice benefit is not money and the owner cannot transfer it, while courtyard acquisition should fail because the recipients were not beside the field? According to what I wrote, Rif and Rambam hold that the benefit of choosing the recipient is not money at all. It is therefore ownerless with respect to the recipients, who can acquire it themselves through agav when the produce is gathered on the rented land. Rava himself, who holds that the benefit is money, cannot use that explanation, because the property still belongs to Rabban Gamliel and the slight proprietary interest cannot be transferred by weak modes of acquisition.
The same resolves Baal HaItur's view. Shach in siman 203 challenges Rama's ruling, from Hagahot Maimoniyot in the name of Baal HaItur, that the choice benefit cannot be acquired through chalipin. Baal HaItur elsewhere writes that mere rental of the place would not have acquired in the Rabban Gamliel case without agav. Thus he evidently explains that episode as agav. How then can he say the choice benefit is not acquired by chalipin but is acquired by agav? According to what I wrote, Baal HaItur can hold that the choice benefit is not money and cannot be transferred by the owner at all. Rama similarly rules in siman 276 that it cannot even be inherited. But when the produce itself is physically in the recipients' possession, they can acquire it on their own through agav as ownerless property. Shach himself distinguishes that although the owner cannot transfer a nonproprietary choice right when it is in another person's hands, a person may obtain it when it is physically in his own control.
Mordechai in the first chapter of Bava Metzia asks against Rav Pappa's answer, 'a conscious transferor is different': if the choice benefit is not money, there is no conscious transferor at all and the recipients should be acquiring on their own. Mordechai answers that because Rabban Gamliel transferred the courtyard to them as well, with respect to the courtyard there was a conscious transferor. According to what I wrote, Rav Pappa's answer works particularly well if, like Rava, he holds the choice benefit is money. Agav and chalipin are weak and cannot transfer that slight interest, but courtyard acquisition is a stronger kinyan and can work through a conscious transferor. Therefore, if the right is monetary, Rabban Gamliel's conscious transfer through the rented courtyard is effective. Rif and Rambam, who hold the right is not money, cannot explain the episode that way and accordingly reject Rav Pappa's distinction, requiring the recipient to stand beside the courtyard even when there is a conscious transferor. Rosh, who rules like Rav Pappa, follows his own view in chapter HaIsh Mekadesh that the choice benefit is money, in accordance with Rabbeinu Chananel. Thus all the approaches fit their broader positions.
There remains another difficulty. The Rabban Gamliel episode occurred in the year of removal, when he had to remove the priestly, Levite, and poor tithes from his possession. Even according to the view that the choice benefit is money, the owner has only the economic value of that choice, not ownership of the entire produce. Tosafos in Bava Metzia 7, regarding a Kohen who seizes a doubtful firstborn animal, explain that even according to the view that the owner's choice right is money, the owner can claim only the value of his choice, not the whole gift. If so, how could Rabbi Yehoshua and Rabbi Akiva acquire the body of the produce through a courtyard and conscious transferor under Rav Pappa? Rabban Gamliel had conscious transferable ownership only in the value of the choice right, not in the body of the gifts.
Ritva at the end of HaIsh Mekadesh writes that according to the view that the choice benefit is money, a thief who steals terumah must pay double according to the full value of the terumah, because the entire terumah is considered the owner's property through the monetary value of his choice. According to Ritva, Rav Pappa's explanation is straightforward: if the choice right is money, all the produce is treated as Rabban Gamliel's and can be transferred through courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor. Yet this creates a difficulty with Tosafos in Bava Metzia 7, who hold that even according to the view that choice benefit is money the owner can claim only its limited value.
It therefore appears that according to Rav Pappa the acquisition in Rabban Gamliel's episode operated through both courtyard and agav. The body of the produce, insofar as it had no owner, was acquired through agav like other hefker, because it was physically gathered on the transferred ground. The monetary value of the choice right, which belonged to Rabban Gamliel and cannot be acquired through the weak kinyan of agav, was acquired through the courtyard with a conscious transferor, which is a strong acquisition. This also resolves Shach's question in siman 200 against Rosh. Rosh rules like Rav Pappa that the Rabban Gamliel episode worked through courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor. Yet Kiddushin 26 asks whether land in a sale can carry movable property given as a gift through agav and resolves it from the Rabban Gamliel episode. If Rav Pappa explains the episode only through courtyard acquisition, how can it prove agav? According to what I wrote, even Rav Pappa must use agav for the body of the produce. The courtyard acquisition with a conscious transferor transfers only the monetary value of the owner's choice right. Thus the episode remains a proof for agav. As for the Gemara's attempt to prove that the movables must be physically gathered there from Rabban Gamliel's renting their exact location, our approach indeed requires physical presence for hefker. But the Gemara rejects that proof on another ground, namely that the exact location was rented merely to spare the recipients inconvenience. The remaining agav proof is still valid.
After I wrote this, a distinguished scholar responded that there is a clear proof against me from Kiddushin 27, which asks whether land can be given to one person and movables to another through agav. Why does it not ask the reverse, where one acquires land from one owner and movables from another and the acquirer says he wishes to acquire the movables through agav of the first owner's land? This shows, he argued, that agav depends entirely on the transferor's intent, and therefore cannot acquire hefker. He further cited Tosafos, Bava Metzia 26, s.v. 'where he remained silent,' who ask why a courtyard should not acquire a found object for the owner of a heap. Why did they not ask that he acquire it through agav? Their answer concerning courtyard acquisition would not address agav.
This is not a refutation. Regarding land and movables owned by two different people, I agree that the distinct owners divide the acquisitions. Where one buys adjoining fields from one person, one act of chazakah can acquire all when no boundary separates them, whereas fields bought from two different owners require a separate chazakah for each even without a physical boundary, because the distinct ownerships divide them. My claim about hefker is only that where movables are physically gathered on the land, they are treated as part of the land itself. With two owners, the distinct ownerships prevent that unity. As for Tosafos in Bava Metzia 26, ordinary modes of acquisition such as meshichah, mesirah, chazakah, and agav require intent to acquire. A person's courtyard is exceptional in that it acquires without his knowledge. Hence Tosafos ask only about the courtyard, which could acquire automatically. Agav would require the owner's intent, and therefore their failure to ask about agav proves nothing. Maharit, part 1 siman 150, likewise distinguishes the hand and courtyard, which acquire without awareness, from ordinary chazakah. See what I wrote in siman 261, seif katan 2.
I later found Rashba's novellae explicitly supporting my position that hefker can be acquired through agav. In Bava Kamma 52 he writes that where one took possession of the land but not the document, the document is nonetheless acquired wherever it is, because the document is the tether of the land. A document attached to land in this way is acquired through agav, as is clear from Bava Batra 86 concerning the three types of documents. The Bava Kamma passage concerns the estate of a deceased convert. Thus Rashba explicitly recognizes agav acquisition in hefker. Although I have written that ordinary hefker agav requires the movables to be physically gathered upon the land, a document evidencing title to the land is itself considered like the land because it is the land's tether. Therefore no express agav statement is needed. This is clear.
