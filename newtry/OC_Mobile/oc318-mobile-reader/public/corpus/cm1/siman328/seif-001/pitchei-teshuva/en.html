For thus he writes. See Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 168, who writes that the phrase "for thus he writes to him" is not the reason that a sharecropper must continue working where the field is capable of producing a minimum crop. Rather, it explains the exemption where the field cannot produce that minimum. Pure logic would otherwise require the worker to perform whatever amount of work he undertook. Chavot Yair therefore ruled concerning Reuven, who acquired a vineyard in another city and contracted with Levi, who lived there, to cultivate and guard it for one half of the produce as his compensation. A severe frost destroyed nearly all the blossoms, and Levi refused to work the vineyard, claiming that it would barely yield his expenses. If the parties entered a binding agreement, Levi must perform according to his undertaking whether the yield is small or large, because contemporary practice no longer follows the Talmudic sharecropping formula and standard sharecropper document. Under Talmudic law, if the field is capable of yielding two seah, the worker must cultivate it. If the worker says it will not yield that amount and the owner says it will, experts must estimate. If experts estimate that it will produce two seah and the worker is therefore compelled to continue, he may satisfy the owner by buying in the market the owner's share, rather than necessarily producing those very crops, because the claim "I want produce from my own land" is not accepted in the Gemara. This applies initially, so long as Levi remains bound and has not withdrawn from the employment arrangement. But if he withdraws, then unless he was bound by a formal oath or especially binding undertaking, a worker may retract even from a ten year written employment agreement under siman 333. Furthermore, if he already neglected the vineyard, then under contemporary practice, where the standard clause "if I leave it fallow and do not work" is neither written nor customarily understood as though written, Levi is generally exempt. Mere failure to work the vineyard is only prevention of another's profit, unless the vineyard itself was physically damaged by being left uncultivated. See there.
