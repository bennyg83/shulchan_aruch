Unless he demands payment. Regarding a hired worker, the employer violates the prohibition against delaying wages only when the worker demands payment. It requires study whether a demand made through an agent suffices when the worker did not authorize the agent to receive the money, but only to demand it. If the agent merely happens to pass the employer and demands payment without agreeing to bring the money to the worker, the employer can arguably say that the worker must come for his money, because unlike a robber he is not obligated to transport payment after the creditor. The precise boundary requires study, for one could otherwise say that the employer need not walk even a single step toward the worker, and he is also not bound to entrust his money to another person. If the worker appointed an agent before witnesses to receive the money, then the employer should apparently be able to pay the agent at the worker's risk. Even then one might ask whether the employer can say, 'Perhaps you will later produce witnesses,' as discussed in Shevuot 37, though I incline that once two witnesses establish the agency this concern does not prevent payment. If the messenger is a minor, however, and the worker merely sends the minor to demand payment, there is no agency in halachah and perhaps no valid demand. Even if the worker had earlier said before witnesses that payment to this minor would count as payment to him, if no demand was made then and the minor later demands it, the employer may still not violate the prohibition because the later demand itself is not legal agency. Where the employer has nothing with which to pay, he does not violate the prohibition. It requires study whether ready ability to borrow, or possession of an item standing for sale that can readily be sold, is treated as having funds. It also appears that less than a perutah of delayed wages may not be treated as a Torah-level half-measure in the ordinary sense, because the elapsed time does not combine with later time to form one violation. The same structural point resembles what I wrote concerning bal yera'eh. In a contractor relationship the matter may differ and deserves study. If a worker was hired for a term and part of the wage agreement was that the employer would lend him money to buy a cow for profit, and the employer later refuses because cows have doubled in price, that is not necessarily a valid excuse. The employee is entitled to what was stipulated. Even an unusual price rise may not undo the obligation, especially in a period when changing currency values already made such developments foreseeable. Though an obligation merely 'to give' can sometimes resemble an ineffective kinyan ethen, this is different in an employment contract, where the promised benefit forms part of the compensation. If specific performance is impossible, the employer may have to pay the value of the benefit of the promised loan, similar to valuation of deferred payment in Makkot. Although charging for waiting is prohibited as interest, the employee could have profited through other permissible commercial uses, and the value of that lost benefit may be payable. This requires careful study.
