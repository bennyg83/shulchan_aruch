Because of the market enactment. Otherwise no one would be willing to buy movable property from another, as explained at the beginning of siman 60.
If he wrote d'akni, the second lender can say, etc. Where the debtor acquired the movable property after borrowing from the first lender but before borrowing from the second, if the first lender had no d'akni clause the property would be bound only to the second. But if the first lender had d'akni, then even if the second also had d'akni the first has priority, as explained in siman 104. One might ask why this seif teaches the absence of the market enactment in a complicated case involving property acquired between the two loans, rather than simply where the debtor already owned the movable property before both loans and pledged it to each through agav. The formulation adds a further novelty: even though the property did not exist in the debtor's hands at the time of the first loan but did exist before the second, and one might therefore compare it to land acquired between two loans, where the second could have a stronger claim, nevertheless, because the first lender also received d'akni, the earlier lien prevails. Baal HaTerumot similarly presents both the simpler and the more novel case.
There is no market enactment in this. Market protection applies to purchasers, who would otherwise refrain from commerce, and likewise to recipients of gifts because a gift is treated like a sale for this purpose. It does not apply between two lenders competing over the same collateral.
