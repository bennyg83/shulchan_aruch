If he wrote an after-acquired-property clause, etc. Why does the Mechaber not state the simpler case, where the debtor owned movables at the time of the first loan, encumbered them through land, and a later creditor came and collected them, in which case the later creditor's collection is ineffective? The Sema answers that the Mechaber teaches incidentally that we should not say that because the movables were acquired between the two loans they are encumbered only to the later creditor. That follows the Sema's own approach that without an after-acquired-property clause later property is indeed encumbered to the later creditor. But according to what I wrote above in simanim 104 and 112, following the Shach, later-acquired land is encumbered to the first creditor. At first glance, then, the express after-acquired-property clause adds no novelty. There is nevertheless an important distinction. With land, whose lien is d'oraisa, if Reuven lends in Nisan without an after-acquired-property clause and the borrower acquires land in Iyar, the moment the land enters the borrower's possession Reuven's preexisting Torah lien can attach automatically. But ordinary movables carry no inherent lien. If Reuven lends in Nisan with a movables-through-land clause but without after-acquired property, and the borrower acquires new movables in Iyar, those movables do not become encumbered to Reuven at all. Movables are not inherently subject to the lien, and Reuven has no after-acquired-property clause to reach them. If the borrower later borrows from Shimon and gives Shimon a valid movables-through-land lien, the movables are encumbered to Shimon. Thus the Mechaber specifically needed to state that the first creditor had an after-acquired-property clause. The Sema's formulation is therefore correct according to all opinions. There is another possible novelty. Many authorities hold that even when a later creditor precedes the earlier creditor in collecting, the later creditor benefits from the market-protection enactment and keeps what he collected. The Beit Yosef rejects this, as I wrote above in siman 60, reasoning that one should not protect a later creditor at the cost of closing the door before the earlier creditor. But if the relevant movables were acquired only after the first loan, one might argue that closing the door is weaker as to the first creditor. He did not even know that the borrower would later acquire these movables, so surely he did not extend the original loan in reliance upon them. Perhaps, then, we should protect the later creditor, as the Mabit suggests. The Mechaber therefore teaches that no market-protection enactment was made for a later creditor at all. We should not expand an enactment beyond its established bounds.
