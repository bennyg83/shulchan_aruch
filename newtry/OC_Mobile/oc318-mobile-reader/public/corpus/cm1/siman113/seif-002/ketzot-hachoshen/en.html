Tosafot explains the principle that a litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses. The Gemara in Perek Chazakat HaBatim asks whether a person may have transferred movables together with land, and answers that witnesses knew he never had land. Tosafot rejects the old custom of justifying the standard authorization formula, "I gave him four cubits in my courtyard," on the theory that every Jew owns four cubits in Eretz Yisrael. The Gemara shows that a person can truly own no land. Rabbeinu Tam explains why the formula nevertheless works today. Because the person himself admits that he owns land, and that admission is disadvantageous to him by allowing transfer of movables, his admission is binding even if witnesses contradict it. A litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses. We do not reject it merely because it appears factually false, just as acquisition by formal admission worked in the case of Issur the convert. Rabbi Chizkiyah asked that the Gemara should then always fear that the party made precisely such a false admission of land in order to transfer movables incidentally. Tosafot answers that the Gemara does not fear such an unusual fabrication. In our times, however, where such clauses are standard in virtually all instruments, the concern becomes common. Therefore even where witnesses say a person never owned land, one who sold him a cow or cloak can still be regarded as financially interested because the standard instrument may have included the formal land admission. The Shach adopts this practical rule.
