And slaves, etc. See Urim, where I cited Responsa Rosh. The matter requires study, because the Mechaber rules without qualification, following the Tur, who likewise states the law in accordance with his father the Rosh. Yet the Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, part 1, ruling 10, writes that according to the opinion that an acquisition of slaves together with land requires the slaves to be standing within the land, because slaves are considered movable property that moves of itself, the same physical-presence requirement applies to an encumbrance. This also appears from the Raavad there, ruling 8. He proves that an animal is not treated as self-moving movable property from the Bava Batra case of one who sold a cow or cloak, where the Gemara says that perhaps the debtor encumbered after-acquired movables through land. His proof makes sense only if, for purposes of lien as well as acquisition, self-moving movables would require the stricter rule. The matter therefore requires study.
The admission of a litigant, etc. The Sema writes that ordinarily a person is not presumed to lie, even though we do not know whether he owns land. His language implies that if witnesses establish clearly that he owns no land, his admission is ineffective. This also appears from the Rosh and from a responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef below in siman 202. But Tosafot in Bava Batra and the Baal HaTerumot appear otherwise. They ask, when the Gemara answers that there are witnesses that he never owned land, perhaps he admitted that he had land. They answer that we do not suspect such an extraordinary lie. This implies that if he did in fact make such an admission, it would be effective even though witnesses contradict him and establish that he never had land. The matter requires explanation. The principle that a person is believed concerning his own liability more than a hundred witnesses applies where he is not thereby imposing loss upon others. Here, by his admission he immediately prejudices future purchasers of the movables, because the creditor could later seize from them even though by strict law the movables were never encumbered. This resembles a debtor's grant of special credibility to a creditor at the time of a loan concerning purchasers who later buy property. The debtor cannot thereby impose liability upon persons who were not yet parties. How, then, can his false words create a right to seize from purchasers contrary to law? One could answer that with respect to movables already in his possession at the time of the lien, his admission is certainly effective. There is no problem of prejudicing others because he has a migo: he could simply give those movables away in a manner that allows the creditor to collect from them. If there is an asmachta concern, he can make a legally effective kinyan that removes the asmachta. But with movables that he acquires only later, he has no such migo, because a person cannot transfer something not yet in existence. Why, then, should his admission be believed to prejudice future purchasers? This could have explained the Gemara's structure. It first answers that the seller bought and immediately sold the item, and then asks perhaps he had an after-acquired-property clause. The Gemara answers that witnesses establish he had no land. It does not say that this replaces the first answer, but rather leaves the immediate-purchase answer in place, as Tosafot often explain in similar passages. Why is both needed? According to what we have written, if the seller had movables at the time, the answer that he owned no land would not suffice, because perhaps he falsely admitted ownership of land, and his admission could affect the movables then in his possession. The immediate-purchase circumstance is therefore needed. But as to the after-acquired-property concern, the testimony that he never had land does suffice, because a false admission contradicted by witnesses cannot give him power over future property he did not yet own and as to which he has no migo. This is what I would have said. However, it does not appear consistent with Tosafot, nor with Rambam, chapter 16 of Hilchot Edut, who does not require witnesses that the purchase and resale were immediate in this manner. We must therefore say differently. Even if the debtor falsely admits that he owns land and encumbers movables through it, why should the lien fail merely because the land does not actually exist? The law is that even if one makes his ox an apotiki, collection from it would be valid by strict law, and the reason a purchaser is protected is only lack of publicity. Here, however, the debtor's admission that he owns land and his creation of a movables-through-land lien are themselves embodied in testimony or writing that generate publicity. Thus, even if the statement about land is false, there is still sufficient public notice to protect future purchasers. The lien can therefore be enforced against them. Tosafot say only that we do not ordinarily suspect a person of making such a false admission, because the statement itself is a lie. But if he actually did make it, the admission can have legal effect because the concern of publicity has been satisfied. This is a clear and sound explanation. According to what I wrote above in siman 112, one could still say that Tosafot agree that where witnesses directly contradict the admission, the admission is void. Their question there would then be that perhaps the seller is indebted to relatives of the very witnesses who testify he had no land. Those witnesses are disqualified as against their relatives, while the seller's own admission that he owns land could still be effective. Tosafot answer that we do not suspect him of lying in that manner. This too fits the passage. The Knesset HaGedolah writes that many authorities regard as primary the reason that every Jew is presumed to own a share in land in Eretz Yisrael, and therefore one must be cautious in the case of a convert. We would then need to explain why the Gemara answers in the cow-and-cloak case that the debtor has no land, since it is strained to say the baraita speaks only of a convert. One can answer that the Gemara is seeking to establish the baraita according to some tannaitic view. The baraita may follow Rabbi Eliezer, who holds in Sukkah that land can be stolen. Under that view, because gentile nations forcibly conquered Eretz Yisrael, one cannot say that every Jew presently has an indefeasible land share there. According to our ruling that land cannot be stolen, however, the land remains halachically ours by ancestral title, and every Jew retains a share. The passage is therefore consistent.
