Which he sold or gave away, etc. The Tur explains that a creditor does not rely upon movables because the debtor can conceal or remove them. The Sema asks that even if the debtor makes movable property an apotiki, the creditor cannot seize it from a purchaser because the designation lacks publicity. The Tur should therefore have said that the reason is absence of publicity, rather than lack of reliance. The Sema's answer is strained. The Shach answers that the Tur needed the reliance rationale after the Geonic enactment, when movables once again became generally collectible from heirs like land. But I am surprised: what will he say about Rashi in Bava Kamma 33b? The Gemara asks rhetorically whether we can infer that if a borrower sold movables a creditor collects them, and Rashi explains: movables are not items that necessarily remain in existence, the lender does not rely upon them, and therefore why should he seize them from the purchaser? Rashi speaks about the law of the Gemara itself, before the Geonic enactment, yet gives precisely the Tur's reason that movables can disappear and the creditor does not rely upon them, rather than saying simply that there is no publicity. In truth, I do not understand the question against them. Under our present law, movables are generally not encumbered, but an express apotiki is plausibly different because the creditor specifically relied upon that item. Indeed, most authorities therefore hold that such an apotiki is collectible from heirs. When it comes to a purchaser, however, we say that the apotiki designation itself lacks publicity. Although the witnesses to the loan publicize the loan, they do not necessarily publicize that this particular movable item was designated as the creditor's apotiki. But if the basic law were that all movables become encumbered because creditors ordinarily rely upon them, what would "lack of publicity" mean? A written loan is publicized by its witnesses. Once a purchaser hears that the seller has a written debt, he would automatically know that the seller's movables are encumbered and would refrain from buying them. There would be no additional fact requiring separate publicity. Therefore the primary reason ordinary movables are not encumbered must be that the creditor does not rely upon them. Only where a particular item is expressly made an apotiki is there reliance, and then the purchaser is protected because that special designation lacks publicity. The matter is simple and correct.
Not as an asmachta. I have a doubt where the document encumbers "movables and land" without an agav clause, which nevertheless permits collection from heirs under the law of the Gemara as I wrote above in siman 107. Must the document also state "not as an asmachta"? On one hand, without such language the creditor may not truly rely upon the movables because an asmachta concern remains. On the other hand, there is no ordinary proprietary lien here in any event, since there is no agav clause. The entire basis of collection is that the express reference to movables shows the creditor relied upon them. If so, perhaps no further anti-asmachta language is necessary, because the reliance itself is already established. One cannot prove the matter from the rule that if the debtor makes his ox an apotiki the creditor collects it from heirs, even though the ordinary designation apparently lacks the phrase "not as an asmachta." An apotiki itself does not involve asmachta, as stated in Bava Metzia 66b. See the Rosh there. I have found no proof either way.
From now. See Urim, where I explained that here even the Tur agrees that "from now" is effective, although it does not cure every ordinary asmachta. This resembles the sale of a field discussed in the chapter Eizehu Neshekh, which the Gemara establishes where the party said "from now." The Rosh explains that because this form is lighter than ordinary asmachta, "from now" is sufficient. The effectiveness of "from now" applies to movables already in existence, which the debtor presently encumbers. But how can "from now" operate upon after-acquired property that is not yet in his possession? The lien can attach only when the property later enters his possession, and at that point it no longer operates "from now." This is clear. This helps explain the Gemara in Bava Batra in the discussion of one who sold a cow or cloak. The Gemara asks that perhaps the debtor encumbered movables through land, citing Rabbah's rule that such an encumbrance is effective, and Rav Chisda's qualification that the document must say "not as an asmachta" and so forth. Why does the Gemara insert Rav Chisda's qualification into the question? Rabbah's basic rule that movables can be encumbered through agav appears sufficient; Rav Chisda's anti-asmachta formula seems unrelated. The explanation is that the first questioner retained the Gemara's earlier answer that the debtor bought and sold immediately, but anticipated the next question concerning after-acquired property, as the sugya proceeds through one continuing line of objection. Without Rav Chisda's rule, one might have said that encumbering movables through land is effective only where the debtor says "from now," which removes the asmachta. If so, the answer that he bought and sold immediately would be sufficient, because an after-acquired-property lien cannot meaningfully operate "from now" on property not yet in the debtor's possession. The Gemara therefore first cites Rav Chisda, teaching that there is another way to remove the asmachta without "from now," namely by expressly writing "not as an asmachta." Once that possibility exists, the immediate-purchase answer no longer suffices, because perhaps the debtor expressly included an after-acquired-property clause together with the anti-asmachta formula. This also resolves another difficulty discussed above in siman 112. Why does the Gemara's answer that he bought and sold immediately apply to the cow or cloak but not to land? The flow is as though the Gemara says: now that Rav Chisda has taught that an anti-asmachta formula can cure the defect without "from now," we can no longer rely upon immediate purchase and resale, because an after-acquired-property clause may have been written. If "from now" were indispensable, immediate purchase and resale would still solve the movable case, since future movables cannot be encumbered "from now." Land, however, has no asmachta problem in the first place, and therefore an after-acquired-property clause can apply without "from now." Once Rav Chisda provides an alternative formula even for movables, the same concern exists there as with land. The passage is coherent. There remains a practical doubt whether a kinyan sudar itself suffices. We rule that a kinyan sudar generally operates from now, since the cloth immediately returns to its owner. According to those who hold that ordinary asmachta is cured by "from now," this might appear sufficient. Yet the authorities explain that for purposes of eliminating asmachta, a kinyan sudar is not automatically treated as an express "from now." If so, perhaps it also fails here. According to the Rosh, however, the matter may differ. He holds explicitly in Eizehu Neshekh that even an express "from now" does not cure an ordinary asmachta, but it does cure this lighter form. If the reason a kinyan sudar is not enough elsewhere is merely that ordinary "from now" is not enough, then here a kinyan sudar may indeed suffice. Similarly, a dated document is treated below in siman 258 as operating from now because the date of the document proves present effect. Perhaps, then, any dated document would be sufficient here. According to those who say "from now" generally nullifies asmachta, this cannot be automatic, because otherwise every dated document would cure even a full asmachta, a rule we do not find. One must say that for ordinary asmachta a mere date is not treated as an explicit "from now." But according to the Rosh and those who agree with him, who say "from now" itself does not cure ordinary asmachta but does cure this lighter case, it remains plausible that the date or kinyan sudar suffices here. The Bava Batra passage does not prove the contrary even though a lien document obviously has a date, because the Gemara's concern there is specifically after-acquired property, to which "from now" cannot apply, as explained. The matter therefore requires study. This also resolves a difficulty against the Baal HaTerumot, Gate 43, who cites Rav Hai Gaon as holding that if one expressly makes movables an apotiki, the creditor may collect them even from purchasers. The Gidulei Terumah asks that if so, the Bava Batra difficulty remains: why may one testify concerning a sold cow or cloak merely because the seller had no land? Perhaps he made the cow itself an express apotiki. According to what we have written, the Gemara never abandoned the answer that the seller bought and sold immediately. The only renewed difficulty was an after-acquired-property clause. An express apotiki cannot naturally be created over something not yet acquired, because one cannot designate as the exclusive source of payment an asset not yet in existence or possession. Thus there is no difficulty.
