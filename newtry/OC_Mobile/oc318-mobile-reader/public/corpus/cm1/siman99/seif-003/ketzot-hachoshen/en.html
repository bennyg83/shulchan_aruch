The debtor is not believed even though he has a migo that he could have transferred the property or denied possessing it. The ordinary presumption is that whatever a person possesses belongs to him. His claim that the property really belongs to someone else is therefore a migo against a strong possession presumption. Ran says that a migo based on a present power to perform a genuine legal act can sometimes defeat a presumption. For example, one who could actually give a get has more than an ordinary alternative plea. Why does the same not apply here, since the debtor could actually give the property away? The answer is that he does not truly wish to make an outright gift. If he did, he would lose the property permanently. He wants the other person to receive it only as a device and later return it. His actual alternative is therefore to arrange a sham deposit before showing the assets to beit din, or to deny possession entirely. That is an ordinary deceptive migo, not a genuine present legal act that independently changes the truth. This also answers a proof drawn from Tosafot concerning one who says a document is an accommodation document. Tosafot asks why he should not be believed through the migo that he could waive the debt. A real waiver is an actual legal act and therefore could overcome a possession presumption. Tosafot answers that he does not wish to waive and lose the debt for nothing. Once that is so, his only remaining alternatives are deceptive devices, and such a migo does not overcome the strong presumption that property found in a person's possession is his. The distinction between a true present power and a merely manipulative alternative is therefore essential.
