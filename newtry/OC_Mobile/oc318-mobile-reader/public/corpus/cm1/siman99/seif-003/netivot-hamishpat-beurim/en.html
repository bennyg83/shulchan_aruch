See Shach, seif katan 13, who writes that there must also be witnesses who saw the item in the holder's possession, because otherwise he could claim that he purchased it by a migo that he returned it. It appears that witnesses of prior possession are required, according to the Shach, only where Levi's debt is oral. If Levi's debt is documented, such witnesses are unnecessary. The Shach explains in siman 72, seif katan 97, that a holder is believed to claim purchase by a migo of return, even though a purchaser does not stand in the position of witnesses who saw the item, because the holder could have said that he returned it to the claimant and that the claimant then sold it to the purchaser. Here he has no such migo. If he said, “I returned it to Reuven and Reuven sold it to Levi,” he would indeed exempt himself from Reuven by the claim of return, but he would remain liable to Levi on Levi's documented debt. By contrast, when he says that he purchased it from Reuven and it then came to Levi in satisfaction of Levi's debt, he exempts himself from both. There is therefore no migo. Where Levi's debt is oral, however, there is a proper migo: he could exempt himself from Reuven by claiming return and from Levi by claiming payment. It further appears that where Levi has already seized the object for his debt, witnesses of prior possession are necessary, but where the object is still in Shimon's possession and Shimon has a migo that he could give it to Reuven, he is believed, because Shimon has no chazakah of ownership in the object and the migo therefore does not stand against a chazakah. The Shach's basic ruling is difficult. Where Shimon himself has a chazakah, that chazakah can indeed benefit his creditor or purchaser, and we may advance the claim of purchase for them, because the chazakah itself gives us affirmative evidence that Shimon truly purchased it. But where Shimon has no chazakah and would be believed to claim purchase only by a migo that he could have returned it, the migo is merely evidence that his present claim is true, since if he wished to lie he could have made another claim. If Shimon himself admits to Reuven that he did not purchase it, there is no evidentiary basis for a purchase claim. Why should beit din advance such a claim on behalf of another when it contradicts the original chazakah, since Reuven had prior possession? This is unlike the rule that we advance for purchasers the claim that a promissory note was paid by virtue of a migo that it was forged, even though the debtor admits that it was not paid. There, because the debtor could have claimed payment, he was not concerned about leaving the note with the creditor, as the Sema explains in siman 46, seif katan 2. The strength of the note is therefore weakened, and there is no affirmative proof that it remains unpaid, so it cannot be collected from purchasers. Nor is it comparable to the rule in siman 146 that land is not removed from a purchaser where the seller could have claimed that he purchased it by a migo that it never belonged to the claimant. There another reason applies. Taking the land from the purchaser is effectively taking from the seller, because the seller will become liable to reimburse the purchaser. Since it is tantamount to extracting from the seller, it can be done only with two witnesses that the land belonged to the claimant. The purchaser's own knowledge that it belonged to the claimant is only like one witness and is insufficient. In addition, at the time of sale the seller claimed that he had purchased it and that it was his, which is itself equivalent to his asserting the purchase claim. See Responsa Mahari Chagiz, part 1 siman 9, that even for heirs we do not advance a purchase claim where their father could have asserted purchase only by a migo of return. Certainly, then, we should not do so here where the person himself admits that he did not purchase it. Although the later authorities imply that even in such a case we do advance a purchase claim for heirs, even where the father would have been believed to claim purchase only by a migo of return, the reason appears to be as the Rosh writes in a responsum: because had their father made that claim the property could not have been taken from him, this is a case of “open your mouth for the mute.” We assume their father certainly would have asserted that claim and would have been believed by the migo. Here, by contrast, Shimon himself admits that the property is not his. Tumim likewise concludes as a matter of law that witnesses of prior possession are unnecessary. This also follows from the Mechaber and the other poskim, who did not mention such witnesses here.
