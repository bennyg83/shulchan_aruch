With witnesses. It appears to me that the case also requires witnesses who saw the item in his possession. The same applies to a broker and to items normally lent or rented. Without witnesses who saw them, the debtor could claim that he purchased them through a migo that he returned them. See the gloss to siman 72, seif 18, and below siman 297. Shach.
Broker. This applies specifically to a broker who does not buy and sell for himself, or to objects of a type that he commonly sells for others. Brokers who also buy and sell for themselves lose that presumption. Their property may be seized for their own debts until they prove that a specific item was given to them by another person to sell, and we do not suspect collusion. Radbaz I, 292. Knesset HaGedolah.
Are believed. Bedek HaBayit writes that this is only where the creditor makes a definite claim, and the Bach agrees. The Shach writes that in his view it applies even where the creditor is uncertain. A creditor has the legal status of a purchaser or heir in many contexts, and beit din advances claims for such parties. The possessor must therefore swear even against an uncertain claim, as stated in the gloss to siman 133, seif 5, regarding an heir. The end of that siman explains that the same is true of a purchaser. Bedek HaBayit follows its own position there that no oath is required on an uncertain claim. Since we rule there that an oath is required, the same applies here, unlike the Bach. See also siman 72, seif 34. The Taz inclines to the same view.
Two. The Tur concludes that if the claimant has only one witness that the object is his, this does not suffice to remove it from the creditor who seized it. The Sema explains that this applies also to the first case, and that we do not say the debtor is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear and must therefore pay, because that rule applies only where two witnesses would obligate him to pay without the claimant first swearing. Here, even with two witnesses the owner would have to swear in order to collect. The Shach finds this difficult. Even in the classic case of the silver ingot of Rabbi Abba, if two witnesses testified that one person grabbed the object and he claimed, "I grabbed my own," he must return it, but the other party would still afterward take a shevuat heset that it was his. The same is true in every case where one who cannot swear must pay. Siman 75 means only that the claimant cannot collect through two witnesses unless he first takes an oath, as in siman 90. There the collection comes through both the witnesses and the oath. Here, by contrast, with two witnesses he takes the item regardless, and any later oath arises only because the defendant then brings an oral claim. Perhaps the Sema understood the oath here as a prior oath while holding a sacred object, but that is certainly not so; he swears only afterward by shevuat heset. Thus, here too the ordinary rule of inability to swear should apply. The Tur's point is different: one witness cannot remove the item from the creditor who seized it because the creditor may say that it never belonged to the debtor. If the creditor contradicts the witness, he swears against that witness. The debtor cannot, through his own admission, create a liability toward the witness's version, because property in a person's possession is presumed to be his. This is clear. Shach.
