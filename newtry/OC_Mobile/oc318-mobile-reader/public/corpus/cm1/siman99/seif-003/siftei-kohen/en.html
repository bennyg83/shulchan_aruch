If it is known through witnesses that this article belongs to Reuven, etc. The witnesses must testify that it still belongs to Reuven, such as where they were present when Reuven delivered it to Shimon for sale. Shimon's admission alone is ineffective unless witnesses establish the nature of the delivery. This is the correct interpretation of the Rosh's responsum, contrary to the Bedek HaBayit and Bach in the name of Maharshal. The case also requires witnesses who saw the article in Shimon's possession. The same applies to a broker and to articles commonly lent or rented, for otherwise the possessor could claim purchase through a migo that he returned the article.
The broker or owner of the article is believed upon taking an oath. The Bedek HaBayit and Bach limit the oath to a case in which the creditor asserts certainty. In my view, even if the creditor is uncertain, a creditor is treated like a purchaser or heir, and beit din advances the claim that could have been made for them. The claimant must therefore swear even against an uncertain claim, just as an heir must swear in the parallel case. The same rule applies to a purchaser. Since our accepted ruling requires an oath there, it is required here as well.
And he has two witnesses, etc. The Tur concludes that one witness that the article belongs to the claimant does not suffice to remove it from the creditor who took it as security. The Sema explains that this is because even two witnesses would not entitle the owner to collect without an oath. This is astonishing. Even in the classic case of a seized ingot, two witnesses require restoration of the object, while the possessor may afterward demand a rabbinic oath concerning ownership. The rule that one who cannot take the required Torah oath must pay applies whenever two witnesses would establish the monetary obligation itself, even though a later rabbinic oath might arise from a separate claim. Here, however, the creditor can deny the single witness and swear against him. The debtor cannot harm the creditor through his own admission agreeing with that witness, because property in the debtor's possession is presumed to be his. Thus the Tur's ruling is correct, but not for the reason given by the Sema.
