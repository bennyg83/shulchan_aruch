Through artifice and scheming, etc. One might ask why this special rationale is needed. If the sale was made on condition that it be void whenever the creditor sought to collect, then once the creditor comes the sale is cancelled. The purchase money becomes a debt owed by the seller to the purchaser, and the creditor or wife should be able to reach that debt through the rule of Rabbi Natan. Rabbi Yehudah, son of the Rosh, expressly gives this reason in a responsum, and the same principle appears below in siman 111. Why then do we need the separate rule that because the arrangement is an artifice, Chazal nullify the scheme? It is strained to say that the land may have risen in value in the meantime, so that if the condition works the creditor can recover only the original purchase price while the land is now worth more. That would hardly justify describing the arrangement as such a wicked scheme that the sages must defeat it. The creditor originally had only the value represented by the purchase funds, and if no sale had occurred he likewise could not claim more than the debtor actually owned. The increase in the property's value does not sufficiently explain the language. It therefore seems that the condition may have been framed more strongly: if the wife or creditor seeks to collect, the sale is void and the purchase money becomes a gift to the seller. There would then be no debt in the seller's hands for the creditor to reach. Alternatively, after the wife's ketubah was created, the husband may have issued debt instruments to his sons. If the land sale is later voided, the only asset remaining in the seller's hands is movable money. The wife has no priority in ordinary movable property, and the heirs who already received it are possessors whose completed collection can stand. In such settings the special duty of beit din to frustrate fraudulent schemes is genuinely necessary. Chavot Yair, responsum 4, was asked about a man who gave his daughter a debt instrument for a fixed amount, collectible from that day but one hour before his death, and afterward married a woman and wrote her a ketubah. Upon his death the widow sought to collect, and the daughter produced her earlier debt instrument. The questioner thought that because the father did not merely create a sham but genuinely intended the daughter to collect before the future widow, the document should be valid and the daughter should have priority. Chavot Yair ruled that since the father's purpose was nevertheless to defeat the future wife's ketubah, the widow collects. He characterized the arrangement as an artifice, adding that the father suffered no real loss from it even once, and therefore his stratagem should not succeed. I searched for a proof or close parallel to this ruling and did not find one. It is true that Maharam, in a responsum cited by Mordechai in the chapter Get Pashut, explains why a wife collects her ketubah despite a deathbed gift designed to defeat it. Otherwise every husband could nullify the ketubah by giving his property on his deathbed to sons or relatives. Maharam concludes that whenever the circumstances show that a person is scheming to defeat a rabbinic enactment, he lacks the power to do so, just as Chazal strengthened liens against vows where necessary. If everyone could avoid a rabbinic enactment through such devices, the enactment would become a mockery. This somewhat supports Chavot Yair. But Maharam's case is materially different. A person making a deathbed gift loses nothing during his lifetime. He continues using the property as he wishes, and at death the wife would otherwise collect her ketubah. It is therefore easy and costless to devise a nominal gift to children or relatives whom he prefers. That is precisely the sort of scheme Chazal had to prevent. In Chavot Yair's case, by contrast, the father created a genuine and unconditional debt and lien in favor of his daughter. He thereby restricted his own property during life. He could no longer freely sell or encumber the assets without exposing them to the daughter's collection. Why would a person ordinarily impose such a substantial present disadvantage upon himself merely to defeat a woman he might later marry? The original question expressly states that this was a genuine obligation and not a nominal concealment device. I therefore do not understand Chavot Yair's statement that the father suffered no loss. His potential loss is very great, because all his assets became encumbered. If the document contained a condition allowing him freely to sell or borrow against the property, then of course the later wife's ketubah would have priority and the issue would disappear. Chavot Yair's ruling therefore requires study. The questioner's position appears more correct. On what basis can we invalidate a genuine debt instrument without proof, especially according to Rabbenu Tam and those who agree with him that even an actual concealment gift can vest title in the recipient? All the more so should a genuine prior obligation to the daughter stand.
