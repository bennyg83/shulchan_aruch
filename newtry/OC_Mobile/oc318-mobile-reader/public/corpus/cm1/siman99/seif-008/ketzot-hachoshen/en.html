Only a genuine gift. The distinction is as follows. If a person transfers all his property to another only as a sham, intending the assets to remain functionally his while appearing to belong to someone else, the property never truly leaves his domain. Even an oral creditor can collect from it. But if he gives a true gift to a relative or son, intending them actually to acquire the property, and his motive is merely to prevent creditors from reaching it, the gift is nonetheless a gift. He may have chosen the relative only because he trusted him and wished to defeat the creditors, but the transfer itself is real. Therefore an ordinary creditor cannot collect from such a genuine donee merely because the donor's motive was evasive. If, however, the creditor had an effective lien on movables together with land, the result can differ. Ordinarily the market overt enactment protects a bona fide donee, because a gift is treated like a sale and we assume the donor gave because he wished to favor the recipient. But where the gift was made only to defeat creditors, the policy of the market overt enactment is absent. The lien can therefore remain enforceable. This explains the language in siman 60. The case is not necessarily one where the gift is wholly void. Sometimes it is a real gift whose only purpose was evasion. In such a case an ordinary creditor cannot collect absent a stronger lien, because title actually passed. Where the transfer was merely a sham and no title passed at all, even an oral creditor may collect. The two situations must not be confused.
