The Shach writes that a debtor is not believed by his own admission that particular property belongs to another person where that admission harms his creditors, even in movables where there is no priority among creditors. Tumim argues that because a formal admission can itself create a new obligation, the newly acknowledged creditor should at least share equally. That is incorrect. A formal admission can function as acquisition only so long as it does not prejudice others. Where the admitting party's statement harms existing creditors, he is treated like any other interested person and is not believed. Once the statement lacks evidentiary force against them, it also cannot operate as an acquisition by admission against their rights. I explained this in siman 194. The Shach further says the debtor is not believed even if he made the admission while everyone thought he had no assets, rejecting Bach's argument that there was then no reason to suspect collusion. Tumim sides with Bach because if there were no assets, what collusion could there be? In my view the Shach is correct. The exclusion is not merely a suspicion of collusion. A person cannot establish facts by his own admission where those facts prejudice third parties. Kiddushin teaches that even where both alleged spouses agree, their admission is ineffective when it harms others. There need be no specific collusive plan. Once the statement affects third parties, the matter requires valid evidence, normally two witnesses. Even Moshe and Aharon would not be accepted merely because they are personally trustworthy when Torah law requires witnesses. Thus absence of obvious collusion does not restore the debtor's credibility against existing creditors.
