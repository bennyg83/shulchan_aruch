It appears to me that beit din, etc. The Shach is uncertain whom beit din compels. Does the borrower himself give half the newly acquired funds to the second creditor, to whom he did not swear, or does the borrower give everything to the creditor in whose favor he swore, after which beit din takes half from that creditor and gives it to the other? In my view there is no room for doubt. The case concerns movable property in which there is no priority among creditors. We rule that if a later creditor preceded the earlier creditor and collected such property, his collection stands. Beit din does not remove it from him. How then could the court take back half after the sworn creditor has already received it? This is especially so because the borrower's oath supports that payment. The meaning must be that beit din compels the borrower, before payment, to divide the money according to law and give half directly to the second creditor. Yet there is a deeper difficulty. The case must concern movable property without a binding property lien. The Tur and Rosh state that the borrower cannot encumber property not yet in existence unless the instrument contains the appropriate after-acquired-property undertaking, and the Derishah likewise understands the case as lacking such a clause. Even according to those authorities discussed below in siman 104 who hold that once after-acquired land enters the debtor's possession the creditor's Torah lien immediately attaches, that reasoning does not apply in the same manner to ordinary movable property. There is no fixed lien on the object. The debtor merely bears the personal mitzvah of paying his debt. If so, the borrower swore to give all future profits to one creditor. With respect to the half that legally belongs to that creditor, the oath is entirely proper. With respect to the other half, the oath conflicts with the mitzvah of paying the second creditor. This resembles an oath that includes both permitted and prohibited performance. Just as one who swears not to eat matzah can have the oath attach to the matzah of Pesach through its inclusion with ordinary matzah eaten during the rest of the year, perhaps the oath here attaches to the entire future profit because it validly attaches to one half. At first glance one might therefore say that there is no real dispute between the Mechaber and the Rosh and Tur. The Mechaber may agree that so long as the debtor has not regretted the oath and obtained release, he cannot simply pay the second creditor and violate his oath. Rather, because the oath includes a commitment to neglect the mitzvah of paying a creditor, beit din compels him physically to fulfill the mitzvah. Through that pressure he may regret the oath and find grounds for release. Since release is sought for the sake of a mitzvah, it may be granted even in circumstances where ordinary release would be more restricted. See Yoreh Deah, siman 239, Shach seif katan 20, citing Maharshal that one who swore to nullify a mitzvah is compelled to seek release rather than violate the mitzvah. Perhaps this is also the Mechaber's intent. This explanation is still insufficient. Apart from the strain in the Mechaber's language, the Mechaber rules in Even HaEzer, siman 118, seif 16, that if a man swore to give another person half of every profit he earns and his wife claims maintenance, the wife has no right in those profits, even for maintenance already accrued. Yet accrued maintenance is a complete debt. Above, siman 97, seif 24, it is explained that such a claim shares with an ordinary oral creditor. Likewise here, seif 1, the Mechaber states broadly that the debtor swears to pay future profits to the creditor and not give them to his wife for maintenance, including maintenance accruing between the oath and the later profit. This is difficult. The wife's accrued maintenance is a debt, and payment of debts is a mitzvah. Both obligations should therefore stand on the same footing. Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer explains that the profits promised by oath are, relative to the wife, like encumbered property, and maintenance is not collected from encumbered assets. This follows a responsum of the Rosh, rule 8. But that explanation fits the Rosh's own broader view that a later documented obligation can be treated as encumbered property relative to an earlier oral obligation, as discussed above in siman 97 and below in siman 104. The Mechaber, however, rules that an earlier oral loan takes priority over a later documented debt. He therefore does not generally treat the later document as though the property were already in a purchaser's hands. All the more so, a mere oath concerning future profits creates no property lien at all. The borrower has not expressly encumbered assets. The oath binds only the debtor personally. It cannot by itself convert future assets into encumbered property. If the Mechaber thought an oath had such an effect, why does he rule here without distinction that beit din compels division? He should have distinguished a second creditor whose claim is only oral, since such a creditor ordinarily cannot reach property already encumbered to another. The matter therefore requires a different explanation. The Rosh explains the issue by saying that a person cannot encumber something not yet in existence. He does not distinguish whether the earlier creditor's instrument contains the standard movable-property-with-land and after-acquired-property clauses, and he does not simply say that movable property cannot be encumbered. This suggests that where there is no such clause, the matter is obvious: the second creditor has no property lien at all, and the oath can validly attach to the future profit, including through the principle of an inclusive oath described above. Rather, the Rosh's difficult case is where the earlier creditor does have a valid clause encumbering movable property together with land and after-acquired property. Even then, the Rosh holds that the lien on property not yet in existence does not become fixed until the property later enters the debtor's possession. In addition, the Rashba holds that before after-acquired property comes into existence the debtor may retract from the prior undertaking. See below in siman 115, where I establish that the Rosh follows the Rashba on this point. Accordingly, when the debtor later swears to give the future profits to the second creditor, that oath itself constitutes the clearest possible retraction from the first creditor's attempted lien. The first lien therefore never attaches to the future property, and the second creditor's oath can control it. The Mechaber, by contrast, does not rule like the Rashba that the debtor may retract from an after-acquired-property undertaking before the property enters his possession. As appears from his omission of the Rashba's opinion below in siman 112, he treats the earlier lien as irrevocably binding. Once the property later enters the debtor's possession, the first creditor's lien attaches automatically. The debtor's later oath cannot uproot that preexisting right. Just as a debtor cannot defeat an earlier land lien by swearing to pay a later creditor, he cannot defeat this lien. Thus there is no effective oath to the extent it would impair the first creditor, and beit din compels the lawful division. This also explains why the Mechaber agrees that an oath concerning the wife's maintenance can be effective. The wife ordinarily has no comparable movable-property-with-land and after-acquired-property lien on future profits. There is therefore no existing property right for the oath to uproot. The profits go to the person in whose favor the oath was made. It appears, however, that even the Rosh's practical ruling may be limited to a case without a valid after-acquired-property clause. Where such a clause exists, the Rosh too may agree with the Mechaber rather than necessarily following the Rashba's retraction theory. A responsum of the Rosh at the end of rule 8 states that one who swore to give another person half his profits defeats his wife's maintenance claim because maintenance is not collected from encumbered property. If the Rosh thought the oath itself always displaced the prior lien, that additional rationale would be unnecessary. One must say that in the Rosh's setting the relevant assets were subject to an after-acquired-property undertaking. Therefore the ordinary rule would otherwise have required division, and he needed the special rule that maintenance is weaker because of tikkun olam. This also resolves the apparent contradiction between that responsum and another responsum in rule 8, law 7, concerning a man with two wives who swore to give all profits to the second wife for maintenance. There the Rosh rules that the oath does not deprive the first wife, and the profits are divided for maintenance. The commentators have offered strained distinctions. According to what was explained, both wives had equivalent marital claims to future support, and the later oath could not destroy the first wife's corresponding right. Thus the Rosh too can substantially agree with the Mechaber where a genuine prior lien exists.
