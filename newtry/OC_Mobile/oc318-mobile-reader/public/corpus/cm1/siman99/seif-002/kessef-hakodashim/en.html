Just as property found in a person's possession is presumed to be his, where property is known to be partnership property there is ordinarily a presumption that the partners contributed equal capital. Perhaps, however, this presumption does not apply in a commercial partnership where profits are divided per person rather than in proportion to contributed capital, and where the circumstances show that the partners were not accustomed to contribute equal sums. The rule that profits are divided per person itself indicates that they did not insist that each partner contribute an identical amount. Accordingly, if one partner says that he supplied most of the capital and that the merchandise came directly from him, the ordinary possession presumption can at least establish that his claim is not contrary to an existing presumption. If one witness testifies that the merchandise was received from a particular partner, while the person presently holding it says that he knows the parties were partners and that half the capital belonged to his own creditor, the consequences depend upon the level of his knowledge and the oath rules. If a party admits that the other was his partner but claims that all the capital was his own and none belonged to the creditor, at first glance he may have a migo because he could have denied the partnership entirely. Yet the person opposing him may also possess a migo, such as the ability to claim that he repaid or returned the property, and in some circumstances a claim of unavoidable loss may also be available. Where the opposing party does not know with certainty that the creditor was a partner, but relies only on hearsay or inference, the rule of one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear may sometimes arise, particularly where the matter is one he should have known. If his ignorance is itself reasonable, that rule may not apply. If half the money used to purchase the merchandise was delivered into the hands of one partner's creditor and the other half remains seized, there is no clear basis to say that the first partner's own share was necessarily paid first. Partners act as agents for one another. Even if the other partner was not informed, that alone may not determine the matter after the fact. See also the Shach concerning a partner who pays a debt, where there is a presumption that he ordinarily pays on his own behalf.
