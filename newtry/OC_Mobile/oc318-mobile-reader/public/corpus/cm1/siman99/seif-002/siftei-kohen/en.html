It appears to me that beit din, etc. The Mechaber's reasoning appears to be that an oath itself does not effect acquisition. It only obligates the swearer personally to fulfill his oath. A creditor may therefore say, 'What concern is your oath to me, and by what act did Levi acquire?' To allow the debtor to satisfy his oath, beit din gives the property to Levi on account of Shimon's oath, and then compels Levi to give half to Reuven, the competing creditor. The Rashba's responsum concerning a debtor who swore that all future profit beyond his support would be paid to Levi deals with the lien on after-acquired property. According to the Rashba, such a lien may be retracted before the property comes into existence. It determines rights against purchasers, not the division of assets still in the debtor's possession. When both creditors seek collection from assets presently held, they share. Accordingly, only half may be given to Levi, and the other half is given directly to Reuven. Beit din does not distort the law because of Shimon's oath. His oath was only to give what remained within his power to give, and once beit din prevents the transfer he has not violated it. This is the principal ruling, contrary to the Sema.
