He must fulfill his oath. Where the first creditor's document does not include movables together with land and an after-acquired-property clause, everyone agrees that the debtor must fulfill the oath he made to the second creditor. But if the first document contains those clauses, the debtor's property is already encumbered, and he has no power to remove that lien by his oath. The issue depends on whether a person can retract an after-acquired-property lien. If he can retract it, then swearing to give the property to the second creditor is no less a retraction. If he cannot retract it, then the lien already took effect and his oath cannot uproot it. This appears to underlie the dispute between Rosh and Mechaber. See Tumim. I concluded that although the legal distinction itself is correct, Rosh also appears to agree that where an after-acquired-property clause was written, the oath does not take effect and the creditors divide. I further wrote that even where the oath does take effect, beit din may compel him physically because repayment of a debt is a mitzvah. If as a result he regrets his oath, they may release it without the first creditor's consent. If the creditor to whom he swore has already collected, however, everyone agrees that what he collected remains collected and we do not remove it from him.
