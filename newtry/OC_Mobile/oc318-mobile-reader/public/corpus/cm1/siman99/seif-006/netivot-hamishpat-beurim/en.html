Even with land. See Shach, seif katan 18, who leaves the matter requiring study. In my opinion there is no difficulty. The essential law of a deed intended to evade creditors is that the recipient acquires fully, except that the donor retains the right to retract whenever he wishes, as the Rosh writes in Ketubot 79. The reason the creditor can nevertheless collect is that, because the donor can retract, the property has not truly left the donor's domain. The Rosh writes that if the recipient does not acquire it, the husband acquires it, because so long as the woman may retract whenever she wishes the property has not left her domain. The Rosh further explains that it is called an evasive deed because her intention was to evade the husband's rights, but she erred in thinking that this method would accomplish the evasion. Once she learns that the scheme cannot achieve its purpose, we are witnesses that she prefers the recipient not to retain the property and consume its fruits. The Rosh's reference to consuming the fruits means after she becomes aware and retracts, as is clear to anyone who examines his words. Accordingly, fruits already consumed with her permission certainly need not be returned. This is no worse than a mistaken waiver, which is still a waiver according to the Rosh in Bava Metzia 66, specifically where the person knew that he was waiving and merely erred in the law, such as the case of the fruits of a palm tree. The Ran explicitly writes regarding an evasive deed that fruits already consumed need not be returned because the case resembles the fruits of a palm tree. It follows that whether the fruits were consumed before the donor learned the law or after, they need not be returned. Indeed, the case is stronger, because an evasive deed is a fully effective acquisition, except that the donor retains the right to retract. So long as the donor has not retracted and cancelled the gift, the property belongs entirely to the recipient.
If it appears to beit din. At first glance the wording is difficult. If he did not intend a mere ruse, how can it still be said that his intent was to evade his creditor? The explanation appears to be that the classic evasive deed is one in which the donor retains the right to retract, and therefore the gift appears to be merely a device. But if he intended a complete and irrevocable gift, for example where the document states that it is an absolute and perpetual gift, then it is a genuine gift, as the Ran writes in Ketubot there. Even if he gave it only so that his creditor would not collect from him, and it is obvious that he would not have given the gift were he not indebted to others, nevertheless, because he has no right to retract from this gift, it has left the donor's domain. The recipient acquires it and the creditor does not. Only where the donor retains a right of retraction does the creditor acquire, for the reason stated by the Rosh in Ketubot: since the donor can retract, the property has not left his domain.
