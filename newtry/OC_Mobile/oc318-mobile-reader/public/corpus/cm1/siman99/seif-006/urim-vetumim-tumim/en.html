Even land, etc. The Shach asks: granted that in an actual sale or gift the recipient becomes owner, but where the transfer was only a device to hide assets from creditors, the recipient should owe the property back to the transferor. The transferor's creditor should then collect it through the rule of Rabbi Natan. Why is a special doctrine of fraudulent transfer necessary? This objection should not be directed to the responsum of the Rosh from which the ruling derives. The Rosh himself says that the creditor possesses the standard lien on movable property together with land, so by strict law he could collect from the gift recipient, and only the market-protection enactment would ordinarily shield the recipient. In a fraudulent transfer that enactment does not apply. Why did the Rosh need this entire framework? If a fraudulent transfer is automatically void, the creditor should collect even without any such lien. One answer is that the Rosh meant his ruling to apply even according to Rabbenu Tam and those who agree with him, who hold in Ketubot, chapter HaIshah Shenaflu, that a sham transfer designed to hide property from creditors can nevertheless vest title in the recipient. Even Rabbenu Tam may agree that in the Rosh's case the ordinary market-protection enactment does not shield the recipient. More likely, the Rosh's particular case involved uncertainty whether the transfer was truly fraudulent or was a genuine gift. Because the recipient held the property, that doubt by itself would prevent extraction. The Rosh therefore reasoned that under strict Torah lien law the creditor would collect from the recipient, so the recipient's only defense is the rabbinic market-protection enactment. A doubt concerning the scope of a rabbinic enactment is judged according to the underlying law. The creditor can therefore recover. This also explains why the Tur copied two responsa of the Rosh consecutively concerning fraudulent transfers. The Derishah asks why both are needed. In one case the fraudulent intent was clearly established, so the Rosh ruled that even an oral creditor could recover from the recipient. In the second case the intent was uncertain, so ordinary doubt would favor the possessor. The creditor could recover only because he had a formal movable-property-with-land lien under the underlying law and the market-protection enactment was not extended to a doubtful fraudulent transaction. The two responsa therefore address different situations. Accordingly, the Shach's question is answered with respect to the Rosh's responsum. The creditor cannot recover the fruits merely because the transaction might have been a sham, since the fraudulent character itself is uncertain. Strict lien law did not reach those fruits through the ordinary clause, as the Rosh explains, and the burden of proof remains upon the one seeking to remove them from the recipient. The Mechaber, however, states broadly that even an oral creditor can recover from the recipient, implying a case where the transfer is clearly known to be fraudulent. The Shach's question therefore remains against the Mechaber unless we clarify the recipient's rights to the fruits of a sham transfer. That issue itself requires examination. The Rosh, in the chapter HaIshah Shenaflu, writes concerning a woman's sham transfer that the recipient eats the fruits. The Mechaber in Even HaEzer, siman 90, seif 7, appears to rule likewise. The Rosh gives two reasons. First, the woman intended to prevent her husband from benefiting. If the fruits remained hers, they would belong to the husband, defeating her purpose. Second, there is a strong presumption that she intended the recipient to enjoy the fruits, because otherwise no one would agree to exert effort cultivating and guarding property for nothing. These reasons lead to different results with an ordinary creditor. The first is specific to a wife and husband. It reflects Rabbenu Tam and Rabbenu Shimshon's reasoning that if the recipient does not acquire, the husband will, while the woman clearly prefers the recipient. Yet the Rosh generally disagrees with that reasoning regarding the corpus of a sham transfer. He explains that the transferor may simply be mistaken, thinking that neither recipient nor husband or creditor will acquire. Once informed that the scheme fails, he may prefer his own legal counterparty over the recipient. Why then does the Rosh use the first rationale for fruits? The answer appears to be that the Rosh limits the transferor's mistake to the corpus, where the transferor may have imagined a special device preventing both sides from acquiring. With fruits, however, there is no corresponding necessity to assume such a legal mistake. If the fruits do not go to the recipient, they necessarily go to the husband under ordinary marital law. The woman's purpose therefore indicates that she preferred the recipient with respect to fruits. In that respect the Rosh can agree with Rabbenu Tam. However. This first rationale is specific to a wife, where the category of property unknown to the husband may support her expectation. With an ordinary creditor, the Rosh necessarily says that the transferor was mistaken even about the corpus, because no analogous category exists. The creditor can therefore take the corpus. The same mistake can extend to the fruits, and one cannot infer from the wife's case that the recipient permanently acquires them against an ordinary creditor. The second rationale, compensation for the recipient's labor, could apply equally to an ordinary creditor. Why would a recipient cultivate and guard the transferor's property without benefit? At first glance this suggests that even in a creditor case the fruits should belong to the recipient. Yet later in that same discussion the Rosh explicitly says that once the transferor learns the scheme is ineffective and that the creditor can seize the property, the transferor prefers to keep the fruits himself until actual foreclosure rather than enrich the gift recipient. That statement seems to contradict the second rationale if the latter were independently decisive. The reconciliation is that before discovery of the scheme, while the transferor believes the sham transfer successfully blocks the creditor, he intends the recipient to enjoy the fruits as compensation for managing the property. After the fraud is uncovered and the property is exposed to immediate foreclosure, the reason for giving away future fruits disappears. The transferor can manage the property himself until foreclosure and has no reason to continue compensating the recipient for a device that no longer works. Thus, where the Mechaber discusses fruits already consumed by the recipient before the fraudulent nature of the transfer was established, those fruits can indeed belong to the recipient outright. This also accords with the Ran, who writes that the recipient acquires fruits because the transferor effectively waives them. Although the Ran follows Rabbenu Tam regarding the principal transfer, his reasoning about fruits can apply more broadly. The Mechaber's ruling is therefore correct, though not for the reason assumed by the Shach.
As an absolute gift, etc. Although his intention was to hide the property from creditors, the wording at first appears self-contradictory. If he intended an absolute gift, how can we say his intention was merely concealment? And if he intended only concealment, how can it be called an absolute gift? The Rashba and Ramban responsa that serve as the source do not use this formulation. Moreover, if the donor plainly intended an actual gift, its validity would be obvious and the Rama would teach nothing. It appears that the Rama rules like Rabbenu Tam, who holds that a document of concealment can nevertheless vest title in the recipient. The Gemara's logic is that if the recipient does not acquire, the creditor will, and since the donor clearly prefers the recipient to the creditor, we infer a real gift. Maharam and the Rosh disagree, and the Rosh reports that leading authorities sided with Maharam. Maharik likewise follows them. But Rabbenu Tam, Tosafot, and Rabbenu Shimshon support the recipient, and the Rashba, as the Beit Yosef reports in a responsum, agreed that one who relies on that view has support. Raah, Ritva, Ran, and Rivash, siman 142, likewise rule that way. Where the recipient is already in possession, who can remove the property against so many authorities? This also appears to be the Darkei Moshe's understanding. He cites many authorities who agree with Rabbenu Tam that a gift made to avoid a creditor can be acquired by the recipient and does not mention a contrary view. His gloss here is therefore directed to Rabbenu Tam. Rabbenu Tam did not mean that every transfer survives even where there is conclusive proof that the donor never intended the recipient to acquire. Where a clear and specific circumstance demonstrates that no gift was intended, everyone agrees that the recipient acquires nothing. We cannot invent a gift solely because otherwise the creditor would acquire property that the donor did not want him to receive, where an unmistakable factual presumption establishes that the donor never intended to give the property away. The Rosh's responsa and Maharik support this limitation. Tosafot in the chapter HaIshah Shenaflu prove the same point. According to Rashi's explanation of the concealment case, the woman expressly told the witnesses that her sole intention was to hide the assets. Tosafot ask why Rav Anan and Rava, who disagree with Rav Nachman, would nevertheless hold that the recipient acquires. If Tosafot adopted Rabbenu Tam without limitation, there would be no question: even an intentionally concealed transfer should vest title because otherwise the husband would acquire. The Gemara's answer that Rabbi Shimon treats the property like assets unknown to the husband would matter only according to Rabbi Shimon, whereas the other authorities could follow the Sages. Tosafot's question therefore assumes that when the evidence clearly establishes that the woman intended no gift at all, even Rabbenu Tam agrees the recipient cannot acquire. The inference that the donor prefers the recipient over the creditor cannot overcome an express manifestation of contrary intent. This also clarifies Tosafot in Bava Batra 150b, in the passage beginning "and these are," where they ask how Rav Nachman can count a concealed transfer among five cases in which a transfer of all property is ineffective, since he does not ordinarily list matters dependent on dispute. Maharshal and Maharsha struggled to identify the dispute. Maharsha says it is merely the dispute of Rav Anan and Rava against Rav Nachman, but that is difficult. Amoraim regularly state their own rulings even when other amoraim disagree. "Do you challenge one person from another?" If a sage could not state a halachic position because a colleague disputed him, very little law could ever be taught. Rather, Tosafot follow Rabbenu Tam. The Gemara asks that if the recipient does not acquire, the husband should acquire, and answers that Rabbi Shimon treats the assets like property unknown to the husband. According to the Sages, however, even unknown assets are acquired by the husband. The original difficulty would remain, and the recipient would have to acquire. Thus the validity of a concealment transfer depends upon the Tannaitic dispute between Rabbi Shimon and the Sages. Tosafot therefore properly ask how Rav Nachman could list concealment among the five undisputed cases. Tosafot answer that where there is an explicit disclosure that the transfer was made only because she was about to marry, everyone agrees the recipient does not acquire. That is exactly the limitation stated above. Even Rabbenu Tam agrees that where the donor's intention not to make a gift is conclusively revealed, the recipient cannot acquire merely because the alternative is that the husband or creditor acquires. The point is clear and compelling. The Rama's phrase can now be read precisely. "If it appears to beit din that he did not intend mere artifice but an actual gift" does not mean that he explicitly announced an unconditional gift, which would be obvious. It means that he did not expressly reveal an intention that the transfer be only a sham. In such a case we infer an actual gift because we know that he prefers the recipient to the creditor. Thus, even though his motive was to hide the property, the recipient acquires under Rabbenu Tam's rule. That is the Rama's novelty. This suggests that the rule is strongest where the property has actually entered the recipient's possession. If it never left the donor's possession, the transaction may be treated by everyone as a pure device. Otherwise the Rama should have noted the contrary positions of the Rosh and Maharam, since the donor who remains in possession could invoke kim li in their favor. When the recipient is the possessor, however, the numerous authorities supporting Rabbenu Tam prevent removal from him. The Bach likewise holds that where the property remains in the donor's possession, even a transfer of only part of the property can be treated as concealment because the circumstances reveal the artifice. The Shach leaves that ruling in doubt. There is reason to question the Bach. The Rosh compares the presumption governing a concealment gift to that governing a gift by a person on his deathbed. A gift of all property by a person on his deathbed is revocable, but a gift of only part is not, even though the property may physically remain in the donor's possession. We do not say that if he truly intended an absolute gift he would have placed it immediately in the recipient's hands. It is therefore difficult to make possession alone decisive. Maharam's responsum cited by Mordechai in the chapter Get Pashut also requires analysis. A man purchased land and caused the deed to be written in the names of relatives in order to defeat his wife's ketubah. Maharam ruled that it was only a concealment device and that the wife could collect. The facts appear to indicate that the husband retained some property. If so, one might infer like the Bach that because the property effectively remained under the husband's control, even a partial transfer was treated as concealment. But Maharam explains his concern differently: otherwise no woman could reliably collect a ketubah, because every husband could find relatives to hold nominal title while trusting that they would not keep the assets for themselves. If a partial transfer to relatives were never concealment, Maharam's solution would not address that danger. The correct inference is that where there is a clear factual presumption demonstrating fraudulent purpose, even a partial transfer and even property physically held by relatives can be treated as concealment. The Gemara likewise says that where the woman expressly reveals that the transfer is because of impending marriage, everyone treats it as concealment, and Tosafot indicate that this can apply even to a partial transfer. Maharam's case involved that kind of clear evidence. He therefore had no need to distinguish between a transfer of all property and part of it. It does not provide a general proof for the Bach's broader rule. Likewise, the Bach's claim that Rabbenu Tam himself agrees that whenever the property remains with the donor the recipient acquires nothing is not conclusively established. A responsum of the Rosh, rule 78, may at first appear to support it, but that case involved clearly proven concealment and therefore does not decide the broader question. And we should not invent new distinctions without compelling proof. See the Rama's responsum, siman 67, discussing Maharshal's correction of Mordechai. Mordechai's text said that "he collects from the gift recipient." Maharshal objected that according to Rabbenu Tam the gift recipient acquires, and proposed deleting a word so that the text instead means that the gift recipient himself collects. The Rama says he does not understand the correction: what "collection" is needed if the recipient already possesses the property? He therefore proposed a different emendation. Our printed Mordechai follows Maharshal's correction. Maharshal must have understood that in Mordechai's case the gift recipient was not physically in possession but merely came to collect under his document. In that setting it is meaningful to say that he "collects." This shows that Maharshal understood Rabbenu Tam's rule to apply even where the recipient was not already holding the property, contrary to the Bach's categorical distinction. The matter therefore requires study.
