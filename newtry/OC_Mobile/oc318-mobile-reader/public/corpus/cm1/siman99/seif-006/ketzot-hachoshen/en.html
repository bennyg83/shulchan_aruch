And he gave away everything he owned. The Shach refers to Bach's distinction between a gift of all one's property and a partial gift in which the donor retained something. The source is the responsum of the Rosh, rule 78, siman 3. Rosh describes people who first transfer their property and afterward borrow money, intending thereby to defeat later creditors. In the case before him, Reuven had executed a document transferring all his property to one of his sons, leaving himself only one gold coin, while simultaneously acknowledging a very large debt to that son. Rosh ruled the transfer void because the circumstances clearly demonstrated that he never intended a true gift but only to hide his assets from creditors. We follow compelling circumstantial intent in monetary law, just as with a deathly ill person who gives away all his property and then recovers, and with a woman who makes a sham transfer to prevent a husband from acquiring rights. Rosh explains that retaining a merely nominal amount does not defeat the presumption of evasion. The retained property must be enough for the donor's support. He compares the case to a deathly ill gift, where one opinion requires retaining sufficient property for livelihood. Rif, by contrast, holds that any retention suffices. Rosh applies the same underlying presumption to an evasive gift: a person does not ordinarily give away everything and leave himself dependent on charity. In Rosh's specific case, even the retained gold coin was not a real retention because the son's alleged large debt would immediately consume it. Therefore nothing useful remained for the donor. It follows that if the gift document was executed before the donor later borrowed from other people and he genuinely retained enough for his livelihood, the gift can stand. The Rosh invalidated the case before him because the apparent retention was already swallowed by the son's asserted debt. If the transfer was executed only after the donor was already indebted to others, then retaining merely enough to equal the creditors' claims is insufficient, because those assets are destined for collection and leave the donor nothing for himself. If he retained enough both to satisfy creditors and still support himself, there is no compelling presumption of evasion. According to Rif and Rambam, who require only some retention, even a transfer after indebtedness may stand because beit din must still leave the debtor the basic protected necessities of food and clothing. According to Rama, who follows the requirement of sufficient livelihood, that protected minimum is not the same as a true retained livelihood and therefore does not remove the presumption. Bach argues that even according to Rif, the retained gold coin failed because the large acknowledged debt gave the son immediate rights in it. In my view the Rosh's own argument was specifically according to his requirement of sufficient livelihood. Under Rif's approach, a minimal retention can suffice. Bach further writes that even according to Rabbeinu Tam, who validates an evasive gift in some cases, the transferee must have taken actual possession of the property, and where the assets remained under the donor's control the gift is ineffective. I find no such general distinction. Actual possession is necessary where the document is a concealed gift and lacks proper publicity, but today such documents can be valid according to the established rules of siman 242. Rosh himself did not base his decision on absence of physical possession. The decisive issue is the donor's true intention and whether the retention was genuine.
