And the creditor collects, etc. Even if the debtor gave the property to a minor, the creditor may collect it because the transfer was merely a device to conceal assets. This is the view of Rashba and Sema.
And the recipient of the gift swears. The donor is not believed to say how much property he transferred, even if he swears, because he cannot extract money from another merely through his own oath. This is simple, as Sema writes. Shach asks why the recipient must swear when the creditor's claim is uncertain. He explains that Rosh imposed this as an additional stringency. Perhaps it is included in the same category as partners and one who conducts household business, whom the Torah or Sages require to swear even on uncertainty because they may rationalize taking something in compensation for their efforts. The recipient too may have labored with the property and rationalize retaining some of it, and therefore an oath is imposed even on an uncertain claim.
Even from land, etc. See Tumim. Shach questions this because the recipient owes the produce to the donor, in which case the creditor should collect it through the rule of Rabbi Nasan. In Tumim I brought proof from Ran and Rosh that before the concealment is exposed in beit din, the produce belongs to the recipient. Once the concealment is established, however, the produce accruing until the completion of the public announcement period may belong to the donor. Examine there carefully.
Nevertheless, if it appears, etc. See Tumim. I concluded that where it is definitely known that the transfer was made to conceal assets, even an oral creditor may collect from the recipient. If beit din is uncertain whether the transfer was a sham or a genuine gift, an oral creditor cannot collect from the recipient on the basis of doubt. But if the creditor has a written note containing a lien on movable property together with land, and ordinarily could not collect only because of the market protection enactment above in siman 60, then in this doubtful concealment case he may collect from the recipient according to all opinions.
Although his intent was to conceal. See Tumim. Rama's qualification that this applies only to an absolute gift does not mean that we know he specifically intended a genuine gift, for then how could it simultaneously be described as concealment? Rather, where he did not expressly intend a sham in which neither the creditor nor the recipient should truly acquire the property, we say that because he intended to remove the property from the creditor, the recipient acquires it. But where it is known, or there are strong grounds, that he intended only a sham, the recipient acquires nothing. If the property remains in the donor's possession, Bach holds that this itself is compelling evidence of sham intent. Shach is uncertain. See Tumim, where I too remained very doubtful. Nevertheless, if his intent was merely concealment, the recipient has no right to the property because the donor, who remains in possession, may say that he relies upon Maharam and Rosh, who hold that in such a case the recipient never acquired it.
