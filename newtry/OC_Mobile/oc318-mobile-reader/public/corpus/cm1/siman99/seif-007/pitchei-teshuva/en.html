Only for an absolute gift. Responsa Chavot Yair, siman 4, discusses a widower who married a widow and, before the marriage, executed a debt instrument to his daughter for five hundred Reichsthaler, payable one hour before his death. After his death, when the widow sought payment of her ketubah, the daughter produced her earlier debt instrument and sought to take the house he left, which would leave the widow with less than half her ketubah. One distinguished dayan argued that the instrument was not a fraudulent conveyance because such a document exists only where the transferor does not intend the recipient actually to acquire and the document is merely for appearance, as in the Rosh's responsum. Where the transferor intends the document to be fully effective, it is not a fraudulent conveyance, and Rama in siman 99:6 appears to say so. Chavot Yair disagrees. The defining feature of a fraudulent conveyance is that the circumstances show an intent to employ a device to harm the opposing party, whether the gift is merely nominal or legally absolute. Rama begins by saying that the parties did not intend a device; his concluding reference to an 'absolute gift' reflects the case in Shulchan Aruch, where the gift is visibly not genuine and that lack of genuineness itself constitutes the device. Where a person genuinely gives away his own property, the mere inward motive to shield it is only an unexpressed intention and does not invalidate the transfer. Thus, had the father issued a note payable in ten years, so that the due date might arrive during his lifetime and the daughter could demand payment from him, the fact that he trusted her not to demand during his life but only after his death would be an inward intention and not a device sufficient to invalidate the act. But in the actual case the device is evident from the instrument itself: he fixed the due date precisely one hour before his death, so that the note and his wife's ketubah would mature together, the daughter's earlier instrument would take priority, and the father himself could never suffer any loss because the note could not be demanded during his lifetime. This is clearly a fraudulent instrument, and the majority of the court so ruled in practice. Tummim, seif katan 11, cites Chavot Yair briefly and rejects him, concluding that the questioner's view is correct: there is no basis to invalidate a document without a reason, especially according to Rabbeinu Tam and those who agree with him that even in a deliberate shielding transfer the recipient acquires. Responsa Pri Tevuah, siman 56, discusses a similar question where the daughter was in possession of her father's house. After analyzing Chavot Yair and Tummim, it concludes that in that case the daughter may retain the house on the strength of her debt instrument, because at least she can plead kim li in accordance with Rabbeinu Tam and his school that the recipient acquires. See also Ketzot HaChoshen and Netivot HaMishpat on this law.
