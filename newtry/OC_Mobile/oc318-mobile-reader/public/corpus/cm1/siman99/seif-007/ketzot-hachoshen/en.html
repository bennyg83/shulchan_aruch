Even land. The Shach argues that since the original gift was void from the outset, the fruits or profits belong to the donor and can be reached by his creditor under Rabbi Natan's lien. In my view, even though the donor's purpose was to shield the property from creditors, the relative who received and managed the assets did so with the donor's intention that the creditor not acquire them. The donor did not personally engage in the business. His relative handled it and supplied him whatever he needed from principal or profit. In that situation the creditor cannot claim the profits merely because the original owner hoped to benefit from them. The relative's work was undertaken on the understanding that the creditor would have no rights in the resulting gains. This resembles one who acquires a found object for another on condition that the other's creditor have no rights in it. Since the donor himself had no direct ownership share in the profits produced by the relative's labor, the creditor cannot reach them merely from the donor's underlying evasive purpose.
