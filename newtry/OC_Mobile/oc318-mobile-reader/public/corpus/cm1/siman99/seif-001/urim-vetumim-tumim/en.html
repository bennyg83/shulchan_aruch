A gift on condition that it be returned, etc. See Urim for what I wrote in the name of Baal HaTerumot. That discussion is necessary according to the view that there is no meaningful distinction between an oath concerning the future and an oath concerning the past. According to the Shulchan Aruch's ruling above in siman 87 that the two are different, the matter is simpler. If a debtor presently possesses property given to him on condition that it be returned and swears, "I have nothing," he utters a falsehood at the moment of the oath. Such an oath is especially severe. By contrast, if he merely swears that whatever he later earns he will pay to the creditor, and he subsequently earns money and fails to pay, the oath was not false when spoken. His later violation is less severe in that respect. One might ask that if an oath about future payment is lighter, why not require the debtor to renew it every thirty days? At the time of each renewed oath, if he already possesses assets, the oath would concern a present fact and be more severe. The answer is that this entire oath is itself an added stringency instituted by the Geonim. They did not impose repeated new oaths every thirty days merely to increase pressure. But when the debtor is already taking the original required oath, we include within it every relevant present matter that can make him take the oath seriously. Therefore he swears that he has not hidden assets and has not transferred them as a gift on condition of return. This is simple and correct.
It appears, etc. See Urim for what I wrote in the name of the Maggid Mishneh, that the debtor is not believed to say that he owes money to others. The Maggid Mishneh writes that from this ruling it follows that the Rambam holds that an earlier oral loan has priority over a later documented loan. Otherwise, what collusion is there? Even if the debtor's statement were true and he owed the earlier oral debt, the later documented creditor would still collect first. The Shach rejects the proof. He says the case may involve movable property, or property acquired only after both loans where the documented instrument did not contain an after-acquired-property clause. In such property there is no priority, so if the alleged oral debt were genuine the two creditors would divide. The Rambam therefore says the debtor is not believed because of the concern for collusion. This is not clear. In property where priority does not matter, suppose the debtor truly owes one creditor from long ago, and today, before that creditor collected through beit din, the debtor appears before witnesses and executes a new document acknowledging that he owes another person a separate debt. The two claims would divide that property. That is obvious. Accordingly, when the debtor appears before beit din and says that he owes another person an oral debt, he has an obvious migo if his goal is collusion. He could simply execute a valid debt instrument before witnesses today in favor of that person, thereby creating a second debt that unquestionably shares equally in property to which no priority applies. Moreover, even without an underlying debt, a formal admission before beit din that he owes the person money can itself create a binding obligation from the time of the admission, as explained above in siman 40. Certainly an admission before beit din produces a complete present debt. Thus, with respect to movable property and other assets in which earlier and later debts have equal rank, there would be no reason to disbelieve the debtor's statement on account of collusion. He can accomplish the same result openly and validly without the disputed assertion. The Rambam must therefore be speaking of property where priority matters. In that setting, if the debtor were believed, the alleged earlier oral creditor would collect before the later documented creditor. This confirms the Maggid Mishneh's proof and is clear.
Before he swore. The Bach holds that if, at a time when the debtor is presumed to have no assets, he says that he owes money to someone else, he is believed. There is no apparent collusion, because according to the known facts he has nothing to distribute. The Shach disagrees, arguing that perhaps the debtor secretly owns assets and falsely claims another debt in order to protect them. I do not understand this. We do not presently know of any such assets. If the Shach merely fears that the debtor possesses hidden property, then the debtor has a clear migo. He could simply transfer those concealed assets to the alleged creditor without our knowledge. The Shach himself writes in seif katan 6 that where a debtor says that he owes another person money and has a migo that he could have given that person the assets, he is believed. Here, because we do not know that any assets exist, any hidden assets could be transferred directly. The migo is complete. One cannot answer that perhaps the debtor is unwilling to move or transfer the hidden property because he fears the secret will be discovered, and therefore prefers the false claim of debt. The Gemara in Bava Batra, chapter Chazakat HaBatim, disproves such speculation. There it discusses Reuven who sold land without responsibility and cannot testify concerning the land because he may wish to preserve it for his own creditor. The Gemara asks: if he has other property, the creditor can recover from him; if he has none, what difference does it make to him whether this field remains available? According to the Shach's concern, the Gemara's question would fail. Perhaps Reuven has secret assets and fears that sooner or later they will be discovered, allowing his creditor to collect from him. He therefore prefers that the sold field remain available for the creditor. If the hidden assets are later discovered, the earlier sale was without responsibility and the buyer has no claim against him. The Gemara nevertheless does not entertain such a possibility. Evidently, once a person is presumed to lack property, we do not invent the possibility of secret assets merely to create a suspicion of collusion. All the more so here, after the debtor has actually sworn that he has nothing. The Bach's ruling is therefore clear.
Some disagree with this, which is the view of the Darkei Moshe. The Rashba writes in a responsum about a person who gave his brother a handwritten document stating that half of his property belonged to the brother, after which a woman's ketubah claim was presented against him. The Rashba says that we need not suspect collusion, because if he wished he could simply have given the property to his brother as a gift. From this the Darkei Moshe infers that even against the chazakah that property in a person's possession belongs to him, a migo can be accepted. The Darkei Moshe also brings support from a responsum of the Rosh, who rules that where a person holds investment property for another, he is believed to say that the property belongs to that other person because he could have given it to him. The Rama therefore writes here that some authorities disagree with the view rejecting such a migo. The Shach argues at length that the Rashba should not be interpreted this way. The Rashba himself elsewhere writes that such a migo is a migo against a chazakah. To avoid contradiction, the Shach says that in the first responsum the assets were actually in the brother's possession, so there was no chazakah that they belonged to the debtor. Likewise, the Rosh's case involved property known to have been received as investment property from others, so the ordinary chazakah that everything in a person's possession is his never arose. The Shach therefore concludes that there is no real dispute: all authorities agree that a migo cannot overcome this chazakah.
But his explanation does not appear correct. It is true that the Rashba's responsa appear inconsistent, but they can be reconciled without the Shach's forced assumption that the property was physically in the brother's possession, a fact not stated in the responsum. The brother held an earlier handwritten acknowledgment that half the assets belonged to him. The only concern was that the debtor might have fabricated the document recently and antedated it in order to collude against the creditor. Even without a migo, that is a relatively remote suspicion where the document has not fallen, been impaired, or otherwise become suspect, as Maharam of Lublin explains. See what I wrote above at the end of siman 43. When the earlier document is combined with the migo that the debtor could have given the property away, we need not suspect collusion. The ordinary chazakah of ownership is already weakened by the prior document itself. The migo merely supports the conclusion that the document is genuine. There is therefore no contradiction in the Rashba. The Shach's explanation of the Rosh may be correct, but the Rama was nevertheless justified in saying that authorities dispute the broader principle. Many authorities accept a migo even against this kind of chazakah. Tosafot in Bava Batra 51b, in the passage beginning "he received," write that where money entrusted by a woman is hidden property that legally belongs to her husband as nichsei melog for purposes of fruits and inheritance, if she says that it belongs to a third person she is believed because she could simply take it and give it to that person. Only when she makes the statement at the time of death, when she can no longer practically take and transfer the money, does she lose credibility. The Rosh writes the same, and the Shulchan Aruch rules accordingly in Even HaEzer, siman 86. Yet those assets are presumptively hers as nichsei melog, with the husband's rights attached. How can her migo overcome that chazakah and remove the husband's rights? This is even stronger according to the Rama there and the Sema above in siman 62, who hold that even a wife who conducts business in the household is believed through such a migo although the property is presumptively hers and her husband's. One cannot distinguish merely because the property was deposited with a third party. Once it was deposited by the woman, it is legally treated as though it remained in her possession, as Beit Shmuel explicitly writes in siman 86. The proof therefore stands. I am also surprised by the attribution to Ramban. The Maggid Mishneh cites Ramban as uncertain whether such a migo should fail against the chazakah. Yet Ramban himself, in his novellae to Bava Batra in the same sugya, writes that a wife is not believed to say that property in her possession was given to her on condition that her husband have no rights in it. He proves this by saying that if she were believed, then when she says that the property belongs to another person she should likewise be believed through a migo that she could have claimed, "It is mine and was given to me on condition that my husband have no right in it." The Maggid Mishneh cites this in Ramban's name in Hilchot Ishut, chapter 22. But such a migo would itself appear to operate against the chazakah that property in a woman's possession belongs to her in the ordinary marital form, whatever the precise marital category may be. How can Ramban use it as an unquestioned migo if he definitively rejects migo against the chazakah? It seems that the Maggid Mishneh accurately described Ramban as only raising the concern as a possibility, while Ramban's own novellae show that he did not treat it as conclusive. A mere doubt cannot override an explicit proof from his own words. This requires study. There is further proof from the Gemara in Sanhedrin, chapter Zeh Borer, cited by the Rif and Rambam in Hilchot Zechiyah, chapter 10. If a person says, "I saw your father hiding money, and he said that it belonged to so-and-so," the witness is not believed. But if the money was hidden in a place from which the witness could himself have taken it, he is believed because he could have taken it and given it to that person. See below, siman 255, seif 8. Why is this not a migo against the chazakah that property in a person's possession belongs to him? The case can plainly involve money hidden on the deceased's own property, as the Rosh states explicitly in responsum rule 68, law 2. It is strained to answer that because the observer could have taken the money at the time of concealment, the owner thereby trusted him and gave him the legal status of a shalish, who is believed in his assertions. In the present case too, a creditor necessarily trusted the debtor with possession of his own assets and the debtor could have transferred them away, yet we do not automatically give him the evidentiary power of a shalish against the creditor. Indeed, it would be paradoxical if the person who hid the money himself would not be believed to say that it belonged to another, yet an observer who heard him say so would be believed. One could distinguish that collusion is more commonly suspected in a debtor than in a neutral third person, as Ramban may imply, but that too is strained. If the third party has a concrete interest in the outcome, the concern of collusion can arise as well, yet the Gemara and poskim state the rule generally. It therefore seems more correct as practical law that a migo can be accepted even against this kind of ownership chazakah. The Shach cites the great Rabbi Eliyahu ibn Chaim as holding likewise. The Rif also supports this in a responsum cited by Baal HaTerumot, sha'ar 2, part 1. He rules that a debtor is believed to say that property belongs to another person, although that other person must take the property with an oath. Baal HaTerumot is surprised and suggests a textual error: why should the debtor be believed, and what is the purpose of the other person's oath? There is no difficulty. The debtor has a migo that he could have transferred the assets, and is therefore believed. Nevertheless, the property is presently in the debtor's possession and is subject to the earlier creditor's claim. The alleged owner is now taking property away from that creditor. He therefore resembles one collecting from encumbered property. Whether the property was transferred to a purchaser or is claimed by a later creditor or owner, the person removing it from the earlier creditor should not collect without an oath. The debtor's migo does not eliminate that oath, because according to this approach we do not say that a migo exempts a claimant from an oath. This resembles a creditor holding a pledge who is believed through a migo of purchase regarding the amount of his claim, yet still must swear because he is collecting. The same applies here. The Rif's responsum is therefore coherent and supports the practical conclusion stated above.
If it is known that he has investment funds belonging to others, etc. The Shach understands this to mean that witnesses establish the existence of the investment and that it is still within its term, so that it is certain the debtor has not returned it. Otherwise, we could claim that he may already have returned the investment. According to the Shach, the debtor's own credibility is needed because without his statement we would say that he already spent the investment funds and they are no longer extant. His words are strained. There is no need for these additional conditions. The debtor has a migo that he could simply give the property to the investor. Even if he said that he had not returned the investment, he could be believed through that migo. The only weakness ordinarily affecting this migo is that it stands against the chazakah that whatever property is in a person's possession belongs to him. But where it is already known that he regularly holds investment property belonging to others, that chazakah no longer applies in the same force. His possession is comparable to that of a broker, for whom we do not presume that everything in his hands is his own. He therefore has a proper migo and is believed that the assets are investment property, without any need to establish that the investment has not yet been returned. This is simple and clear.
They are from the investment of so-and-so, and the Shach writes. The Shach says that this protects only the half of the investment that has the status of a deposit. The half legally treated as a loan must share with the debtor's other creditor, because loaned money is given for expenditure. This follows, he says, from the rule in siman 108 that an iska is half loan. His words are astonishing. The Gemara in the chapter HaMekabel explicitly states that investment property does not become ordinary movable property in the hands of the investor's heirs. Rashi explains that even the half treated as a loan does not become part of the heirs' ordinary assets. On this basis the Rif, Rambam, and Rosh derive the rule that if the investor holds an iska and also owes money to creditors or a wife's ketubah, those creditors have no claim against the investment property. This necessarily includes the loan half, as is clear from all the poskim there, because they equate the creditor and ketubah cases with the rule regarding heirs, and the Gemara speaks of the entire investment. The same is codified in Yoreh Deah, siman 176. It is true that those sources speak of a case established by witnesses or a document, so that it is certain the property is the investment of the other person. But once the debtor here has a valid migo and is believed, that evidentiary status is equivalent for this purpose to proof. What difference is there between witnesses and a migo once beit din accepts the fact that the property belongs to the investment? The debtor then has no beneficial ownership that his personal creditor can seize. This is entirely unlike siman 108, where it is uncertain whether the investment was already returned. Here the accepted fact is that the specific property presently held is the other person's investment. The debtor's creditor therefore has no right even in the half classified as a loan. The Shach's ruling is exceedingly difficult.
