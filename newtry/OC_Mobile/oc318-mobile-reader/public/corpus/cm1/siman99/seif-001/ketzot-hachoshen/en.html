If visible property is found in the debtor's possession, the Shach distinguishes objects and movables from money. With ordinary objects, proof that they belong to a particular person can remove them from the debtor's estate. With money, merely proving that the money was originally deposited may not suffice if the debtor had permission to use it, because he then becomes a borrower. The Shach's statement concerning iska funds is too narrow. He implies that even if the iska is clearly identified, the investor is prior only as to the deposit half. The Gemara in Perek HaMekabel says precisely the opposite: because it is called iska, even the loan half does not become ordinary movables in the hands of the recipient's heirs. Earlier authorities and the Shulchan Aruch in Yoreh Deah likewise give the investor priority in identified iska funds over ordinary creditors, even as to the loan portion, because the money was given specifically for the venture. Likewise, the Shach's assertion that deposited money becomes a loan merely because the custodian has permission to use it is too broad. Chacham Tzvi correctly proves that so long as the original deposit money remains intact and has not actually been used, it retains the status of a deposit. The owner is therefore prior to ordinary creditors in taking back his own property. This is even stronger under contemporary commercial enactments that protect an identifiable seller's merchandise when still intact. If the owner's own money remains physically identifiable, he is certainly prior. Even an identifiable loan fund can in some circumstances be prior to general creditors. See siman 120, seif katan 10.
