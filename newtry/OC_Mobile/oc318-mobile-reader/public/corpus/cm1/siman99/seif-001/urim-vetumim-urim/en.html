Anything more. This is not literal. Whenever he lacks enough property to pay the entire debt, even though he has some property available for partial payment, the oath is imposed. The intent is that he lacks sufficient money, movable property, and land to satisfy the debt. If he has any of these categories, even though they are not presently liquid, the oath is not imposed. See Sema.
The Geonim instituted, etc. They did so because they saw fraudulent debtors who had wealth in their homes but claimed to possess nothing in order to evade repayment of their loans.
Similar to a Torah oath. It resembles the oath of one who admits part of a claim, where we are concerned that he is merely delaying payment. Here too, the debtor reasons that he is not denying the debt but only seeking additional time. It also resembles the custodial oath requiring one to swear that the article is not in his possession. It appears that the suspicion must involve at least two silver ma'ah, since the Geonim modeled the enactment upon a Mishnah oath, and just as a Mishnah oath requires a claim of two silver ma'ah, this enactment should resemble Torah law in all its essential features.
A gift on condition that it be returned. Baal HaTerumos asks why this oath is needed. If the debtor receives such a gift and it is later returned to him, the property should already be included in his oath that if he later acquires means he will pay his creditor. He answers that we are concerned that he may have made only a sham gift on condition of return, not a genuine gift, solely to conceal the property, in which case the gift is void. The meaning is as follows. One could still ask that when the recipient returns the gift, the debtor would then become obligated under his oath to pay the creditor. The difference is that if the gift is void, the property is already the debtor's now, and when thirty days of support are set aside for him, that property should be counted among his assets. If the sham is not discovered until later and the recipient then returns the property, it will be treated as newly acquired wealth, and another thirty days of support would have to be set aside before payment, causing loss to the creditor. For the same reason the debtor must swear that he has not concealed property in another person's possession, even though such property would eventually fall under the oath concerning future gains. See Gidulei Terumah and what I wrote in Tumim.
According to his manner. This means according to the lifestyle of an average person. We do not follow his personal habit if he lived either as one of the poor or as one of the wealthy, but give him the standard needs of an average person, as explained above in siman 97.
And if money later appears in his possession. If he held money as a deposit, even if he proves that it belongs to the depositor, Shach writes that since the custodian has permission to use it, it is regarded as being in the custodian's possession and all his creditors acquire rights in it. I already clarified at the end of siman 73 that this ruling is incorrect. The money remains in the depositor's possession, and the custodian's creditors have no rights in it.
It appears, etc. Likewise, he is not believed to say, 'I also owe others,' in order to reduce this creditor's share, unless witnesses establish the other debt and that it has not been paid, such as where it is still within its term. Shach, citing Maggid Mishneh, further holds that even before any money later appears, he is not believed to claim other debts, although at that moment the claim has no practical effect because he has no money. Bach disagrees. See Tumim, where I wrote that Bach's view appears correct as practical law, and that when the debtor is not presently established to possess money he is believed. I also concluded that if he has only movable property or land acquired after the loans, and the promissory notes contain no after acquired property clause, so that there is no priority and the creditors would divide, he may at the time of the oath say that he owes others even though he presently has assets. See there at length.
And some disagree. Shach argues at length that no authority actually holds this view, and therefore the debtor is never believed, because his migo stands against a strong presumption. In Tumim I showed, however, that Rif, Tosafos, Rosh, Shulchan Aruch in Even HaEzer, and Ramban himself in his novellae all hold that the migo is accepted, and this is the proper ruling. This applies where he had a migo to give the property away before witnesses saw it and before it was established in beit din. If the property was already seen, and his only migo is that he could give it away now, Shach's objection has force, as Maggid Mishneh writes. Rosh similarly writes in chapter 2 of Kesubos regarding a trust note that a person prefers to say the property belongs to another, because then he can rely on that person not to deny his claim or seize the property, whereas if he gives it away the recipient may keep it and leave him without recourse. See above, siman 47, and Sema there.
If it is known, etc. Shach understands this to mean that the business funds are still within the period in which the recipient could not claim repayment, so that his statement is needed only to establish that the money before us is business capital and was not already spent and replaced by other funds. See Tumim, where I wrote that this limitation is unnecessary. Since the debtor has a migo, and it does not contradict a presumption because it is known that he regularly receives business capital from others, he is believed.
He is believed to say. Shach holds that the investor takes half the money alone, while in the other half he shares equally with the debtor's other creditors. See Tumim, where I wrote that this does not appear correct. All of the money belongs to the investor's claim, and the other creditors have no right to it. See there.
