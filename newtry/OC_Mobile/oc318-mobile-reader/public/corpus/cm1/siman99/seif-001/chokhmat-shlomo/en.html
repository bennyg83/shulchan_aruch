Seif 1: Rather, he says that he has nothing with which to pay. Note: I was asked from the community of Skala about a man who was owed money by another, and when the debtor refused to pay, the creditor seized movables from him toward the debt. A third person then claimed that the movables were his because the debtor had bought them from him on credit and had not yet paid the purchase price. I answered that I cannot believe any Torah scholar would think the seller is automatically believed merely by saying he has not yet been paid, so that he could take all the movables for himself. Even if the debtor admits that he has not paid the seller, that admission is not believed to the detriment of other creditors. If the seller had made his claim before the creditor’s seizure, when he still had a migo to support it, he could have been believed. His claim does not contradict the apparent ownership of the goods, because even according to him the goods were already sold and belong to the debtor; he merely asserts that the debtor still owes him money. Such a claim can be supported by migo, as Shach explains in this siman. But without a migo he is not believed to obtain priority. Even if the seller holds a promissory note against the debtor, he is not thereby believed to say that these particular movables are his or that he alone may take them under the communal enactment. He has only the share due him together with the other creditors, depending on whether he seized and according to the applicable enactment, as discussed here and in Beit Shmuel, Even HaEzer siman 53. The questioner cited Tzemach Tzedek, responsum 117, who writes that because a particular rule is merely an enactment it applies only to the woman herself and not to one who comes through her. He wished to infer that similarly the enactment here applies only before seizure and not after seizure. But from Tzemach Tzedek we enter a dispute among major authorities, because Bach and Beit Shmuel in Even HaEzer hold the opposite, that one who comes through her is treated like her. Tzemach Tzedek stands alone and cannot support a claim of kim li. A stronger proof could have been brought from Shach, siman 353, in the name of Maharam Alshich, with whom Shach agrees, regarding the marketplace enactment: if the stolen object comes back into the original owner’s hands, he need not reimburse the purchaser. This shows that where a rule is merely an enactment, even one enacted in the era of the Gemara, seizure can leave the possessor with the underlying strict law. All the more so regarding later regional enactments. Similarly, with the rule that one who is obligated to swear and cannot swear must pay, because the Gemara says we should not extend that rule beyond its established cases, where the creditor has already seized property, even if the borrower died during the lender’s lifetime and afterward the lender died, we do not remove the property from his heirs, as stated in the Gemara and Choshen Mishpat siman 108. Accordingly, in our case too, because the special priority would rest only on an enactment, seizure may leave the possessor with the strict law. Even if there were doubt concerning the scope of the enactment, we would revert to the underlying law. Thus the practical ruling given by the questioner was correct. This is what I answered.
