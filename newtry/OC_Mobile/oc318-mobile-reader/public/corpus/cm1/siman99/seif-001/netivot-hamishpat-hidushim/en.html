And some disagree. The Shach concludes that this latter view is primary. Specifically, if the debtor says, "These movable items belong to so and so," he is not believed, because his migo conflicts with the presumption that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. But if he says, "I owe so and so," he is believed, because he has a migo that he could have given the property to that creditor, provided the assets have not yet been placed under beit din control. Once beit din has taken control of them, even though he could presently make an acquisition to the creditor in a manner that would legally prevent others from recovering the property, this is not considered a strong migo and he is not believed. Likewise, if he says that certain movables belong to another person and he has a migo, where those movables are actually in that person's possession, then even if it is known that they formerly belonged to the debtor and there are witnesses who saw them, he is believed. Similarly, if he gives a handwritten acknowledgment, even though there is concern that he intentionally antedated it, he is believed through a migo that he could have written the correct date. Shach:

Likewise, the Shach's statement that the owner of the investment shares equally with other creditors regarding the half treated as a loan is incorrect. The investor first takes his own money before the other creditors. The Shach's further statement that loose deposited money, which the custodian may use, ranks equally with other creditors is also incorrect.
