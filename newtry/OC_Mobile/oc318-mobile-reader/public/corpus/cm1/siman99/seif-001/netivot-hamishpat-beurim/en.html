And if it appears to him. See Shach, seif katan 1, through his statement that even before the debtor changed his circumstances there is still concern for collusion. Tumim questions this, because the Shach himself writes in seif katan 6 that if the debtor says he owes another person and has a migo that he could have given the property to that person, he is believed by virtue of the migo. Here too, since we do not know whether he has assets, he could simply give away assets that he has concealed, so he appears to have a clear migo. In my opinion there is no difficulty. In the case discussed by the Shach in seif katan 6, the movable property is physically before beit din and the debtor had the immediate ability to give it to that other person. There is therefore no possible motive for collusion, because if that were his intent, he could simply have transferred it. Here, however, there is reason to suspect that money may later come into his possession in the presence of witnesses, for example because he expects an inheritance, or he has money held by another person who is not presently here and fears that the person may later deliver it to him before witnesses. At that later time he will no longer have a migo. He therefore schemes now by saying that he owes a certain person, so that he will later be believed when the money reaches him and the migo is gone. At present he also lacks the ability to give that money to the other person, because the money is not yet in his possession. He therefore now claims that it belongs to that person and thereby creates the collusive arrangement. The Shach's ruling is thus well understood. See also Shach, seif katan 3, where there is a textual error. The correct text is: “In every case he is not believed.”
And there are those who disagree. See Shach, seif katan 6, and see what I wrote below and in siman 62, seif katan 9. The Shach writes that if the debtor says, “These movables belong to a certain person's iska,” and it is established by witnesses or by a document that he holds such and such an amount as an iska from that person and has not returned it, for example where that person received a clause of trust, then the debtor is believed. This is difficult, because a clause of trust does not prevail against a later creditor, as explained above in siman 71. Perhaps, because the other party has such a clause and there is certainly some obligation owing to him, the presumption that everything in a person's possession belongs to him is weakened. The debtor is then believed by a migo that he could have transferred the movables to that person, and the migo is no longer opposed by an intact chazakah. This is strained. The primary question whether witnesses of prior possession are required where the holder admits that the item was not purchased will be discussed below in seif katan 4. Regarding the Shach's statement that in an iska the loan half is equal among all creditors, this is incorrect. Yoreh Deah siman 176 establishes that because the recipient may not spend the loan half freely, it remains in the investor's presumptive ownership. Tumim and Ketzot HaChoshen write similarly, and the matter is straightforward.
However, if it is known. See Shach, seif katan 7, through his statement concerning a debt that is still within its due date. It appears that this applies specifically where the debtor is not ordinarily engaged in iskas with other people's funds, and we merely know that on one occasion he had someone else's iska in his possession. In that case it must be established that he cannot claim to have returned it. But where a person regularly trades with other people's money, even if he could claim that he returned a particular iska, the debtor is believed by a migo that he could have given the property to that person. Such a migo is not opposed by the ordinary chazakah of ownership, just as in the case of a broker in seif 3.
