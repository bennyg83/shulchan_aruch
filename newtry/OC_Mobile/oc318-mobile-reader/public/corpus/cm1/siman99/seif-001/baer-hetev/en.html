That we arrange. The laws of arranging basic necessities for a debtor are stated by the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in siman 97, seif 23. The Tur expressly writes that this oath was enacted when the Sages saw that fraudsters had become numerous and lenders were consequently refusing to lend. See what is written below in seif 7. Sema.
To return. Baal HaTerumot makes clear that only where the transfer was made in order to hide assets from creditors is the gift entirely void, allowing creditors to take the property immediately. If, however, the donor did not intend to hide assets, the gift remains valid until the end of the period for which it was given, and only after that period may the creditor collect from it. Sema.
In his usual manner. The meaning of "in his usual manner" is explained in siman 97, seif 23. See there, seif katan 27. Sema.
Property. This means objects and movable property, as the Rambam states explicitly. Cash, however, is different. Even proof that the money belongs to another is not sufficient to give that person exclusive rights to it; he is treated like any other creditor unless he proves that the money was entrusted to the debtor as an investment, as explained above. If he merely proves that it was deposited with the debtor, that does not help because the debtor has permission to use money and therefore becomes a borrower of it. See the end of siman 47 and siman 75, seif 4. Shach.
Others. Likewise, if he says that the notes were given in trust or were already paid, he is not believed, as stated in siman 47. The Rambam writes that whenever a person is liable on a promissory note and, on his own initiative, admits owing another debt, if his property is insufficient for both debts, the holder of the promissory note alone collects, lest the debtor and the alleged second creditor collude. The Maggid Mishneh infers that this applies only where there are no witnesses to the second loan. The Shach writes that this is not precise. The same applies even where there were witnesses to the loan, provided it was an ordinary loan concerning which the debtor could claim repayment. It does not apply where the second debt is still within its term, or where the debtor was required to repay only before witnesses, or in similar cases. This is unlike the Kesef Mishneh, whose distinctions do not appear correct to the Shach. The Maggid Mishneh further appears to understand the Rambam as giving an earlier oral creditor priority over a later documented creditor, contrary to the Geonim. The Shach writes that nothing can be inferred from the Rambam on this point, because he may be discussing movable property, where priority does not apply. Normally the creditors would therefore divide it, but once the debtor is not believed about the second debt, only the documented creditor collects. See the Shach's full discussion.
Investment. Meaning that another person gave him merchandise or money to trade with for half the profits. Anything held by him not as a loan remains legally in the owner's possession, and the debtor's creditor cannot collect it, as stated in Yoreh Deah 177. Sema.
Proof. The Rif, however, wrote in a responsum that if the debtor claims, "Everything in my possession belongs to another," that other person swears that it is his and receives it. The Bach suggests that the Rambam may agree where the other party swears. The Shach rejects this. The Rambam's wording, "until he brings proof," indicates that actual evidence is required. The Shach suggests that the Rif responsum contains a scribal error. He found in Baal HaTerumot, Shaar 5, end of part 1, that after quoting this responsum it says that the text must be corrupt because the Gemara before us states otherwise. Shach.
Chazakah. The Shach writes that if the debtor says, "I owe so-and-so," and he has a migo that he could have paid that person from these funds, for example because witnesses did not see the money in his possession, he is believed according to all views at least to give that other creditor a share in the money alongside the holder of the promissory note. He is not making a claim contrary to an established chazakah. The Rambam writes similarly, and the Tur in siman 93 states that if Shimon claims he owes Levi and could have given the money to Levi, he is believed. See the Shach at length. The Taz writes that although siman 78 recognizes a migo even against a chazakah, the Maggid Mishneh distinguishes a particularly strong chazakah. Against such a chazakah there is no migo because a person may collude to deprive his creditor. Rivash 481 writes that if a document states that a debt is payable whenever the lender demands it, and that lender himself owes money to others, those creditors may collect the debt even if their debtor does not wish to demand payment. See also siman 86, seif 9, Responsa Maharit I, 45, Maharashach I, 124, and Responsa Mahari MiBeit Levi 77.
They divide. See the Shach, who argues at length against the Rama and maintains that there is no real dispute here. He derives several practical rulings from the matter. See there.
From an investment. The Shach writes that this refers to the deposit half of the investment. Regarding the loan half, the investor is only an ordinary creditor. This is implied by siman 108, seif 4.
