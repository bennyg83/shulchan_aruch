Rambam, Hilkhot Malveh chapter 3, law 2, cited by the Tur. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this enactment is recorded in the books of the earlier authorities, and so too in the Ittur. Kesef Mishneh writes in the name of Sefer HaTerumot, Gate 2, that this oath resembles the oath of one who admits part of a claim. We say that he is evading payment because circumstances are pressing him, and he thinks, 'When I have money I will repay him.' The Sages therefore imposed an oath so that he will admit the truth. He further writes that even if the creditor claims only that perhaps the debtor has property, we administer this oath. The early authorities called it the oath of suspicion.
Kesef Mishneh further writes in the name of Ba'al HaTerumot that Rambam intended the Ramban's explanation of the incident concerning the gift of Beit Choron, Nedarim 48a. The debtor must swear that he did not now give property as a gift on condition that it be returned merely to evade his creditor, for such a gift is ineffective, as his debt demonstrates that his sole intent was to evade the creditor. Ba'al HaTerumot explains there in part 2 that whenever the loan preceded such a conditional gift, the gift accomplishes nothing, because the creditor had already acquired his lien upon the property. Ba'al HaTerumot writes in Gate 2, part 1, that later authorities enacted this procedure, and they enacted well, so that Hashem's name should not be invoked constantly in oaths. This is the proper practice.
The language of the Tur, seif 3. Rambam likewise writes this there, as does Ba'al HaTerumot in the aforementioned Gate. He writes that it is derived from the statement in Ketubot 94a: one oath serves for one hundred claimants.
Rambam likewise writes this in chapter 4, law 4. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this rule is not stated explicitly in the Gemara. As for why he is not believed through a migo that he could have given the property away, some explain that this is a weak migo, because had he given it away, the recipient would not have been obligated to return it. Ramban explains that there is a chazakah that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him, and we do not invoke migo against a chazakah, because a person is prone to collusion, as Rama writes. Kesef Mishneh writes that proof may be brought from the conclusion in Ketubot 19a, where the lender says so and the admission harms others.
