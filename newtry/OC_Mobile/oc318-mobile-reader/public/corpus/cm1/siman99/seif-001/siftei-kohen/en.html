And if it appears to him, etc. The Tur writes in seif 9 that if he took the oath and later acquired more than is necessary for his support, and he says that he owes debts to others, those others do not collect from him. The Bach writes that before he acquired additional means he is believed, because at that time his statement caused no loss to the holder of the note, since he then had nothing from which to pay. This does not appear correct to me, because there is still concern for collusion, for perhaps he does have property. This also follows from the Rambam, chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh, law 5, cited below in seif katan 4, who writes that if he admitted other debts and later acquired means, the surplus is taken only by the holders of notes. The Semag at the end of positive commandment 93 and the Mordechai in HaMekabel likewise imply contrary to the Bach.
Property, etc. This means objects and movable property. The Rambam states this explicitly in chapter 1 of Hilchot Malveh, law 4. With money, even proof does not establish that the money belongs exclusively to that other person rather than standing like the debtor's other creditors, unless he proves that it was given to him as an iska, in which case the deposit half is treated accordingly, as explained below in seif katan 7. Proof that the money was merely deposited with him does not help, because since he has permission to use the money he is considered a borrower with respect to it, as explained above at the end of siman 77, seif katan 9, and siman 75, seif katan 15.
And he says that it belongs to others, etc. Likewise, if he says that the notes were given in trust or have been paid, he is not believed, as explained above in siman 47. The Rambam writes at the end of chapter 2 of Hilchot Malveh, law 6, that anyone against whom there is a promissory note who voluntarily admits another debt, when he lacks sufficient assets for both creditors, must allow the holder of the note alone to collect, lest they have arranged a collusion. The Maggid Mishneh writes that this language appears to concern a case without witnesses to the other loan, and from this he infers that the Rambam holds that an earlier oral loan takes precedence over a later written loan, unlike the Geonim cited above, for otherwise there would be no concern for collusion since the holder of the note would in any event collect first. In my view, the absence of witnesses is not essential. The same rule applies where there were witnesses to an ordinary loan if the debtor could still have claimed that he paid it. It excludes a case in which witnesses establish a debt in circumstances where he cannot claim payment, such as during the term of the loan or where he was told not to repay except before witnesses. This is unlike the Kesef Mishneh, who proposed distinctions that do not appear correct to me and wrote that if the other creditors sued first the debtor is believed. The principal rule, in my view, is that whenever he could have claimed payment and his liability to them now rests only on his own admission, he is not believed to their detriment of the holder of the note. The apparent repetition in the Rambam, in laws 5 and 6, can be explained as follows. First, while discussing the enactment of the Geonim, he stated the rule in that context, and afterward he stated it as the underlying law. Moreover, in law 5 he first teaches that one who became obligated to take this oath because of a debt, who admitted other debts before he later acquired surplus assets, does not thereby make the other creditors entitled to that surplus. One might have thought that because he was already subject to an oath he would be believed, since he would not swear falsely, and also that because the admission preceded his acquisition of means there was no reason to suspect collusion. Thus the first ruling contains two additional points. He then states the case of Reuven who owed Shimon and said that the note was one of trust, and more generally anyone bound by a note who voluntarily admits another debt. One might have thought that collusion is suspected only in the former case, where he sees that all his property is being taken and he is being required to swear that he has no more, so he has an incentive to invent other debts. The Rambam therefore teaches that even where he volunteered the admission before his assets were taken, if he lacks enough for both creditors we suspect collusion. As for the Maggid Mishneh's inference that the Rambam gives priority to the earlier oral loan, I do not see this in the Rambam. He may be speaking of movables, where there is no priority and the creditors would otherwise divide them. Since the debtor is not believed, where he lacks a migo that he could have given the property to the alleged creditor, as explained below in seif katan 6, only the holder of the note collects. Alternatively, he may be speaking where the debtor acquired the assets after the loan and the note did not include after acquired property, in which case there is no priority as explained below in siman 104, seif 6. This is clear to me.
We do not listen to him until he brings proof. The Bach cites a responsum of Rav Alfasi stating that when the debtor claims that everything in his possession belongs to another, that other person swears that the property is his and receives it, and suggests that perhaps the Rambam agrees when the other person swears. This does not appear correct to me, for the Rambam's statement that we do not listen until he brings proof plainly requires proof. I also examined the rulings of Maharam Recanati, siman 306, and he does not state that the Rif disputes the Rambam. Rather, after quoting the Rambam he quotes the language of a responsum of Rav Alfasi. It appears that there is some scribal error in that responsum and that Maharam Recanati cited it for other novel rulings contained there. I likewise found this stated explicitly in Baal HaTerumot, gate 5, end of part 1: since there is a chazakah that whatever is found in a person's possession belongs to him until he proves otherwise, the creditor collects from what is found in the debtor's possession, and we do not believe the debtor even under oath when he attributes his property to others, because we suspect collusion. He cites the Rambam that either the debtor brings proof or his creditor collects. He then cites a responsum attributed to the Rif that if the debtor says that property in his hands was deposited by Reuven, Reuven swears that it is his and takes it, and remarks that the text may have been corrupted by scribes, since the Gemara expressly teaches concern for collusion and that an admission is not believed when it harms others. He asks where such an oath allowing one to swear and collect is found among the oaths of the Mishnah or Gemara. For this reason this responsum was not brought by the other decisors, and the principal ruling is as stated by the Rambam and Baal HaTerumot.
Even though he has a migo that he could have returned them, etc. This is a migo only because before his admission in beit din he could have handed the property to the other person and said that he was returning that person's movables, and the court would not then have known otherwise. Even so, the migo does not prevail here because of the chazakah. But once he has come before beit din and the movables have been established there, or they were previously seen in his possession, there is no migo based on his ability now to convey them as a gift before beit din through chalipin. That is not a sound migo, because it would be evident that he is doing so to remove property from his creditor, and he would be deliberately acting improperly by giving away property while remaining an unpaid debtor. I wrote similarly above in siman 47, seif katan 7, in the name of the Ran. The Maggid Mishneh also cites an explanation that this is not a migo because if he actually gave the property away the recipient would not be obligated to return it, whereas if he falsely claims that it was deposited with him, then to satisfy his obligation toward Heaven he would have to leave it with beit din or return it. Tosafos, the Rosh, and other decisors write similarly regarding one who says that a note is a note of trust. He is not believed by a migo that he could have waived the debt, because he does not intend to waive and lose his debt, while in saying that the note is one of trust he is not making himself a robber. Before coming to beit din, however, these concerns do not apply in the same way, for just as he now says that the other person deposited the property with him, he could previously have said that he had already given it to that person or that it was no longer in his possession.
And some disagree, etc. The Rama wrote this on the basis of his comments in Darkei Moshe, where he treated the matter as a dispute among the decisors. In my view there is no dispute, as I will explain, and because several practical rules emerge from this discussion I must elaborate somewhat. Darkei Moshe writes that the Rambam and the Maggid Mishneh in the name of the Ramban hold that everything found in a person's possession is presumed his, while the Mordechai cites Rabbi Avigdor that a debtor is believed to say that money in his hands belongs to others, even if he and his household are supported from it, for perhaps others gave it to him on condition that his creditors not collect it. Hagahot Maimoniyot rules like the Rambam, and Darkei Moshe concludes that this should be followed because most decisors agree, unlike Binyamin Zev. He also cites the Rashba and the Maggid Mishneh that the debtor is not believed even where he has a migo that he could have returned the property, because the chazakah that property in a person's possession belongs to him defeats the migo. Darkei Moshe then understood a responsum of the Rosh and a responsum of the Rashba cited by Beit Yosef at the end of siman 47 as supporting the contrary view. This does not appear correct to me. I found an explicit responsum of the Rashba stating that when a man owes a ketubah and another creditor and says that movables in his possession belong to someone else, he is not believed if his remaining assets are insufficient, even where the ketubah did not include movables together with land and he therefore would have had a migo that he could simply have given those movables away. The reason is that whatever is in a person's possession is presumed his, and he is not believed against that chazakah even with a migo. The Rashba compares this to the rule that a creditor who says his note is one of trust is not believed when this harms others, despite the fact that he could have waived the debt. I therefore explain the responsum cited by Beit Yosef as a case where the movables were actually in the hands of Nemuel, although Reuven had knowledge of them and there were witnesses who had seen them, so Nemuel could not have prevailed without Reuven's admission. Reuven's admission then assists Nemuel, who is himself in possession. This understanding also fits the following responsum of the Rashba cited there. It likewise resolves the difficulty raised in Gidulei Terumah concerning the apparent contradiction in the Rashba. Darkei Moshe also understood the responsum of the Rosh to dispute the Maggid Mishneh, but that too is not necessary. The Rosh says that merely designating money for another does nothing, because a loan is given for expenditure, and even if the debtor has cash available he cannot say that it belongs to someone else before it has reached that person's hands. But if he is known to hold an iska for another, he is believed to identify particular property as belonging to that iska by a migo that he could have delivered it to the owner in payment. The case is therefore one where it is independently known that he received an iska from that person, as the Rama himself writes immediately afterward. The Sema and Maharashdam also explain the responsum of the Rosh in this manner. Thus the Rama's own gloss appears to retreat from the position he expressed in Darkei Moshe. Accordingly, the phrase 'and some disagree' cannot properly be based on the Rosh, and the responsum of the Rashba likewise supports the Maggid Mishneh. In truth, there is no decisor who clearly disputes the basic chazakah in the case under discussion. It further appears that if the debtor says, 'I owe money to such and such a person,' and he has a migo because he could have taken money that was still within his control and paid that person, for example where the existence of the money is known only through his own statement, he is believed according to all opinions to the extent that the other creditor shares in the money with the holder of the note. Here he is not contradicting the chazakah that the money is his. Rather, he admits that the money is his but says that he also owes another person, and he has a migo because he could have given that person the money and then claimed to have nothing. The Rambam at the end of Hilchot Sheluchin and the Tur above in siman 93 state a comparable rule. The rulings of the Rambam, the Semag, and the Tur that an insolvent debtor who says he owes others is not believed to diminish the holder of the note therefore concern a case where he has no such migo, for example land, money previously seen in his possession, property held by a third party, or property already established before beit din. On this reading the Rambam and Semag also agree with Rabbi Avigdor and Binyamin Zev. Indeed, the Mordechai cites the Semag and Rabbi Avigdor side by side without presenting them as disputants. Rabbi Avigdor is speaking where the debtor's possession of the money is known only from his own statement, so he is believed that others gave it to him on condition that creditors not collect from it, because he could have returned it and claimed to have nothing. But if he claims that specific movables already known to be in his possession belong to someone else, he is not believed according to anyone. I later found that Mahari ben Lev likewise ruled that one is believed, wherever he has a migo that he could have transferred the goods, to say that the goods belong to another, relying on the responsum of the Rashba cited above. In my view this is incorrect for the reasons already given. It appears that he overlooked the explicit words of the Ramban and Maggid Mishneh that a person is not believed to say that movables in his possession belong to another even where he has a migo, because of the chazakah that whatever is in a person's possession belongs to him. The Maggid Mishneh also makes clear that the Rambam agrees. Mahari ben Lev further cited Tosafos in the second chapter of Ketubot, who explain the case of one who says a note is a note of trust as involving a note already established in beit din or held by a third party. That is not a proof. A note is not itself the money, so possession of the note does not create a chazakah that the money represented by it belongs to the possessor in the same way that possession of movables creates a chazakah in the movables themselves. This also resolves any apparent contradiction between the rulings in siman 47 and the present rule. The Rashba's statement cited by Beit Yosef at the beginning of siman 98 likewise means that at the time Reuven executed notes in Levi's name he did not yet owe Shimon at all. But if he already owed Shimon at the time, even if that debt became known only later, his admission is not believed to Shimon's detriment when there are insufficient assets, despite any migo, because the chazakah remains that whatever was in his possession was his. Gidulei Terumah asked against this chazakah from the rule concerning one who attributes his money to a powerful non Jew, where beit din compels him to sell land. If the money in his possession is presumed his, why not compel immediate payment from that money? The answer is that because of fear of the non Jew, whose power is overwhelming, beit din cannot compel him to surrender those particular funds. The practical result is this. A debtor who owes one creditor by note and lacks sufficient assets, when movables are found in his possession and he claims that they are deposited, rented, or borrowed property belonging to another, is not believed, even if that other person swears and even if the debtor had a migo that he could previously have handed the property over without anyone knowing. The chazakah that property in a person's possession belongs to him defeats that migo. If, however, he says that the movables are from a known iska of another person, and witnesses or a document establish that he received a specified amount as an iska and has not returned it, for example because the agreement contains credibility provisions or the term has not expired, then although it is not known that these particular movables are from the iska, he is believed if he had a migo that he could have transferred them to the owner. The owner of the iska takes the deposit half first, up to the amount of the iska. But if the movables had previously been seen in the debtor's possession, were established in beit din, or are held by a third party, he is not believed, even though he could now convey them by chalipin. The same applies to money: if he says that money in his possession is from a known iska and he could have handed it to the owner, the owner takes the deposit half first and the holders of notes divide the remainder. He is also believed to say that he owes another person, to the extent that the other person shares proportionately in the money with the holders of notes. But that other creditor receives no more than his proper share merely because the debtor designated particular money to him or proves that this very money was originally lent or deposited with the debtor, because once the debtor had permission to use the money he became a borrower and the money became his. If the money was previously seen in his possession, was established before beit din, or is in the hands of a third party, he is not believed to assert additional debts so as to reduce the holders of notes. Whenever such an admission is not believed because it harms existing creditors, the result is the same even if the earlier debt was unknown at the time of the admission and only later discovered to have existed then. Likewise, if the debtor says that money in his possession was given to him on condition that his creditors have no right in it and that he may use it only for his own needs and the support of his household, he is believed if the money became known only through his own statement and was not previously established before beit din, and the holders of notes do not collect it. Proof that others lent him money does not help if the alleged lenders' claim is one to which he could have answered that he already paid, because we suspect that he did repay and is now colluding with them by saying that he did not. This remains so even if those others sued him first. If, however, they prove that the debt is still within its term, or that he undertook not to pay except before witnesses, or another circumstance in which a claim of payment would not be available, then they share like other creditors in movables or money, where there is no law of priority.
However, if it is known, etc. This means that witnesses or a document establish that he holds an iska in the amount he claims and that he could not have asserted that he returned it, for example because the agreement granted the investor credibility or because the term has not expired. He is then believed to say that these movables are from that person's iska, even though it is possible that he spent the original iska and these are other assets, because the rule of an iska is that wherever it is found it remains in the ownership of its original owner. This applies only where he has a migo that he could have handed these movables to the owner. If they were previously seen in his possession, established before beit din, or are held by a third party, he is not believed, as explained above.
Regarding money in his possession, these are from the iska of such and such a person, etc. Meaning that the money was given to him as an iska, as explained in the preceding seif katan. But if he says that the other person merely deposited this money with him, his claim is ineffective even where he has a migo, according to all opinions, for the reason explained above.
