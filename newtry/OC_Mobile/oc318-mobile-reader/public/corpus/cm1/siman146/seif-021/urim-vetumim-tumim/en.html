He is believed by migo that he could have said, "I bought it from you." Ramban, in his novellae and even more fully in Milchamot Hashem, writes that the possessor has such a migo only where there are no witnesses that he had already possessed for six years. If witnesses establish the six years, he cannot say, "I bought it from you," because they prove that he was occupying the field even before the challenger's purchase. To say, "at first I entered only for produce and afterward I bought it from you," would itself require a migo. Where no migo exists, that explanation is not believed. Here, therefore, no migo remains. It is quite possible, indeed nearly certain, that Tosafot and those who follow them do not disagree with Ramban on this law. Tosafot phrase their question specifically: even if he brings witnesses only that he possessed for three years, why should he not be believed by migo? They do not ask more simply: if witnesses establish six years, why does the Gemara say that he lacks a chazakah? Evidently, in the six-year case Tosafot agree with Ramban that there is no useful migo. Their question is only why the Gemara requires witnesses to all seven years. If witnesses establish merely the final three years, why should the possessor not be believed by his own statement that the possession actually began seven years earlier? Tosafot reject the alternate explanation cited by Rashbam and follow Rashbam himself, who requires witnesses to seven years. Maharsha therefore correctly explains that Riva's question is not against the Gemara's statement that six years are insufficient. That can be answered either as Ramban does or according to the alternate reasoning discussed below. Rather, the question is why witnesses to all seven years are needed. Once there are witnesses to three years, why is the possessor not believed regarding the earlier four by migo? For the alternate explanation cited by Rashbam, the matter is different. According to that view, where there are witnesses to the final three years, the possessor is in fact believed by his own statement that he possessed for seven. The phrase "seven but not six" must then be understood as follows: if he claims only six, his migo is against a chazakah, because we treat the public deed as the strongest possible protest and a person who is protested against is presumed to preserve his deed. Thus a six-year claim cannot be supported by migo. But if he claims seven, the claim can be accepted because the relevant three years preceded the challenger's deed. The Shach in seif katan 21 writes that Maharsha did not understand Tosafot correctly and that their question applies even according to the alternate explanation. This is difficult. According to that view, if the possessor claims seven years, he is indeed believed without witnesses to all seven. If he claims only six, his migo is against the presumption created by the challenger's deed, as just explained. What then is Riva's question against that interpretation? Maharsha's reading is therefore correct.
