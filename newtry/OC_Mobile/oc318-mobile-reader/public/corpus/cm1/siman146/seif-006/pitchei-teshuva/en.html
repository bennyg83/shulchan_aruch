And advised him to purchase it. See Responsa Raanach, part 2, siman 43, who writes that merely speaking in favor of the purchase does not cause the challenger to lose his right. If, however, the challenger actually served as broker between the parties, he loses his right, for this is comparable to signing the deed as a witness, as at the beginning of the next siman, since he performed an affirmative act. This applies only where there are witnesses that he served as broker. If the possessor claims that he served as broker and the challenger denies it, the possessor is not believed by a migo that he could have said, "I purchased it from you," despite his three year chazakah. The claim that the challenger acted as broker does not itself create the chazakah so that we could first accept the claim and thereby award him the land. Rather, the brokerage is evidence that the challenger had no right in it, for otherwise he would not have assisted in the purchase, and such evidence cannot be established solely by the statement of the interested litigant. See there. Shaar Mishpat cites this and writes that he does not understand the reasoning. Why should the migo not help? According to the possessor's account, the challenger's brokerage is evidence that the challenger presumably sold the field to the person who sold it to him. This resembles siman 149, seif 16, regarding one who derives title through a non Jew and says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," or has a deed, where he is believed by the migo that he himself purchased it from the challenger.
