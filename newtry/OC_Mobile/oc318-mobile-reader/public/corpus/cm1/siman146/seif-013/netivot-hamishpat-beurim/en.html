And he said to him, "We know." See Sema, seif katan 29, who writes that this applies even when the possessor knows only from the seller. At first glance this is difficult. Why should this be stronger than any chazakah without a supporting claim, where the land is not removed from the possessor until the claimant brings witnesses? Here too the possessor should say: even if the seller is not believed that he bought the field, he is likewise not believed merely by saying that the field belonged to the claimant, for that is only the statement of one witness. Although if the field were still in the seller's hands he would be believed to admit that it belonged to another, where he was not living there at the time of sale why should he be stronger than one witness? It appears, however, that before the property came into the possessor's hands even one witness can establish the prior ownership. Ran in Chullin writes that one witness is believed concerning a lost object. Even if one may question his formulation, where the property is not yet held by anyone and there is no monetary chazakah against the witness, one witness is certainly believed. Therefore, if the field was already in Levi's hands through a chazakah unsupported by a claim, and only afterward Levi bought it from Shimon, who said that he had bought it from Reuven, then even according to Sema, Reuven needs another witness that it was his. Without that, he cannot remove it from Levi, because when Shimon said that it was Reuven's the field was already in Levi's possession, and one witness cannot remove land already held by another.
That it was yours. See Shach, seif katan 9, who agrees with those who hold that if the purchaser knows that it belonged to the claimant only from the seller, the claimant cannot remove it from him, because the seller himself was believed through a migo. Tumim writes that if the seller stated before witnesses at the time of sale, "It belongs to the claimant, but I bought it from him," the purchaser can be dispossessed according to Sema's position in siman 133, seif katan 4. There, where witnesses saw the object and the holder had claimed before them that it was purchased, his migo is lost because we accept what he said before witnesses to his detriment but not to his benefit. Thus here too, once the seller stated before witnesses that the field belonged to the claimant and that he bought it from him, his migo is lost and the land can be removed from the purchaser. According to this reasoning, however, one who says outside beit din, "This field belonged to your father, but I bought it from him," would no longer be believed to repeat before beit din that he bought it, even though Ketubot at the beginning of HaIshah Shenitarmelah explicitly treats such a statement under the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted. No posek ever states that saying it before witnesses outside beit din destroys the defense. The distinction is that a migo of "I returned it" is a weak migo, as Sema explains in simanim 64 and 133 and elsewhere. A person does not want to claim return, because then he can no longer display the object. He therefore displays it and claims purchase, relying on the migo. In such a weak migo, the statement outside beit din can matter. Here, however, the Gemara in Ketubot bases the rule on "the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted." That principle certainly applies even outside beit din. The Gemara itself compares it to cases of "I was a married woman and I am divorced" and "I was captured and I am pure," and derives the principle from "I gave my daughter to this man." In all those cases the statement works even outside beit din. The same applies here.
And he bought it from you. Shach writes that even after leaving beit din the possessor may later add, "He bought it from you in my presence," because this is not a retroactive migo. He says that this was his original intent. See what I write below in seif 24.
To recover his money. Tumim writes that according to the opinion, cited by Sema in seif katan 29, that the purchaser must return the field even if his knowledge that it belonged to the claimant comes only from the seller's admission, the purchaser may then recover his purchase money from the seller. The purchaser merely repeated what the seller told him, and the seller never warned him not to disclose it. In my view this is incorrect. The seller himself was believed that he bought the field because of his migo. The purchaser caused his own loss by saying something unnecessary, namely that he knew that it belonged to the claimant. Why should the seller become liable merely because he did not tell the purchaser not to repeat this? At most that is indirect causation.
