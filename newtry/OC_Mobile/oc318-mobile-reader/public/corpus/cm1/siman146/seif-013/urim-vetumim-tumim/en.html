And he said to him: I know that it was yours, etc. See Shach, seif katan 9, who explains that the Mechaber disagrees with the Rosh and refers to a case where the purchaser independently admits that the property belonged to the challenger, not where he knows this only because his seller told him so. In the latter case, the purchaser is believed to the same extent that the seller would have been believed. The Shach writes well, because the Rosh's formulation is strained. The Mishneh LaMelech in Hilchot To'en already struggled with it. The Pilpula Charifta offers an explanation that answers the Mishneh LaMelech's question, but the overall reading remains difficult. In my view, the Tur's citation in the name of Rabbenu Yonah, and similarly what appears from the Rif, that the seller need not reimburse the purchaser because he may say, "you caused your own loss," follows their own approach. They speak where the purchaser independently admits that he knows the field belonged to the challenger, not where he knows this only because the living seller told him so. In such a case the seller can properly say: what did I do to you? You already knew independently that the field belonged to the challenger. By adding your admission you deprived me of the migo I otherwise had. The added statement harmed only you, and I am not responsible for it. According to the Rosh, however, the purchaser says nothing independently. He merely repeats exactly what the seller told him: "so-and-so owned this field, and I purchased it from him." How can the seller then say, "you caused your own loss"? The purchaser did not alter the seller's words. The seller himself created the problem by telling the purchaser that he bought the field from the challenger. He should instead have told him simply, "the field is mine," or instructed him not to repeat the statement before the challenger. It is unreasonable that an innocent purchaser who merely repeats what he heard from his seller should be penalized and lose the purchase money from his own pocket. It is therefore clear that Rabbenu Yonah's reimbursement rule does not apply according to the Rosh's legal approach. This explains why the Rosh, whose practice is generally to cite the Rif's rulings that he accepts, omits the Rif's statement that if the purchaser suffers a loss, he caused it to himself. The Maggid Mishneh indeed understands the Rif as Rabbenu Yonah. According to the Rosh, however, that ruling has no place for the reason explained. This also resolves the Tur's structure. Earlier, in paragraph 21, he writes simply that if there are no witnesses that the seller lived there for even one day, the field must be returned and the purchaser seeks reimbursement from his seller, even where the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership. Later, in paragraph 25, the Tur cites the dispute between Rashbam and Rabbenu Yonah. Why did he first rule simply like Rashbam, rather than treat Rabbenu Yonah's contrary view as at least a dispute of major authorities in which the burden of proof should control? The commentators have not adequately resolved this. According to what I have written, it is clear. The dispute between Rashbam and Rabbenu Yonah depends on their broader approaches. Since the Tur follows his father the Rosh, Rabbenu Yonah's ruling is inapplicable, and the seller must reimburse the purchaser. This is why the Tur initially states that ruling simply. Accordingly, since the Mechaber explicitly follows Rabbenu Yonah and the Rif and rules that the purchaser cannot recover his money from the seller, it is clear that he adopts the view requiring an independent admission by the purchaser, not merely repetition of what the seller said. The Shach's interpretation is therefore compelled. If the seller told the purchaser before witnesses that he bought the field from the challenger, it appears that even if the purchaser later makes no independent admission that he knows the field belonged to the challenger, the challenger nevertheless prevails. The seller no longer has a migo. Above, in siman 133 seif 3, it is explained that if one displayed an object before witnesses prior to litigation, even if he said, "it belongs to so-and-so but is pledged to me for such and such amount," although had he made that claim in beit din he would have been believed by migo, his out-of-court statement creates prior sight. The part adverse to him is accepted, while the part favorable to him is not, based on the Gemara's rule that a clever claimant can cause the object to become "seen." See also siman 72 seif 18 in the gloss. The same applies here. Once the seller says before witnesses, "this land belonged to so-and-so, but I purchased it from him," his admission that it belonged to the challenger is effective, whereas his favorable assertion of purchase, made outside beit din, does not receive the benefit of migo. It is true that the Shach above in siman 72, seif katan 91, distinguishes between one who says an object is pledged to him and one who says he purchased it. But I already wrote above that this distinction is incorrect. An admission outside beit din can cause one to lose property. Thus the same applies here. Accordingly, if the seller admitted the challenger's ownership privately to the purchaser, the purchaser becomes a single witness to that admission. The matter then depends on the dispute between Rashba and Ri in Tosafot on 30a concerning whether, when the seller has a migo to say the property was never the challenger's but the purchaser is a single witness to the contrary, the seller remains believed by migo. This also resolves Maharsha's words. Tosafot initially write that in the Gemara's case, where the challenger lacked prior witnesses, even if the purchaser had not possessed for three years but merely claimed that his seller lived there one day, he would have been believed by migo that the property was never the challenger's. Maharsha writes that this is only according to Rashba, not according to Ri, and supports himself from the glosses to the Rosh, which cite in the name of Rashba the rule that where the purchaser says the seller lived there one day and the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership, the purchaser is believed by migo. At first glance this is difficult. Ri disagrees with Rashba only where the purchaser himself serves as one witness, whereas the glosses to the Rosh do not mention that the purchaser independently knows the challenger owned the field. Why attribute the rule specifically to Rashba? Likewise, Tosafot's opening discussion concerns the Gemara's ordinary case, where the purchaser merely repeats the seller's account, and Tosafot do not object to Rashbam's description. Why would Ri disagree? According to what I have written, however, once the seller told the purchaser that the field belonged to the challenger, even while adding that he bought it from him, the purchaser is a witness to the seller's admission. The favorable assertion of purchase, being outside beit din, does not preserve the migo. Thus the purchaser functions as one witness to the challenger's prior ownership even though he knows it only through the seller's own admission. This is why the glosses to the Rosh attribute the ruling to Rashba and why Maharsha says that according to Ri the seller cannot rely on migo when the purchaser serves as that single witness. The Mordechai likewise writes that Ri is uncertain where there are witnesses that the seller lived in the property but the purchaser did not himself possess for three years, whether the purchaser is believed by migo that the field was never the challenger's. This seems difficult because Tosafot present Ri's doubt specifically where there is one witness. According to the explanation above, however, the purchaser himself is the one witness by virtue of the seller's admission to him. If the seller made the statement to the purchaser in the presence of one additional witness, there are then two witnesses, the purchaser and that witness, and there is no migo according to anyone. This is clear. Yet according to this, it would appear that even where the purchaser did not independently claim knowledge of the challenger's ownership but merely repeats what he heard from the seller, Rabbenu Yonah's rule that the purchaser loses his purchase money may still be correct. Otherwise, how could it ever be thought that the purchaser's testimony is disqualified by interest? Perhaps he wishes to undo the purchase because he was overcharged or for some other reason, and therefore testifies that the field belongs to the challenger so that he can recover the purchase money from the seller. If Rabbenu Yonah's rule applies, however, he loses the purchase money and has no such interest. This consideration seems to support Rabbenu Yonah even in this case and requires study. Nevertheless, the Rosh and Tur, according to their own approach, clearly do not follow Rabbenu Yonah here, as explained. Consider this carefully.
