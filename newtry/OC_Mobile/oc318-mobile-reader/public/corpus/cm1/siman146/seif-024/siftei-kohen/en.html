If his words can be interpreted, etc. The Tur indeed writes that had the possessor said, 'I inherited it from my ancestors,' language which does not necessarily mean that it had always belonged to them, we would hear him and construe his words to mean that his ancestors purchased it from this claimant or from the claimant's ancestors. I find the Tur's source difficult. Tosafot's discussion follows their own position that when the possessor says the seller lived there one day in his presence and he himself then held it for three years, this is effective just as though witnesses testified to the seller's residence. On that view, when an heir says, 'It belonged to my ancestors,' his words necessarily include that his ancestors possessed it. Even if witnesses prove that it had belonged to the opposing party's ancestors, they have not necessarily contradicted one day of possession by his ancestors. Beit din can therefore advance for him that his ancestors purchased it. Tosafot's wording is exact: we advance claims for an heir and believe his statement that his ancestors lived there one day, by virtue of the migo that he could have claimed a direct purchase. But the Tur earlier rules that the possessor's own assertion that the seller lived there one day is ineffective unless witnesses establish it. On that approach, why should beit din reinterpret an heir's general words as an assertion that his ancestors purchased it? Had that been Tosafot's point, they could have asked simply that we construe his words as a purchase. Their actual question depended on treating his words as proof of one day of ancestral possession and then advancing the purchase for an heir. Moreover, why should the expression, 'I inherited it from my ancestors,' imply that they purchased it more than the expression, 'It belonged to my ancestors'? If anything, inheritance suggests that it had always been ancestral property. Tosafot contrasted the expression, 'It belonged to my ancestors,' not with, 'I inherited it,' but with an explicit claim that his ancestors lived there. Thus even according to Tosafot, the words, 'I inherited it from my ancestors,' may imply ancient ownership and may not permit beit din to advance a purchase. This requires study. And the possessor says, 'It belonged to my ancestors,' which implies, 'and not to your ancestors,' etc. The Tur writes that this formulation implies that it had always belonged to his ancestors, and therefore his words are not reinterpreted. This follows Tosafot. In my view, Tosafot's conclusion is not compelled. Even if he expressly said, 'It belonged to my ancestors from time immemorial,' that does not necessarily exclude the possibility that they later purchased it from the other party's ancestors. His assertion of uninterrupted ownership may be contradicted, but one day of possession need not be. Since he has alleged at least one day of ancestral possession, beit din should advance that his ancestors may have purchased it, as it advances claims for any heir. The Rashba's original question is therefore restored. The answer is that the case concerns witnesses who testified that it belonged to the opposing party's ancestors and that the present possessor's ancestors never occupied it even for one day, such as where the witnesses rented the house from the opposing ancestors and lived there under their authority until the possessor's ancestors died. One day of possession is then also disproved. This explains the Gemara's statement that Nehardea agree only where he says, 'It belonged to my ancestors, who purchased it from your ancestors.' According to Tosafot, one must explain this awkwardly as meaning merely that his ancestors lived there one day and that he therefore seeks to prevail because they may have purchased it. According to my explanation, the words are literal: he must claim that he knows they purchased it, because otherwise beit din will not advance the speculative purchase when witnesses establish that they never possessed it even one day.
Or he said, 'It belonged to my ancestors, who purchased it.' The Sema, seif katan 63, writes that where he claims his ancestors purchased it from the claimant's ancestors, the claimant cannot state with certainty that his ancestors never sold it. I remain uncertain about this. Ultimately the claimant asserts with certainty that the property belonged to his ancestors. The other party's assertion that it belonged to his own ancestors is therefore a chazakah without an accompanying claim, unless he proves that his ancestors occupied it for one day. Examine this.
