One brought witnesses that it had always belonged to his ancestors. The word 'always' does not appear in the Rambam. Rama added it because the Tur writes that if the challenger's witnesses testify that the property belonged to his ancestors continuously and that they never sold it, then in every case we remove it from the possessor. The possessor cannot claim that his own ancestors purchased it from the challenger's ancestors, because the witnesses testified that the latter never sold it. Rama therefore continues at the beginning of his gloss: 'But if the witnesses did not testify that they never sold it,' etc.

And what he writes, 'we listen to the possessor.' Meaning that we accept his explanation even if he did not himself initially clarify his words in that manner. Beit din advances the explanation for him and interprets his earlier statement accordingly.
And what he writes, 'But if he initially claimed that it had always belonged to his ancestors, he cannot return and make another claim.' This means not only that beit din does not advance for him the argument that his ancestors purchased it from the challenger's ancestors. Even if he himself later comes and explains his words that way, we do not listen to him, because that explanation contradicts his original statement that the property had always belonged to his ancestors. The Tur writes the same where the possessor initially says, 'It belonged to my ancestors and not to yours.' This is the same case. He then writes that if the possessor initially said merely, 'It belonged to my ancestors,' we do not advance the explanation for him unless he himself clarifies, meaning that when he explains, 'What I meant by saying that it belonged to my ancestors was that my ancestors purchased it from yours,' we listen to him. This, in my view, is necessarily Rama's intent, and it corresponds word for word to the Tur from seif 38 through seif 40. It is surprising, however, that Rama mixed the Tur's rulings into the Rambam's formulation copied by the Mechaber. The Rambam's words here cannot be understood as a case where the challenger's witnesses testify that it had belonged to the challenger's ancestors continuously and that they never sold it, because the Rambam then concludes: 'Or if he said, It belonged to my ancestors, who purchased it from your ancestors, this is a valid claim.' Such a claim is impossible if the witnesses already testified that the challenger's ancestors never sold it. Nor can we say that when Rama inserted the word 'always' he merely meant that the property had belonged successively to the challenger's grandfather, great grandfather, and earlier ancestors, rather than that it had never been sold. First, why then add the word at all? Second, Rama immediately begins his gloss, 'But if the witnesses did not testify that they never sold it,' which shows that the preceding case did involve testimony that they never sold it. We must therefore say that Rama knew that the Rambam's own rule concerns a case where the challenger's witnesses did not testify that his ancestors never sold it. But since the Mechaber did not identify the passage as a quotation from the Rambam and instead arranged the rulings consecutively, Rama chose to supplement the Mechaber by combining the Tur's additional rules with the Rambam's. Accordingly, the Mechaber's later words, 'the possessor then returned and said,' through, 'or he said, It belonged to my ancestors, who purchased it from your ancestors,' refer back to Rama's gloss in the case where the witnesses testified only that it belonged to the challenger's ancestors, but did not testify that they never sold it. Although it remains difficult why Rama chose to combine the formulations in such a confusing manner instead of writing near the end of the Mechaber's words: 'When does this apply? Where the challenger's witnesses did not say that it had always belonged to them. But if they said it had always belonged to them, we do not listen to the possessor who says that his ancestors purchased it from theirs,' perhaps Rama did so merely for greater brevity. Examine this carefully.
Or if he said, 'It belonged to my ancestors, who purchased it from your ancestors.' The Mechaber does not also say, 'or my ancestors purchased it from you,' because in such a case we do not listen to him unless he has proof that his ancestors lived there for at least one day. The challenger makes a definite claim that he never sold it to the possessor's ancestors. This differs from a claim that the possessor's ancestors purchased it from the challenger's ancestors, because the challenger cannot state with certainty that his ancestors never sold it. This is clear.
When he first makes his claim before beit din, etc. This means that the possessor's initial claim, namely that the property belonged to his ancestors, was made before beit din. In that case, if he later leaves beit din and returns with an explanation that his intent was that his ancestors had purchased it, we say that someone taught him to formulate a false claim. But if his original statement that it belonged to his ancestors was made outside beit din, and afterward he comes before beit din and says that his ancestors purchased it from the ancestors of the challenger, we listen to him. We do not say that while he was outside beit din someone taught him this explanation and instructed him to lie. Rather, we apply the principle that a person does not fully disclose his claims outside beit din. Therefore, in such a case he may return and make a new claim even if it contradicts his earlier words, and certainly if he merely supplies an explanation for them.
Even if he said it outside beit din, etc. Some disagree. The reason for their dispute is explained in the Tur. Rabbeinu Yonah writes that he cannot later retract and make a different claim, because one who says, "It belonged to my ancestors and not to yours," is considered as though he had said, "My ancestors purchased it from your ancestors." Thus he has effectively made an admission outside beit din, and a litigant's admission to his own detriment is like the testimony of one hundred witnesses even when made outside beit din. Although one who admits that he owes another person money may, if he did not say, "You are my witnesses," claim that he was merely joking, this case is different. Here he made the statement in order to advance his own claim and obtain the property. His own assertion therefore necessarily contains an admission against his interest.
