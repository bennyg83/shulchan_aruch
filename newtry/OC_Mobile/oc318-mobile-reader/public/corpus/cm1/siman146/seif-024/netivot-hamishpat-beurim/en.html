See Shach, seif katan 23, who asks that since the Tur rules that saying, "He lived there one day in my presence," does not help even through a migo, one is forced to interpret his words as meaning that the predecessor purchased it in his presence. If so, when a purchaser merely says, "I bought it," beit din should likewise plead for him that his seller purchased it in his presence. In my view, the Tur is discussing one who claimed, "It belonged to my ancestors," and also said that he inherited it from them, meaning, "It belonged to my ancestors and I inherited it from them." Once he says, "It belonged to my ancestors," we treat this as a definite claim, as though he said, "I know with certainty that it belonged to my ancestors." Similarly, if a purchaser says, "This field belonged to so and so and I bought it from him," that can be interpreted like the claim, "I bought it from so and so, who bought it from you," concerning which the Shach in seif katan 10 writes that beit din must interpret his words as meaning that he knows with certainty that his seller bought it from the challenger. What the Shach writes here, that we do not plead this for him, contradicts his own ruling there. The Rama's intent is therefore as follows. If the possessor first says, "It belonged to my ancestors, from whom I inherited it," we interpret him as saying that it belonged to his ancestors at the time he inherited it from them. But if the challenger first said, "It belonged to my ancestors," meaning that it always belonged to them, and the possessor then responded, "It belonged to my ancestors," in order to contradict him, we understand the possessor to mean likewise that it always belonged to his own ancestors, because his statement was made as a direct answer to the challenger. The Tur explicitly gives this reason. Tumim seeks proof that the phrase, "I inherited it from my ancestors," does not itself mean that it always belonged to them from the Mishnah that one who comes by inheritance need not make the predecessor's claim. He assumes the heir says, "I inherited it from my ancestors," even though the other party has witnesses of ancestral ownership, which would contradict an assertion that the property always belonged to the heir's ancestors. This proof is difficult, because that Mishnah necessarily concerns an heir who has evidence that his ancestors lived there for at least one day, as the Gemara on 41 states that although no claim is required, proof is required. With such evidence, the rule is effective, as stated in the gloss here. Tumim further writes, following Sema, seif katan 43, that where the challenger makes a definite claim Ramban requires witnesses that the predecessor lived there one day, but where the challenger is an heir and claims only uncertainty, witnesses are unnecessary and the possessor is believed to say that his predecessor lived there one day in his presence. Ramban expressly writes this. If so, the Shach's question is entirely resolved. This is true and clear. The Shach raised his question only according to his own view disputing the Sema in seif katan 25. The primary ruling is nevertheless like the Sema, as Ramban states explicitly. Practically, there is little difference because the Shach himself concludes in seif katan 12 that the possessor is believed when he says, "He lived there one day in my presence."

Ketzot HaChoshen questions the Shach's explanation that the case concerns witnesses who testified that the possessor's ancestors never lived there at all. The Shach elsewhere rules that one who merely says, "I bought it from so and so," may not later add, "He bought it in my presence," because that would be a retroactive migo. How, then, can he add here, "My ancestors bought it from your ancestors in my presence"? In my view the cases are different. There he initially said only, "I bought it from so and so," and adding, "in my presence," introduces a new claim rather than interpreting the first. Here he initially said, "It belonged to my ancestors." When he later adds, "in my presence," he merely explains that his original words were a definite claim, meaning that he knows with certainty that it belonged to his ancestors because he saw them purchase it. Had he originally said only, "I inherited it from my ancestors," without saying that it belonged to them, that would be like saying, "I bought it from so and so," and he could not later add, "in my presence," because that would indeed be a retroactive migo. The proposed explanation that because he has a migo and his first claim is contradicted by witnesses he may retract and restate the claim so as not to contradict them, on the theory that a migo is like witnesses and resembles two sets of witnesses that can reconcile their testimony, does not appear correct. No posek has stated that possession of a migo permits a litigant to retract a claim once it is contradicted by witnesses.
