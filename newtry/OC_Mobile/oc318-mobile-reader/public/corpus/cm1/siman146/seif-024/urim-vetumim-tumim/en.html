If the possessor's words can be interpreted, etc. The Shach in seif katan 23 asks that Tosafot write this according to their own view, that if a purchaser claims with certainty that his seller lived in the property one day, he is believed as though witnesses established that residence because the purchaser has a migo. The same should then apply to an heir. But the Tur above in seif 14, and likewise the Mechaber, rule that the purchaser's own assertion is insufficient despite his migo and that witnesses are required. The same should apply to an heir. Here, where there are no witnesses that the heir's ancestors lived in the property even one day, how can his migo help? In truth, the Shach did not need to discuss Tosafot at such length, because one could distinguish logically between a purchaser, who caused his own difficulty at the time of purchase and should have arranged witnesses that the seller lived there one day, so that if no witnesses existed he should not have bought the property, and an heir who receives the property automatically and could do nothing to create such proof. The stronger source is the Tur in paragraph 17 and the Mechaber in seif 10, who state that even an heir must bring proof that his predecessor lived there one day and is not believed merely on his own assertion despite having a migo. The Perishah and the Sema in seif katan 63, however, explain that we refuse to advance a claim for a purchaser or heir until there is proof that the predecessor lived there one day only where the challenger asserts with certainty, "I never sold it." In such a case uncertainty cannot displace certainty until the predecessor's connection to the land is established by evidence. That is the case above in seifim 10 and 14. But where the challenger himself is uncertain, because all he knows is that the land once belonged to his ancestors and from that alone he cannot say with certainty that the possessor's ancestors never lived there one day or purchased it, then the challenger too has only an uncertain claim. Since the present possessor has a migo, we may advance the derivative claim for the heir even without witnesses. The Shach in seif katan 25 hesitates even about this, arguing that the challenger ultimately claims with certainty that the land belonged to his ancestors, so the possessor should still need proof that his ancestors lived there one day. But the matter is compelled by Ramban's novellae on the passage where Rabbah says, "what reason would he have to lie?" Ramban writes: if their claims were stated generally, why should the heir not be believed? Every heir's claim is that the property belonged to his ancestors, while he does not know how it came into their hands; perhaps they acquired it from the ancestors of the present challenger. Ramban answers that the actual case was where the challenger claimed with certainty that the property had always belonged to his own ancestors, and the possessor likewise claimed with certainty that it had belonged to his ancestors and that the challenger's ancestors had never lived there even one day. Therefore, if the possessor came only with an uncertain claim, we would not advance anything for him. Ramban is known to hold that the possessor's own statement about the predecessor's one day of residence is not believed by migo and that witnesses are ordinarily required, as the Shach himself explains above. Yet Ramban nevertheless asks that where the challenger's claim is uncertain, we should advance the heir's ordinary claim even without witnesses of one day. His answer is specifically that the challenger asserts with certainty that the other ancestors never lived there even one day. Ramban thus explicitly supports the Sema's distinction, and I am surprised that the Shach overlooked it. According to this, the Tur's present case must be one where the challenger did not assert that the property had belonged to his ancestors forever. If he had, the heir's later interpretation would not help, because we do not advance an uncertain derivative claim against the challenger's certainty, as Ramban writes. The Tur should perhaps have made this explicit. There is another possible explanation based on what I wrote above in seif 14. The reason one is not believed by migo to establish that his seller lived there one day is that the alternative claim, "I bought it directly from you," is a migo involving brazenness, and in such an already weak migo we do not accept the more brazen alternative. Here, however, there is no such brazenness. The possessor could have claimed, "I bought it from your ancestors." That is not especially brazen. Were his ancestors somehow continuously bound to the challenger so that there was never any moment in which they could sell the land? Since the alternative is not brazen, the migo can be accepted. This too deserves consideration. In any event, as a matter of practical law, the Sema's distinction between a challenger asserting certainty and one asserting uncertainty is the more convincing, especially because it is explicit in Ramban. The Shach also objects to the Tur's statement that where the heir said, "I inherited it from my ancestors," this language does not necessarily mean that the land had belonged to those ancestors forever. His objection is not sufficient to reject the Tur. The Mishnah says, "one who comes by inheritance need not present a claim," which implies that he simply says, "I inherited it from my father." Yet surely the challenger has witnesses that the land belonged to his own ancestors. Otherwise why would he have any claim against the present possessor at all? Even if the possessor were not coming through inheritance, no explanation would be required because the challenger would not be his legal opponent. Thus the challenger necessarily has ancestral-title witnesses. If the heir's phrase "I inherited it from my father" meant that his father's ownership had existed forever, his claim would directly contradict the challenger's witnesses and would have to be reinterpreted as meaning that his father purchased it from the challenger's ancestors. But that would itself be a substantive claim, whereas the Mishnah says that one who comes by inheritance need not state such a claim. It follows that the phrase "I inherited it from my father" does not inherently mean that the father owned it forever. The Tur is therefore correct, and this is clear.
