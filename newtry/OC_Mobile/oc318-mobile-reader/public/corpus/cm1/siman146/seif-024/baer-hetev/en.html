Nothing. Meaning that his claim that the property belonged to his ancestors has already been disproved. His only possible right is the chazakah created by his own consumption of the produce for three years, yet he does not claim that he consumed the produce because he himself purchased the field from the challenger. It is therefore a chazakah without an accompanying claim. Shach.
To explain. The Sema explains that beit din does not formulate a claim for him and interpret his words this way when he himself did not explain them. The Shach disputes this at length. See there.
From your ancestors. The Sema explains why the rule does not also mention a claim that "my ancestors purchased it from you." In such a case we do not listen to him unless he can prove that his ancestors occupied the property for at least one day, since the challenger makes a definite claim that he never sold it to the possessor's ancestors. By contrast, where the possessor claims, "My ancestors purchased it from your ancestors," the challenger cannot definitely state that his own ancestors never sold it. The Shach questions this. Ultimately the challenger definitely claims that the property belonged to his ancestors. Therefore the possessor's bare claim that it belonged to his own ancestors would still be a chazakah without an accompanying claim unless he proves that his ancestors occupied it for one day.
Initially. This means the possessor's first claim that "it belonged to my ancestors" was made in beit din. If he later leaves beit din and returns with an explanation, we suspect that others taught him to make a false claim. If, however, his original statement that it belonged to his ancestors was made outside beit din and he later comes before beit din and claims that his ancestors purchased it from the challenger's ancestors, we hear him. A person does not necessarily disclose his claims except in beit din. It may therefore be that in such a case he can change his claim even if it contradicts the earlier statement, all the more so where he gives an explanation. Sema.
That disputes. By his own words he has effectively admitted that his ancestors did not purchase it. A litigant's admission is equivalent to one hundred witnesses even outside beit din. Although a person who admits owing another money may ordinarily say, absent the words "you are my witnesses," that he was merely jesting, this case differs. He introduced the statement in order to establish his own right, and within the very claim by which he sought to benefit himself he admitted a fact against his interest. The Tur writes this in the name of Rabbeinu Yonah. Sema.
