Rashba discusses a man who executed gifts of houses and vineyards to his brother, placed the deeds with a custodian to be delivered after death, later married and had children, and died. The brother then received the deeds. The widow and heirs argued that after making the deeds the donor had retaken the properties and possessed them for three years without protest. The brother answered that he had not known of the gift until now and therefore had no reason to protest. Rashba accepted this defense if the heirs admitted he had only now received the deed. If they denied it, he required him to prove his lack of knowledge, because one who says he did not know property was his, or did not know another was possessing it, normally must prove the special circumstance. Ramban's treatment of distant residence might seem to place the burden on the possessor instead. The distinction is that ordinary possession is known even at a distance because owners investigate their properties. Thus physical distance is only an excuse for failing to protest and the challenger must prove it. But where the challenger says he did not know the property was his at all, the defect goes to the very evidentiary force of possession. An owner who does not know of his ownership is not expected to investigate possession. In that circumstance the possessor may have to prove that the challenger knew enough for silence to count. Maharik cites Rabbi Avigdor that where the possessor does something unlawful which the owner could reasonably fail to recognize as unlawful, the owner may later object. If the situation is one in which people commonly make such a mistake, the possessor must clarify his possession. If people do not ordinarily make such a mistake, the challenger cannot defeat a settled possession merely by saying he did not know he could protest. Thus the burden depends on whether the alleged ignorance is objectively plausible.
