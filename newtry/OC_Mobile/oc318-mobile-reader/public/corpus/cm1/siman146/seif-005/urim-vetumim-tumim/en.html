A protest before two. The Shach in seif katan 4 writes that even if the two witnesses heard the protest separately, they combine, citing the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ra'ah, and he concludes that this is the primary view. He thereby rules against the responsum of the Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef, who holds that they do not combine. Perhaps the dispute depends on the reason why protest before one witness is ineffective. If the reason is that the possessor can deny it, then when two separate witnesses testify, that reason no longer applies, and the Nimukei Yosef appears to adopt this reasoning in context. But the Rashba's reason, cited by the Beit Yosef, is that when an owner protests before only one witness, the possessor regards the protest as mere mockery. If so, there is no difference between one witness and two heard separately. Since the Shulchan Aruch above cites the Ramban's reason rather than the Rashba's, the Ra'ah's ruling follows, and this appears to be why the Shach ruled like the Nimukei Yosef. Nevertheless, the matter requires study, because the Rashba explicitly disagrees, and in this law it is possible that even the Rosh agrees that one witness, when the statement was not made in the litigant's presence, is insufficient, as Maharit writes in a responsum.
And they should write it in language of agency, etc. See Sema, who explains the Tur's omission of this requirement by saying that the Tur follows Rabbenu Tam, who holds that witnesses may send their written testimony to beit din. I already wrote above in siman 39 that Rabbenu Tam's rule is unrelated to the present case. Even Rabbenu Tam said only that when the witnesses actually send their writing to beit din while they remain fit to testify, their writing can be treated as testimony, by the principle that whatever is fit for oral expression is effective. But here they write a protest merely for safekeeping, and in the meantime the witnesses may die or forget the testimony and protest. How could a ruling then be issued on the basis of their writing when they are no longer fit witnesses? Nor can it be treated as though the testimony had already been examined in beit din, because the writing never had the legal status of a deed, as the Sema himself writes. See what I wrote above in explanation of Tosafot's wording in the chapter Chazakat HaBatim. This reasoning remains firm. The Tur's omission appears instead to be because Tosafot and the Rosh do not accept the Nimukei Yosef's requirement. According to the Nimukei Yosef, absent the principle that one may act beneficially for a person without his presence and that the owner presumably desires the writing, the owner would have to say explicitly, "write," so as to appoint the witnesses as agents to testify. We treat him as though he said so only because the writing benefits him. The Rosh, however, writes that a protest is effective even without witnesses when made directly in the possessor's presence, and that the baraita's wording "protest before two" serves only to exclude the view requiring three. Earlier, concerning a kinyan before two, where the rule is that one need not say "write," the Rosh explains that even a private kinyan would be effective and the Mishnah mentions two only in order to teach that one need not say "write." If so, why not say the same regarding protest, namely that two were mentioned because of the rule that one need not say "write"? The answer must be that regarding protest there is no special novelty in the owner's instruction to write. In a loan, when the lender instructs the witnesses to write, it is the borrower whose liability is being recorded, and therefore permission to write is significant. Here, however, the owner himself is the one protesting. What additional legal force could his instruction to write provide? Rather, the Sages permitted the writing as an enactment to protect an oppressed party, as Tosafot explain. Thus there is no novelty in saying that explicit instruction is unnecessary. This is contrary to the Nimukei Yosef, and Tosafot likewise labor to give the reason of protecting the oppressed rather than the Nimukei Yosef's agency rationale. The Tur therefore did not accept the Nimukei Yosef's rule, but followed Tosafot and the Rosh. Even the Rama, in my view, agrees that after the fact a protest written in another formulation is valid, because the Sages authorized its writing to protect the oppressed. He merely advises that initially it should be written in the formulation that is more legally effective according to the Nimukei Yosef in the name of the Ran. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat responsum 79, appears to invalidate it even after the fact if it was not written in this agency formulation. I do not think that is correct, because Tosafot and the Rosh clearly indicate otherwise, the Tur and Mechaber omit the requirement entirely, and the Nimukei Yosef stands alone against the other authorities. Maharit also writes there that once three years have passed and the owner can no longer protest, the witnesses may no longer write down the protest that was made within the three years. His words are difficult. What reason is there for this? The authorization to write was given to protect the oppressed. What difference is there whether they write within the three years or afterward? I have found no support for this in any authority. One distinction is possible, however. If the witnesses delay writing until the owner and possessor have already come to litigation, then they should no longer write. The entire reason the Sages permitted such writing was to protect the owner lest the witnesses forget the protest, die, or go overseas. But once they are present at the litigation, remember the protest, and are standing before beit din, there is no need for the enactment. The ordinary law should apply and they should testify orally in beit din. This distinction is sound and accords with the law, but Maharit's broader statement remains difficult.
