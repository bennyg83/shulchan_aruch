And even if he admits the protest, because he could have said, etc. According to the reason given, namely migo, it follows that where there is no migo, such as where the possessor admitted before witnesses that the owner protested against him and at that time did not claim, "I purchased it," but only later came to beit din and claimed purchase, he should not be believed, because his migo would be retrospective. Yet the Ramban appears to have withdrawn from this reasoning. At first he explains the rule by migo, and says that even one witness does not create a Torah oath concerning land. He then writes that if their dispute concerns the produce, this migo cannot be used, but nevertheless reason inclines that the protest is not a valid protest. Thus, in the end he does not rely exclusively on migo, and the rule should apply even where no migo exists. The reason is as Rashbam writes in a responsum: the possessor answers, since you protested against me without proper witnesses, I regarded it as mere mockery and did not concern myself to preserve my deed. According to this reasoning, even without a migo the protest is ineffective. The Shulchan Aruch's explanation in terms of migo is therefore not exact, because according to this view the protest is ineffective even without migo. However, I did not initially understand the Ramban's underlying law. His words imply that if the owner claims, "you consumed the produce of my field for three years, pay me for it, for here is one witness that I protested," and the possessor denies the protest, he is obligated to swear a Torah oath. This is difficult. Regarding ownership of the land, the possessor is believed, because one witness is ineffective concerning land. Once we accept that the land belongs to him, how can he be required to pay its produce to the original owner? If so, every three years the owner could use that same single witness who testified to the protest, and thereafter protest with two witnesses, forcing the possessor forever to pay the produce. What kind of chazakah would this be if the possessor must continually pay the owner the fruits of the land? It therefore appears that the Ramban follows Tosafot's view concerning a case where there are witnesses that the possessor consumed produce for two years while he claims that he consumed for three. Tosafot explain that he is exempt from paying for the third year's produce because if we believe his statement, his three-year chazakah is established and the entire land is his, not because of migo, for migo is inapplicable where the land would otherwise return. According to this, if the owner protested before two witnesses and the possessor says that he consumed for three years, he must pay for the third year's produce. Although his statement is believed concerning what he consumed, he does not retain the land because the protest defeats the chazakah. Now, if the owner protested before one witness and the possessor says he consumed for three years, the owner is claiming, on the basis of that witness, payment for the third year's produce. The possessor does not acquire the land on the strength of his own statement in any event. Even if he denies the witness and says there was no protest, he still cannot acquire the land merely through his own assertion that he possessed three years, without witnesses to establish the chazakah. Thus the testimony of the single witness, whether admitted or denied, is relevant only to the produce. If the possessor admits that the protest occurred as the witness says, he must pay for the produce, because we say: you consumed it, but did not acquire a chazakah. If he denies the protest, he is exempt from payment by the following dilemma: if there truly was no protest, then his possession was valid and the produce was his. Accordingly, the dispute with the single witness affects only a monetary claim for produce, not the land. He must therefore swear a Torah oath to contradict the witness. If he admits the facts but claims that the protest was legally ineffective, then wherever one does not contradict a single witness the witness is treated like two for purposes of the oath, and he is one who is obligated to swear but cannot swear. The Ramban's words are therefore clear. Still, I am not fully satisfied because of the difficulty I raised in the previous siman. If the owner can impose a Torah oath by asserting with certainty that the possessor consumed for three years, then the owner's admission should be equivalent to one hundred witnesses, establishing that the possessor held the land for three years. If the owner then claims that the possession was unlawful, he has no migo arising from silence, because had he remained silent he could not have claimed the produce and imposed an oath. Thus neither ordinary migo nor the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted seems applicable when the owner affirmatively claims for the produce. In my view it therefore remains a matter requiring study whether one who admits that another possessed his land for three years, but claims rent or payment for the produce, is bound by his admission as by one hundred witnesses so that his claim for the produce fails, where he has no migo, or whether we say that his admission is not binding because its refutation is contained within it, since he says the possession was unlawful or that he protested. The Ramban's words indicate that it is not treated as a binding admission when the qualification accompanies it, but the precise reason remains unclear to me. May Hashem illuminate my understanding.
