According to this, even without a migo the protest would be ineffective. Ramban himself, cited by Tumim, concludes that even if their dispute concerns produce and no migo exists, it is not a valid protest. Tumim questions Ramban: how can the dispute concern produce in a manner that obligates the possessor to swear against one witness and thereby potentially trigger the rule that one who cannot swear must pay? How could the possessor prevail regarding the land yet repeatedly pay the fruits to the claimant? The answer is simple, as Tumim himself suggests. The case can be one where there are no witnesses establishing chazakah, but the claimant sues based on a single witness who says that the possessor consumed the produce for three years and that the claimant protested. If the possessor says there was no protest, he must swear against the witness that no protest occurred, and he is exempt from payment because of the either-or reasoning stated by Mechaber in siman 145, seif 3: if we believe the witness that he ate for three years, then he has established chazakah. If the possessor admits that the claimant protested but says that he nevertheless purchased the land, he has a migo to avoid payment of the produce because he could have denied the protest. If there is a single witness to the protest, he becomes one who cannot swear and therefore must pay. Tumim asks that if we impose a Torah oath, the claimant must be asserting with certainty that the possessor ate for three years, and a litigant's admission is like one hundred witnesses, so the possessor should have chazakah in the land. I do not understand the question. There is no admission for the possessor where the claimant says that the three years were consumed despite constant protest and in complete robbery. Such a claim is itself fully sufficient to defeat chazakah, just as a claim that he ate for only two years defeats it. Three years of consumption while the claimant continuously protests is no chazakah at all. Moreover, the claimant can sue based on the single witness even without a definite personal allegation. Ramban's words can also be explained differently. If their dispute regarding the land is that the claimant says he never sold it, and a witness also says that it was not sold or that the claimant protested, while the possessor has witnesses to three-year chazakah, then once the claimant's allegation and the witness's testimony are rejected as to the land, they are likewise rejected as to the fruits, which derive from the land. But if the claimant admits that he sold the land, or the possessor has an undated sale document, while the claimant says that before the sale the land had been rented to the possessor for three years and he now demands rent for those three earlier years, and the possessor says that from the outset it came to him as a sale and proves this by the owner's failure to protest during those three years, then the witness may testify like the claimant that it was originally held as a rental and that the possessor owes one hundred, or that the claimant protested during those years. Their dispute and the witness's testimony then concern only a rental debt, which is movable property. A Torah oath is required and the rule that one who cannot swear must pay applies. This is clear in my view.
