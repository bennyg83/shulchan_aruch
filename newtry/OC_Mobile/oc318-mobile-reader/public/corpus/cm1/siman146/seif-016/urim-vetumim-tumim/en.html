But if he did not make this claim himself, we do not make it for him. These are the Rambam's words. The Maggid Mishneh appears to understand that the Rambam derives this from the Gemara's wording, "I say that he told him, I bought the litigation," implying that only where the litigant himself says so may the explanation be accepted, but beit din does not make the claim for him. If that were the source, however, the same should apply in the following case where the challenger advised the possessor to purchase the field. There too the Gemara says that the challenger himself answered, "the second person is more convenient for me and the first is more difficult." One would then expect the Rambam and Rama to require that he actually make that claim. Yet no such requirement is stated, as the Sema observes in seif katan 33. It therefore appears that the Rambam derives his rule from the weakness of the present claim itself. The Gemara says that people sometimes purchase their own litigation, meaning they buy a disputed property merely to avoid legal trouble. Yet in the later discussion of one who had consumed for many years, the Gemara says that seven years suffice but six do not, because the challenger's deed is the strongest possible protest. Tosafot ask why the possessor should not be believed by migo that he bought the land from the challenger before the challenger's acquisition. The Tur later answers that this is not a valid migo because the possessor does not know whether the challenger holds a deed. If he were to say that he purchased from the challenger, the seller could sue him. But why can the possessor not say instead: I first purchased it from the seller and afterward purchased it again from the challenger, and I possessed for three years? This would protect him in either event. If the challenger produces a deed, the possessor claims a later purchase from him. If no deed is produced, the original seller cannot complain, because the possessor maintains that he also purchased from the original seller. Nor is it impossible for someone to purchase the same property twice. The Gemara itself says that a person may buy litigation simply for peace of mind. We must therefore say that purchasing the same property twice merely to settle competing claims is an unusually weak and uncommon explanation. Because it is not a normal occurrence, it does not generate a migo. We do not rely on a migo based on a highly uncommon claim. Once that is so, beit din likewise should not advance such a weak and unusual claim for a litigant who did not make it himself. The Rambam's ruling is therefore compelled. By contrast, in the case where the challenger advised the possessor to buy, and then explains, "the second person was more convenient for me than the first," the Gemara gives no indication that this is an inherently weak claim. Beit din may therefore advance it where appropriate. There is also a logical distinction. In the present case, if the possessor's actual intention had truly been to buy the litigation, he should naturally have said so when litigating. His failure to do so suggests that this was not his reason. In the advice case, however, even if that was the challenger's true motivation, he may be embarrassed to say openly in beit din that he deliberately manipulated another person into buying disputed property. As the Sema writes in seif katan 39, such conduct is improper and resembles placing a stumbling block before the blind. He may therefore conceal the embarrassing explanation unless prompted. This resolves the distinction.
