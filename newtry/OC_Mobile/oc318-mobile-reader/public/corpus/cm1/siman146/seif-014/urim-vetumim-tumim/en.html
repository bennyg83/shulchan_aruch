If this possessor brought witnesses that the person who sold it to him lived there even one day, etc. This is the Tur's view, that regarding the seller's one day of residence the purchaser cannot rely on migo and therefore requires witnesses. The Shach in seif katan 11 discusses this at length and writes that many disagree and that their view is more reasonable: the purchaser has a migo and is believed even without witnesses. I agree with him where the migo is that the field was never the challenger's. But where the challenger has witnesses that it was his, and the purchaser's migo is only that he could have claimed, "I bought it from you," it appears that even Tosafot agree he is not believed, because this is a migo involving brazenness. Although in many monetary matters we do accept a migo involving brazenness, here the migo is already weak, as the Baal HaMaor and Ramban explain at length. It is therefore enough to recognize such a migo where it does not also involve brazenness, but we should not recognize it where it does. This resolves Tosafot's wording. Rashbam writes that if one possessed for three years and claims, "I bought it from so-and-so, who had previously bought it from you," he is believed by migo that he could have said, "I bought it from you." Rashbam adds that the same applies if he has witnesses that his seller lived there one day. Tosafot write that Rashbam's words are correct where the challenger has witnesses that the land was his, but where there are no such witnesses, if the purchaser claims that he personally saw the seller buy it from the challenger, or that the seller lived there one day, he is believed by migo that it was never the challenger's even without having possessed for three years. The difficulty is obvious. Why do Tosafot say that Rashbam's words are correct when the challenger has witnesses? Even then, why should the purchaser need witnesses that his seller lived there one day? Why is he not believed by migo that he himself bought it from the challenger, just as he is believed when he says he personally saw the seller purchase it? Maharsha therefore writes that Rashbam's mention of witnesses is not exact and that the purchaser's own statement would suffice. That is very strained in Tosafot. The correct explanation is that when the purchaser says, "I personally saw my seller buy it from you," he can properly rely on the migo, "I myself bought it from you," because the level of brazenness is comparable in the two claims. But to say merely that the seller lived there one day, by migo that the purchaser himself bought it, is more brazen. Therefore he is not believed unless he has witnesses to the seller's one day of residence. Where the challenger has no witnesses at all that the land was his, however, the purchaser has the migo that he did not know it was ever the challenger's. There is no brazenness in that claim, and therefore he is believed regarding the seller's one day of residence even without witnesses. This is simple and clear in Tosafot, and should not be departed from, especially since the Baal HaMaor and Ramban in any event deny the migo in the stronger case. This also explains why the Tur rules that regarding one day of the seller's residence the purchaser's migo is ineffective, apparently against Tosafot, which is unusual for the Tur, particularly where the Rosh does not expressly disagree. According to what I have written, Tosafot themselves do not recognize a migo that the purchaser bought directly from the challenger for this purpose. The only migo they recognize is that the land was never the challenger's. The Rosh rejects that migo because the purchaser fears that witnesses may easily be found establishing the challenger's ownership, and therefore prefers a claim less vulnerable to direct contradiction. Once that migo is removed, Tosafot provide no basis for believing the purchaser without witnesses to the seller's one day of residence. The Tur therefore rules that witnesses are required. The Bach writes that a claim that the field was never the challenger's is itself brazen. I do not understand this, because the purchaser can say simply, "I do not know that it was yours." What brazenness is there in that? According to this distinction, namely that a migo of "it was never yours" may suffice while a migo of "I bought it from you" does not, even after three years of possession, one may ask from the Gemara in Ketubot. The Gemara asks why the Mishnah does not teach a case where one says, "this field was yours and I purchased it from you," and answers that the latter clause involving witnesses cannot be established, because if he has a chazakah he should prevail, and if not, the result is obvious. Why not teach instead a case where he says, "this field was yours and I purchased it from so-and-so who lived there one day"? When there are no witnesses of prior ownership, he would be believed by the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted, but where the challenger has witnesses, even if the purchaser has three years of possession, this weaker migo should not suffice. This requires study.
For he has a claim together with his chazakah, etc. The phrase "for he has three years of chazakah" clearly indicates that the purchaser is believed specifically where he has a three-year chazakah and therefore a migo that he himself bought the land from the challenger. Where he lacks that migo because he did not possess for three years, and has only the migo that the land was never the challenger's, he is not believed. This also appears from the Rif. The Shach wishes to explain that the case is one where the challenger has witnesses of ancestral ownership, but this is strained. The entire passage addresses a case where the purchaser himself admits that the field belonged to the challenger's ancestors. Witnesses are nowhere mentioned. The Rambam expressly writes, "since he admitted that it was his," and then continues, "or he said that in my presence you sold it to him," showing that the case does not depend on outside witnesses. The Rif is even clearer. The reason appears as follows. Where the migo depends on the purchaser, who claims that he personally saw the seller purchase the field and would otherwise have the migo to deny the challenger's ownership, the Rosh's objection is compelling. This is a weak migo. The purchaser trusts his seller's statement that the seller bought from this challenger, and therefore does not wish to claim that the field was never the challenger's, because he suspects that the fact of the challenger's prior ownership is genuine and may easily be proved by witnesses. But whether the purchaser personally witnessed the seller's purchase is not something likely to be disproved by outside evidence. He may therefore falsely say, or mistakenly convince himself, that he personally witnessed the purchase. One cannot answer that this too can be disproved by witnesses who testify that the challenger never left their sight, as the Pilpula Charifta suggests. The purchaser is relying on the seller's assertion that the seller truly bought the field. If witnesses appear, the purchaser assumes they will confirm the sale, not disprove it. By contrast, the migo that the purchaser himself bought the property from the challenger is not weakened by the same fear. Even if witnesses later testify that the challenger was constantly with them, they would necessarily also know whether he sold the field to the seller. In any event, if the purchaser is contradicted on his direct-purchase claim, the field may still be established as belonging to the seller, from whom the purchaser has a deed, as Rashbam explains. The migo therefore remains meaningful. The more difficult case is where the migo belongs, conceptually, to the seller rather than to the purchaser. The purchaser says only that his seller told him he had bought from the challenger, and the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership except the purchaser's admission. One can argue that had the seller himself been before us, he would have been believed by migo to deny that the field was ever the challenger's, and the Rosh's concern does not apply to the seller in the same way. If the seller is lying about having purchased the field, then the same witnesses who could disprove the challenger's title could also disprove the alleged sale. Why should one false claim be safer than the other? Thus, if the purchaser comes through the seller and merely repeats the seller's own account, perhaps we should advance for the purchaser whatever claim the seller himself could have made, since we generally advance claims for a purchaser. It is very strained to say, as the Pilpula Charifta does regarding the Rosh, that because the purchaser himself could not have successfully made this claim, we do not advance it for him on the seller's behalf. The entire point of advancing claims for a purchaser is to disregard the purchaser personally and ask what his predecessor could have claimed. The purchaser comes through the seller, and the rule that the first could convey only what he had should work in the purchaser's favor as well. For this reason Ri had to explain that the purchaser independently admits that he himself knows the field belonged to the challenger. Then the case is one of certainty against uncertainty. The challenger has definite prior title, while the purchaser's derivative claim is uncertain. One might object that when the purchaser says, "I personally saw the seller buy it from you," his claim too is definite. But we do not believe that statement independently; it is believed only by virtue of a migo, and here the migo fails for the reasons stated. Thus the purchaser remains in the category of uncertain claim against definite title. This appears to me to resolve the Rif and Rashbam according to Ri's approach. According to the Rambam, one need not rely on the definite-versus-uncertain principle. One can instead use Tosafot's explanation that where the purchaser himself is effectively one witness to the challenger's ownership, the seller's theoretical migo is ineffective because the purchaser would not accept the seller's denial. Even where the purchaser says he personally saw the sale, the seller has no migo to deny the challenger's ownership, because the purchaser would then see that his seller is lying and would not stand by him. When the seller claims the purchase occurred in the purchaser's presence, however, the purchaser is already inclined to believe his seller and may be mistaken in his certainty. But if the seller now denied the challenger's title entirely, the purchaser would recognize the contradiction and turn against him. There is also an aspect of migo involving two persons, since seller and purchaser would have to coordinate their positions. Tosafot's reasoning is thus sound. According to Ri, however, who grounds the rule in definite versus uncertain, the explanation above is necessary: because the purchaser is not independently believed that he personally saw the sale, his position remains uncertain. This is somewhat strained, especially since the Rosh's words in the name of Rashbam appear to say the opposite. The Rosh writes that if the purchaser had claimed, "I bought it from you," or "in my presence my seller bought it from you," he would be believed by the principle that the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted, since the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership. This necessarily refers to a case without three years of possession, because with such possession the purchaser would be believed even where the challenger had witnesses. Rashbam's own language at the end of the Gemara also indicates that where the purchaser knows whether the seller bought the field, he is believed. Rashbam writes that in the case at hand, even if witnesses came establishing that the seller lived there one day, it would not help if they did not know whether he had purchased it, because there was no three-year possession. The implication is that if the purchaser himself knows that the seller purchased it, he may be believed by migo that the field was never the challenger's, giving a definite claim against a definite claim, as the Shach argues. It is possible, however, that the Gemara text should read "they do not know" rather than "he does not know," referring to the witnesses. That would remove the Shach's inference. The Tur therefore requires explanation. The Derishah discusses the matter at great length without reaching a clear resolution. It appears that the Tur understood the Rosh's initial presentation of Rashbam only according to the earlier assumption that Ri's entire rule depends on definite versus uncertain. On that assumption, where the purchaser has a definite claim that he personally witnessed the sale, the migo would indeed suffice. But after the Rosh explains Rashbam's position by saying that the migo that the field was never the challenger's is weak because the purchaser fears contrary witnesses, the same weakness applies even to the purchaser's claim that he personally witnessed the sale, unless he has the stronger migo based on three years of chazakah that he himself bought from the challenger. Thus, in the Rosh's final view, even Rashbam requires the stronger form of migo. The Tur therefore rules accordingly. The Shach further argues at length that if there are witnesses that the seller lived there one day, then even where the purchaser lacks three years of chazakah and does not claim that he personally saw the seller buy it, he should nevertheless be believed, because we advance for a purchaser whatever the seller could have claimed. He cites Ramban's proof from one who deposited property with another before witnesses and died, where we advance for the heirs whatever their father could have claimed by migo of return. The Shach distinguishes that there it is certain the object came to the heirs from their father, whereas here who says that the purchaser acquired the field from the particular person who lived there one day? If the purchaser has proof that he acquired it from that person, the Shach agrees with Ramban's law. I do not understand this distinction. The purchaser has a migo that the field was never the challenger's. When he instead says, "yes, it was yours, but I bought it from so-and-so, and that person lived there one day," why should he not be believed by the same migo? Rashbam's wording seems to support the Shach, because Rashbam infers from the purchaser's failure to say, "and I possessed it for three years," that the purchaser had a deed from the seller. Rashbam does not say that the purchaser would simply have been believed by migo that the field was never the challenger's without needing a deed. This indicates that Rashbam too does not recognize the migo in that form. Still, Rashbam may follow either the Rosh, that the migo "it was never yours" is weak because the prior ownership can be proved, or the view that a migo involving brazenness is not accepted. The case may be one where the challenger knows that the purchaser himself knows the field was his, so denying prior ownership would be brazen. But the Shach rejects the Rosh's approach and generally accepts a migo involving brazenness. It is therefore difficult to understand his distinction between proof of the transaction and a definite derivative claim. In truth, I already discussed above in siman 72 seif 1 that this general rule is disputed by the authorities. Even those who there recognize the derivative claim may have spoken only about movables, which are fully in the current holder's possession. Land remains in the chazakah of its original owner, and the purchaser's present possession, especially before three years, is not complete. His migo therefore resembles a migo used to extract. One should not simply equate the law of movables and land here. Since major authorities disagree, this remains a doubt of law, and the land should remain with the prior owner. This appears clear.
But if he merely said, "I purchased it from so-and-so," etc. The Shach in seif katan 15 holds that initially the possessor must have said, "I bought it from so-and-so, who bought it from you." Only then may he later explain his words and say that by "who bought it from you" he meant that the purchase occurred in his presence. But if he initially said only, "I bought it from so-and-so," how can he later add this new assertion where he has no migo? The Shach writes correctly. However, the Shach supports himself from Tosafot and appears to understand that Tosafot agree with this rule. That is not so. Tosafot disagree here with Rashbam and the Rosh. In the underlying case, Rashbam's text does not say that the seller told the purchaser that he had bought it from the challenger. Rashbam's version is simply, "I bought it from so-and-so, who had bought it from you," and Rashbam explains: meaning, the seller told me that he had bought it from you. Thus the Gemara itself does not say that the seller made that statement. Rashbam therefore writes that if the purchaser later explains, "he bought it from you in my presence," he is believed by migo. Rashbam thus agrees with the Rosh that one may explain one's earlier words. Tosafot, by contrast, although they cite Rashbam's formulation, write: "but now that he said, I bought it from so-and-so who bought it from you, and thereby admitted that it had been yours, he cannot later claim, he bought it from you in my presence," because that would be a retrospective migo. Thus even with this formulation Tosafot do not permit him to repair his claim afterward. Their disagreement with Rashbam is precisely whether such a later explanation is treated as merely clarifying the first statement or as a retrospective migo. Tosafot say the same explicitly in the case of the man who lived in the upper room. The Gemara says that had he claimed from the outset, "in my presence he bought it from you," he would have been believed. Tosafot explain that this does not mean he remains able to make that claim later by migo. Rather, only had he made that claim initially would he have been believed. This is expressly contrary to the Rosh, who holds that where the original wording was, "I bought it from so-and-so who bought it from you," and not, "he told me that he bought it from you," the purchaser may still clarify that he meant, "he bought it in my presence." This also explains the dispute in Ketubot concerning the old woman who said, "I seized them during his lifetime." Tosafot write that she is not believed by migo that she could have said she seized them during his lifetime, because that would be a retrospective migo. The Rosh, however, establishes the case where there are witnesses who saw the property and witnesses to the deposit, thereby removing the migo altogether. Why did the Rosh need this additional factual assumption? Because he follows his own view that a person may clarify an earlier statement. She could explain that when she said, "I seized them during his lifetime," she meant, "he demanded them from me during his lifetime and I did not return them." Tosafot, who do not allow such reinterpretation, can rely simply on the retrospective-migo problem. This is clear, and I wrote similarly above in siman 64. It therefore follows that the Shach's ruling itself is certainly correct. Since Tosafot in any event reject the Rosh's broader rule allowing later clarification, once we rule like the Rosh we should at least limit the principle to a case where the later words genuinely explain and follow from the earlier ones. We should not permit a claimant to add a wholly new fact to an earlier statement that was entirely silent about it. Consider this carefully.
