That the person who sold it to him lived there even for one day, etc. The Tur writes that if he brings no witnesses, then even if he himself claims with certainty that the seller lived there for one day in his presence, this does not constitute chazakah. I found the same in Baal HaMaor, and Sefer HaMilchamot there also appears to imply it. Tosafot on 30a and the Mordechai there, however, state that the same rule applies if he claims, "He lived there one day in my presence," and that this does constitute chazakah. Hagahot Ashri states this in the name of the Rashba. Tosafot explicitly treats the claim "He lived there one day in my presence" like testimony of witnesses. The Agudah, Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en, and Semag, positive commandment 95, beginning of page 184, likewise support this. This appears correct to me. Baal HaMaor's reason, that if the seller had lived there one day witnesses would have known of it, is difficult, for the witnesses may have forgotten, died, gone overseas, or simply never seen it. Sefer HaMilchamot itself answers similarly. Sefer HaMilchamot also explains that in such a case beit din does not advance a claim on his behalf through a migo, because we plead that the first possessor purchased it only when there are witnesses that he lived there. Ramban writes at length similarly in his Chiddushim concerning the incident involving Rabbi Chiya, as does Nimukei Yosef, and the Bach writes along these lines. In my humble opinion this is not primary. Since the present possessor makes a definite claim, why should he not be believed through a migo? The authorities I cited are therefore primary, that even in such a case he is believed. Moreover, if one claims concerning a utensil, "I bought it from so and so," would he not be believed merely because it is not otherwise known that the utensil had been in that person's possession? Certainly he is believed, as I wrote above in siman 72, seif katan 97, and in simanim 133 and 134. We therefore say that he is believed that the utensil was in that person's possession through a migo that he could have said, "I bought it from you," or, "I returned it to you." We then plead on behalf of that prior possessor that he purchased it from the challenger, because he himself would have had a migo that he returned it. The same applies here. The current possessor is believed that the seller lived there one day through a migo that he could have said, "I purchased it from you," since he himself held the property for the years required for chazakah. We then plead that the seller purchased it, because this possessor held it through the seller's right for three years. It further appears to me that even if the present possessor did not hold it for the years required for chazakah, if he claims, "I purchased it from so and so, and I know that he lived there one day," it belongs to him where the challenger's prior ownership is known only through the possessor's own admission. He is believed both that he bought it from that person and that the seller lived there one day through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." We then plead for that seller that he purchased it from the challenger, since the seller himself could have said, "It was never yours." Just as, if the possessor brought witnesses that the seller lived there one day, we would plead this for the seller, as I explain below following Ramban and those who agree with him, so too when the possessor himself claims with certainty that the seller lived there one day. Otherwise a comparable chazakah involving movable property would likewise never help. Examine carefully. Hagahot Ashri in the chapter Chezkas HaBatim writes in the name of the Rashba that even if the possessor did not hold it for three years, if the challenger has no witnesses that it was his, the possessor is believed to say, "He purchased it from you in my presence," or, "He lived there one day," through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." The Or Zarua is cited there, and Mahari Chalat writes at greater length. Piskei Tosafot likewise states that where the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership, the possessor is believed to say, "He purchased it from you in my presence," or, "He lived there one day," through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en states this in the name of Ri: their position is correct where the challenger has witnesses that the land had belonged to him, but in the case under discussion, where he had no witnesses, even if the possessor did not hold it for three years, when he says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," or, "He lived there one day in my presence," he is believed through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." This is primary. See Responsa Mabit, volume 1, siman 111.
He lived there even for one day, etc. If the present possessor afterward claims that he purchased it from that person, it constitutes chazakah even if he does not say that the seller purchased it from the challenger, but merely says that he believed the seller had purchased it from him, provided he brings witnesses that the seller lived there one day and the present possessor then held it for three years. The same applies if he claims that he knows with certainty in some other manner that the seller purchased it from the challenger. Baal HaItur, page 86, writes this, and it is also evident from Rashbam on 41b. See Responsa Maharit, siman 111.
For he has a claim together with his chazakah, etc., for he has the years of chazakah, etc. In my humble opinion, this requirement is needed only where the challenger brings witnesses that the property had belonged to him. If the challenger brings no such witnesses, then even if the possessor did not hold the property for the years required for chazakah, he is believed when he says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours," or that he himself purchased it from the challenger. There is no stronger migo than this. The Bach explains that because there is one uncertainty whether the present possessor bought it from the seller and another uncertainty whether that seller bought it from the challenger, this is not a migo. That is incorrect. Since the present possessor states all the facts with certainty, this is a strong migo. Consider movable property that is normally lent or rented, or a case where witnesses establish that an item was deposited with someone. If the present holder says, "I purchased it from so and so, who purchased it from you in my presence," would he not be believed, where there are no witnesses who saw the item currently in his possession, through a migo that he could say, "I purchased it from you"? Likewise, where witnesses now see the item but there are no witnesses that it had been deposited with the seller, he is believed through such a migo even if the challenger's prior ownership is established. This is explicit in Bava Batra 45 in the discussion of an artisan: because the holder could have said, "I purchased it from you," he is believed when he says, "I purchased it from the artisan, and he purchased it from you." All the authorities, as well as the Tur and Mechaber above in siman 134, rule this way. Our case is identical. It is clear that the Bach assumed that Rashbam and the Rosh explicitly held otherwise and therefore forced this explanation. Had he seen that Rashbam never said this and that the Rosh actually wrote the opposite, as I explain below, he would not have written so. Nor can I accept the Beit Yosef's explanation of Rambam as applying even where the challenger has no witnesses of prior ownership, for then Rambam's rule would have no rationale. I also do not understand the Tur's attribution of this view to Rashbam. In my humble opinion, Rashbam agrees with what I have written. Rashbam himself asks why the possessor should not be believed through a migo and answers that he does not know that the seller purchased it except on the seller's own statement. This explicitly implies that if he knows it with certainty, he is believed through a migo. Rashbam concludes that in the case under discussion, even if witnesses testify that the seller possessed it for one day, it does not help because the present possessor did not hold it for three years. He does not conclude that even if the possessor says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," it would not help, although Rashbam had mentioned that formulation immediately beforehand. This indicates that in such a case Rashbam agrees he is believed. Rashbam's repeated emphasis that the case under discussion is different likewise shows that if the possessor actually knows that the seller bought it, such as where he says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," it helps even without three years. Rashbam's requirement of three years is where the challenger's ownership is established and teaches a further point: if the possessor merely says that the seller told him he had purchased it from the challenger, even three years of possession do not help because the fact of purchase remains uncertain to him. This is also evident from Rashbam's opening words, where he explains the claim "I bought it from so and so, who bought it from you" as meaning that the seller told the possessor at the time of sale that he had bought it from the challenger. Rashbam had to explain it this way so that one should not understand the case as one where the possessor says that the purchase from the challenger occurred in his presence, for in that case he would be believed. The Gemara's incident itself involved no three year possession, as Rashbam states repeatedly, yet if the possessor had claimed that he knew with certainty that the seller purchased it from the challenger, he would have been believed through a migo that the property had never belonged to the challenger. Tosafot also appears to have understood Rashbam this way. Tosafot disagrees only with Rashbam's ruling that witnesses showing one day of possession by the seller do not help when the challenger lacks proof of prior ownership, and Tosafot also disagrees with Rashbam's reading of the sugya. The Rosh explicitly writes: if the possessor had claimed, "I purchased it from you," or, "The person from whom I purchased it bought it from you in my presence," he would be believed because the mouth that prohibited is the mouth that permitted, since the challenger has no witnesses that it belonged to him. The Rosh then cites Rashbam, which implies that he understands Rashbam in the same way. Even if the Rosh's citation of Rashbam does not refer back to his opening words, the Tur is still difficult for presenting Rashbam as the final opposing view against the Rosh. The Rosh's concluding statement that this is not a good migo with respect to the person from whom the possessor bought applies only where the purchaser himself is uncertain, meaning where he says merely that the seller told him that he had purchased it from the challenger. The Bach's claim that the Rosh also denies a migo where the possessor says, "He purchased it from you in my presence," is therefore incorrect. We cannot say that the Rosh refers to a case where the possessor already held it for three years, because he is discussing the Gemara's case, where there was no three year possession. Moreover, if there had been three year possession, there would have been no need for the Rosh's reason that the challenger lacked witnesses of prior ownership, since even if he had such witnesses the possessor would be believed to say, "I purchased it from you," by virtue of the chazakah. Ramban's Chiddushim likewise show that he understood Rashbam as I have explained. Even the Rif, whose wording differs more substantially from Rashbam's, is explained by Ramban as agreeing that a possessor who knows the purchase with certainty is believed. This also appears to be the correct reading of the Rif. The Rif says that a doubt cannot remove a certainty, implying that if the possessor is certain he is believed through a migo. It would be strained to interpret the Rif's "doubt" as meaning that beit din is uncertain whether the seller told him he bought it. Every migo involves beit din being uncertain while the claimant asserts certainty. Rather, the Rif's requirement of three years refers to a case where the challenger's prior ownership is known, as Ramban explains. The Rif's statement that there are no witnesses that the property had belonged to the challenger refers to the claim, "I bought it from so and so, who told me that he bought it from you," not to a case where the present possessor knows the earlier purchase with certainty. Therefore, where prior ownership is known only through the possessor's admission, and the possessor says, "I purchased it from so and so, who purchased it from you in my presence," or says that he knows with certainty in some other manner that the seller purchased it from the challenger, he is believed even without three years of possession through a migo that he could have said, "It was never yours." This is explicit in the Rosh, and Baal HaItur in the entry Mecha'ah, page 84, rules likewise, although he understood the Rif as Beit Yosef did and therefore argued at length against the Rif. In truth, the Rif's view is as Ramban explains. Even if the Rif meant as Beit Yosef understood him, we would not rule that way. Tosafot, Hagahot Ashri, Hagahot Maimoniyot, and Semag, positive commandment 95, page 183, likewise support this, and this is primary. However, where the possessor does not claim that the seller purchased it from the challenger in his presence, but only brings witnesses that the seller lived there one day, and the present possessor himself did not hold it for three years, Rashbam clearly holds that the land must be returned even if the challenger brings no witnesses that it was his, because the possessor himself admits the challenger's prior ownership. The Mordechai and Agudah write that Ri was uncertain about this. Ramban also states that this is Rashbam's position and that the Rif appears to agree. The reason is that in such a case beit din would have to plead on behalf of the possessor that perhaps the seller purchased it, and we do not advance such a claim for this purchaser. Ramban explains Rashbam this way. The Maggid Mishneh also writes that the commentators dispute this case, some requiring the years of chazakah, meaning Rashbam's view, and that the Rashba agreed; Rambam's language likewise appears so. His proof is not from Rambam's phrase that the possessor has a claim together with his chazakah, because that phrase must refer to a case where prior ownership is known. Rather, it is from Rambam's opening ruling that because the possessor admitted that it had belonged to the challenger and did not buy it from him, the land returns, without distinguishing whether the possessor brings witnesses that the seller lived there one day. Tosafot and Hagahot Ashri, however, write that the Rashba held that if the possessor brings witnesses that the seller lived there one day, then even without three years he is believed and beit din does plead for the purchaser. This is a different Rashba from the one mentioned by the Maggid Mishneh. Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en states this in the name of Ri, whose language I cited above. It appears that Hagahot Maimoniyot understood Ri in Tosafot as disputing only the proof for the Rashba's law, not the law itself. The Mordechai and Agudah understood Ri's rejection of the proof as leaving the law in doubt. Maharsha's statement that Ri disputes the Rashba's actual ruling is therefore incorrect. Baal HaItur, entry Mecha'ah, page 87, rules like the Rashba, and Ramban in his Chiddushim likewise holds this view. Ramban compares it to one who deposited an item with another in the presence of witnesses, the depositary died, and the depositor claims it from the heirs who now possess it. Even if there were deposit witnesses but no current sighting of the item, would we not plead for the heirs whatever their father could have claimed, namely that he returned it or purchased it, since he would have been believed through a migo that he returned it? This remains so even though the heirs admit that the item originally came as a deposit. Nimukei Yosef cites this. Some later great authorities nevertheless express doubt even in the case of the depositary's heirs. In my humble opinion, the law concerning the heirs of a depositary is correct, as I write below in siman 297, but the analogy to our case requires study. There, the depositor admits that the item came to the heirs from their father, so we plead that perhaps their father purchased it. Here, by contrast, it is possible that the present possessor never purchased the land from that person at all. Why should beit din plead for him when we do not know with certainty that he is a purchaser from that seller? It is true that where he has held the land for three years he need not prove that he is an heir or purchaser, as the Tur writes in this siman in the name of Ramban and the Rosh, and as the Maggid Mishneh agrees in chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en. But there the three years themselves justify pleading that the seller purchased it even without a migo, because the present purchaser held it for three years through the seller's right and the original owner would presumably not have allowed such possession had the seller never purchased it. Thus the claim of purchase is independently substantial. Here, however, the seller himself would have been believed to say that he purchased it only through a migo, and therefore we do not advance that claim for the present purchaser so long as he does not establish that he actually bought from the seller, given that he admits the land had belonged to the challenger. According to this reasoning, if he proves that he purchased from that person, or the challenger admits that he did, everyone would agree that we plead that the seller purchased it from the challenger through the migo that the seller could have said, "It was never yours." Examine carefully. Nevertheless, the primary ruling appears to follow Ramban and those who agree with him. This is comparable to a person who claims, "This utensil of mine is in your possession," and the holder answers, "I purchased it from so and so, who told me he had purchased it from you." Suppose the challenger brings witnesses that he had once deposited it with that seller, but there are no witnesses who now see it in the holder's possession. Is not the holder believed that he purchased it from the seller, and do we not then plead that the seller himself purchased it from the challenger through a migo that he could have said, "I returned it"? We require the purchaser to return an item where he says an artisan told him that he had purchased it from the owner only when there are current sighting witnesses, as the Sema writes in siman 134, seif katan 12, and as the authorities imply. Without such witnesses the holder is believed through a migo that the artisan sold it to him, and we plead for the artisan that he purchased it through the migo that he could have said he returned it. This is so even though an artisan who received the item without witnesses is treated like one who received it with witnesses, and even for property normally lent or rented. Otherwise no one could safely purchase an object from another, for someone could later bring witnesses that it had once belonged to him and that he had once deposited it with the seller. Rather, even then we plead that the seller acquired it through the migo that he could have said he returned it. I reached this conclusion above in siman 72, seif 18, seif katan 97. The same applies here, and there is no valid distinction based on the present holder's admission that the item had once come to the seller as a deposit, for testimony establishing that deposit would be no less certain. The primary ruling is therefore like Ramban and those who agree with him. This appears correct to me. Examine very carefully.
But if afterward, etc. We do not listen to him, even if he now makes this claim before beit din. Since he already admitted that the land had belonged to the challenger, he can no longer be believed through a migo that he could have avoided that admission. A retroactive migo is not accepted. He had already admitted prior ownership because he thought he would prevail with his original claim that the seller told him he purchased it from the challenger. Once he sees that this argument will not prevail, he cannot change his claim to say that the purchase from the challenger occurred in his presence, because he no longer has the migo. Tosafot and Hagahot Maimoniyot state this.
But if at first he said simply, "I purchased it from so and so," etc. This ruling is astonishing to me. The Rosh said only that where the possessor initially stated, "I purchased it from so and so, who purchased it from you," he may later explain that what he meant was that the seller purchased it from the challenger in his presence. He can say that his initial admission that the land had belonged to the challenger was made within that very formulation, and therefore his later clarification is not a retroactive migo. But if at first he merely said, "I purchased it from so and so," then he cannot now return and claim that the seller purchased it from the challenger in his presence. He now has no migo, while his initial statement admitted the challenger's prior ownership. This would be the clearest possible case of a retroactive migo. He cannot say that when he first said simply, "I purchased it from so and so," he intended that this person had purchased it from the challenger, because the whole legal question turns on precisely that fact. Why did he not say it then? Once he initially admitted without qualification that the land had belonged to the challenger and did not state that the seller had purchased it from him, he can now be believed only through a migo that he could have avoided the original admission, which is a retroactive migo. Tosafot in Chezkas HaBatim, Hagahot Maimoniyot at the end of chapter 14 of Hilkhot To'en, and the other authorities say this. The same is explicit from Tosafot in Ketubot 85a concerning the woman who seized a bundle of documents and said she had seized them during the debtor's lifetime. Ri explains that she is not believed to say that she had demanded them during his lifetime through a migo that she could have said, "They are purchased in my hand," because she can no longer make that claim after saying that she seized them during his lifetime. The fact that at the time she first said she had seized them she could have claimed purchase does not help now, because at that time she thought her original claim was sufficient and did not know that she needed to say she had demanded them from him during his lifetime. We do not accept a retroactive migo. Other authorities explain that incident differently, but only because the phrase "I seized them during his lifetime" can itself imply that she had demanded them from him then. Here, by contrast, saying merely "I purchased it from so and so" does not at all imply that this person purchased it from the challenger. There is therefore plainly no migo, since it would be retroactive. See Responsa Maharit, siman 7, and what I wrote above at the end of siman 55. It appears certain to me that the Tur contains a scribal omission of two words and should read: "But if at first he said, I purchased it from so and so who purchased it from you, without further specification, and he afterward adds..." This is also the reading implied by the Remezim composed by the Tur, unlike the Beit Yosef and Sema, who clearly read the Tur as the Mechaber did. Alternatively, one could say that the Tur speaks of a case where he never initially admitted that the land had belonged to the challenger, but merely said, "I purchased it from so and so." Yet that interpretation is difficult. If he never admitted prior ownership, it is obvious that we listen to his later clarification. Indeed, even without adding anything, beit din would already have ruled that he need not return the land, because he never admitted that it had belonged to the challenger. The Beit Yosef and Sema likewise do not understand the Tur this way, for they call his ruling a greater novelty than the Rosh's ruling, whereas on this interpretation it would be obvious and the Rosh's case would be the novelty, since there the possessor initially admitted prior ownership by saying that the seller had purchased it from the challenger. The Sema also writes in seif katan 35 that the clarification is accepted only before the claimant leaves beit din. If no prior ownership had ever been admitted, it should remain accepted even after leaving beit din, because even without any clarification he would have prevailed. We must therefore be dealing with a case in which the possessor admitted prior ownership, such as by saying, "It was yours, but I purchased it from so and so." The original difficulty then returns, because his later claim is supported only by a retroactive migo. I therefore conclude that the Tur certainly contains the scribal omission I described, unlike the Beit Yosef and Sema. Examine carefully.
