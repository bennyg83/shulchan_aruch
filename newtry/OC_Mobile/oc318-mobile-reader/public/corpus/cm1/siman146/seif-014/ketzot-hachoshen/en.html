Tosafot records Rashba's view that if the purchaser proves the seller lived in the land for one day, the court can leave the land with the purchaser even though the purchaser personally knows the land had once belonged to the challenger. The reason is that the seller himself, had he been litigating, could have said, "It was never yours," and through that migo been believed to say he purchased it. We therefore advance the seller's valid defense for the purchaser. Rabbeinu Yitzchak disagrees. He says the purchaser cannot rely on a plea he personally knows to be false. This parallels the dispute concerning a husband who says he has sons and is believed through a migo that he could have said he divorced his wife. Rashbam limits the effect for a kohen, because the divorce migo would itself prohibit her to a kohen. Tosafot and Ramban say that once the husband's statement is accepted to remove yibbum, the resulting status is accepted fully even for a kohen. Rashba's purchaser rule follows that broader approach: once the seller's claim is accepted as sufficient, its consequences apply fully to the purchaser. Rabbeinu Yitzchak follows the narrower approach that where the particular recipient knows the alternative basis is false, the migo cannot benefit him.
The Shach objects to the Tur and Rosh for allowing a purchaser who initially said only, "I bought it from so and so," later to add that he personally witnessed the seller buy it from the challenger. He calls this a classic retroactive migo. There is strong proof for the Tur and Rosh from the Gemara concerning one who said, "It belonged to my ancestors," and later added, "My ancestors bought it from your ancestors." The Nehardeans agree that this later addition can be accepted. According to the Tur and Rosh, an heir or purchaser ordinarily does not receive a claim unless there is at least some proof that the predecessor possessed the property. Thus the later statement must amount to adding personal knowledge of the predecessor's acquisition. The Gemara nevertheless accepts the addition. The key is that the later statement is not accepted because of a retroactive migo. It is itself a substantive ownership plea attached to three years of possession. One who says, "I saw my predecessor buy from you," presents a complete claim accompanying his possession, just as one who says, "I bought directly from you." The Gemara sometimes uses the word migo loosely to mean that just as one complete possessory claim would be believed, so another complete possessory claim is believed. It is not always the technical inference, "Why would he lie?" Rashbam's language supports this. He says that "It belonged to my ancestors because they bought from yours" is a complete claim and the possession is a possession with a claim. The same applies to "I bought from so and so and saw him buy from you." Thus the Tur and Rosh are not relying on a retroactive migo at all. Tosafot appears to treat the "I saw the sale" plea as dependent on technical migo, and therefore is more restrictive. The Rosh and Tur follow the alternative conceptual structure reflected in Rashbam.
