The seller lived there for one day. See Shach, seif katan 11, through his question that if with movable property one claims, "I bought this utensil," he is not required to prove that it was ever in the seller's possession. I find this surprising. The entire chazakah of movable property rests on the presumption that the item reached the present holder through a valid acquisition. We do not suspect theft or finding a lost object, except with things ordinarily lent or rented, and even there the concern is specifically a loan claim. Thus, by definition the present holder must have acquired it from some seller, and that is equivalent to knowing that the seller lived on the land for one day. Even with objects ordinarily lent or rented, if the claimant says that he lent the item to the seller, the seller's possession is still comparable to living on land for one day. Otherwise, even where it is known that the item was in the seller's hands, we could never advance a purchase claim for the purchaser. Since the claimant himself admits that he did not lend it to the present holder, but only claims that he lent it to the seller, it is as though the seller's one day possession is established. Land is different. A house may remain empty and there is no present physical possession demonstrating title. Land chazakah derives from the owner's failure to protest. Ramban therefore correctly requires actual witnesses that the seller lived there one day, and a mere migo does not allow us to advance such a claim for the purchaser. Ramban himself uses movable property only to prove that the present holder need not prove that he is in fact a purchaser or heir. That proof is clear from objects ordinarily lent out, for although they could have reached him by loan, he may nevertheless say, "I bought them from so and so," with a migo, allowing us to advance the seller's claim for him. But it does not prove that witnesses are unnecessary to establish one day of possession in land. It appears that even Ramban would agree that if the purchaser says, "In my presence the seller lived there for three years," this is equivalent to saying, "In my presence he bought it," for he claims to have personally observed a complete chazakah and thereby knows that it was in the seller's hands as owned property.
He has a claim together with his chazakah. See Shach, seif katan 13, who questions the opinion that where the seller has no three year chazakah, even though the seller himself would have been believed to claim purchase through a migo that the land was never the claimant's, we do not advance that claim for the purchaser. Tosafot in Bava Batra 30 explain that the seller's migo is not a migo for the purchaser, because if the seller said, "It was never yours," the purchaser would not thereby be entitled to retain the land. Shach asks from the rule that we advance for an heir or purchaser a claim of purchase regarding movable property not seen by witnesses, even for objects ordinarily lent out, based on the predecessor's migo of return, although the heir or purchaser knows that the item was not returned because it is presently in his own hands. At first glance this is a major difficulty. In my view it can be resolved as follows. Logically, we should not advance for a purchaser or heir a claim that the seller or predecessor himself could establish only through a migo. When the seller makes the claim, the migo is evidence that he speaks truth, because had he wished to lie he could have chosen a better claim. But where the seller is absent and no one is making the claim, what evidentiary force remains? Maharit indeed rules that we do not advance for an heir or purchaser a claim that their predecessor could have maintained only through a migo. The reason for the other authorities appears to be the purchaser's ability to say, "If you remain silent, well and good; if not, I will return the deed to my seller." As appears below in siman 148, and as Mishneh LaMelech learns from there, whenever the claimant could not recover from the seller, he likewise cannot recover from the purchaser, who can say, "What loss have I caused you?" Anything that can be returned is treated as though already returned, and the purchaser receives whatever rights the seller possessed. This raises the Gemara's question, "Do you not admit that the land belonged to him?" Why can the purchaser not say, "If you remain silent, I will remain; otherwise I will return the deed to my seller, from whom you could not recover because he would be believed through a migo that the land was never yours"? If the seller had already litigated and been discharged, the claimant certainly could not recover from him, as stated in seif 15, and Rashba in Yevamot writes that one who is already in possession is treated as though judgment had been entered in his favor. The answer is that Yevamot 37 connects the dispute of Admon and the Sages concerning one who lost his path among another's fields with the principle of finality of judgment. Terumat HaDeshen writes that this principle rests on despair. Therefore, the "if you remain silent" argument applies only where the matter is not likely to become clarified, so that the claimant can be viewed as having despaired. Where clarification is likely, there is no despair. Here the seller is believed only because he could say, "It was never yours." If witnesses later arrive proving that it was the claimant's, the seller loses. The claimant therefore does not despair, because he expects that witnesses may yet be found. Accordingly, where the seller has only a migo that can later be defeated by witnesses, we do not treat the purchaser as though the matter has already been finally decided. By contrast, with movable property not seen by witnesses, if the purchaser returned the item privately to the seller and the seller then displayed it before beit din and claimed purchase, he would be believed forever through a migo that he could have kept it hidden and claimed return. The claimant could never remove it. The purchaser may therefore say, "If you remain silent, well and good; otherwise I will return it to the seller," and we treat it as already returned. The same applies to heirs. Since their father could have maintained a purchase claim that would remain effective, we treat the matter as though he had already prevailed, and the heirs need not return the item.
I purchased it without qualification. See Shach, seif katan 15, who objects that when the possessor later says, "In my presence the seller bought it from you," this is a retroactive migo. Ketzot HaChoshen disagrees and writes that "in my presence he bought it from you" is itself a complete claim without need of any migo, and that where Bava Batra 41 uses language of migo it means only that just as one claim is believed, so too the other is believed. Besides being strained, this appears incorrect. In Bava Batra 29, concerning tenants' testimony, the Gemara asks that they are interested witnesses. Yet seif 30 establishes that one who says, "I descended for the produce," is believed. Why then do we not advance for the tenants the possibility that the landlord had purchased the produce rights? That alone might not suffice because a purchaser's chazakah can support future possession, whereas a claim to produce rights is effective only for produce already consumed. But the tenants should at least be believed through a migo that they could have said, "In our presence the landlord acquired the produce rights for more than three years and then rented them to us," a claim under which they would be believed through a migo of "we ourselves descended for the produce." If Ketzot were correct that a claim of "in our presence he purchased" is independently strong and needs no evidentiary migo, then the tenants should be believed by saying that in their presence the landlord purchased produce rights, since that claim would not expose them either to the claimant or to the landlord. The fact that they are not so believed proves that a claim based on what the seller did in one's presence works only by force of an actual migo. The reason is that since the present possessor does not come by his own title, his personal chazakah is insufficient unless he has the evidentiary support of a migo.
