The Shach in seif katan 26 writes that even the Rif agrees in law that in such a case the migo can be effective, and that the Rif explains the Gemara in terms of a trust deed only because it is unusual in practice to be able to validate a forged document. In principle, however, what difference is there between a trust deed and a forged deed? I do not understand his words. The Rif writes explicitly that the Rabbis explain the case as a trust deed, but where one forged a document by imitating a signature, even Rabbah agrees that it is mere pottery and we do not apply "what reason would he have to lie?" If the Shach were correct, why mention Rabbah and why discuss migo at all? If the document is actually forged and cannot be validated, then obviously it proves nothing. The entire institution of document validation exists precisely to determine whether a signature is genuine. The Shach's reading is therefore difficult to understand. There is, however, a difficulty in the Rif's own explanation where the claimant says that the document was originally a trust deed. Why is such a document treated as mere pottery? According to the claimant's own story, the transaction was ultimately completed through this very deed. True, initially it was written in trust without an actual decision to transfer, but once the sale later took place, why should the deed remain invalid? At most it is an antedated deed, written in Nisan while the actual sale occurred later in Tishrei. With a loan, one can understand the distinction. The claimant admits that no loan was ever made on this particular document, but says that another loan was made on a different instrument. The later loan has no relation to this trust deed, which remained a trust deed from beginning to end. With land, however, the deed identifies the same field that the claimant later says he actually purchased. Once the sale occurred, it seems that the deed should become operative, albeit with an earlier date than the real transfer. The Rif himself holds that an antedated deed can be valid against the debtor personally and is invalid only for collection from encumbered property. Moreover, where there is no warranty one may write multiple deeds for the same field. We are not believing the claimant that some different deed existed. If he says that the very field identified in this document was later sold to him, why is the document treated as mere pottery rather than simply an antedated deed? One must answer as I wrote above in siman 43: in deeds of sale and gift, an antedated document is invalid according to all even against the immediate party, because the incorrect date itself can cause direct harm to creditors or later purchasers. For example, one writes in Nisan that the field was sold, but in fact no sale occurred then; in Iyar the owner sells it to someone else; later, in Tishrei, he actually transfers it to the holder of the Nisan document. The holder could use the false Nisan date to dispossess the purchaser from Iyar, claiming that his acquisition preceded the latter's. Since the document itself creates a risk of wrongful extraction, it is invalid in its entirety, just as an antedated loan document is invalid against purchasers. Therefore it is indeed treated as mere pottery. Consider this carefully.
