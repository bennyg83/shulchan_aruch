But when the first possessor sold it, he need not protest against the purchaser, etc. These words are from a responsum of the Rashba, and the Mechaber copied them as settled law without citing any disagreement. The matter requires great study. The Rashba proves his ruling from the case of the man who lived in an upper room for four years, etc., arguing that otherwise the owner should have said: since you did not protest explicitly, it proves that you sold the property to the person who sold it to me. He also proves it from the statement that this rule applies where one consumed seven years, but not six. To understand his proof, especially from the latter case, where the challenger sold the field while our case concerns the possessor selling it, one must recognize that his reasoning is based on the view cited by Rashbam, which is also the view of the Baal HaMaor, that the reason an owner must renew his protest at the end of every three years is lest the possessor later claim, "after the first protest you sold it to me." Rashbam rejects this explanation because once the possessor initially entered without a purchase, he should not later be believed to say he purchased it. Yet the Nimukei Yosef cites the Rashba as holding that he is believed to say he purchased it afterward, distinguishing land from movables in a case of witnesses and prior sight. It is therefore clear that the Rashba follows the alternate explanation. Accordingly, if the owner protested, and the possessor then sold the field to another who remained in possession for a long time, the purchaser does not know whether his seller sold lawfully. The original owner need not protest again. He can say: why should I protest against you? I trusted you not to lie to my face and claim that you purchased it from me. I relied on your integrity, and therefore had no need to protest. This explains the Rashba's first proof. In the case of the man who lived in the upper room for four years and claimed to have purchased it from someone else, the Gemara rules that if the purchaser cannot establish that his seller lived there even one day, his chazakah fails. Yet the owner's claim appears weakened: if the field was truly his, why did he not protest during all four years of this person's possession? The answer is that the owner can say: I knew nothing of your seller, who never occupied my house in a way that required a protest. As for you, I did not wish to protest because I trusted you not to lie and claim that you bought directly from me. The Rashba's second proof concerns the rule that if the challenger sold the field and the possessor had consumed only six years, there is no chazakah, while seven years suffice. Why should six not suffice, when three years passed without a renewed protest? It must be because the reason for renewing protest every three years is indeed the alternate explanation: otherwise the possessor could claim a later purchase. But where the new purchaser is involved, the possessor cannot claim that he purchased from that purchaser if he did not know of the purchaser's deed, as Tosafot explicitly write and as the Shulchan Aruch states below in seif 21, which speaks specifically where the purchaser's deed was unknown. See also the Baal HaMaor, who expressly cites the case of six years versus seven as proof for Rashbam's alternate explanation. The Rashba's words are therefore internally clear and correct. Maharit, Choshen Mishpat responsum 26, was troubled by the Rashba's proof, but according to the Rashba's own position it is very sound. Nevertheless, all of this depends on the alternate explanation. Rashbam, Ramban, the Rosh, and the Nimukei Yosef reject it. They answer the question from the six-year case by saying that where the owner sold by deed, no renewed protest is ever required. The reason one ordinarily must protest every three years follows Rashbam: otherwise the owner is deemed to have retracted or abandoned his first protest, whereas after a public sale by deed that rationale no longer applies. This is also the rule adopted by the Shulchan Aruch several times in this siman. Tosafot themselves ask from the rule requiring renewed protest every three years against the statement that six years are insufficient, and leave the question unresolved. This shows that they do not accept the alternate explanation, for according to it there is no difficulty at all: Tosafot themselves write that the possessor cannot claim a purchase from the purchaser when he did not know of the purchaser's deed. Thus, the Rashba's present ruling lacks foundation according to the halachic approach adopted by the other authorities and by the Shulchan Aruch. It requires great study why the Mechaber cites it without qualification and without dissent. The Sema in seif katan 17 writes that the rule applies specifically where the owner is confident that the purchaser will not lie and claim that he bought directly from him. Clearly, the Sema means this only as prudent advice to the owner. If he does not trust the purchaser, he should protest lest the purchaser later boldly claim a direct purchase. But if he failed to protest and the purchaser in fact does not claim that he bought directly from the owner, even the Sema agrees that the owner has not lost his right. The purchaser admits that he did not buy from him, while the owner already protested against the first possessor. Maharit understood the Sema as saying that failure to protest itself causes the owner to lose his right, and therefore challenges him. There is no difficulty. The Sema is merely giving sound practical advice, and this is obvious.
