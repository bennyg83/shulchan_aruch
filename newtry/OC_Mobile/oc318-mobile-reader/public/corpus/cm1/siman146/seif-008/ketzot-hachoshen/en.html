Only when the first possessor remains in it. Beit Yosef cites Responsa Rashba concerning two brothers, one of whom sold land to Yehudah claiming it was his because his father had given it to him. His brother protested. The possessor later sold it, and the purchaser then held it for the years of chazakah. Rashba ruled that the purchaser did not acquire a chazakah. The requirement to protest again at the end of every three years was stated only where the original possessor remains there. If the first possessor sells to another, the claimant need not protest against the purchaser, because the purchaser comes only through the first possessor, against whom the claimant already protested. The claimant may say, 'I did not concern myself to protest against you, because you do not claim that I sold it to you, but only that you derive your right from the first possessor, against whom I already protested.' Rashba brings proofs from the cases in Bava Batra involving one who lived in an upper room for three years and from the rule that a person sometimes calls many years 'years of chazakah.' Maharit, Choshen Mishpat siman 20, argues at length against Rashba. He asks why this should differ from any purchaser who proves that his seller lived there one day and then himself possesses it for the years of chazakah. One day should not be stronger than three years in the face of an earlier protest. Yet after the purchaser himself possessed it peacefully for three years without protest, why should the claimant be able to say that he did not bother protesting because the purchaser came only through the first possessor, whose own chazakah was deficient? Maharit explains Rashba's proof from the upper-room case differently, saying that a purchaser is not treated as a purchaser at all until he proves that his seller lived there even one day. He ultimately attempts to preserve Rashba's ruling by limiting it to the precise facts, where the brother had protested in the purchaser's presence and the purchaser nevertheless proceeded, thereby causing his own loss. Maharit admits, however, that this reading is not reflected in Rashba's wording, while Beit Yosef codified the rule without that limitation.
In my opinion Beit Yosef's ruling is correct, and Maharit's reconstruction of Rashba's words does not stand. His principal question, that one day cannot be stronger than three years after a protest, is mistaken. One day is stronger in the relevant respect. The reason one must renew a protest after every three years, according to Baal HaMaor, is to prevent the possessor from saying that he later purchased the property after the first protest. Baal HaMaor holds that even one who originally entered without permission can later establish a chazakah, and Rashba agrees. So too, one who originally entered with permission, such as through a mortgage, may establish a chazakah after the mortgage period ends. Nimukei Yosef cites Rashba's question from the son of an artisan, who cannot establish chazakah merely by relying on his father's claim, even though the artisan himself can eventually establish chazakah after his period of artisan possession ends. The resolution is that we advance a claim for an heir or purchaser only when the seller or decedent lived in the property for one day. That one day supplies some indication that the seller or decedent actually purchased it. Rashbam explains in chapter Chazakat HaBatim that the proof of one day's residence is required 'to substantiate your words.' This is also why the Gemara says that merely being seen on the land is not enough, because a person may inspect land without buying it. Without some indication of acquisition, we do not advance a claim for an heir or purchaser. Maharit understood the one day as proof merely that the claimant before us is indeed a purchaser, but that is not so. Its function is to create an evidentiary indication that his seller acquired the property.
This explains why the son of an artisan has no chazakah when he comes through his father's claim. His father's one day of residence carries no indication of purchase, because the father entered as an artisan. The son's own three-year possession does not help without an evidentiary basis for advancing the father's claim. Even if the father's artisan period had ended a year before his death, that isolated day or year still does not supply the required indication, because one who originally entered with permission establishes ownership only through a full three-year chazakah based on the owner's failure to protest. Thus, when the claimant protested against the original possessor, we can no longer advance for the purchaser a claim that the seller bought it after the protest. If he bought it after the protest, he was then one who had entered without permission, and no mere one-day residence supplies an indication of purchase. Only a new three-year chazakah by that seller could do so. The purchaser's later three years cannot substitute for the missing evidentiary basis in the seller.
The same conclusion follows according to Rashbam, Ramban, and Rosh, who explain the need to renew protest after three years on the ground that the possessor may treat the earlier protest as idle talk once he himself has a definite claim of purchase and a clear three-year chazakah. A purchaser's or heir's three-year possession is not itself sufficient without the additional indication of one peaceful day of possession by the seller or decedent. Once the claimant protested against the first possessor, the first possessor's residence is not peaceful and supplies no such indication. We therefore cannot treat the earlier protest as idle merely because the purchaser later possessed for three years. Only where there was no protest against the original possessor, so that his one day of residence was peaceful and supplied an indication of purchase, do we advance the seller's claim for the purchaser. All this is clear, and therefore the essential ruling follows Shulchan Aruch rather than Maharit.
He need not protest against the purchaser. Sema writes that this exemption applies only when the claimant trusts the purchaser not to lie by claiming that he himself bought the property from the claimant. Otherwise it is advisable to protest so that the purchaser cannot later make such a claim. The intent is not that the purchaser's chazakah becomes effective whenever the claimant does not trust him. Rather, if the purchaser does not claim an independent purchase from the claimant and relies only upon his seller's right, the property is certainly removed from him because the claimant already protested against the first possessor. Sema merely gives prudent advice. Maharit, siman 26, misunderstood Sema as limiting the legal rule to a purchaser who admitted that he derived title only from the first possessor, and therefore challenged him from one who purchases through a non-Jew. But Sema agrees that when the purchaser comes only through the first possessor, he loses. He says only that if the claimant does not trust the purchaser, it is wise to protest lest the purchaser later invent an independent claim of purchase. The same practical advice applies to one who purchases through a non-Jew.
