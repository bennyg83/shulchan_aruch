And he has witnesses that the seller lived there one day. The Sema writes that such witnesses are unnecessary because the challenger himself admits that the seller lived there, since the challenger claims to have purchased from that seller; the Gemara merely happened to describe the case as having witnesses. The Shach in seif katan 18 writes that the Gemara does not in fact say that this was merely the historical fact of the case. The Sema's intention, however, can be understood as follows. The challenger comes to the present possessor and asks, "what are you doing in this field?" He plainly cannot initially be relying on his purchase deed. If he had already disclosed that deed to the possessor, the Tur and Shulchan Aruch in seif 21 rule that the possessor would not need seven years, because he would have a migo to say, "I bought it from you." We must therefore ask: on what basis did the challenger come against the possessor at all? Why did the possessor have to answer him rather than say, "who are you that I should respond to you, and what concern is this field of yours?" The case must be that the challenger also had ancestral title. In truth, the possessor's seller had purchased the field from the challenger's ancestors, while the challenger later acquired it back from that seller. The challenger therefore first comes with witnesses of ancestral title. When the possessor responds that he purchased from the intermediate seller, the challenger then produces his deed showing that he later bought it from that seller. If so, once the challenger first arrives with ancestral-title witnesses and the possessor answers that he acquired from the intermediate seller, the possessor necessarily needs proof that the intermediate seller lived there at least one day. Otherwise the possessor's own chazakah cannot help. He is not believed by a migo that he bought directly from the challenger to establish the seller's one day of residence, as the Tur, Baal HaMaor, and Ramban hold, and as discussed above in seif 14 and in the Shach. If, however, the challenger himself admits that the intermediate seller lived there one day, then the challenger has a migo that he could have denied the seller's residence. By admitting it and saying only that he later purchased from that seller, the challenger should be believed without needing a deed. This is not a migo to extract in the objectionable sense, because the challenger also has ancestral title, as explained above. Why then does the law require specifically a deed? This demonstrates that the Gemara's case indeed involved independent proof of the seller's one-day residence rather than relying simply on the challenger's admission. In this way the Sema's underlying concern can be understood, though the precise historical wording of the Gemara is as the Shach notes.
But if he consumed it for fewer than seven years, etc. See Shach, seif katan 19, who explains that although a deed creates publicity and the possessor certainly knows that the property was sold by deed, the public report does not necessarily identify the particular purchaser. People know that someone bought it, but not who. The Sema's explanation above in siman 144, seif katan 4, is more convincing. Even though a deed creates publicity, it does not mean with certainty that this particular possessor actually heard the report. Rather, the owner did what the law required and protested in the legally effective manner. The Sages did not require more of him. It then became the possessor's responsibility to investigate, and if he failed to do so he caused his own loss. But for purposes of migo, we cannot simply assume with certainty that he knew all the details of the deed. He may genuinely not know them, and therefore his failure to make a particular alternative claim does not create a migo against him. This is simple and correct. According to the Shach, there is difficulty with Tosafot on 31a, who answer Riva's question as the Tur later explains. Even if the possessor does not know the purchaser's identity, why should he not be believed when he says that he truly consumed for seven years? Otherwise his claim would still not protect him against the original seller. The seller's deed itself serves as a protest, and according to the Shach the possessor did hear the general protest. Unless he knows that he had already possessed for three years before that protest, the original seller could still remove him. One cannot answer that the original seller would have needed to renew his protest every three years, because Tosafot on 39b expressly write that the sugya there does not accept the requirement of renewed protest every three years. We must therefore understand the matter like the Sema: the possessor may have heard no report at all, not even a vague one. Thus if the original seller later comes, the possessor can rely on the final three years because he never heard any effective protest. This fits Tosafot and should be preferred.
