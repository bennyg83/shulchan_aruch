And whoever prevails shall descend into it. Tumim writes that this applies specifically when the witnesses contradict one another concerning identified years, because that is a matter capable of later clarification. Other witnesses may yet come concerning the earlier or later three years. But if one set says the land always belonged to one party's ancestors and the other set says the same for the other party, so that the contradiction can never be resolved because even a thousand witnesses would not outweigh two, the law of a purchaser applies. I do not understand how such a case can truly be considered incapable of clarification. Witnesses may still come, or a sale document may be produced, showing that one party's ancestors purchased it from the other's ancestors. Such evidence would not contradict either set's testimony about earlier ownership and could resolve the title.
