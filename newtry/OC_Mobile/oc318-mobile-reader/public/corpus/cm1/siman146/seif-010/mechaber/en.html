One who derives his right through inheritance and claims, 'I inherited it from my ancestor,' need make no further claim. {Rama: A purchaser and a creditor who collected land have the same law as an heir, and the seller or borrower is not believed to say that the land did not belong to him.} He must, however, bring witnesses that the ancestor lived in or used it for even one day. Once he himself consumed its produce for three years by virtue of his ancestor's right, he is maintained in possession. The same applies if the ancestor held it for three years, even though the heir never lived there. If he brings no proof that his ancestor ever lived there, the field and all its produce are returned to the protesting claimant who has witnesses that it belongs to him. Proof that the ancestor was merely seen on the property is insufficient, because he may have come only to inspect it and never acquired it. If it was a field and witnesses testify that the ancestor plowed it, he is presumed to have acquired it. {Rama: If a woman possessed her husband's land after his death, her son then possessed it for three years, and the husband's heirs claim it belonged to the husband, while the son says it fell to his mother through her ketubah, the son is believed through a migo that he could have claimed, 'I purchased it from you,' because he consumed its produce for the years establishing possession. If he did not consume it for those years and therefore has no migo, his ancestor's possession for three years has no effect, because a wife does not establish presumptive ownership of her husband's property. This applies specifically to an heir and similar persons. If someone purchased the land from the woman and she sold it publicly, the land remains under the purchaser's presumption. Had the land not belonged to the woman, the heirs would not have allowed her to sell it publicly as though it were hers. The same applies in comparable cases. See Responsa of the Rosh, rule 99, responsum 4, and note 107.}
