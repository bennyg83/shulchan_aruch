He does not need another claim. The Shach, seif katan 7, writes that he does not need proof that he is an heir or purchaser. Once he himself possessed the land for three years and says he is an heir or purchaser, he is believed. The Tur says this in the name of Ramban and the Rosh, and Maggid Mishneh likewise. From Responsa Rashba cited by the Beit Yosef in this siman, letter 14, however, it appears that this is specifically where the present claimant himself held the land for three years. If the person who sold or bequeathed it to him had held it for three years, while he now comes only through that predecessor and cannot prove by witnesses that he is in fact the purchaser or heir, we do not simply believe him. We leave the land in the original owner's chazakah and say that if the predecessor were present he might admit that he was placed there only for the produce or under some similar arrangement. Some later writers cited by Knesset HaGedolah say Rashba disagrees entirely with Ramban and the Rosh and follows Ramah and Raavad. This is not correct. Those authorities require proof even when the current heir himself held the land for three years, as the Tur and Ramban's novellae make clear. Rashba agrees with Ramban in that case. His limitation concerns only where the predecessor alone had the three years and the present claimant cannot prove his status as heir or purchaser. We have no clear evidence that Ramban and the Rosh disagree with Rashba on that narrower case. We should therefore not multiply disputes unnecessarily, especially where Rashba provides a middle position. This is the practical law.
The law of a purchaser and of a creditor who already collected land is like that of an heir. The Rama carefully writes "a creditor who collected land," meaning that the creditor has already descended into and possesses that land. If he has not yet collected it and is not in possession, but merely seeks to collect from land previously possessed by the debtor, whose title is uncertain, we do not make for the creditor the claim that the debtor had purchased it. We make claims for an heir or purchaser in order to preserve possession. But a creditor who is now trying to extract does not receive such claims, because we rule that a creditor acquires only from the time of collection onward. This reconciles two responsa of the Rosh. At the end of rule 98, the Rosh says a creditor and an heir have the same law because there the creditor was already in possession of the property. In rule 99, siman 7, the creditor had not yet possessed the property and sought to collect from land merely held by his debtor. There the Rosh rules explicitly that we do not make claims for him, because he is extracting and a creditor collects only from now onward. Maharam of Brisk writes similarly. Maharshal, responsum 1, seems to say that even where the creditor has not taken possession we still make claims for him like a purchaser. This requires study, because the Rosh states the opposite. Perhaps Maharshal wrote only by way of additional argument: even if we assumed such claims could be made, they would not help in his case. He may not have intended to decide the underlying rule. The Rama's precise wording is primary.
Or the borrower. The Rosh's responsum concerns a seller and holds that the seller is not believed to say, "the land was never mine." The Rosh likewise writes regarding the rule of six versus seven years that even if the seller says, "it was stolen property in my hand," he does not have credibility to destroy the purchaser's established right. The Rama compares a borrower to a seller. Maharshal, however, in the responsum just cited, writes that a borrower can say, "This field is not mine and I never acquired it," even though this harms his own creditor. He does not regard the principle that everything in a person's possession is presumed to be his as strong enough here, because chazakah of land requires a supporting claim. At first sight Maharshal has a proof from the Gemara's case where Reuven robbed land from Shimon and sold it to Levi. Shimon cannot testify for Levi against Yehudah, because he has an interest in having the land return to Levi so that he can later recover it. The Gemara asks: once Shimon testified that it belongs to Levi, how could he afterward extract it from Levi? Why not answer that Shimon wants to preserve it for his own creditor? Shmuel says there that if Shimon's creditor later comes, he can take the land from Levi based on Shimon's prior chazakah, and Shimon's admission should not harm the creditor where it prejudices another. If the land belongs to Yehudah, however, Shimon's creditor has no way to reach it. This seems to show that even against a creditor, Shimon's admission that the land is not his has effect. There are several answers. First, as explained, there is a difference between a creditor who has already collected and one who has not. Second, the rule that the borrower cannot admit against the creditor applies where the borrower, purchaser, or creditor is still in possession of the land, so that "whatever is in a person's possession is presumed his" operates. But where another person is already in possession and the borrower merely has an ancestral claim, his admission that the possessor owns it is credible even though it harms a creditor. Otherwise almost every chazakah in the world would be nullified. If a person sold land, owed an oral debt, and died, the creditor could always come and collect from the possessor on the theory that the debtor's failure to protest, or even his express admission, cannot prejudice creditors. That cannot be. Therefore, when the land is no longer in the borrower's possession, he can admit the possessor's title even though that harms creditors. The Gemara's question is accordingly sound: once Shimon admitted that Levi owns the land, his creditor cannot remove it from Levi merely through Shimon's old ancestral claim. Shimon's admission is effective in that setting. There is another difficulty in the Gemara: why would Shimon testify merely to benefit his creditor? If Shimon has other land, the creditor can collect from him directly. If he has none, what difference does the disputed land make? The Gemara's answer elsewhere that a person does not wish to be a wicked borrower does not apply simply. Since Shimon has already admitted the land belongs to Levi, if his creditor nevertheless forcibly takes it from Levi in payment of Shimon's debt, Shimon would owe Levi compensation. His creditor's collection would therefore leave Shimon as a wicked borrower toward Levi. Thus the incentive still exists, and the Gemara remains coherent. Maharshal's broad ruling therefore requires study. The correct distinction appears to be as stated: if the creditor has already collected and possesses the land, the borrower cannot defeat him through a later admission. If the creditor has not collected and is not in possession, then even if we say claims may sometimes be made for him, the borrower can acknowledge another person's ownership, as Maharshal says. Mahari ben Lev likewise appears to support this distinction. See below in siman 248, seifim 7 and 10, where there is additional support.
He used it for one day. Once the heir himself then held the property for three years, that one day of the father's use joins the heir's years just as one year of the father and two of the son can combine. The reason the heir may still need three years here is only that he cannot prove he is an heir, so he needs the migo that he himself purchased the property. If his heir status is independently known, the father's one day can join the heir's years toward the three year chazakah. This is straightforward, and Mahari Ashkenazi writes similarly. The reverse is different. If the father held the land for three years and the heir only one day, the heir still needs proof that he is the heir. What migo does he have? His own possession lasted only one day. The Bach's statement that one day alone removes the need to prove inheritance is incorrect. Only where the heir himself possessed for three years does he have the migo that he purchased the land. Mahara Sasson writes this explicitly. Responsa Rosh, rule 99, initially considered requiring the heir at least to say, "My father told me he purchased it from you." It appears, however, that the Rosh ultimately retracted even that requirement, as seen from his proof from one who purchased a courtyard containing projecting structures and is presumed to own them. That is why the Rosh did not codify the proposed requirement.
And she sold it publicly, we leave the land in the purchaser's chazakah. The Sema, seif katan 23, infers from the Rama's order and wording that the purchaser need not himself possess the land for three years. This is correct where the woman herself already possessed it for three years. Ordinarily a married woman's possession may not establish chazakah, but if the husband heard that she publicly sold the land and did not protest, that is evidence that her prior possession was genuine. What difference does it make whether the purchaser also later held it? The chazakah derives from the woman's three years together with the public sale and the husband's silence. But if the woman herself did not possess the land for three years, we cannot say that the mere public sale proves ownership because the husband remained silent. He may have treated the transaction as foolishness and mockery because she had no established possession at all. We rule that a sale by document ordinarily has publicity, yet in the standard case of father one year, son one year, and purchaser one year, all three years are still required. We do not say that the purchaser's single year suffices merely because a public sale occurred and the owner failed to protest. One can distinguish levels of publicity, and Rashba's responsum may have involved a synagogue announcement, but this law has no direct Talmudic root and rests on Rashba's reasoning. We should therefore apply it only to the case he stated, where the woman had possessed the property for many years. Rashba never ruled that a public sale alone substitutes for three years of possession. This is clear.
