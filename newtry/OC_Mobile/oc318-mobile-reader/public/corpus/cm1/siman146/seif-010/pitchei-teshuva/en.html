And the law is. See Baer Heitev, which states that the purchaser or heir need not prove that he is indeed an heir or purchaser. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat explain that this is only where the heir or purchaser himself possessed the property for three years and the predecessor possessed it for at least one day, for then he has a migo that he could have claimed, 'I purchased it from you.' In the reverse case, where the predecessor possessed it for three years and the heir or purchaser for only one day, he lacks that migo and must prove his status as heir or purchaser; otherwise the challenger may say that the predecessor would have admitted that he was placed there merely to take the produce or in some similar capacity. This is also evident from the Rashba's responsum cited by the Beit Yosef. Lechem Mishneh understands the Maggid Mishneh to mean that even where we know that the claimant is the deceased's heir, he must prove that the property came to him specifically by inheritance, for perhaps the father had occupied it only as a sharecropper. According to that reading, even under the Ramban's view, in the reverse case just described the heir would have to prove not merely kinship but that the father left him this property as an inheritance. Responsa Beit David, Choshen Mishpat, siman 2, strongly rejects this interpretation of Lechem Mishneh, and Shitah Mekubetzet likewise indicates that proof of being a relative fit to inherit is sufficient, whereupon beit din advances the inherited claim on his behalf. See also the discussion concerning whether claims are advanced for a tenant.
And a creditor who collected land. Tumim and Netivot HaMishpat write that beit din advances claims for a creditor only where he has already taken possession. If he is still seeking to collect, we do not do so, because a creditor acquires the property only from the time of collection and is therefore seeking to extract it. Maharam of Brisk similarly reconciles two responsa of the Rosh: one saying that we advance claims for a creditor as for an heir or purchaser, and the other saying that a creditor is not comparable to them. Where the creditor has already descended to the debtor's property and taken possession, we do not remove it from him and we advance claims on his behalf. Where the creditor and the challenger arrive together, each seeking possession, the creditor is an extractor and must establish a clear and definite claim. Responsa Beit David clarifies that the 'possession' spoken of by Tumim and Maharam does not mean three years of chazakah, but an act of acquisition over the land. Once the creditor has made such a kinyan he is like a purchaser and claims are advanced for him. Thus, where the creditor had already locked the house with his own key, that was a complete act of chazakah under siman 192. Even if he acted only with respect to the house and not the adjacent open area, possession of one of several fields sold together acquires them all, and the same applies here because the debt corresponds to the entire property. Accordingly, his act concerning the house is effective also for the adjoining area, and beit din advances claims for him as for a purchaser.
That he bring witnesses that the seller lived there. Mishneh LaMelech cites Maharit that even without witnesses that the seller lived there for one day as owner, it is enough if witnesses or a deed establish that he sold the property to the present possessor. If it had not been his, Maharit argues, he would not have sold it, and this is even stronger than witnesses that he lived there one day. Mishneh LaMelech disputes this and leaves the matter requiring study. Ketzot HaChoshen and Shaar Mishpat likewise disagree with Maharit and conclude that even a purchaser holding a deed from the seller must prove that the seller occupied it for one day, which provides at least some evidence that it was his.
