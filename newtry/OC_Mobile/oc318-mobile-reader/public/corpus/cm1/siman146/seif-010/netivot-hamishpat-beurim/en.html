See Shach, seif katan 4, citing Mabit, that we do not advance claims for a tenant. In Mabit's case, a landlord rented premises after having opened a window for light while the tenant was already residing in the house. One authority ruled that this created a chazakah against the challenger, and Mabit objected for three reasons: there had not been three years; the landlord had not personally occupied the property for even one day beforehand, so even if a tenant were like a purchaser the chazakah would fail; and a tenant is not legally like a purchaser, so when the landlord is absent we do not plead on the tenant's behalf. These arguments are difficult. The tenant's chazakah is the landlord's chazakah, as the Gemara at the beginning of Perek Chezkas HaBatim states, "We rented and lived there." We plead for the landlord even in his absence when his chazakah is established through a tenant who lived there for three years. Surely no one would suggest that if a person possessed property for three years and then went overseas, another person could simply take the land because the owner was absent. We plead for an absent possessor just as for a purchaser or heir, just as we plead forgery or payment in their absence where appropriate. Mabit's claim that the landlord had not lived there even one day is likewise difficult, because Nimukei Yosef at the beginning of Perek Chezkas HaBatim writes that the combination of landlord and tenant does not require the landlord's prior one day residence. His objection that there were not three years is less difficult, since the first authority may have followed Rashbam that opening a window requires a claim but not three years, and therefore held that we plead for the absent landlord. In my view, one should not rely on the rule cited by the Shach from Mabit.
He requires no additional claim. See Shach, seif katan 7, who writes that the heir or purchaser need not prove that status. This applies where the heir or purchaser possessed the property for three years and the decedent or seller held it for one day. Then proof of the succession or purchase is unnecessary because the possessor has a migo that he could say, "I purchased it from you." But in the reverse case, where the decedent or seller held it for three years and the heir or purchaser only one day, he must prove that he is in fact the heir or purchaser. Otherwise the challenger can say that the seller or decedent admitted that he had been allowed into the property merely to consume its produce or for some similar limited purpose. Tumim rules likewise.
And the law of a purchaser. The same issue applies to a creditor. Tumim concludes that we plead for a creditor like a purchaser only after the creditor has already collected the land. If he has not yet collected it and merely comes to seize it for the debt, the debtor is believed to say that he never acquired the land and holds it only as a tenant, since it is known that the land formerly belonged to someone else. This is correct. It is not comparable to movable property found in the debtor's hands, where he is not believed to say it belongs to others, as explained in siman 99. Possession of movables itself proves ownership. With land there is no comparable physical possession, only the inference from the former owner's failure to object, and the debtor can say that the owner trusted him not to deny the true arrangement. Indeed, the debtor's present admission supports that explanation.
And she sold it publicly. See Sema, seif katan 23, who holds that even three years of possession are unnecessary. At first glance this is difficult. Why should this differ from any purchaser who buys publicly, where three years are still required? Tumim therefore rejects the Sema and requires three years. But even three years appears problematic, because a wife does not establish chazakah in her husband's property, so her possession is not equivalent to a seller having occupied the property for one day, and the purchaser himself cannot make a definite claim. It therefore appears that a sharecropper or craftsman who sells publicly does not confer any special advantage, for no posek says otherwise. A wife is different because she may sell property without beit din in order to collect her ketubah. If she is present and states that she is selling for her ketubah, her sale is effective, and even if the heirs claim that the ketubah was already paid they are not believed, because a claim against an established judicial obligation is ineffective. Thus, even when the property is now in a purchaser's hands and the wife is absent, or even if she admits something adverse, we can reason that if the land had not been subject to her ketubah the heirs would not have allowed her to sell it, since her sale could validly transfer title. With an ordinary purchaser, by contrast, publicity proves nothing. The owner can say, "I had no reason to protest your purported sale, because no one can sell property that is not his and your sale was meaningless." On this basis the Sema's ruling is also correct that three years are unnecessary.
