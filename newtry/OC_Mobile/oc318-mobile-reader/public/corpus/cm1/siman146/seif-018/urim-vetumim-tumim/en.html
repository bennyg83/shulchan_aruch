For he certainly gave it, etc., as a gift. The Shach in seif katan 22 questions the Rashba's derivation that the halachah follows Shmuel, who treats the money as a gift. After all, below in siman 373 we rule that where a buyer knew the property was not the seller's, the money is treated as a deposit, whereas only regarding one who betroths his own sister do we rule in Even HaEzer siman 50 that the money is a gift. The Rosh already explains that rule by the special fact that she is his sister. It appears that the Rashba reasoned differently. If kinship alone were decisive, then one who knowingly buys a field from his brother even though it is not the brother's should likewise be treated as having given the purchase money as a gift. Why distinguish brother from sister? No authority states such a rule. Moreover, if kinship were the reason, why do Rav and Shmuel formulate their dispute separately concerning betrothing one's sister and concerning knowingly buying property that does not belong to the seller? They could have disputed a single intermediate case, such as one who knowingly buys another's property from his brother versus from a stranger, from which both principles could be learned. Likewise, if the gift result in betrothing one's sister depends only on her being his sister, then where a man gives kiddushin to another woman with whom kiddushin cannot take effect, such as a married woman or a maidservant, the money should be treated as a deposit because she is unrelated to him. Yet the authorities do not draw such a distinction. The Rambam in chapter 6 of Hilchot Zechiyah writes that everyone knows kiddushin do not take effect with forbidden relations, implying that the same rule applies generally to all such forbidden women. The Maggid Mishneh likewise indicates that only in ordinary mistaken kiddushin is the money returned, not in these cases. Here too, if kinship were essential, the Gemara could have framed Rav and Shmuel's dispute as one who betroths his sister or a maidservant, thereby teaching both distinctions together. The Rashba therefore holds that wherever there is a plausible reason other than gift, we prefer it, because why should a person give away his property without cause? A person is naturally protective of his own property, and in this sense everyone is like a stranger to him. The Gemara's phrase that people do not simply give gifts to strangers expresses this general presumption, not a technical distinction between relatives and strangers. In the case of betrothing one's sister, however, no plausible non-gift purpose exists. It is difficult to say that he intended a deposit. There are many trustworthy people in the world with whom he could deposit money. Why would he specifically hand it to his sister under the guise of kiddushin? Therefore Shmuel says he must have intended a gift. By contrast, where one knowingly buys property that does not belong to the seller, there is a clear reason to classify the payment as a deposit connected to a real transaction. Meanwhile the buyer is using the field and consuming its produce as though it were his. If the true owner never appears, the buyer may effectively continue benefiting from it, and the seller cannot simply eject him. The Gemara and Rosh indicate as much. Even in an ordinary sale Shmuel holds that a person may knowingly purchase land for only a day. All the more so here, where the money will ultimately be returned if the true owner successfully claims the field. The buyer therefore gains useful possession in the meantime. We have no reason to assume an outright gift, especially because the presumption is that a person does not gratuitously give his money away. In kiddushin that cannot take effect, by contrast, the giver receives no practical benefit from treating the payment as a deposit under the guise of kiddushin. Therefore we say that he intended a gift. The Rashba's reasoning is thus sound, even if the Gemara's word "strangers" is somewhat imprecise in this broader sense. The same applies in the present case, where a person purchases what is already his. He gains nothing through the purchase because he would in any event remain in possession indefinitely, since the property is his. The case therefore closely resembles giving kiddushin to a woman with whom kiddushin cannot take effect. We rule like Shmuel that the money is a gift because no meaningful deposit purpose exists. The Rashba's words are thus well founded in the Gemara and the authorities.
Now, all of this refers to a case where the buyer did not expressly receive warranty at the time of the transaction. If the seller expressly accepted warranty, even Shmuel, who treats the money as a gift where the buyer knew the property was not the seller's, agrees that the payment is not a gift. Tosafot in Bava Metzia 17b write this, and the Rashba himself writes it later at length. The same applies here. How could one regard the money as a gift where the recipient expressly accepted liability to reimburse? The case must therefore be one of an ordinary transaction without explicit warranty. Although we generally rule that warranty is presumed even if omitted, that principle applies where the buyer did not know the defect. Where he knew the property was already his, as here, his payment is conclusively given as a gift absent an express warranty. This is clear and correct.
