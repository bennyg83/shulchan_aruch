As a gift. See Shach, seif katan 17, who questions this because we rule like Rav that the money is a deposit. In my view, Rama holds that here everyone agrees there is no concern that it was deposited. Yevamot 115 says that when one man's name is the same as another's, the first does not deposit money with him, because the second could later claim that the money is his. Here the purchaser gave the seller money before witnesses specifically as the price of a sale, and at that point the seller was believed on any claim he might make, as Rama writes. He need not even swear. Even if he had some claim that would not legally establish ownership, he could say that he bought the right to litigate. In such circumstances no one would entrust money to him merely as a deposit. The money must therefore have been given as a gift. This differs from one who knowingly buys property that is not the seller's, or one who gives kiddushin money to his sister. There the seller or recipient will certainly be obligated to return the money, and no alternative claim can save her. Even a claim of return would not help the sister, because she received it expressly for kiddushin. Since Shmuel holds that in such a case the money may have been a gift, the other opinion can reasonably say that perhaps the payer intended it as a deposit until he later sought return. Tumim writes that if the seller accepted warranty, the money cannot be considered a gift, comparing this to Tosafot in Bava Metzia 15 regarding a purchaser who knowingly buys property not belonging to the seller. In my view this is not comparable. There the seller's warranty gives the purchaser a real benefit if the land is taken from him, because the seller becomes liable for improvement, produce, and related losses. The purchaser also benefits from using the produce until dispossession. One can therefore say that he paid for those benefits rather than making a gift. Here, however, the purchaser buys what already belongs entirely to the seller. The purchaser receives no benefit at all from the warranty, and the seller has no separate obligation under the warranty to the person who actually owns the field. Therefore the poskim state without qualification that the money is a gift. As to Rama's ruling that the purchaser is believed to say that he made a mistake, although "I was mistaken" is generally a weak claim, here it is different because the seller himself admits that he had no legal basis at all. It is reasonable that the purchaser erred. If, however, the seller says that he had some arguable right, even one insufficient to prevail in law, and claims that the purchaser paid in order to acquire that litigation right, the seller is believed and the purchaser cannot prevail merely by saying, "I was mistaken," for that is a weak claim. See Sema, seif katan 44. His statement that the case concerns one where it is known that the purchaser bought twice is difficult. An admission by the seller together with the purchaser's mistake claim should suffice, because there is no migo where the purchaser cannot definitely claim that the money was a gift. Nor can Sema's remark be read merely as addressing the earlier clause in which the seller claims that he later repurchased the land. Even there the stated reason remains necessary to explain why no oath is required, since a migo alone would still require an oath.
