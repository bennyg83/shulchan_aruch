The Tur says that where a person possessed land without any accompanying ownership claim, the land returns to the challenger, who takes an oath and recovers. Bach derives the oath from the Mishnah's wording that possession without a claim is "not a possession," rather than saying it is entirely nothing. He understands this to mean that the possession cannot keep the land but still forces the challenger to swear that he never sold or waived it. A difficulty arises from the Gemara where one first said, "It belonged to my ancestors," and later tried to change his plea to, "It belonged to your ancestors and I bought it from you." Where his first words expressly excluded the challenger's ownership, the Gemara says he cannot retract. If an ordinary possession without a claim already required the challenger to swear, one might have expected the possessor still to retain enough procedural footing to modify his claim before the challenger swore. The answer is that the case of a bare possession differs. The owner may have sold or waived, and therefore a rabbinic or Mishnah oath can be required before land is returned. But where the possessor himself said, "It belonged to my ancestors and not yours," he effectively admitted that the challenger never sold or waived it to him. That admission removes the very doubt that would require an oath. He has therefore created an absolute liability and cannot later retract into a new claim.
