If he added one thirtieth. Rambam rules that if a renter loads an animal with one thirtieth more than the agreed weight and the animal dies, he is liable, even though animals of that class ordinarily can carry more. Ramban disagrees. If a donkey was hired to carry half a standard load and the renter loaded it up to the ordinary full load, the renter is not automatically a robber liable for every accident. He is liable only if it is established that the animal died because of the added burden. Beit Yosef treats Rosh's responsum as supporting Rambam. In my view Rosh actually fits Ramban. Rosh's case involved clear evidence that the particular animal was weak and died because the renter changed the agreed conditions, such as taking a mountain route instead of a valley route or making the animal return sooner than agreed. Ramban also agrees that where the death is shown to have resulted from the unauthorized change, the renter is liable. Nimukei Yosef states the rule precisely. If one loads beyond the ordinary carrying capacity by at least one thirtieth, he is liable for later death even if the immediate cause is unknown, because he has wrongfully subjected the animal to a burden beyond its normal use. If he merely exceeds the private agreement but remains within ordinary carrying capacity, he is liable only when the death is shown to have been caused by the excess. The one-thirtieth threshold itself still matters even when causation is certain. The Gemara's law of adding a kav to a porter indicates that below one thirtieth the added load can be too slight to create legal liability, even if injury follows. Thus Nimukei Yosef's statement about less than ordinary capacity should be understood as at least one thirtieth above the agreed load. If he exceeds the ordinary capacity by that amount, he is like one who wrongfully uses the animal beyond its normal function and becomes liable even for accidents. This reconciles Ramban, Rosh, and Nimukei Yosef.
