A porter who added to his load. In chapter HaOmanin, 80b, the Gemara discusses a case where the worker could not throw off the load. Abaye explains the case where he collapsed immediately, while otherwise the employer may have thought that he had merely been seized by weakness. Shitah Mekubetzet cites Ramban: I am astonished. Is the employer a guardian of the worker's body that he must pay for his injury? Even if one rents another's slave, he is exempt from negligence. And if you say that these are damages caused to a person, this is difficult, because the damage did not come from the employer's force, even if he was the one who loaded him. Beit Yosef cites this question in the name of Rivan and leaves it unresolved. Shitah Mekubetzet, however, cites Ritva on Rav Ashi's explanation, that the worker thought he had merely been seized by weakness. Ritva asks that even so, the worker misled himself, and why should the other person be liable? He answers that the employer caused the worker's mistake, because the worker assumed that the employer had spoken truthfully. Since the worker was injured based on the employer's statement and reliance upon him, this is direct causation, like one who shows a coin to a moneychanger, who says it is valid when in fact it is not, and similar cases. This appears to be the reasoning of Ramah cited by the Tur, who writes that it is reasonable that the employer pays only damages, but not the other four categories of bodily injury unless he injured him directly. Since this is not direct bodily injury but liability through dina d'garmi, he is liable only for the damage itself and not for the other four categories. But where the worker collapsed immediately under the load, liability extends to the other four categories as well. See what I wrote in siman 332, seif katan 3.
And if the donkey is normally available for rental, he is not considered a robber. Terumat HaDeshen, siman 316, discusses Reuven, who had a horse in his house and went to the market. Shimon came to Reuven's house and took the horse to ride quickly in order to collect a debt that would certainly be lost if he did not ride immediately. The debt was worth approximately one hundred litrin and no other horse was available. Reuven's wife told Shimon not to take the horse at all because her husband also needed to ride it immediately for a business matter. Shimon asked the value of the business matter. She said ten litrin. He replied that he needed the horse for one hundred litrin and would immediately pay for Reuven's business loss if it occurred. He therefore took the horse, intending also to pay the rental fee, since the horse was ordinarily rented to anyone. The horse then suffered an unavoidable accident on the road. Terumat HaDeshen initially writes that Shimon would appear liable, because Reuven's wife told him that her husband needed the horse and had no intention to rent it now. Thus he is a renter without the owner's knowledge, who is called a robber just as one who borrows without permission is treated as a robber, since at this time Reuven certainly did not consent to the rental. He concludes, however, that Shimon took it with legal permission, because beit din authorizes such conduct under the enactment that if one person's barrel of honey breaks, another may pour out his wine to save the honey and receive payment for the wine. Shimon therefore has the status of a renter, who is exempt for unavoidable accidents. Rama understood from Terumat HaDeshen's language, that the wife said her husband needed it and had no intention to rent it now, that if Shimon had taken it without the owner's knowledge and no one had protested, he would not be a robber because the horse ordinarily stood for rental. In my opinion, however, this exemption applies only to an unavoidable accident, because he is not treated as a robber when the animal ordinarily stands for rental. If he took it to save his own property, even an express protest does not make him liable for an unavoidable accident because he acts with beit din's permission. But if the animal dies as a result of the work, he is liable. Although an ordinary renter is exempt when the animal dies because of its work, one who took it without permission, even if it ordinarily stands for rental, is liable. This is proven in chapter HaGozel Kama, 97a, concerning one who forcibly takes another's boat. Rav says the owner may take either the rental or the depreciation, while Shmuel says he may take only the depreciation. The Gemara first distinguishes between a boat normally rented out and one not normally rented out, and alternatively establishes both rulings where it was normally rented, distinguishing between one who descended with intent to pay rental and one who did not. It follows that even where the boat ordinarily stands for rental and he intended to rent it, the owner may take the depreciation when it exceeds the rental. Rambam rules this way in chapter 3 of Hilchot Gezeilah, and so does Shulchan Aruch, siman 363. The same therefore applies to a donkey normally rented out that deteriorated through the work: if the rental exceeds the depreciation, the owner may take the rental, and if the depreciation exceeds the rental, he may take the depreciation. Ramah's statement that he is not a robber therefore means only that he is not liable for unavoidable accidents. But for deterioration or death caused by the work, he does not have the law of an ordinary renter and must pay. Rashi's language concerning a boat that ordinarily stands for rental, where the owner may take the depreciation, is that since he took it against the owner's will he is a robber. This implies that he is literally a robber and would be liable even for unavoidable accidents. This requires study.
