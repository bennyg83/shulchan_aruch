In the two-year-old finale, Dickel said, “And the Rambam and the author wrote land in the place of Dial d’Émile al-Qatah, but because of these marches that belong to the small of his land and the oppressor, it is impossible to buy land in them until our lie exists, and we have some places in the land of which we eat.”
Some say a Torah scroll. Meaning, the minors may study from it. Their study is the produce of the Torah scroll, while the principal remains for them until they mature. In the Derishah I asked why this differs from the Gemara concerning one who receives a deposit from minors, where we do not return the deposit directly to the minor. There too one opinion says to purchase a palm tree and another says a Torah scroll, yet the Tur rules there to buy a palm tree. One can answer that here the money is certainly the minor's. We refrain from handing it to him only because he may squander it. It can therefore be in his best interest to purchase a Torah scroll for him to study from, even if use may cause some deterioration, because it is his own property and this use benefits him. In the deposit case, however, there is concern that the true owner may later appear and identify the property. Therefore the principal should be preserved through purchase of a palm tree rather than an item subject to wear. The Rambam's rulings remain difficult. Here he rules like the opinion to buy a palm tree, while in Hilchot Pikadon he allows purchase of a Torah scroll or palm tree from which the child enjoys the produce. Perhaps the distinction is that here the money has not yet entered the minor's possession, and beit din should not itself cause the principal to diminish by buying a Torah scroll and handing it to him. In the deposit case the minor had already possessed the money and the custodian removed it only out of concern for returning lost property if the true owners later appear. There is therefore less reason to be stringent, and one may buy even a Torah scroll for study.
