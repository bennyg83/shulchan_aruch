One who injures his children, etc. See Responsa Shvut Yaakov, part III, siman 140, concerning a teacher who became angry with his student and struck him for purposes of instruction, injuring him, and whether he is liable for the four heads of damages or exempt. He answers that it appears obvious that he is exempt from all of them. Makkot teaches, regarding the verse concerning chopping wood, that this refers to an optional act and excludes a father striking his son, a teacher disciplining his student, and an agent of beit din. One cannot say that this Scriptural derivation exempts them only from exile for accidental killing while leaving liability for the four heads of damages, because the Gemara itself explains that these persons are exempt because they are performing a mitzvah. For that same reason they should be exempt from all liability, just as one who runs on Friday at twilight for the needs of Shabbat is exempt. Moreover, the Gemara treats these three categories alike, and an agent of beit din is exempt even if he causes injury, as explained by the Beit Yosef at the end of siman 8 and by the gloss to the Shulchan Aruch there, without dissent. This is also clear from Terumat HaDeshen, siman 218, cited above in siman 421, seif 13 in the gloss, that the rule is not limited to one's son or student. Rather, one may strike anyone subject to one's authority in order to separate him from transgression, and certainly for neglect of Torah study. Although we rule that one should strike a student only lightly, even with something comparable to a shoelace, and not cruelly, as stated in Shulchan Aruch, Yoreh Deah 245:10, nevertheless after the fact the teacher should not be fined merely because he exceeded that measure. Although Responsa Raanach, part I, siman 111, writes that an agent of beit din who struck someone out of anger and wrath is liable, as cited by Knesset HaGedolah above in siman 8, nevertheless, in the heat of Torah instruction a teacher's temper may flare, especially because it is deeply distressing to teach a student who does not apply himself. He should therefore be exempt from all liability. Still, so that he not become accustomed to acting in this manner and as a protective measure, I ruled that he must pay the physician's fee. These are his words; see there. According to his approach, the laws in this seif concerning one who injures his children must refer to a case in which he did not strike them for disciplinary purposes. Responsa Kiryat Channah, siman 22, however, does not write this way, as I cited briefly in Pitchei Teshuva to Yoreh Deah 245. In his view, the present seif applies even when the father struck the child in order to discipline him. The teaching that excludes “a father who strikes his son” concerns only exemption from exile, whereas with respect to the four heads of damages, accidental conduct was included together with intentional conduct. He discusses this at length; see there. The matter requires study.
